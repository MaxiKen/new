# Sūrah Luqmān (Chapter 31) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After a Man, Not an Event**

Most sūrahs of the Qur'an are named after something that anchors their content: an event (al-Baqarah, "The Cow," from the story at its heart), a day (al-Qāriʿah, al-Ḥāqqah), a prophetic figure whose narrative dominates the chapter (Hūd, Yūsuf, Ibrāhīm, Nūḥ, Muḥammad ﷺ in name if not in narrative). Sūrah Luqmān belongs to that last, relatively rare class — and yet it is unusual even among them. The sūrah does not recount Luqmān's biography at all. It quotes him, for eight verses, speaking to his son. No battle, no miracle, no court of a king. The chapter is named after a man because of what came out of his mouth: a father's counsel, preserved in revelation for fourteen centuries of fathers and sons. Already this tells the reader what kind of chapter this is. Its subject is not the drama of nations but the quiet, decisive drama of a household, a conscience, and a transmission of wisdom from one generation to the next.

The name also carries a lesson in what the Qur'an considers honorable. Luqmān is described in the tradition as a man of Africa — in most reports an Ethiopian or Nubian, in many accounts a freed slave, in some dark-skinned and physically unimpressive by the standards of worldly prestige, with, it is said, cracked feet and a nose that drew the teasing of passers-by. He is not known for a kingdom, an army, a fortune, or a famous lineage; a minority of the early exegetes held him to be a prophet, while the majority, following the tone of the verse itself, held him to be a righteous sage (*ḥakīm*) to whom God granted understanding. That a chapter of the Qur'an should bear the name of such a man is itself a preached argument: in God's economy, rank is a function of wisdom, speech, and conduct — not of blood, color, or office. The Prophet ﷺ said precisely this about the scale God uses:

> *"God does not look at your appearances or your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Date, Setting, and the Company It Keeps**

The sūrah is Makkan by the consensus of the early authorities, revealed in the middle Makkan period, and the classical listings place it immediately after Sūrat al-Rūm (chapter 30) — which is also its position in the canonical order of the muṣḥaf. That adjacency is meaningful and will be tracked through the commentary below. Chapter 30 ended by telling the Prophet that the dead cannot hear and that the deaf cannot be made to hear their call (30:52): the preacher is not responsible for hearts that will not open. Chapter 31 opens by handing the reason for that deafness: *lahw al-ḥadīth*, "idle discourse" (v. 6), the purchased distraction that competes with revelation for the ear. And where 30:52 ended in rebuke, 31 begins in comfort — the Book is "guidance and mercy for the virtuous" (v. 3). The transition is deliberate: after the thunder of divine warning comes the whisper of a father in a Madinan, indeed a world, home.

A minority opinion in the classical literature holds that certain verses of this sūrah — most often vv. 27–29, sometimes vv. 6–7 — were revealed in Madinah; these are the verses that touch on the dispute with the People of the Book. The reports of the occasional verses (asbāb al-nuzūl) below will show why: the question of whether knowledge is "little" or "much" was argued in Madinah's interreligious atmosphere. The sound and widely held position, however, remains that the sūrah as a whole is Makkan, and it reads as such: its argument is creedal, its imagery is cosmic, its address is to a society that still conceded God's authorship of creation while worshipping other beings beside Him.

In the standard division of the Qur'an into thirty equal parts (ajzā'), Sūrat Luqmān begins the twenty-first part, opening the stretch of the Qur'an that will carry the reader through the sūrahs of moral psychology and steady, compact argument. It consists, by all readings, of thirty-four verses. Its vocabulary is spare and its structure is architectural, and the combination is why generations of teachers made it the first long sūrah children memorized after the short ones: it is a complete syllabus of belief, worship, character, and cosmology in thirty-four lines.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The sūrah is best studied as a set of four movements, and this commentary will keep the movements in view while treating each verse individually.

*First movement (vv. 1–5): the Book and its readers.* Disconnected letters, the Wise Book, and a portrait of the believers defined by three things — prayer, alms, and certainty about the Hereafter. Verse 5 ends with the word the whole sūrah is pursuing: *falāḥ*, thriving, success.

*Second movement (vv. 6–11): the counter-book and its readers.* Idle discourse, mockery, arrogance, turning away — and then the creation-argument in verses 10–11, the heavens without pillars, the mountains, the beasts, the plants, and the sharp question: "Show me what those apart from Him have created."

*Third movement (vv. 12–19): Luqmān's counsel, the heart of the sūrah.* Wisdom given, and then nine verses of pedagogy: gratitude, tawḥīd as the first command, parents, the mustard seed in the rock, prayer, enjoining right and forbidding wrong, patience, the anatomy of arrogance in walk and glances, and moderation in pace and voice. A complete education arranged in the right order: God first, then the family, then the self, then society, then the soul's manners.

*Fourth movement (vv. 20–34): the world as evidence and the horizon of the Hour.* Subservient creation and blessings outward and inward; the pathology of inherited religion; the handhold; the consolation of the preacher; the argument from the conscience of the idolaters; the sea of ink that cannot exhaust God's words; the ease of resurrection; day, night, sun, moon, and ships; the Truth and the false; the storm as a parable of the human heart; and finally, in verse 34, the five locks whose keys no human being holds — the Hour, the rain, the wombs, tomorrow's earnings, and the land of one's death.

The movement is not decorative. It moves from the Book, to the alternative to the Book, to wisdom lived in a home, to the universe that testifies to what the home teaches. Scripture, character, and cosmos: three witnesses agreeing. The final verse closes the sūrah with divine unknowability exactly as the opening verses opened with God's unchallengeable speech — the circle shut.

**Why a Father's Voice?**

The Qur'an could have delivered this content in the first person. Instead it embeds it in a human parent speaking to a human child, and the choice carries instruction of its own. Truth spoken by a father is truth in the form that survives; creeds are not transmitted by arguments alone but by households. Luqmān's method is also a curriculum: he begins with God, not with rules; he grounds the first command in a warning about injustice rather than in a threat about hell; he uses the smallest measurable object — a mustard seed — to teach an infinite God; he teaches his son about worship and social responsibility in a single breath (v. 17), and then immediately, in the very next breath, teaches him about humility (vv. 18–19), because a person who enjoins right and forbids wrong is in permanent danger of conceit. The whole sequence is an antidote to religious pride: serve, correct, and then walk gently and speak softly.

A striking feature will be noticed verse by verse: Luqmān never says "I command." He says "O my son" (*yā bunayya*), the diminutive of endearment. Authority in this sūrah is exercised as affection. That is why the chapter has remained, to our own day, the natural text for weddings, for the naming of children, for fathers' letters, and for the last counsels of the dying.

**Themes That Will Run Through the Commentary**

*Tawḥīd as an ethics, not only a doctrine.* Verse 13 calls shirk "a tremendous wrong" (*ẓulm ʿaẓīm*). The sūrah frames idolatry not primarily as a metaphysical error but as an injustice — a misplacement of what is owed, harming the self, the family, and the world.

*Gratitude as the master-key.* The sūrah opens the Luqmān section with "Give thanks to God" (v. 12), links it to thanking parents (v. 14), and closes the cosmic section with "signs for all who are patient, thankful" (v. 31). Between those brackets thankfulness is analyzed as the only rational response to unearned gift.

*God's names in service of the argument.* Self-Sufficient and Praised (vv. 12, 26), Subtle and Aware (v. 16), Mighty and Wise (v. 9), Hearing and Seeing (v. 28), Knowing and Aware (v. 34). Each pair is chosen to do a specific work in the verse, and the commentary will make that explicit.

*Scale.* A mustard seed, a rock, the heavens, the earth, an ink of seven seas: this sūrah is preoccupied with tiny and huge, and it dissolves the difference before God. This is the psychological hinge of the whole chapter — because the man who believes nothing escapes the Subtle One will behave when unseen as he behaves when seen, and that, in the end, is taqwā.

*Human responsibility and the limit of knowledge.* Verse 34 is the sūrah's seal and one of the Qur'an's great statements of epistemic humility. Against the pretensions of soothsayers, astrologers, and every modern over-confidence in forecast, the chapter ends by fencing off five regions of knowledge as God's alone — while making clear that what lies inside the fence is precisely what a human being does not need in order to be righteous.

*The Hereafter as the reason seriousness is possible.* Certainty of the Hereafter (v. 4), the promise in truth (v. 9), the return and the informing (vv. 15, 23), the day no parent avails his child (v. 33). Eschatology here is not morbid decoration; it is the mechanism that gives weight to small deeds.

**Reading Rules for This Commentary**

What follows is generated verse by verse — every verse treated separately and at full length, with the grouped passages of the source (vv. 13–14 and vv. 25–26) opened into individual treatments. Each section works through the Arabic vocabulary and morphology, the cross-references inside the Qur'an, the transmitted hadith and its source, the reports of asbāb al-nuzūl and the views of the classical exegetes (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others as they are reached in the chain of transmission), the doctrinal questions of ʿaqīdah, divine justice, human freedom and the Hereafter, and then the psychological, social and practical reach of the verse in ordinary life. Qur'anic, prophetic, and scholarly quotations appear as block quotations, and where an equivalence is my own reasoning rather than a transmitted position it will be marked as such. The reader with this project's commentary on al-Fātiḥah and al-Baqarah beside him will find the present chapter deliberately continuous with them: the same terms, the same standards of evidence, and cross-references to the earlier treatment where a verse has already been worked out at length.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:1

> **Alif. Lām. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Three Letters, a Sixth Time**

*Alif. Lām. Mīm.* The reader who has come this far in the Qur'an has met these three letters before, and that is precisely part of their design. Of the 114 sūrahs, twenty-nine begin with letters spelled out as letters — the *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the "disconnected," "cut-off," or "broken" letters, so called because they stand unattached to any word that would give them a syntactic job. Five of those twenty-nine open with exactly this combination, *Alif Lām Mīm*: the Cow (2:1), the Family of ʿImrān (3:1), the Spider (29:1), the Byzantines (30:1), Luqmān (31:1), and Prostration (32:1). Five sūrahs, of which the first three are among the very longest in the Book and the fourth and fifth sit consecutively at the hinge of the Qur'an's middle. Luqmān's letters therefore do not fall in isolation: they fall in a series, and the series has a family resemblance. Al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān establish the Book and the covenant-community; al-ʿAnkabūt establishes the testing of belief; al-Rūm establishes God's control of history; Luqmān establishes wisdom; al-Sajdah establishes the certainty of resurrection. Read in sequence, *Alif Lām Mīm* behaves like a recurring signature over the Qur'an's foundational argument: the alphabet of human speech, placed in front of the claims of revelation, as if to say that what follows is made of the reader's own language and yet exceeds it.

The commentary on 2:1 in this project worked through the range of positions in detail — the challenge theory (the Arabs were defeated on their own ground, the letters of their own tongue), the "only God knows" theory, the oath theory, the letter-numerology (*jafr*) readings, and al-Ṭabarī's careful conclusion that every defensible meaning may be held as *possible* while the intended meaning is reserved with God. Nothing of that needs re-arguing here; what belongs here is what *this* *Alif Lām Mīm* does, at the head of *this* sūrah.

**Opening the Sūrah of the Tongue with Bare Speech**

The first observation is structural, and it is more than an elegant flourish. Sūrat Luqmān will devote its most quoted passages to the ethics of speech. A father will teach his son not to raise his voice (v. 19), because "the loudest of voices is the braying of asses." The sūrah will condemn *lahw al-ḥadīth*, "idle discourse" (v. 6), the competing audio of distraction. It will call the Qur'an "the signs of the Wise Book" (v. 2) and will compare all the trees as pens and all the oceans as ink to the Words of God (v. 27). Its subject, from beginning to end, is what words do — to a soul, to a child, to a civilization, to a claim on truth.

Into that sūrah God places, as the first thing the mouth produces, three words that say nothing in the ordinary sense. The effect is to re-tune the reader's instrument before the music. A listener who opens a chapter on wisdom expecting maxims instead receives the material out of which maxims are made — letters, uncombined, waiting. In a chapter that will insist on the gravity of speech, the opening speech is deliberately, conspicuously *withheld meaning*: a demonstration that in the Qur'an even the atoms of language are carrying weight, and that a believer's relationship to this Book is not the relationship of a reader to information but of a servant to a mystery that speaks.

There is a corollary for the ears. These letters are recited, not translated; they are transmitted in the muṣḥaf in exactly the letters they were written with; and the sound one makes for *alif* is not the sound one makes for the letter's name in a dictionary lesson, since *alif* here is pronounced as the bare vowel-bearing stroke *a*, while its name adds syllables. That tiny fact — that the recited form differs from the naming form — trained Muslim readers for centuries in the distinction between a word as a tool and a word as a thing. In an age of information overload, that lesson is not antiquated. The opening of Luqmān tells the reader that before he extracts anything from this Book, he will have to *sound* it out, in the most literal sense.

**Alif, Lām, Mīm and the Mustard Seed**

The second observation concerns the sūrah's obsession with scale. Luqmān will hold up the smallest seed anybody in that market could have imagined — a mustard seed, *ḥabbah min khardal* — and place it on one side of a balance with the heavens, a rock buried in the earth, and the whole of human history, and God will bring it forth (v. 16). The theological point of that verse is that for the Subtle, the Aware, there is no small.

Letters are the mustard seeds of language. Nothing is smaller that still carries meaning; a single letter, in Arabic, can change a word from mercy to curse, from promise to threat. The Arabic language, with its root-and-pattern morphology, is exceptionally conscious of this: *k-t-b* produces writing, book, desk, author, copy, subscription; remove or replace one letter and the family changes. A sūrah about the weight of the smallest things and the One who overlooks nothing begins, therefore, with the smallest units of the very thing it is made of. If we are permitted to say what the rhetoric is doing rather than to claim we know what God intended, the letters function here as an enacted parable of the sūrah's own doctrine: as the meaning of a whole sentence can hide inside a single letter, so the balance of a whole life can turn on a single act so small that nobody, not even the doer, thought to write it down.

**What the Early Community Said, and Why They Stopped Short**

The reverence with which these letters were treated is documented from the first generation. The sayings transmitted from Abū Bakr and ʿAlī about the "secret" and the "quintessence" of the Book — cited in our commentary on 2:1 — belong here too, as does the striking fact that no authenticated practice of the Companions involved explaining the letters in sermons as though their meaning were settled. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās is reported in the chains the exegetes gathered with readings of these letters as divine names or attributes, including, for *Alif Lām Mīm*, senses such as "I am God, the All-Knowing"; al-Ṭabarī transmits such reports alongside his preferred position while refusing to bind the community to any of them, and Ibn Kathīr, after cataloguing the interpretations in his Tafsīr, closes with the same phrase he applies at the head of al-Baqarah and here: *Allāhu aʿlam bi-ṣawābih* — "God knows best what is meant." Al-Qurṭubī gathers the interpretive options as a list to be surveyed, not a ladder to be climbed, and al-Suyūṭī, in his science of the Qur'anic sciences, will later count the opinions on the letters into the dozens and observe that their common ground is their function: to arrest attention, to announce the text, and to humble the intellect.

What the tradition did *with* the letters is instructive. They were taught to children first, since a child learns to sound before it learns to understand, and the sound of *Alif Lām Mīm* is thus among the first words a Muslim mouth ever shapes in revelation. They were written large in manuscripts, where the opening letters of the great sūrahs are often gilded or extended in a line of their own, because the copyists felt something in them that did not invite interpretation but did invite display. They were preserved in prayer, since in the practice of the congregational prayer the imām begins audible units with al-Fātiḥah and the sūrah after it, so on ordinary days a Muslim's ears are washed in these letters two and three times a day for a lifetime. A community does not do these things with a problem to be solved. It does them with a presence to be honored.

**The Discipline of Not Knowing**

It would be a mistake to treat the honest answer — "God knows best" — as an abdication. Its function is the opposite: it installs, at the very start of a sūrah about wisdom, an act of intellectual self-restraint, which the sūrah's own definition of wisdom requires. Luqmān's counsel to his son ends, as we will see, in an admission that five regions of knowledge are simply closed to human beings (v. 34). A sage who begins with what he cannot know and ends with what nobody can know is teaching one continuous lesson: wisdom is not the accumulation of answers, it is the correct ordering of the mind before what exceeds it. The disconnected letters at the head of this sūrah give the reader his first exam, and the passing mark is not a clever reading but a composed silence: here is speech from God in which even the alphabet is under His management, and the reader's first act is to let the letters be what they are before making them say what he likes.

Practically, that is also why these verses are read as a preparation rite before study. In the traditional classroom, a circle gathered over a book of wisdom would often recite the opening of the sūrah and pause on the letters, letting the noise of the day fall away. The pause is the point. Attention is the faculty the entire sūrah is trying to recover — the distracted man of verse 6 has purchased "idle discourse" precisely at the price of attention, and the wise man of verse 12 has been granted wisdom precisely because he pays attention where it matters: to God, to his son, to a mustard seed, to the two years of a mother's exhaustion. *Alif. Lām. Mīm.* stands at the door of that education and asks the one thing the whole chapter will reward: come in with your attention, not with your conclusions.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:2

> **These are the signs of the Wise Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"These": The Finger Pointed at Something Already in Hand**

The demonstrative *hādhihi* — "these" — does the same work the *dhālika* of 2:2 does, in reverse direction. Al-Baqarah pointed at the Book as though from afar, in the register of exaltation: "*That* is the Book, in which there is no doubt." Here the pointing is close and plural: "*these*" — that is, the verses being recited right now, in front of the listeners, audible in the Prophet's own voice. The Qur'an repeatedly validates the Qur'an in this way before asking anything of its audience. It does not open by demanding belief in an abstraction; it says, in effect, *these* — the sentences you are hearing, the words you could stop me on and repeat back. What is being authenticated is present to the senses.

That grounding matters in a sūrah that will spend its later verses arguing from what is present to the senses: the heavens you see (v. 10), the beasts spread about (v. 10), the plants sent down with rain (v. 10), the ships on the sea (v. 31), the turnover of night and day (v. 29). This is a chapter that reasons from observation, and it announces that method in verse 2 by anchoring its first claim in observed recitation. The verse 2 opening and the verse 10 "Have you not considered?" belong to one epistemology: the revealed sign and the created sign are read by the same faculty, and that faculty is *baṣīrah*, insight — sight with understanding in it.

**Āyāt: One Word, Two Registers**

*Āyāt*, "signs," is the plural of *āyah*. In ordinary Qur'anic usage it means three closely related things: a verse of revelation; a portent or miracle granted to a prophet (the she-camel of Ṣāliḥ is an *āyah*, 54:27; the staff of Moses, the flood, the locusts); and an evidence in nature pointing beyond itself (the alternation of night and day, the rain, the embryo). Al-Zamakhsharī and after him the rhetoricians liked to note that the primary lexical sense of *āyah* is something *known by which what is unknown is known* — a sign in the semiotic sense, a mark on a road, a token on a contract. A sign does not draw attention to itself; it draws attention to what it signals. An *āyah* that is treated as the destination rather than the indication has stopped functioning as an *āyah*.

Set against the whole sūrah, that distinction becomes an argument. Here, *these* are the *āyāt* of the Wise Book (v. 2). Then, after the creation-argument, the sūrah says of the ships sailing by God's blessing: "in that are *āyāt* for every patient, thankful one" (v. 31). And it warned in v. 7 about the man "when Our *āyāt* are recited to him, he turns away as if he had not heard them." The same word travels through the chapter: verses recited, signs in creation, and a human being's response to both. The verse in front of us is thus a small piece of the sūrah's total theology of knowledge — God speaks in sentences and God speaks in stars, and the person who is deaf to the first will not learn from the second, because it is not volume that is lacking but attention and humility.

**"Wise": Ḥakīm as Description of a Text**

The epithet is unusual and the classical commentators pause on it, because *ḥakīm* applied to a Book must be handled carefully: a Book does not have a mind. Arabic, however, attributes a quality to a thing by naming the quality it produces or the quality of its source. The word *ḥakīm* in the Qur'an describes God ("the Wise," *al-Ḥakīm*, appearing dozens of times), a man who is given wisdom (Luqmān, 31:12; David and Solomon, 38:20; and, as a general rule, "*He grants wisdom to whom He wills*," 2:269), and here a Book. The exegetes extracted two main senses from the root *ḥ-k-m*, both of which the sūrah's own cross-references support.

First, *ḥakīm* in the sense of *muḥkam*: firmly set, precise, free of defect, "determined," as in 11:1:

> *"A Book whose verses are made firm (uḥkimat), then detailed, from the Hand of One Wise, Aware."* (Qur'an 11:1)

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both record this reading here, and Ibn Kathīr adds the consequence: a Book whose structure and content have been made firm is a Book that admits no correction from outside — which is the sense in which 41:42 is brought forward, "*falsehood does not come upon it from between its hands nor from behind it*, a revelation from One Wise, Praiseworthy" (41:42–43). The metaphor of a building whose load-bearing walls cannot be tampered with is not decorative. It says the Book's wisdom is structural, not merely stylistic: it does not need supplementing, and it cannot be repaired by its readers.

Second, *ḥakīm* in the sense of *muḥkim*: that which *makes others wise*, a Book with wisdom in it the way a medicine has healing in it — efficacious. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss, as reported here in the source, runs in exactly this direction: the Qur'an is called *ḥakīm* because it *faṣṣala* and *bayyana*, because it distinguishes and makes plain. And the practical corollary is stated constantly by the jurists: a wise speaker does not waste words, and a wise law does not command pointlessly. This is why the classical theory of the *maqāṣid*, the purposes of revelation, was built on verses of this kind. Al-Shāṭibī (d. 790/1388), in his *al-Muwāfaqāt*, argued that the whole of the Shariah resolves, in the final accounting, into the protection of the essentials of human life — religion, life, intellect, lineage, and property — and that a reader who cannot find the wisdom in a ruling has not concluded his inquiry, only paused it. "The Wise Book," read this way, is not a claim of authority but an offer of intelligibility: submit, and then investigate, and the investigation will not disappoint you.

**The Mother of the Book and the Book on Earth**

The source notes that "the Mother of the Book" — the heavenly archetype of revelation, or the source from which revelation comes — is likewise described as wise: "*It is in the Mother of the Book with Us, exalted, wise*" (43:4). The parallel is not incidental. The Qur'an's wisdom is not a property acquired in its transmission; it belongs to it in its origin. A text that descends carries its author's care with it. This is also why the sūrah will not permit the Book to be treated as one discourse among others, a competitor in the market of *lahw al-ḥadīth*, "idle discourse" (v. 6). The opposition being staged in verses 2–7 is not sacred against secular but wise discourse against pointless discourse — discourse that builds a person against discourse that only passes time. Verse 39:23, quoted in the source at v. 6, names the alternative precisely:

> *"God has sent down the most beautiful of discourse (ḥadīth), a Book consistent, reiterating, from which the skins of those who fear their Lord shiver; then their skins and their hearts settle at the remembrance of God."* (Qur'an 39:23)

Note that the same word, *ḥadīth*, means "discourse" there and is the second half of "idle discourse" here. The Qur'an is *ḥadīth*; it is *the best ḥadīth*; and the man of v. 6 buys a different *ḥadīth*. The chapter is asking its hearers to audit their listening. What did you purchase today with your hours? What is the shape of your consumption? That is a verse-by-verse question of this sūrah, and it is posed before a single command is issued.

**A Definite Claim in a Plural World**

Finally, the phrase sets the tone for the whole chapter's claim structure. Elsewhere the Qur'an describes itself as guidance *to* a people, or as a reminder *for the worlds* (81:27). Here, in the third verse of a sūrah named after a sage, it is *the signs of the Wise Book* — a definite article on both nouns, no hedge, no "perhaps." This is the register commentators call *qatʿ*, decisiveness, and it is worth pausing on not to marvel at the claim but to notice the honesty of the test. A text that claims wisdom invites the only verification that could matter: does acting on it make people wise? Fourteen centuries of moral psychology, legal history, and ordinary household piety are, in Islamic self-understanding, the laboratory record of that claim. The sūrah itself, as we will see at vv. 12–19, hands the reader the specimen: a man made wise by this kind of teaching, and a son made a man by his father. The Book is called wise before a single one of its instructions is given. Wisdom will be the thing measured, and the measuring instrument is the life lived under it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:3

> **guidance and mercy for the virtuous,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Nouns Without a Verb**

*hudaw wa-raḥmatun li-l-muttaqīn.* The phrase has no verb, no "We made it," no "We sent it as." Two nouns stand after the sentence that identified the Book, and the effect is to make the description of the Book's function as stable as the description of its essence. The grammar is doing something rhetorically precise, and both readings of the case-ending recorded by the exegetes confirm it rather than weaken it. Read in the nominative, the words are in apposition to what precedes — "these are the signs of the Wise Book: guidance and mercy for the virtuous" — so that guidance and mercy become part of *what the Book is*, not merely what it does. Read in the accusative as the object of purpose (*mafʿūl la-ajlih*), which al-Ṭabarī and Ibn al-Jawzī both record as the other viable parsing, the sense is "We made these signs to be guidance and mercy," so that guidance and mercy become *why the Book was sent*. Nothing is lost by choosing: the Book either is guidance by nature or was made for guidance by design, and in either case the reader is being told that this text is not neutral material. It moves. It has a direction. It either accomplishes or is intended to accomplish — and if it does not accomplish in a given reader, the diagnosis must look for the obstacle on the reader's side.

The pairing itself is a mini-doctrine. *Hudā* answers the question of knowledge: which way? *Raḥmah* answers the question of capacity: can I walk it? Guidance without mercy would be an instruction manual delivered to a wounded person. Mercy without guidance would be sentimentality that leaves the wounded person bleeding where they fell. Revelation is, in this verse, both the map and the strength for the road. It is worth noticing, as we will see repeatedly in this sūrah, how often the Qur'an refuses to give one without the other: punishment is never mentioned in this chapter without the attributes Mighty and Wise attached (v. 9); the threat in v. 7 is bracketed by "guidance and mercy" here and "Gardens of bliss" in v. 8; and even the verse of divine unknowability that ends the sūrah ends with *ʿAlīm, Khabīr*, Knowing, Aware (v. 34) — knowledge as care, not knowledge as surveillance.

**Guidance: The Word That Means a Road, Not a Feeling**

*Hudā* comes from *h-d-y*, whose field of sense is showing the way, leading along it, and — in the form *hadīyah* — the gift sent to someone to guide them to a place. Arabic uses the same conceptual family for a route and for a present, and that is a lesson in itself: guidance is a gift whose content is a route. The Qur'an keeps *hudā* stubbornly practical. It is not an interior glow but a way of going. When a sūrah elsewhere promises that God "guides," the promised effects are things like: *He guides you to the ways of peace* (5:16); *God guides whom He wills to a straight path* (2:269 in the sense of the verse's second half, and see 24:46). The commentators on this verse note the same thing from the other end: they explain "guidance" here as *al-irshād ilā al-ṭarīq*, directing to the road, and then say, in the register of al-Jurjānī and al-Rāzī, that guidance can mean the showing of the way (*dalālah*) and the enabling and granting of success (*tawfīq*) — two distinct acts, only the first of which is given to every hearer of revelation.

That distinction — showing the road versus walking it — matters pastorally and intellectually, and this verse places the second half of it under human responsibility. It says guidance is *for the virtuous*: not because God withholds the offer, but because the offer is received in a particular posture. The Book is guidance in the sense of *available direction* for all; it becomes guidance in the sense of *effective direction* for those who do not use it as décor. Verse 6 will describe the man who instead "purchases idle discourse" — the same hearer, another purchase, a competing map.

**Mercy: The Portion That Is Not Repaid**

*Raḥmah* is derived from *r-ḥ-m*, the root of *raḥim*, the womb — a word whose etymology carries the image of an attachment that does not calculate. The Mercy of God in the Qur'an is repeatedly described as that which reaches a creature without being earned by it, and yet, in this verse, the effect is tied to a condition. The tension is ancient and the tradition resolved it by distinction, not by evasion: mercy as universal provision (existence, rain, children, strength) is given to the grateful and the ingrate alike — as the sūrah itself will say in v. 20, "He has spread out His blessings upon you, outward and inward," addressed to everybody; mercy as salvific embrace in the Hereafter is the special portion of those who did not squander the first. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record in their Ṣaḥīḥs from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb:

> *"God sent down one hundred portions of mercy, and from them He sent down one portion among the jinn, the humans, the animals, and the insects; by that one portion they show mercy to one another, and by it the beast lifts its hoof over its young, and the bird spreads its wings over its young. And He deferred ninety-nine portions with which He will show mercy to His servants on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 6089; Muslim 2753)

If the ordinary kindness of a mother, in this world, is one one-hundredth, then "mercy" in v. 3 is not a soft word. It is the remaining ninety-nine, held back, and the sūrah will attach them to precisely the three qualities named in the next verse. A reader who finds the phrase "for the virtuous" narrow should test what the verse is actually withholding: not the existence of mercy, but its final, decisive application.

**Who Is al-Muttaqī? The Commentators' Own Definition**

The source notes al-Ṭabarī's practical gloss: "for the virtuous" means those who act in accord with it. That is a definition by behavior, not by claim, and it fits the etymology established in our commentary on 2:2: *w-q-y*, to place a shield between oneself and harm, so that the *muttaqī* is the one who keeps putting himself behind the protection rather than in front of the danger. In this sūrah the description gets its content in v. 4 — prayer, alms, certainty — and its mirror image in vv. 6–7 — the man who buys distraction and turns away. Al-Ṭabarī's phrasing is therefore not arbitrary. The *muttaqī* is identifiable in the sūrah's own terms as the person who acts on what he hears; the *fājir* is the person who hears and adjusts his attention to the entertainment channel. Between them, in the sūrah's realism, is everybody else.

It also deserves noting that the restriction of verses 3–5 is answered by the very next move in the chapter. Verses 8–9 will open "Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them are the Gardens of bliss." The *believers* are not defined by additional qualities beyond those of the virtuous; the sūrah continues with the same class in new clothes, and in vv. 20–22 the address widens to all of mankind with "Have you not considered that God subjected to you what is in the heavens and the earth?" The Book is guidance and mercy *for the virtuous*, and God's blessings are spread out for everyone; the sūrah does not need to lie about the second to assert the first. What is restricted is not the offer but the *benefit received*.

**The Taqwā Analogy, Revisited in a New Key**

The standard illustration for *taqwā*, used already in our 2:2, is the traveler on a dangerous path: the one who walks carefully, gathering his garments, is not the one who enjoys the walk more but the one who arrives. Luqmān's chapter makes that analogy more concrete than al-Baqarah does, because the sūrah itself will hand us a sea-voyage as a parable in vv. 31–32:

> *"And when waves have covered them like awnings, they call upon God, sincere to Him in religion... "* (Qur'an 31:32)

Every sailor knows the precise quality of attention that a storm produces: it is not terror exactly, it is the sudden total irrelevance of everything not bearing on survival. In the middle of the Atlantic at three in the morning, no one asks whether the ship's library is well stocked. The *muttaqī* walks through the ordinary hours with that same clarity of priority — the sea still there, the cargo still to be handled, but with nothing disguised about what is decisive. The "guidance and mercy" of this verse are, for him, exactly the two things a navigator has: a chart, and a seaworthy vessel. The verse 4 will supply the chart-work and the vessel-work: prayer, alms, and the certainty that there is a harbor.

**A Verse That Reorders Reading Itself**

For anyone reading with commentary in hand, verse 3 does one last, easily missed job. It tells the reader that the purpose of the text in front of him is not information about a past sage or a Makkkan quarrel, but *hudā* and *raḥmah* — and thus that a reading which terminates in knowledge alone has stopped short of the Book's stated function. That is a claim with a built-in diagnostic: take any passage of this chapter, apply it for a week, and ask what it did — did it clarify (guidance) and did it soften (mercy)? The sūrah was recited in a society that had plenty of eloquence, plenty of poetry, plenty of argument; what it offered, and what v. 3 stakes its uniqueness on, is a discourse whose surplus is not cleverness but benefit. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim transmit the Prophet's ﷺ image of precisely that surplus:

> *"The example of the believer who recites the Qur'an is that of a citron (utrujjah): its smell is pleasant and its taste is sweet; and the example of the believer who does not recite the Qur'an is that of a date: it has no smell but its taste is sweet..."* (al-Bukhārī 5427; Muslim 797)

Guidance is the taste; mercy is the scent; and "for the virtuous" means only: for those who eat it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:4

> **who perform the prayer and give the alms and who are certain of the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Marks, Not Fourteen**

After describing the Book, the sūrah describes its beneficiaries; after describing the beneficiaries, it gives them their marks. Three, in one clause: prayer established, alms given, the Hereafter believed with certainty. That shortness is a theological statement. The Qur'an does not say "who fast, make pilgrimage, never tell a lie, and give to orphans," though all of those belong to the moral universe it teaches. It selects one act of the body addressed to God, one act of wealth addressed to the community, and one act of the mind addressed to the future. Everything else in the chapter — the mustard seed, the walk, the voice, the sea, the storm — elaborates the same triad.

This same three-part cluster recurs across the Qur'an in exactly this shape. Verse 27:3 of Sūrat al-Naml, which our source flags as the closest parallel, reads: "*those who establish prayer and give zakāh, and it is they who have certainty in the Hereafter*." Al-Baqarah 2:4 does the same but with a weaker verb: "*and in the Hereafter it is they who believe (yūminūn)*." Here in Luqmān — and in al-Naml — the verb is *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, certainty, and the difference is the reason verse 4 of this sūrah is worth a sūrah's worth of attention.

**"Establish": The Verb That Will Not Let Prayer Be Private**

The Arabic is not "pray" but *yuqīmūna al-ṣalāh*, literally "they set the prayer upright" — the verb *q-w-m* in its causative stem. Every English translation is slightly false here, because the idiom treats the prayer as something that can be crooked and needs to be straightened: its times, its ablutions, its postures, its attention, its completion in congregation where congregation is required, its refusal to be squeezed into the leftover minutes of the day. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both note that "establishing" the prayer in the language of the Qur'an means giving it its full due, including the interior due — presence of heart — and that the verse therefore condemns two opposite failures at once: the slovenly and the hollow. A tradition transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ fixes the rank of this act in a single sentence:

> *"The head of the matter is islām, its pillar is the prayer, and its highest summit is striving in God's cause."* (al-Tirmidhī 2616, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

And another, transmitted in the four Sunan collections, fixes its weight in the accounting:

> *"Indeed, the first thing a servant will be questioned about on the Day of Resurrection, from what God has entrusted him with, is the prayer; if it is sound, the rest of his deeds are sound, and if it is corrupt, the rest of his deeds are corrupt."* (al-Ṭabarānī and the authors of the Sunan, from Sahl ibn Saʿd / Abū Umāmah; graded ḥasan by al-Albānī)

The word *ṣalāh* itself, from the root *ṣ-l-w*, is associated in the older lexicography with *ṣilah*, "connection" — a name the Companions' successors liked: the prayer is the connecting cord. In a sūrah about wisdom, the placement of prayer first among the three marks is not accidental. The tradition of the sages defines wisdom as the correct ordering of priorities, and Luqmān's son will be commanded in v. 17 to do the same thing in the same order: "*Establish the prayer... and bear patiently what befalls you.*" A father who teaches his child piety before policy, and patience after both, is teaching the whole sūrah's psychology of religion in a household idiom.

**Zakāh: Wealth as a Testimony**

The second mark is *wa-ātaw al-zakāh*. The verb is again not "give" but "they place it, they render it," and the noun is one of the most ideologically loaded terms in Islamic law. *Zakāh* from *z-k-w* carries two lexical senses simultaneously: purity and growth. The jurists built a whole economics on the pairing — that a portion of wealth surrendered is the portion that cleanses the remainder of its contamination by greed and unlawful claim, and that what is given does not subtract from a person's real estate but adds to it, since God increases what He accepts. The latter is not a metaphor invented by preachers; it is the image the Prophet ﷺ used:

> *"Whoever gives what is equivalent to a date from what he has earned lawfully — and God accepts only the lawful — God receives it with His right hand and rears it for one of you as one of you rears his foal, until it becomes greater than a mountain."* (al-Bukhārī 1410; Muslim 1014)

Why does *this* sūrah put alms, of all the social duties, on the same short list as prayer? Because zakāh is the one act of worship whose entire subject-matter is another human being's need, and it is thus the test case for whether the vertical claim (God) has any horizontal consequence. Al-Qurṭubī, in his treatment of the verse, notes the classical pairing: prayer is the right of God on His servants; zakāh is the right of the poor on the wealth of the rich — and a piety that fulfills only the first is not the piety of these verses. The reader will recall that in the sūrah's very next section, the counter-type of the virtuous man is described as one who "buys idle discourse" (v. 6). The Qur'an thus contrasts a purchasing in both directions: the *muttaqūn* give away what they own; the astray spend what they own on what will not profit them. The verse is a lesson in economics of attention as much as in a tax on capital.

**Yaqīn: The Difference Between Believing a Thing and Being Ruled by It**

The third mark is *wa-hum bi-l-ākhirati hum yūqinūn* — and the double pronoun ("they — with respect to the Hereafter — they") is an emphasis the grammar itself supplies: certainty about the future life. *Yaqīn* in Arabic is not the opposite of doubt in a neutral, academic sense; it is the quality of conviction strong enough to make a person act as though the object were in front of him. The classical theologians, reading the sūrah of *At-Takāthur* ("you were occupied with increase, until you visited the graves; nay, you will know — then nay, you will know with the knowledge of certainty [*ʿilm al-yaqīn*]; then you will surely see the Fire with the eye of certainty [*ʿayn al-yaqīn*]; then you will surely be asked about the Naim") distinguished three ascending stages: knowledge by report, knowledge by sight, and knowledge by realization. Here in Luqmān the believers possess, in this world, the first in the mode of the second — conviction about what they have not yet seen, and the practical effect is exactly what the verse 5 promises.

The Qur'an uses the contrast to expose what ordinary language treats as the same. In 30:7, immediately before this sūrah: "*They know an outer portion of the life of this world; and of the Hereafter they are heedless (ghāfilūn)*" — heedlessness, not disbelief. In 45:32 the inhabitants of the Fire report that they assumed the Hour would not come; in 10:55 "on that Day it will be as though they had remained only an hour of a day." The failure named is not an absence of belief at the moment of crisis — the storm-victims of v. 32 will believe with total conviction when the waves rise — but the absence of *operative* belief in calm weather. That is why *yaqīn* is here a virtue requiring effort: certainty is what survives an uneventful Tuesday.

This is also, in the sūrah's own moral logic, the reason *īqān* sits alongside prayer and zakāh rather than being the precondition of them. The three are arranged as a diagnosis of the whole person: the body is trained daily before God; the wallet is trained continuously toward people; the imagination is trained toward a future nobody can see. Remove any one of the three and the type collapses into something recognizable — the scrupulous worshipper who starves his neighbor, the philanthropist with no discipline of prayer, the prayerful almsgiver whose horizon stops at the grave and whose ethics are therefore negotiable under pressure.

**A Verse About Education, Not Just Salvation**

Read in its place — between "for the virtuous" (v. 3) and "they are upon guidance" (v. 5) — v. 4 answers the question the previous verse raises: how do we know who the virtuous are? The answer is deliberately unimpressive to a modern ear trained to look for credentials of spirituality: three habits, repeated. No ecstatic state, no genealogy, no claimed vision. The sūrah will, in fact, spend the rest of its length describing exactly this kind of anti-heroic religion: a father telling his son to pray and be patient and not be arrogant; a mustard seed weighed; ships that need a wind. The reason Luqmān's education sounds so modest is that it is aimed at *continuity*. Wisdom, in this chapter, is not an acquisition but a maintenance schedule.

The practical inference the commentators drew is worth stating plainly, because it is the most useful thing in the verse: the order is not negotiable. Begin where Luqmān begins — with what you owe God — and then with what you owe others, and only then will your certainty be anything more than a wish. The Prophet ﷺ gave that order its most economical formulation:

> *"Faith is seventy-odd branches; the highest of them is the saying 'there is no god but God,' and the lowest is removing what harms from the road; and modesty is a branch of faith."* (al-Bukhārī 9; Muslim 35)

Prayer, alms, and certainty are the trunk of that tree. Everything else in this sūrah is branch.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:5

> **It is they who are upon guidance from their Lord, and it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence as al-Baqarah 2:5**

The verse is verbally identical, in Arabic, to 2:5 — *ulāʾika ʿalā hudan min rabbihim wa-ulāʾika hum al-mufliḥūn* — and the sūrah's own commentary tradition says so, referring the reader back to the treatment in the Cow. That repetition is not a shortcut; in a Book whose style is deliberate repetition with variation, exact repetition is itself a device. The Qur'an uses it to weld together passages whose *contexts* differ, forcing the reader to conclude that the thing being described is the same regardless of what surrounds it. In al-Baqarah the sentence closes a portrait of the reverent set against two other human types — the disbeliever sealed in unbelief, and the hypocrite who trades in deception. Here in Luqmān it closes a portrait of the reverent set against a very different foil: not a hypocrite and not a denier, but a *distracted man*. That is the meaning of the echo. The prospering described in 2:5 and 31:5 is identical; what changes is the diagnosis of what stops it. In the Madinan context, the obstacle was insincerity. In the Makkan context of wisdom-talk, the obstacle is diversion. The verse thus quietly teaches that a person can be perfectly sincere and still fail, simply by never getting past the noise.

**"Upon Guidance": The Preposition That Puts You on Top**

*hudā* is here not "with guidance" or "having guidance" but *ʿalā* — *upon* guidance. Arabic idiom reserves *ʿalā* for what a rider is on, what a burden-bearer bears, what a person stands above and controls. The commentators of this very verse register the nuance: Ibn Kathīr glosses "they see, have clarity, and are following a clear way"; a transmitted gloss quoted by our source says they "ride upon guidance and are made firm by it"; and al-Ṭabarī, on the parallel of 2:5, adds that they are "*upon a light from their Lord*," borrowing the image of 6:122 — "*one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him a light by which he walks among the people*."

Two ideas are fused in the preposition. First, guidance is a *position*, not a possession: it supports, it can be kept or lost, and it can be abandoned to the ground by a person who decides to stop riding. Second, guidance is *something one stands on*, hence something that holds a person up when the ground would not. That is why a verse of this kind is not consolation but description; it says that the believer is not simply better informed than the man who bought idle discourse in v. 6, but *elevated* — standing above the flood that runs at eye level for everybody else. This is the same image the sūrah will use for the sea in v. 32: waves "like awnings" covering the drowning, and the saved man walking onto land. Guidance, in Luqmān, is what makes a person able to be above the water that is at the same level for the wise and the fool alike.

**"From Their Lord": Guidance Has an Address**

The phrase *min rabbihim* is not filler for humility's sake. *Rabb* comes from *r-b-b*, whose basic sense is to own, to nurture, to bring a thing gradually to completion — the word used in Arabic for the man who raises a child, tends a garden, or repairs a broken thing. The guidance is "from" that kind of Lord: it issues from the One whose ownership of them is identical with His care in perfecting them, and the preposition *min*, "from," marks origin so that the believer never mistakes the guidance for his own discovery. The classical exegetes loved to draw the ethical consequence: whoever knows the guidance is *from his Rabb* will not treat it as a folk custom inherited from fathers — and so v. 5 already anticipates v. 21, "*Nay, but we follow what we found our fathers following*." In one clause, the sūrah plants the antidote to traditionalism in religion: guidance is not something you found; it is something you were *given*, from a Lord who gives it fresh to whoever receives it.

A line beloved in the adab and exegetical literature puts it in the register of the free man rather than the slave: the true-born free man is by nature the servant of his Lord, and no one else's. A Muslim reading *min rabbihim* is being told that accepting guidance from God is not a limitation of autonomy but the only arrangement under which autonomy is not a fiction: every human being is being shaped by something. The only open question is whether it is shaped by its Lord, by its fathers, or by whoever owns the media it consumes.

**Falāḥ: The Agricultural Word for Salvation**

*Al-mufliḥūn* — the prospering, the successful, the saved. The root *f-l-ḥ* means to split, to cut open; *falāḥ* was used by Arab farmers for the act of furrowing the earth, and the phrase *flaḥa al-arḍ* survives in the lexicographers' explanation of the term. Al-Qurṭubī and others note the deliberate aptness: just as the farmer cuts the soil so that what is buried can rise and become grain, the *mufliḥ* cuts open his life — through prayer, alms, and certainty — so that what God has buried in him can rise. And the image carries a second, harder edge: the sūrah 77:19 uses the same root for the day of sorting, "*What will convey to you what the Day of Sorting (yawm al-faṣl) is?*" — prosperity in the Qur'an is not a glow but a separation of wheat from chaff, and a chaff that got to keep its opinions about itself.

That semantic field is why *falāḥ* here is a promise with teeth. It is not "they will feel fine"; it is "they will come out with what is theirs." The sūrah will define it, in vv. 8–9, as Gardens of bliss, abiding, "God's promise in truth," and in v. 33 as the one day when no parent will avail a child. Prosperity in Luqmān's vocabulary is survival of the audit. The Qur'an elsewhere insists on the plainness of the account: "*So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it*" (99:7–8), a verse the sūrah's own mustard-seed passage will echo.

**Where the Adhan Places You**

The Muslim practice most directly bound to this verse is invisible in the text and total in the community: the call to prayer says *ḥayya ʿalā al-ṣalāh* — come to prayer — and immediately *ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ* — come to prosperity. The two summons stand side by side, five times a day, in every town the Qur'an has reached, and together they are a recitation of verse 4 and verse 5 of this sūrah: the mark, and then the fruit. No commentator can improve on that liturgical pairing, but it is worth spelling out what it asserts about causation. The adhan does not invite to prosperity by way of wealth, rank, health, or safety, all of which are granted to the wicked as generously; it invites by way of the prayer the sūrah has named. This is a claim about the order of the world, and it is falsifiable only in the long run, which is precisely the time-scale this sūrah keeps insisting on: "*their* — *abiding therein*" (v. 9); "*each runs to an appointed term*" (v. 29).

**Why Guidance Is Both the Means and the Reward**

Readers sometimes object that vv. 3–5 are circular: the Book is guidance for the virtuous, and the virtuous are upon guidance. But the circle is the point, and the Arabic marks it: guidance is first *offered* as the Book's function, then *possessed* as the believer's state, and only after that *completed* as success. Guidance received becomes guidance inhabited. This is how a Muslim tradition of moral psychology understands habit: what you do with a text determines what the text does with you, and the same words in the ears of the v. 6 man produce arrogance and in the ears of the v. 4 believer produce prosperity. Al-Rāzī generalizes the mechanism in his commentary on 2:5: God "increases in guidance those who are guided" (47:17), and so guidance is not a fixed dose but a compound interest. The verse's last words are thus not a reward appended to the story but the story's endpoint: *hum al-mufliḥūn* — with the definite article in the predicate, and the pronoun of separation, making the sentence read "they, they in particular, are the prospering ones." There is no other species of human success in the sūrah's vocabulary. Verse 6, with its humiliating punishment, is the entire remainder of the possible, and to the pair of them the chapter will give a whole sea of illustration.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:6

> **And among mankind are those who purchase idle discourse to lead astray from the way of God without knowledge and who take it in mockery; for them there shall be a humiliating punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Economics of Attention**

The verse's first word after the relative pronoun is a verb of commerce: *yashtarī*, "he buys." Nobody in Makkah was paying money for *lahw al-ḥadīth* in any ordinary sense, and the commentators noticed this at once: Ibn Qutaybah, al-Ṭabarī, and Ibn Kathīr all record the gloss that "purchase" here means *choosing and preferring* — acquiring something as one's own by paying for it, and the coin paid is the hours of one's life and the ear one will never get back. Al-Ṭabarī's version of the same point is sharper still: they buy the false discourse over the true, and the harmful discourse over the beneficial — a transaction in which the buyer pays the seller for the privilege of being damaged. This is the sharpest single diagnosis of leisure in the Qur'an, and it reads more like behavioral economics than like ascetic preaching. Human beings do not merely consume distractions; they *acquire* them, invest in them, arrange schedules and budgets and friendships around them, and defend them against criticism — because a thing one has paid for must be worth it. The verse's verb therefore indicts not entertainment but the whole apparatus of preference, and it indicts it in the language of loss, which is the only language a merchant in Makkah would have taken seriously.

**Lahw: The Word for What Absorbs You**

*Lahw* — diversion, absorption, the state of being wholly taken up. The Arabs used it for the thing so engaging that the person engaged forgets everything else; hence *lahw* is closer to "engrossment" than to "amusement." Al-Izz ibn ʿAbd al-Salām and, before him, Ibn al-Jawzī, give the working definition our source transmits: whatever *yulhī* — diverts — a person from God. Note the object of the diverting: not from duty in general, but from God. A thing is not here forbidden because it is pleasant; it becomes *lahw al-ḥadīth* when it functions as a substitute for the thing it displaces. Al-Zamakhsharī observes that *al-ḥadīth* — "discourse, narrative, the newest talk" — is deliberately indefinite: any content, in principle, can become *lahw*, and the criterion is not the genre but the effect. News is not *lahw*; news as a compulsion is. Singing is not *lahw* by its nature; a singing-girl hired to drown out the recitation is. Story is not *lahw*; a story market that makes Qur'an feel like the boring alternative is.

This is why the sūrah places v. 6 immediately after vv. 2–5, not before. The Wise Book is a discourse with a *function* — guidance and mercy (v. 3). A competing discourse with no function but absorption is therefore not a neutral leisure but a rival with a job description, and the job it applies for is the one the Book is trying to fill in the same ear.

**What the Reported Occasion Adds**

The most widely transmitted *sabab al-nuzūl* for this verse, recorded in Ibn Isḥāq and Ibn Hishām, al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī's *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*, and Ibn al-Mundhir, names Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, a Qurashi trader who had been to al-Ḥīrah and Persia. He returned with the romances of the Persians — the stories of Rustam, Isfandiyār, and the kings of Khosrow — and, according to the reports, would sit where the Prophet ﷺ sat and say: "Muhammad tells you of the ʿĀd and the Thamūd; I tell you of Rustam, Isfandiyār, and the kings of Persia," and the Quraysh found his telling more delightful than the recitation of the Qur'an. Ibn ʿAbbās's transmitted version is that he bought the *story of the kings of ʿAjāmi*. Naḍr was, on the same authority, a man who could recite and teach music as well; the reports in al-Wāḥidī mention him teaching singing-girls to ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ.

Two things follow from the occasion, and they are more important than the antiquarian detail. First, the Qur'an is not against narrative; it is against a narrative economy in which the entertaining displaces the true. The sūrah that contains this verse is itself the Qur'an's most concentrated piece of storytelling-by-counsel (the Luqmān section), and the chapter will devote twenty-five verses to stories of the ʿĀd-like kind: the storm, the sun, the moon, the ships. The Qur'an's charge is not "no fiction" but "know what you are buying, and at what rate." Second, the man named was a *professional* competitor. He was not accidentally distracted; he was a merchant of attention, and the sūrah's later line about "those who disbelieve and bar others from God's path" (14:3) is the same figure in a different dress. A reader looking for the modern analogue should not have to search long for either half: on one side, the ordinary person who fills every interval of silence; on the other, the industry that designs the filling.

**Singing, Instruments, and the Honest Disagreement**

A large block of the early authorities — Ibn Kathīr transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās, Masrūq, ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, and Jābir that *lahw al-ḥadīth* in this verse means singing (*al-ghināʾ*); al-Ṭabarī transmits the same from several of the Tabiʿūn and also the view that it refers specifically to singing girls hired for the purpose in the pre-Islamic manner; al-Qurṭubī collects the whole range and notes the report from Mālik that the practice of the people of Madinah was the boundary of permissibility. Against this stands a second fact the same commentators concede: the Prophet ﷺ permitted singing in specific contexts — the poetry of Ḥassān ibn Thābit in the mosque ("let him recite, and the Spirit of Mercy is with him," al-Bukhārī 4530 in meaning), the two singing-girls on the day of Eid in ʿĀʾishah's house when Abū Bakr objected and the Prophet said, "*Let them be, O Abū Bakr: for every people has a festival, and this is our festival*" (al-Bukhārī 952; Muslim 892), and the work-chants of the camels. There is also the text often cited in the strict position:

> *"There will be a group among my community who will make lawful fornication, silk (for men), intoxicants, and musical instruments (maʿāzif)."* (al-Bukhārī 5590)

But the hadith's status as an explicit prohibition of all music is contested for the classical reason that its wording is a *report about people who will permit*, not a sentence forbidding the object, and that its chain is incomplete according to several of the hadith masters. The responsible summary given by al-Ghazālī in the *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* (Book of Listening and Poetic Recitation) — and he is not an antinomian — is that listening itself is neutral, permitted by default, and becomes unlawful when it stirs up what is blameworthy, and that the real offense in this verse is the *substitution* and the *mockery*, not the melody. The verse's syntax supports him: what is condemned is not singing but buying. The prohibition falls on the transaction that diverts from God and, in the next words of the verse, on the attitude that makes a game of God's signs.

**"Without Knowledge" — Twice, and Not by Coincidence**

The clause *li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīlillāhi bi-ghayri ʿilm* has been parsed in two ways by the grammarians, and both matter. Taking *bi-ghayri ʿilm* with what precedes: he buys the distraction *in a state of his own ignorance* — ignorant of the harm he is purchasing and of the price (al-Ṭabarī: he does not know what will befall him for it). Taking it with what follows: he seeks to *lead others astray* without knowledge (Ibn ʿAbbās in some transmissions; cf. 6:39, 22:3, 31:20). Most readings here combine them, and the combination is exactly what the sūrah keeps teaching: the man who is ignorant and the man who propagates are the same person, because ignorance that is content with itself always ends in propaganda. Verse 20 of this sūrah will put it as the triple negative — people who "argue about God *without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating book*" — and verse 21 will supply the actual epistemology of such a man: he follows what he found his fathers doing. The chain is airtight: buy the noise, lose the knowledge, then become the noise for others.

**"And Takes It in Mockery"**

*Wa-yattadhirahā huduwan*, "and takes it in mockery." The pronoun's referent is contested precisely because it is important: Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Ṭabarī take it as God's way or God's signs — he makes a plaything of revelation (cf. 5:51, 6:5, 7:151, 23:105). The word is *huduww*, a thing so low it can be used as a target for sport; the same root is used in 5:58 for people who take the religious obligations "as a ridicule and a plaything." The psychology is worth stating plainly, because mockery is not a second sin tacked on to absorption but the *mechanism* that makes absorption durable. A person who merely ignores a claim remains open to it. A person who has laughed at it has paid a social cost for reversing, and so laughter is how a culture makes a conviction irreversible. That is why the Qur'an returns to the *mustahziʾūn* — the mockers — again and again (2:13, 2:142, 8:32, 16:100, 21:41, 23:110, 27:39, 43:47, 45:33, 83:29–34), and why it puts them here, in a verse about entertainment: the danger of a society of amusement is not that it stops listening, but that it learns to heckle.

**"For Them a Humiliating Punishment"**

The verse ends in the register it began in: transaction. *Wa-lahu ʿadhābun muhīn* — a punishment that *humiliates*. Al-Qurṭubī notes the correspondence between the offense and its reward: the man who sought dignity and celebrity in the crowd's laughter will be given the opposite of dignity before the crowd. The sūrah does not say "a painful punishment," which is its usual phrase; it says the punishment of humiliation, which is the specific instrument of the mocker's undoing. The same logic will reappear in v. 9 in the pairing "Mighty, Wise," and in v. 18–19, where the punishment of arrogance is not specified at all but left as the loss of God's love — "*God does not love any boastful proud one*" — the natural consequence of the crime. Sin in Luqmān is not penalized arbitrarily; it is *finished*, run to its end, where it turns out to be self-shaming.

**What the Verse Asks a Reader To Do Tonight**

Nothing in the verse asks a person to stop enjoying what God made enjoyable. It asks for an inventory: what have I bought with my unrepeatable hours, and did the purchase lead me away from God or toward Him, and do I know the difference, or is my religion inherited noise (v. 21)? The Prophet ﷺ gave the discipline its simplest possible rule, one the traditionists placed at the head of the chapters on speech:

> *"Part of the goodness of a person's islām is his leaving that which does not concern him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2317; Mālik, al-Muwaṭṭaʾ)

And on the specific failure of a people who make sport of serious things:

> *"Woe to the one who tells lies to make people laugh. Woe to him. Woe to him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2315; Abū Dāwūd 4990)

Verse 6 is that sentence expanded into a social diagnosis.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:7

> **And when Our signs are recited unto him, he turns away arrogantly as if he did not hear them, as if there were deafness in his ears. So give him glad tidings of a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Explains the Previous One**

Verse 6 introduced a type; verse 7 puts him in a scene. The type was a buyer; the scene is a recitation. *Wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā* — "and when Our signs are recited to him" — is the moment of testing in this sūrah, the one that reveals what the purchase in v. 6 actually cost. The pronoun chain matters: v. 6 said "among mankind are those who buy idle discourse," and v. 7 says "when Our signs are recited *to him*" — singular. The Qur'an's anthropologies are always built out of individual cases, because the transactions it is describing are individual: nobody buys distraction collectively, and nobody hears a verse collectively. The same method was used in 2:264 for the man who spends his wealth "to be seen by people," and it will be used again in v. 18 for the man who turns his cheek. The lesson of the grammar: a sūrah about a whole class of people indicts a single ear.

The verb *tatlā* — "recite, follow, recount" — from *talā*, "to follow," is chosen because a recitation is not a broadcast but a sequence being walked through, one sign after another, and because the same root gives *tilāwah*, whose deeper sense is "to follow" in action as well as in utterance. This is the point on which the whole verse turns. The man of v. 7 does not fail at the hearing; he fails at the following, and so God describes his ears as already shut.

**"He Turns Away Arrogantly": the Body Language of Refusal**

*Yatawallā mustakbiran* — he turns his back, in a state of *istikbār*, taking himself to be above. Arabic marks the difference between *iʿrāḍ* (averting, which can be from disinterest) and *tawallī* (turning away wholly, which is a chosen departure), and between *kibr* and *istikbār*: the latter is the active employment of an assumed superiority. Al-Zamakhsharī notes here that *mustakbiran* is the reason for the physical gesture: the sin is not curiosity unsatisfied but rank asserted. This is the same sin that in the sūrah's opening creation-argument will be called the companion of *baghy*, aggression (v. 20's mention of "outward and inward" blessings is addressed to the un-thankful in that very frame), and it is the sin Luqmān will separately forbid his son, twice, in vv. 18–19. The sūrah has a coherent theory: arrogance is what turns an ear away, and a sūrah about wisdom must therefore legislate over the body.

The classical commentators, following the reports on Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, supply the social mechanism: the mockery of v. 6 had now to be maintained publicly, so the hearer had to *be seen* not listening. That is why the description is so physical — turning, cheek averted, ears blocked — because arrogance lives in postures, and because a person who has performed dismissal cannot easily undo it without losing face. Al-Qurṭubī's treatment of *istikbār* elsewhere in the sūrah, and the hadith defining it, converge on the same formula:

> *"God does not enter Paradise any one in whose heart is an atom's weight of kibr. A man said: but a man likes that his garment and sandal be beautiful? He said: God is Beautiful and loves beauty; kibr is rejecting the truth and despising people."* (Muslim 91, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd)

Two halves: rejection of what is true, and contempt for who is human. Verse 7 shows the first; vv. 18–19 will legislate the second.

**"As If He Did Not Hear... As If in His Ears Was Deafness"**

The double simile is the most analyzed feature of the verse and, in the sūrah's design, the most medically precise. *Ka-an lam yasmaʿhā* — as though he had not heard it. The first comparison describes the *appearance*: he arranges himself so that it looks as though the words had not reached him. Then the second: *ka-annahu yuṣā* — as if something deafened him, the noun *waṣab* denoting in the dictionaries a heaviness, ringing, or dullness in the ear, in some readings a chronic deafness. The escalation is what makes the image frightening: what begins as a performance ends as a faculty lost. He pretended not to hear and has become unable to. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is direct: "he does not understand them because of his aversion to them." The Qur'an uses the same physiology elsewhere as an act of divine *judgment* rather than metaphor: "*In their ears is a deafness (waṣab)*" (2:18), "*God has placed over their hearts coverings, so they do not understand it, and in their ears a deafness*" (6:25), "*shall We not inform you of the worst in regard to deeds, whose labor has strayed in this world's life while they suppose they are doing well? Those whose hearts are averted from God's signs... the deaf and the dumb and those who will not reason*" (18:103–104, cf. 8:22: "*the worst of living creatures in God's sight are the deaf and dumb who do not reason*").

Read together, these texts state a hard psychology: what is sealed from outside is sealed from inside. A person does not lose the capacity to hear revelation by an argument; he loses it by repetitions of refusal, until the refusal is structural. That is the doctrine the commentators call *khatm*, sealing, and it must be read carefully, because it is the point on which a careless determinism is built. The sūrah itself supplies the correction in the ordering of its own verses: the sealing follows the turning, and the turning follows the purchase. Verse 24 will add the last element: "*We grant them enjoyment a little, then We compel them toward a severe punishment*" — respite, not abandonment. The man of v. 7 is not a man whom God refused to speak to; he is a man whom God spoke to until the speaking became a physical irritation, which is the condition the Qur'an calls *ṭabʿ*, and which reason alone cannot reverse.

Why, then, does God say "give him glad tidings" (*fa-bashshirhu*)? Because *bashshar* is the word for the announcement one makes to someone about a thing that will *delight* him. Used of a punishment, it is the sharpest irony available in Arabic rhetoric, and classical exegetes classify it as *tawkhīṭ bi-l-tahakkum* — a notice of abandonment. The same device occurs in 3:21 ("*announce a painful punishment for those who disbelieve God's signs*"), 4:138, 45:8 (the identical phrase in the parallel sūrah), 52:23, and 84:24. Al-Zamakhsharī's comment on the family of such verses is that the tone is not mockery for its own sake but *settled judgment*: the prophet is told to stop arguing and deliver the verdict, exactly as a physician stops arguing with a patient who has decided to eat poison and says instead, "then enjoy it." The rhetorical effect on the Qur'an's own audience is the opposite of cruelty — it is meant to be unbearable, a threat delivered in the register of an invitation.

**The Verse's Modern Face**

The type described here is not the atheist in good faith; it is the person who has decided in advance what evidence is permitted to cost him. Every age produces him, and every age gives him new equipment. The mechanisms have names in the psychology of today — motivated reasoning, the backfire effect, selective exposure, the "prebend" of identity that makes certain corrections socially expensive. The sūrah's description is more economical and, arguably, more accurate: he *turns*, he *acts as if he had not heard*, and in time the pretending *becomes* the not-hearing. A reader wishing to check himself needs only the question the verse implies: when the recitation reached me last, what did my body do? Not what did I think — what did I do, at the moment the cost became visible? Because the verse's diagnosis is not of the intellect but of the direction the person has chosen to face, and it is that, in this sūrah's anthropology, that determines what he will and will not be able to hear.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:8

> **Truly those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be Gardens of bliss,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Antidote Placed in the Same Sentence**

Verses 6 and 7 ended with "*a humiliating punishment*," and verse 8 begins by turning to the believers — not with a contrast-mark, not even with "but," but with a plain new subject: "*Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them are the Gardens of bliss.*" The abruptness is a technique this sūrah will repeat (v. 23 follows v. 22 the same way, and v. 32 follows v. 31). The reason is that the Qur'an does not present reward and punishment as two arguments requiring separate proofs; it presents them as two descriptions of one arrangement, and the reader is meant to notice how quickly the mind travels from threat to promise when a text is honest about both. Al-Zamakhsharī sees here the pairing of *targhīb* and *tarhīb*, incentive and deterrent, the standard method of prophetic speech — the reason the Qur'an describes itself as "*a bearer of good news and a warner*" (41:4, cf. 2:119, 35:24).

The formula "those who believe and do righteous deeds" is the sūrah's own shorthand for the portrait in vv. 3–4, and its appearance signals that the description of the *muttaqūn* and the description of the *muʾminūn ʿāmilū al-ṣāliḥāt* are the same people seen from two directions: inward disposition, outward work. No one reading this verse can consistently maintain that faith in the Qur'an is a private conviction, because the noun that must accompany it is *ʿamal* — deeds, work, labor, the same word used for what a person is paid for.

**Jannah: The Word That Means "Covered"**

*Jannāt* is the plural of *jannah*, and the root *j-n-n* means to conceal, to cover. A garden is a *jannah* because its dense foliage hides the ground beneath it; the *jinn* are so called because they are hidden from human senses; the unborn child is a *janīn* because the womb covers it — and this same sūrah, in its final verse, will declare that only God knows *mā fī al-arḥām*, what is in the wombs (v. 34). That single etymological line is worth holding while reading this verse, because it tells the reader what kind of word *jannah* is. It is not primarily a word about luxury; it is a word about *covering* — about a reality hidden from the present eye and about a place where the covering is lifted for those who were meant for it. In a sūrah whose deepest argument is about hidden things (the mustard seed inside the rock, v. 16; the deeds God "knows," v. 23; the unseen knowledge fenced off in v. 34), Paradise is named in the very vocabulary of concealment: what is now *jann* — hidden — will be unfolded.

*Naʿīm*, "bliss," from *n-ʿ-m*, the root of *niʿmah*, blessing, is a deliberately continuous word: the sūrah has used the same family already for the blessings "outward and inward" that God spread for everyone (v. 20), and will use its cousin in 102:2 for the *naim* about which every soul will be questioned. The Qur'an therefore does not promise the believer a different kind of enjoyment from the one the world contains; it promises the same word, without its two defects — that it ends, and that it can be squandered. Verse 102:1–2 makes the connection explicit by name: "*You were occupied with increase (al-takāthur) until you visited the graves; then no — you will come to know; then no — you will come to know.*" The *naʿīm* of v. 8 is what remains when the *takāthur* of v. 6's market has finally been spent.

**"Nuzulā": The Hospitality Verse Behind This One**

The near-parallel the sūrah's own commentary points to is 18:107, where the reward is given a domestic noun: "*Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them are the Gardens of Firdaws, as a nuzul.*" *Nuzul* is what a host lays out for a guest on arrival — the lodging, the meal, the welcome: the word belongs to hospitality, not to architecture. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both gloss *nuzulā* here as "preparation and lodging," and they remark on the deliberate smallness of the word: the greatest reward in the Qur'an is described as the thing a decent person does for a traveler he respects. The moral consequence is immediate, and it is one the Prophet ﷺ stated in the same register, in a hadith transmitted in the chapters on faith:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest; and whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him uphold his ties of kinship; and whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

The parallel structure is unmistakable: belief named, and then its three visible outputs — the guest, the family, the tongue. Luqmān's sūrah will in vv. 12–19 do the same thing, with the family and the tongue as the test cases of the creed; and the * Gardens of bliss* here is the corresponding promise to the man who was told in v. 4 to establish prayer and give alms. Paradise in this sūrah is a *nuzul*: the welcome extended by the Host to those who welcomed others.

**Two Words About the Garden That Is Also a Warning**

The word *jannah* carries one further resonance that the exegetes never let a reader forget: the first *jannah* in the Qur'an is the one that was lost. Adam's *jannah* in 2:35 was a garden with an instruction attached and a prohibition inside it — "*dwell... but do not approach this tree*" — and the fall there came about by a whispering that made a forbidden thing look desirable (7:20–22). This is why the *jannāt* of v. 8 is not mere consolation for the man who resisted distraction in vv. 6–7: it is the second attempt at the same test, this time with the memory of the first built into the vocabulary. In Luqmān, the *Jardens of bliss* are given specifically to those who *heard* the recitation and did not turn away, and the first *jannah* was lost by one who listened to the wrong voice. Ibn Kathīr's comment on 18:107 fits here exactly: the reward is of the same genus as the trial, so that the obedient inherit the very place type of the disobedient forfeited, with the difference that "*they shall not be expelled from it*" (15:48; cf. 38:86, 50:29).

The other half of the warning comes from the pairing in the previous verse's ending. In v. 7, the punished man was told to be given "glad tidings"; here the believers are promised *naʿīm*. Al-Rāzī notes that the Qur'an thereby teaches that *everyone receives glad tidings* in this world's economy — the difference is only in the content of the news, and the difference is decided by what a person *purchases*. There is, in this sūrah, no third option, and no suspended verdict.

**Why the Plural "Gardens," and Why Degrees Matter**

The verse uses the plural *jannāt* rather than *al-jannah*, and the commentators press that detail: 66:12, 18:107, 76:12 and 21:90 likewise speak of gardens, and the sūrah of al-Muṭaffifīn locates the record of the righteous "*in ʿilliyyūn*" (83:18–21). The plural is the textual basis for the doctrine of ranks in Paradise, which the Prophet ﷺ articulated in the most quoted of the relevant traditions:

> *"The people of Paradise will look up at the people of the chambers above them, as you look at a star that has risen in the eastern or western horizon, because of the difference between them."* They said: "Messenger of God, are those the places of the prophets, which none but they will reach?" He said: *"Yes — and by Him in whose hand is my soul, men who believed in God and confirmed the messengers."* (al-Bukhārī 3256; Muslim 2831)

And, in the same vein, about the nearness that ranks finally amount to:

> *"In Paradise there is a hundred degrees which God has prepared for those who strive in His cause; between every two degrees is the distance between the heaven and the earth."* (al-Bukhārī 2824)

A reader may ask why a sūrah about a father's wisdom would dwell on degrees. It is because Luqmān's whole pedagogy is about the difference between small and small: the mustard seed versus the atom, the lowered voice versus the bray, the moderate pace versus the swagger. Verse 8 tells the believer that the world's indifference to gradations is not mirrored above. Two people can both enter, and their difference will be visible; therefore the difference in what they did with an ordinary week — v. 6 or v. 4 — is worth more than it looks.

**The Verse's Function in the Chapter**

Finally, note what v. 8 accomplishes structurally, and it is the reason v. 9 exists at all. A promise, made in this sūrah's world of mockery and shipwreck, needs a guarantor. Verse 9 will supply one in the four words "*waʿda Llāhi ḥaqqā* — God's promise in truth" — and then close with "*al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*." Verse 8 is thus the statement of the gift; v. 9 is the statement of the One who gives it. The sūrah keeps this order everywhere: description of the human act, then an attribute of God that makes it survivable. Wisdom, in Luqmān's teaching, is not the confident belief that good will win; it is the knowledge of who is bound to make it win.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:9

> **therein to abide—God's Promise in truth. And He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"To Abide": the Word for Staying Put**

*Khālidīna fīhā* — abiding, "lasting" ones. The root *kh-l-d* in Arabic, as Ibn Fāris puts it in his *Maqāyīs al-Lughah*, denotes the remaining of a thing in the place where it has come to be: *khalada al-shayʾu* is "the thing stayed, settled." A *khālid* is not merely long-lived; he is settled where he is, without the possibility of being moved on. This is why the Qur'an, when it needs to foreclose the natural suspicion that "long" merely means "very long," adds an explicit word for ever: "*khālidīna fīhā abadan*" (4:57; 9:68; 18:108 "*khālidīna fīhā abadan, lā yurīdūna ʿanhā ḥiwālā*" — they will not will any move from it). The verse before us omits *abadan* and needs it not, because *khālidiyna* plus *ḥaqqan* does the whole job: they are settled, and the settlement is a promise, and a promise of the Mighty does not expire.

Al-Ṭabarī, in the passage the sūrah's commentators keep citing on this word, notes that the promise of "staying" answers the fear of expulsion which the first *jannah* in the Qur'an made permanent in human memory. Adam was told "*yā Adamu, iskan al-jannata... fa-ʿadā*" — dwell, then was expelled. The believer of v. 8–9 is told "*khālidīna*." Ibn Kathīr notes the pairing with 15:48 — "*no fatigue will touch them therein, and they will not be expelled*" — as one of the Qur'an's most repeated assurances; the same sense appears in Fāṭir 35:35, where the saved say "*ḥamdun li-Llāhi alladhī adhhaba ʿannā al-ḥazana, inna Rabbanā la-Ghafūrun Shakūr*" — the removal of *ḥazan*, of grief, as the first gift of the abode, and then the second, the removal of *nuṣb*, fatigue (35:35). A place you cannot be moved from, and a body that cannot tire, is what the sūrah offers against the man of v. 6 who cannot sit still for a recitation.

**"God's Promise in Truth": Four Words That Do the Epistemological Work**

*Waʿdu Llāhi ḥaqqan.* The promise of God is truth — and the readers of this sūrah needed it said. Everything that precedes is unfalsifiable from inside this world: the punishment of the mocker, the Gardens of the patient, the abiding of the dead. The verse does not ask the reader to weigh the evidence and conclude; it asks the reader to identify the warrant: not "paradise is likely," but "God has said it, and what God says is *ḥaqq*."

The word *ḥaqq* here must be taken in its full philosophical register, and the sūrah's placement of it is deliberate. *Ḥaqq* in Arabic carries the sense of *thābit* (real, established, binding) as well as *ṣādiq* (corresponding to fact). Al-Rāzī's analysis is the sharpest: a promise is *ḥaqq* when the one who makes it has (a) knowledge of what he promises, (b) power to perform it, and (c) no motive to deceive — and the sūrah then names exactly the two attributes that close the argument: *al-ʿAzīz*, whose will nothing can prevent, and *al-Ḥakīm*, whose acts are not arbitrary, and therefore who does not break what He fixes. The logic is a syllogism compressed into four words, and this is why the sūrah puts it in the mouth of a sage rather than in a passage of legal command.

Classical ʿaqīdah pressed further. Why can God not lie? The Muʿtazilites answered: because lying is *qubḥ*, ugly, and God's wisdom obliges Him to the best. The Ashʿarites answered: because lying is a defect and imperfection, and no defect reaches Him — while holding that nothing is "obliging" upon God, and hence a threat of punishment is not enforced as a duty but performed as a truthful declaration of His will. Ibn Taymiyyah's treatment of the divine attribute *al-Ṣādiq*, "the Most Truthful," in his writings on faith and on the *Aqīdah al-Wāsiṭiyyah* takes a third line: God's speech is an attribute of His essence, so that truthfulness in His speech is as necessary as life in His essence, and to deny the binding-truth of His promise is to wound an attribute, not merely to doubt an event. He also insists, against the philosophers who thought the reward and punishment of the Hereafter were figurative, and against the Muʿtazila who bound God, that the promise is *ḥaqq* in the sense of both true and certain, while its execution belongs to His will and justice. Whatever the school, all the parties of Sunni kalām agree on the sentence this verse states: the truth of God's promise is not contingent on human belief in it, and there is no fourth possibility between "He said it" and "it is not so."

**The Attribute That Guarantees the Gift: al-ʿAzīz**

*al-ʿAzīz* is one of the most overloaded names in Arabic. From *ʿ-z-z*, it carries might, invincibility, and — because might in the desert also means a strong backing of kin — the related sense of being precious, hard to obtain, rare. Al-Khaṭṭābī, in his classical treatment of the divine names, summarizes it in the formula transmitted from the early authorities: *al-ʿAzīz* is "the one to whom nothing can be opposed, and the one sought for might" (*al-ghālib alladhī lā yughlab, wa-al-maṭlūb alladhī lā yūrā ilayhi illā bihi*). Read as a commentary on v. 9's promise, this yields the reassuring half: whoever has *al-ʿAzīz* as his obligor cannot lose the thing he was promised, because no one can stop Him from paying it. The sūrah has just said of the mocker "for them is a humiliating punishment"; a punishment is humiliating only if the One who punishes is mightier than every source of the sinner's pride. So *ʿAzīz* here also does the work of *muhīn*, answering back to v. 6 in a single letter-similarity.

**The Attribute That Moralizes the Gift: al-Ḥakīm**

*al-Ḥakīm* in this position answers a different and older objection: that reward is a kind of bribery, or that mercy distributed on the basis of deeds is sentiment dressed as justice. The transmitted glosses, quoted in our source from al-Zamakhsharī, put it plainly: because He is Mighty, He blesses whom He wills and none can stop Him; because He is Wise, the blessing is not bestowed arbitrarily. This is the exact formula for a theology of grace-with-order: God's gifts are free, and their distribution has reasons.

That pairing is why the sūrah opens the *wisdom* section with gratitude, not with reward: the believer in v. 4 is called *muttaqī* who "gives thanks" (v. 12), because in a world governed by an ʿAzīz Ḥakīm, receiving is not passive good fortune but a relationship with a Giver whose distribution can be trusted but never dictated. The same combination of names will return at the end of the sūrah's longest passage, in v. 27 — "*Inna Llāha ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm*" — where the inexhaustibility of God's words makes the same two claims: the supply cannot run out (Mighty), and the supply is never wasted (Wise). A reader who notices that inclusion will understand why vv. 8–9 belong with vv. 12–19 rather than being merely an introduction: the same names bracket the entire counsel of Luqmān.

**How a Promise Changes a Life**

The final dimension of v. 9 is practical, and it is the reason the sūrah bothers to prove the promise at all. Human conduct is governed overwhelmingly by what a person expects to be true in the future — the child who studies for an exam he believes will be held, the builder who works for a house he believes he will inhabit. Psychological research on delayed gratification has made the same point experimentally and in the plainest possible terms: what children can endure now depends on how solidly they believe in what they have been promised later. The Qur'an's entire moral architecture — *yaqīn* in v. 4, *waʿd* here, "*kullu nafsin dhāmat al-mawt*" at v. 34 — is a sustained intervention on precisely that variable, and verse 9 states the mechanism in three short phrases: abiding (so the payoff is not withdrawn), a promise (so it is not merely hoped), and the Mightier-Wiser One (so it cannot be outbid or mismanaged). Luqmān's son is being taught, before he is taught anything about the world, how to hold a future that nobody can see. That, in this sūrah's definition, is not credulity but the beginning of wisdom.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:10

> **He created the heavens without pillars that you see, and cast into the earth firm mountains, lest it shake beneath you, and spread about all manner of beast therein. And We sent down water from the sky and caused every noble kind to grow therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Verbs, One Argument**

Verse 10 contains, in a single breath, the entire natural theology of the Qur'an. Four acts are named in order from the top down and then outward across the surface: creating the heavens; casting mountains into the earth; scattering every kind of living creature upon it; sending water from the sky so that every generous pair grows. The four are not a poetic inventory. They are a graded set of examples chosen to cover every kind of thing a human being can point at: the unreachable (sky), the immovable (rock), the moving (animals), and the growing (plants). Al-Rāzī, who loves this kind of enumeration, notes in his commentary on the parallel of 13:2 that the sequence moves from the most evident to the most wondrous, ending in the one phenomenon that no human being has ever produced or explained: life from water and dirt. And the verb that opens everything is *khalaqa*, "created" — from *kh-l-q*, used in Arabic for measuring something out and shaping it, so that the first claim of the verse is not "there is stuff" but "stuff has been proportioned."

The verse's frame is also its logic. It begins with "*khalaqa*" in the third person, and it ends with "*anzalnā* — *We* sent down" and "*anbatnā* — *We* caused to grow." The switch of person is what Arabic rhetoricians call *iltifāt*, "turning," and its effect here is measurable: the impersonal statement of fact becomes a personal statement of benefaction. The same water that proves a Creator is delivered as a gift from a "We." This sūrah is about gratitude (v. 12), and the grammar has already supplied its object before the argument is finished.

**"Without Pillars That You See": An Astronomical Clause With Two Readings**

*Bi-ʿamadin lā tarawnahā.* The phrase is famously ambiguous and the ambiguity is resolved by nothing in the text, which is why both readings are transmitted in our source, from Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī. Take *lā tarawnahā* as describing the pillars: "with pillars you do not see" — the heavens are in fact supported, but by pillars beyond human sight. Take it as describing the heavens: "He created the heavens without pillars, as you see them" — they stand with no supports at all, and the sight of that is the miracle. Both readings appear among the Salaf, and the difference is theologically harmless: on either parsing, the verse says the sky's stability is not self-explanatory, and on neither reading does it claim to explain the mechanism.

The stronger point is the one made by the parallel verse, 13:2, quoted by the sūrah's own commentary: "*God is He who raised the heavens without pillars that you see, then He settled upon the Throne*" — followed there by the same subject as here: "*and He subjected the sun and the moon, each running for an appointed term*" (13:2), and by the mountains, the plants, the pairs (13:3–4), a passage so close in structure that the classical exegetes treat 13:2–4 as the best tafsīr of our verse. Al-Zamakhsharī's rhetorical question, quoted widely in this verse's exegesis, captures the Makkan context: you see the sky and you see nothing holding it, and yet it does not fall on you — *bi-ghayr ʿamd qawam*, by no visible column, *bi-amr man lā yufāriquhu amr* (by the command of One whom no command frustrates). The Qur'an's cosmological statement is thus always a statement of dependency, not of physics; the heavens are there to remind you that the thing which looks most self-sufficient is the most wholly held.

Readers today often want to add: "and modern astronomy confirms it — no pillars." The verse needs no such confirmation, and to claim it is to misdescribe the verse's genre. The Qur'an's sign-arguments proceed from what the audience can see *right now*, and what we see right now is exactly what a seventh-century Arab saw: the sky is up and nothing is holding it. Whether the physics of a bound universe or of a gravitating sphere is expressed by *ʿamadin* is a separate question, and the sober commentators on 13:2 (Ibn Kathīr, al-Saʿdī) confine themselves to the observable fact, refusing to make the verse hostage to any cosmology. That refusal is itself a lesson in the etiquette of reading: verses like this are meant to produce awe and inference, not to be checked against a textbook.

**"Firm Mountains, Lest It Shake": the Earth as a Ship**

*Wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum.* Three words carry a whole image. *Alqā*, "He threw/cast," is the verb used for putting down something heavy with purpose — a cast anchor, a thrown bundle, a foundation dropped into a trench. *Rawāsiya* is the plural of *rāsiyah*, "firmly fixed," from *r-s-w*, the very verb used of a ship coming to rest on the shore and of a thing becoming settled; the same noun-family gives *rusūkh*, deep-rootedness of knowledge (3:7, "*the firmly rooted in knowledge*"), and *rusūkh al-ʿilm*. And *tamīda* means to sway, pitch, slip, become unsteady — a verb of seasickness, of a boat, of a head swimming.

The combination yields one coherent picture: the earth treated as a vessel, the mountains as the weight that keeps it from capsizing. The parallel verses confirm the picture rather than strain it: "*and mountains, lest it should sway with you*" (16:15), "*and on the earth He set firm mountains, lest it should sway with you*" (21:31 — there with the verb *taḥīda*, "deviate, swerve"), "*Have We not made the earth a spreading, and the mountains pegs?*" (78:6–7 — *awtād*, tent-peggs, with the earth as the hide stretched out over them), "*and the mountains as stakes, by which it might incline with you*" (88:19). The three images — anchor-weight, ballast, and peg — are not contradictory but complementary: mass against motion, depth against slip, fixing against displacement. Modern geology's discovery that mountains have deep "roots" of crust extending into the mantle, and that the crust floats and adjusts (isostasy), is at least a *formal* echo of the peg and root imagery, and preachers have made much of it; the commentators' own reading of *rawāsiya* as "that which stabilizes" does not require that echo, and does not gain anything from it. The verse's work is to make the reader feel the ground as something *given*, not something possessed.

It is worth noticing the last two words of the clause: *bik*, "with you." The shaking that the mountains prevent is not described as a property of the planet; it is described as a hazard to its inhabitants. Al-Qurṭubī's habit of reading creation-verses as benefaction applies here: the earth's stability is told in the register of the *niʿmah*, and the sūrah will make the point explicit in v. 20 — "*He has subjected to you what is in the heavens and the earth, and has spread out His blessings upon you, outward and inward.*"

**"Spread About Every Dabbah": the Word That Means Movement**

*Wa-baththa fīhā min kulli dābbah* — "and He spread in it every crawling thing." *Dābbah* is from *d-b-b*, to move, and in the lexicography it is specifically the animal that moves by day or by night, and thus in common usage any living creature that walks, crawls, or treads — the word is used in Arabic for the beast of burden, and the same form appears at 11:6, "*there is no dābbah that moves upon the earth but that upon God is its provision*" — a verse which, in a sūrah about gratitude, is the correct gloss on this one. The image in v. 10 is the earth as a surface that has been *strewn* with living things, *baththa*, the verb used for scattering seeds, for releasing captives, for dispersing an army: not a zoo but a spreading, an uncontained abundance.

The Qur'an elsewhere completes the picture this verse begins, and the pairing is instructive: "*God created every animal (dābbah) from water; so among them are those who crawl on their bellies, and among them are those who walk on two legs, and among them are those who walk on four. God creates what He wills*" (24:45). The three modes of locomotion are, for the classical biologists among the Muslims — al-Jāḥiẓ in his *Kitāb al-Ḥayawān*, and the physicians who used the same tripartite divisions — the visible signature of design: form follows function everywhere, and nothing in nature is a bare accident. And *kulli*, "every," in our verse is not a rhetorical flourish: it is a claim about the completeness of the census, and the census includes the things nobody admires. In a sūrah that will forbid arrogance in a walk and a voice, the scattering of every crawling thing is the correct backdrop — the same power that made the mountains made the worm, and made both for the same reason.

**"Every Noble Pair": Karīm as a Word About Giving**

*Zawj* means a pair, a spouse, a kind — because a kind in Arabic thought is what comes in matched halves, and the verse at 26:7, quoted by our source, has the identical phrase: "*and We grew therein every noble zawj.*" *Karīm* is one of the Qur'an's richest adjectives, and the source records the double gloss that matters: it means noble, generous, and thus, applied to plants, that they *give abundantly without asking anything in return* (a sense al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī both exploit in their lexicons of the Qur'anic vocabulary). A *karīm* plant is not only fine but free: it bears fruit for you and does not invoice you. Al-Rāghib's own definition of *karīm* in *al-Mufradāt fī Gharīb al-Qur'ān* is "the one who reaches the summit of what he is," so a noble kind is a thing that has become fully what it was made to be. The word thus ties the verse to the sūrah's whole argument about *ḥikmah*: the Book is *ḥakīm* (v. 2), the earth's plants are *karīm* (v. 10), and both are called that because they give what they were made to give without withholding.

Water, in the sūrah's sequence, is the hinge of everything after verse 10. "*And We sent down from the sky blessed water, and We grew thereby gardens and the grain of the harvest*" (50:9); "*and We made from water every living thing*" (21:30); "*have you not seen that God sends down water from the sky, and the earth becomes green?*" (22:63); and in the sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah the deliberate pause: "*af-arʾaytum mā tumnūn, a-antum takhluqūnahu am naḥnu khāliqūn*" — "what you eject — do you create it, or are We the Creators?" (56:58–59). The point of that juxtaposition — rain and semen in the same rhetorical position — will reappear in v. 34, where rain and wombs are named together in the list of things known only to God. Verse 10 has already, in the phrase "*every noble pair*," quietly put human reproduction inside the same order as agriculture: both are growth from water, and neither is anyone's achievement.

**"Show Me What They Have Created": the Verse That Ends the Argument**

Verse 11 turns the four acts into a challenge — "*so show me what those besides Him have created*" — and the commentary on v. 10 has to explain the turn, because the logic is often misread as mere taunt. The structure is what the juricians call *istidlāl bi-l-mawjūd ʿalā wājib* — reasoning from what exists to what must be. The four facts are the premises; the conclusion ("therefore your idols are nothing") is left for the interlocutor to draw, and he cannot draw it without conceding the fourth: if creation is measured, proportioned, beneficial, and life-producing, then whatever has not done any of those four things has no claim to worship. Al-Qurṭubī's comment on the verse's final words, "*bal li-lladhīna ẓalamū fī ḍalālin mubīn*," is the other half of the argument: the wrongdoers are not merely mistaken, they are *in* error, as in a place they are standing in, and it is "manifest" — visible in the way they live, in their arrogance, in their inability to produce the smallest of the four classes. The sūrah's readers were told in v. 6 to audit what they buy; v. 10–11 ask them to audit what they worship, and the criterion in both cases is the same: does it produce, or does it only occupy?

**The Application Luqmān Would Have Made**

A father teaching a son to be grateful does not begin with a list of favors; he takes the boy outside and lets him look. Verses 10–11 are that act, transcribed. Its practical content is the discovery that gratitude is not a mood but an inference: from sky with nothing under it, to dependence; from ground that does not capsize, to mercy; from worms and grain, to a Giver who scatters abundance on a world that did not ask; from rain, to a heart that only grows when something comes down from above it. In the same way that the Wise Book must be *sent down* into a reader (v. 2), the plant must be sent down water — and Luqmān's son, standing in a garden with the sky over him and a mustard seed in his hand (v. 16), is being taught by the sūrah to be a *karīm* kind himself: generous, growing, and aware of exactly where his water comes from.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:11

> **This is the creation of God; so show me that which those apart from Him have created. Nay, the wrongdoers are in manifest error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"This": the Finger, Not the Argument**

*Hādhā khalqu Llāh.* The demonstrative refers back to everything in v. 10 — the unsupported sky, the ballasted earth, the scattered animals, the rain and the growing pairs — and the sentence does not argue from them so much as hand them over. The structure of the whole verse, once the demonstrative is placed correctly, is a courtroom sequence: the exhibit is produced ("this"), the ownership is stated ("is God's creation"), the opponents are called to the bench ("so show me"), and the verdict is delivered before any answer arrives ("nay — the wrongdoers are in manifest error"). Al-Zamakhsharī notices that the imperative "*arūnī*" — "show me" — is a *taʿjīz*, an immobilizing challenge, and that the verse could have said "they cannot create anything," but did not, because a challenge that is left open is more damning than a denial that is asserted: the audience is invited to speak, and the silence that follows the invitation is the proof.

Ibn Kathīr's gloss on the phrase is the plainest: "this which I have described to you — the creation of the heavens and the earth and what is in them — is *khalq Allāh*, the creation of God alone, with no partner; so show me what these idols of yours, whom you call gods besides Him, have created." Al-Ṭabarī records the variant reading of the phrase in the sense of an exclamation of wonder — *hādhā* as "what a creation!" — and while the second reading is weaker, it preserves something the first can lose: the verse's tone is not only refutation but admiration. The sūrah of Luqmān is a sūrah of wisdom, and wisdom begins in wonder at what exists, not in contempt for those who misname it.

**The Logic of the Challenge, Stated Formally**

The argument can be written out as the Muslim dialecticians wrote it, and it is worth doing so once, because this sūrah is precisely about the *quality* of a person's reasons (v. 20: arguing "without knowledge, without guidance, and without a book of light").

Premise one: worship is a payment; it is owed to the one whose relation to you is the relation of maker to made — the Qur'an states this as a question elsewhere: "*who is it that you can call upon besides Him, if a punishment from God touches you or the Hour comes? Is it the false you call upon, or God? Nay, it is He you should call upon*" (39:63–64), and "*Show me what those besides Him have created*" is the same reasoning in a shorter form. Premise two: the universe's observable production — being measured, ordered, sustained, and life-giving — is exhaustively attributed, in the sūrah's exhibit, to God. Inference: since no rival has produced any part of the exhibit, no rival is owed any part of the payment. The challenge is therefore not an argument for God's existence — the address is to idolaters who, according to the sūrah's own next statement (v. 25), *already* believe God created the heavens and the earth — but an argument about the *distribution of worship*. This is a crucial and much-missed point: vv. 10–11 do not try to convert a theist from unbelief; they try to drive a wedge between a person's cosmology and his practice. The Qur'anic assumption, remarkably modern in its diagnosis, is that wrong action almost never requires wrong belief about existence; it requires a misallocation of what one already believes.

That is why "wrongdoers" (*alladhīna ẓalamū*), in the verse's last sentence, names them rather than "unbelievers." The wrong is *ẓulm*: from *ẓ-l-m*, darkness, and in the technical usage of the lexicographers "placing a thing in a place that is not its own." The same word will be used by Luqmān for shirk in v. 13 — "*inna al-shirka la-ẓulmun ʿaẓīm*" — and the recurrence is the sūrah's own definition of the sin it is treating: not intellectual failure but misplacement, an error of accounting, a debt paid to the wrong creditor. Verse 11 and verse 13 thus bracket the entire wisdom section with one word, and the reader who has followed from v. 6 will notice that the misaccounting began in the market: the man who *buys* idle discourse is the same man who *pays* worship to the powerless, and *sharā* and *ẓalama* are in this sūrah one story.

**How the Other Testimony Put the Same Question**

The sūrah literature surrounding this verse supplies its Qur'anic parallels in a chain, and they are worth setting out because together they show the argument's range. To the Meccans, the sharpest version: "*Or were they created from nothing, or are they the creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Nay — they have no certainty*" (52:35–36). To those who grant a Creator but want intermediaries: "*Say: who provides for you from the sky and the earth, or who owns hearing and sight, and who brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living, and who regulates the affair? They will say: God. Say: then will you not reverence Him?*" (10:31). To the abstract monotheist who does not obey: "*They will surely say, 'God' — then how are they deluded?*" (43:87). And to anyone tempted to think that creation is an act that can be repeated by a lesser agent: "*God begins creation and then repeats it, and to Him you will be returned*" (30:27), which is the link to v. 28 of this sūrah, where resurrection will be declared as easy as a single soul.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note the function of the last clause, "*in ḍalālin mubīn*" — manifest error. *Ḍalāl* is going astray from the road; the word's ordinary use is for the traveler who has missed the path and does not know he has. The "manifest" qualification (*mubīn*) is not added for the sake of the wanderer's embarrassment but because, in this sūrah, the error is visible *in the landscape*: a person who worships what cannot create has lost the road in a country with no trees to hide behind, under a sky with no pillars to blame. The evidence is not obscure; the man is.

**Ibn Taymiyyah's Objection to Over-Philosophizing This Verse**

It is worth pausing here on a methodological disagreement inside the Islamic tradition about verses exactly like v. 11, because the disagreement is instructive about the nature of the verse's evidence. The tradition of kalām built on creation-arguments the famous cosmological proof: the world is *mumkin*, contingent, and a contingent thing requires a *wājib*, a necessary existent, or its multiplicity of possibilities would never have tipped into being; Ibn Sīnā and then al-Ghazālī deployed this with great subtlety, and the *Iḥyāʾ* contains the famous epistle on "the wisdom in creatures." The philosophers' version is an inference from the *fact* of existence to a necessary ground.

Ibn Taymiyyah repeatedly objected to treating that as the *sharʿī* proof, and his objection is directly relevant here: in his analysis (in the *Darʾ Taʿāruḍ al-ʿAql wa-l-Naql*, the *al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ*, and his *Sharḥ al-ʿAqīdah al-Aṣfahāniyyah*), the Qur'an never argues from "contingency requires a Necessary"; it argues from what a human being *already knows when he looks* — the fitrah, an innate recognition of a Maker — plus the *āyāt*, the signs seen in the sky, the camel, the ship, the seed. He argued that the kalām route produced at most a first cause that has to be re-proven to be merciful, powerful, and one, whereas the Qur'anic route presents the Creator as immediately *recognized* in the thing made, and the sūrah of Luqmān is one of his standard exhibits: creation, then "show me," then the verdict — with no premises about contingency and necessity anywhere. In his reading, the "manifest" in *ḍalālin mubīn* means that the error is obvious to the untutored heart, and that the elaborate proofs are needed only to treat a sickness that argument itself caused.

A commentator working between these schools — and most of the great ones did — keeps both. The Qur'an's argument is fitrah-first, as Ibn Taymiyyah insists, and that is why the verse commands looking, not reasoning. But reason's formal scaffolding, which al-Rāzī supplies, is what protects the fitrah when a culture builds elaborate reasons to distrust it. Verse 11 itself gives the criterion for telling the two uses apart: its target is not a mind that cannot find a first cause, but a will that pays reverence to what has done nothing. On that point, the philosophers, the dialecticians, and the sage of this sūrah agree entirely.

**What a Father Is Doing When He Says "Look Up"**

The practical wisdom of placing vv. 10–11 *immediately before* Luqmān's counsel should not be missed. The sūrah could have introduced the sage in v. 12 and let him start teaching. Instead it takes the reader through the sky and the harvest first, so that when "give thanks to God" arrives, it has a content. Gratitude in a vacuum is a formula; gratitude after a census of gifts is a diagnosis. Any teacher who wants a child to thank God has to do exactly what these two verses do: walk the child outside, point at four things, name the Giver, and ask the rude question — "does anything else deserve this?" Luqmān's nine verses of counsel are spoken into a world that has already been, in two verses, given back to its Owner. That is why his son hears it as advice and not as doctrine, and it is why this sūrah has remained, for fourteen centuries, the easiest text in the Qur'an to teach to a child and the hardest to outgrow.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:12

> **And indeed We gave Luqmān wisdom: “Give thanks to God!” And whosoever gives thanks, he gives thanks for his own sake. And whosoever is ungrateful, truly God is Self-Sufficient, Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Who Was Luqmān?**

Verse 12 introduces the sūrah's namesake in a single clause — *wa-laqad ātaynā Luqmāna al-ḥikmah* — and gives no biography, no lineage, no era, no nation. That silence is what generated the tradition rather than what suppressed it; al-Bukhārī, in the tafsīr chapters of his Ṣaḥīḥ, heads the material on this verse with the words of 2:269 — "*and whoever is given wisdom has indeed been given much good*" — signaling that the sūrah's point is the gift, not the man; and the exegetes, with a few exceptions, treated his identity as a matter of reports rather than of doctrine.

The transmitted data, gathered by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, fall into four claims. First, that he was a prophet — a minority position, held by some early authorities who took the divine grant of *ḥikmah* and the imperative "give thanks" as revelation; the majority answered that the verse does not say "We revealed to Luqmān," and that the sūrah's framing of his words as *waʿẓ*, admonition (v. 13), is the speech of a sage, not of a law-bearing prophet. Second, that he was a righteous man (*ṣāliḥ*) and a sage (*ḥakīm*) of the Children of Israel, contemporary with David, from whom — in one report preserved by Ibn ʿAbbās's students — he took knowledge, and who was, in another, his nephew; some said he lived an enormous age, and there are reports making him the judge of his people. Third, the striking and recurrent ethnographic claim: that he was a *ḥabashī* (Ethiopian), or a Nubian, or an Egyptian, and in several transmissions a slave — a *ʿabd* — whose owners set him free. The reports about his physical appearance are candid and, given the sūrah's teaching on arrogance, pointed: he is described with a high nose, flat feet, cracked heels, dark skin, and, in a couple of the chains, one ear; the traditionists transmit it and also transmit the report that when he was taunted for his looks and answered with the line that a wise man's dignity is not in his shoe. Fourth, a modern fringe that tried to identify him with the Greek *Aesop*, with the sage of Delphi, or with the "wise man" of the Arabic proverbial tradition: the identification with Aesop (whose fables were translated into Arabic and who shares the animal-tale register) is superficially attractive because both are remembered as teachers of short, home-made moral wisdom, but there is no historical ground for it, and the wise judgment is our source's own: "it is most likely that he was a pre-Islamic Arabian sage revered in the time of Muḥammad ﷺ," known to the audience by reputation, whose story the Qur'an did not need to supply in order to make a point.

The reason the identity question matters at all is that the verse's claim about him is not about him. *Ātaynāhu al-ḥikmah* — God gave him wisdom — is the sentence doing the work: whatever the man was, he received. Al-Qurṭubī draws the inference exactly: "the granting of wisdom here is comprehension, understanding, and the correctness of judgment; it does not establish prophethood, since God says of Iblīs's people and of others things granted without prophethood."

**Ḥikmah: The Word That Means the Right Thing at the Right Weight**

Wisdom in the Qur'an is ascribed to servants of every sort: to Luqmān here, to ʿĪsā (3:48), to Ṭālūt (2:247), and above all to David and Solomon ("*and We gave them wisdom and decisive speech*," 21:78–79, "and We taught him the making of coats of mail" 21:80; "*We gave him kingship and wisdom*," 38:20), and it makes the definition's shape plain in 2:269:

> *"He grants wisdom to whom He wills, and whoever is given wisdom has indeed been given much good; and none remember except the possessors of insight (ulū al-albāb)."* (Qur'an 2:269)

From these texts the scholars assembled the operative definition. Ibn al-Qayyim, who devotes a long section of the *Miftāḥ Dār al-Saʿādah* to *ḥikmah*, defines it in the four registers it has in the Qur'an: the knowledge of things as they are; putting a thing in its place; doing what is right at the right time in the right measure; and, in the case of revelation itself, the wisdom that lies in its rulings. He then gives the summary most useful here: *al-ḥikmah al-muttabiʿah lil-ʿamal* — wisdom is what is followed by action; knowledge that stops short of practice is information, not *ḥikmah*. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is thinner but compatible: wisdom here is "understanding of God, correctness of speech, and faithfulness in action."

Note, then, that the sūrah says "We gave Luqmān wisdom" and at once quotes the *content* of that wisdom: *an ishkur Llāh* — "that: give thanks to God." Not "believe," not "argue," not "know many things." The first and governing output of a mind made wise is gratitude, and the sūrah has prepared for this in vv. 10–11, where the whole visible world was inventoried and handed back to its Owner. A wise man looks at a sky with no pillars under it and says "thank You." The order of the sūrah is the order of the education, and it is why the classical Islamic curriculum put akhlāq before kalām.

**Shukr: Exposing the Blessing; Kufr: Burying It**

The Arabic of this verse is a closed argument built on a single etymological opposition, and translations necessarily lose it. *Shukr*, in the lexicography of Ibn Fāris (*al-Maqāyīs al-Lughah*) and al-Jawharī, has one root sense: *al-izhār*, to manifest, to reveal, to make visible — as in the *dirham mashkūr*, the coin whose good metal is exposed by rubbing, in contrast to the plated one. To be grateful in Arabic is, at bottom, to make the favor visible: on the tongue (ḥamd), in the body (ʿibādah), in the wallet (ṣadaqah) — the three locations the scholars name when they divide *shukr* into the gratitude of heart, tongue, and limbs, following the hadith:

> *"Indeed, God is pleased with a servant who says, when he eats a meal or drinks a drink, 'al-ḥamdu li-Llāhi' — God is pleased with him for it."* (Muslim 2734)

Against it stands *kufr*, whose root *k-f-r* means to cover, to hide — the very word the Qur'an uses for a farmer who buries seed in the earth and marvels the sowers at it (57:20: "*like rain whose vegetation delights the sowers (kuffār)*"). The pairing yields the sūrah's entire psychology of ingratitude without a single additional word: to be grateful is to *uncover* the fact that you have been given something; to be ungrateful is to cover it up, to act as though the blessing arose from you, and — as in farming — the covering is not nothing: it buries, and what is buried either grows into something else or rots. Luqmān's son is being taught a skill of attention: to notice gifts. The *kāfir* of the sūrah's first verses was defined not by a creed but by an act of purchase (v. 6) and a posture of turning (v. 7) — that is, by inattention; and this verse names the same man by its opposite habit: whoever is given wisdom is given the habit of noticing what he has received.

Verse 14 will extend the same verb to the parents — "*be grateful to Me and to your parents*" — and the sūrah thereby supplies the practical test of whether a man's gratitude to God is real: it is measurable in what he does with the human beings he is not allowed to forget. The Prophet ﷺ stated the connection as a condition:

> *"He who does not thank people has not thanked God."* (al-Tirmidhī 1954; Abū Dāwūd 4811, from Abū Hurayrah; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

**"He Thanks for His Own Soul": the Arithmetic of a Self-Interested Universe**

*Wa-man shakara fa-innamā yashkuru li-nafsih.* The clause does something rare and valuable: it removes God from the transaction as a beneficiary. Al-Ṭabarī's comment: giving thanks is for one's own benefit, because God gives generously in proportion to a servant's gratitude — citing the sūrah's own law of increase, "*If you are grateful, I will surely increase you*" (14:7). The ingratitude is likewise "*ʿalayhā*," against the ungrateful one, per 30:44 and 35:39, "*whoever disbelieves, his disbelief is against himself*." Al-Rāzī's analysis, which is the most useful, notes that if God had needed thanks, ingratitude would have been an injury to Him, and injury to a being who can be injured is a kind of blasphemy: what a human being's thankfulness accomplishes is entirely on his own side of the ledger — his perception is corrected, his heart is opened, his goods are purified, his future is increased, and his past is honored.

This is not a quibble about divine ego; it is the sūrah's most direct answer to a modern difficulty. Many people today cannot bring themselves to thank God precisely because they suspect gratitude to a higher power is flattery — a payment demanded by a needy recipient, an ego-tax on the successful. Verse 12 declares the suspicion a category error, and does it by grammar: "God is *Ghanī*" — self-sufficient, needing nothing, having no lack into which anyone's praise could pour. Whatever thanks a person offers, he has put the coin in his own pocket; whatever thanks he withholds, the loss is his own. That is an unusually candid piece of moral arithmetic, and the sūrah's own closing verse on the five hidden keys (v. 34) rests on the same premise: God is not watching to catch you; He is watching because you cannot be watched over better.

**"God is Self-Sufficient, Praised" — Independence and Praiseworthiness Together**

*Inna Llāha la-Ghanīyyun Ḥamīd.* The two names were paired before in the Qur'an's treatment of spending and gratitude: "*God is Self-Sufficient (ghaniyy) and Praised*" occurs at 2:267, in the context of giving from one's wealth; and at 14:8, in the context of a town's ingratitude after blessing, where the pairing is exactly our verse's. The reason for the pairing is that either name alone can be misused. *Ghanī* without *Ḥamīd* would leave a remote omnipotence, thanked out of prudence rather than love. *Ḥamīd* without *Ghanī* would leave a being who collects praise like a rent. The two names together describe a power so complete that it needs no tribute, and a goodness so complete that everything that exists declares it.

*Ḥamīd* is an intensive active form with a passive sense: not only "the One who praises" but "the One who is, by His very nature, praised." Al-Qurṭubī's gloss here is the one our source preserves: it is in God's nature to be praised whether human beings praise Him or not — and the sūrah's own universe corroborates that claim, since in the Qur'an praise is not a human invention but a cosmic constant: "*The seven heavens and the earth and whoever is in them glorify Him, and there is nothing but that glorifies with His praise, but you do not understand their glorification*" (17:44); "*whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies God*" (57:1; 59:1; 61:1; 62:1; 64:1); and in the hadith the Prophet ﷺ reported that a stone in Makkah greeted him before his prophethood — the inanimate among the praising (Muslim 1777). Gratitude, then, is not the introduction of praise into the world; it is the joining of one's voice to a chorus already underway. Al-Tustarī and the Sufi exegetes who follow him in this verse's margin draw the devotional consequence: the very capacity to thank is itself the gift that must be thanked for — and so gratitude, correctly performed, never finishes, which is precisely why the sūrah makes it, rather than belief, the summary of wisdom.

**The Verse as the Chapter's Fulcrum**

Set down carefully, v. 12 does three things at once, and the next twenty-two verses unfold them. It closes the creation-argument of vv. 10–11 with the only response it leaves (give thanks). It opens the Luqmān section with a definition of wisdom in terms of practice (wisdom = gratitude, not cleverness). And it fixes the entire sūrah's account of God in two names (Ghanī, Ḥamīd) that make praise non-transactional and ingratitude self-harming. Every element of the counsel to follow — the prohibition of shirk, the rights of parents, the mustard seed, prayer, enjoining right, patience, humility in walk and voice — is a specification of "*give thanks*." And the sūrah's final word, "*God is Knowing, Aware*" (v. 34), is the counterpart of this one: He knows what you do not, so you should thank Him for what you do not deserve.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:13

> **And behold, Luqmān said to his son, admonishing him, “O my son! Do not ascribe partners unto God. Truly ascribing partners is a tremendous wrong.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Wa-Idh Qāla": a Clause Without a Verb of Time**

The Arabic begins *wa-idh qāla Luqmānu li-binihi* — "and when Luqmān said to his son" — and then, after the quotation, the sentence simply ends. There is no "recall," no "We relate," no completion. That suspended *idh*, "when," occurs frequently in the Qur'an and is one of its characteristic rhetorical instruments: it opens a scene and leaves the hearer inside it. The sūrah has said "We gave Luqmān wisdom" (v. 12), and now, without transition, without a genealogy, without a single word about who asked what, we are in a house, in a conversation. The effect on the audience is the same one a good teacher produces: he stops talking about the subject and starts the subject talking. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that *idh* placed thus gives the sense of *dhkur* — "remember" — as in 2:51, "*wa-idh waʿadhnā Mūsā*," and that the missing verb is not a defect but an invitation: the listener supplies "reflect on this," because the sūrah's whole method (v. 10's "*have you not considered?*") has already trained him to.

The participle that follows — *wa-huwa yaʿiẓuhu* — "while he was admonishing him" — is grammatically a *ḥāl*, a circumstantial clause, and it tells the reader the tone before the content. *Waʿẓ*, from *ʿ-ẓ*, is described in the classical lexicons of Qur'anic diction as a reminder that softens: al-Rāghib defines it as *al-tadhkīr bi-l-ʿawāqib*, calling to mind the consequences, and notes that the word's imagery is of melting — the hard heart made liquid — since in older Arabic *al-māʿiẓ* is the stone that melts. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *yaʿiẓuhu* here as "turning him toward obedience by means of what softens him," and the term's distribution in the Qur'an confirms the sense: the *mawʿiẓa* is what *ḥasanah* must qualify — "*invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and the good admonition*" (16:125). It is used of the Qur'an itself — "*here is a clarification for mankind, and guidance and mercy for a people who believe*" (3:138) — and of the Qur'an as "*a mawʿiẓa from your Lord, a healing for what is in the breasts, and guidance and mercy for the believers*" (10:57); and Noah is made to say to his people "*and I advise you [naʿiẓakum]*" (11:29, 11:30). The register, in short, is not command but care: an admonition is what one person gives another because the other is worth more than the embarrassment.

**"O My Son": Only Three Fathers in the Qur'an Use the Diminutive**

*Yā bunayya* is the vocative with the possessive suffix and the diminutive of tenderness. Its form is not decoration; *bunayy* means "my little son," and Arabic uses the diminutive (*taṣghīr*) for endearment, pity, or softening rather than for belittling. This exact address appears only six times in the entire Qur'an, and the distribution is worth recording, because it is a whole educational theory in a statistic. It occurs four times in the counsel of Luqmān (vv. 13, 16, 17, 19); once in Yaʿqūb's warning to Yūsuf about his dream — "*O my little son, do not tell your brothers your dream*" (12:5); and once in Ibrāhīm's words to the son he was commanded to sacrifice — "*O my little son, I see in a dream that I am sacrificing you; what do you think?*" (37:102).

Three fathers, and the three moments are: a secret that would get a boy killed, a command to die, and a lifetime of creed. In every case the diminutive precedes the hardest possible information. The Qur'an's own stylistics thus tell the reader what *waʿẓ* is for: softness is not the opposite of seriousness — it is the condition under which seriousness can be received. Note also the reciprocal form: the son's address "*yā abati*" — "O my father" — used by Yūsuf's brothers, by Yaʿqūb's sons, by the two sons of Yaʿqūb, and above all by Ibrāhīm's son when he answers the command to be sacrificed: "*O my father, do what you are commanded*" (37:102). The Qur'an's model of a righteous household is one in which both parties keep using words of affection while saying the hardest things imaginable.

**"Do Not Associate Anything with God": the Negative First**

The content of the first command is a prohibition, not an order to "believe." The reason is instructive. Luqmān does not tell his son to affirm God — the sūrah has already argued, at vv. 10–11 and 25, that everyone affirms a creator, even the idolaters. He tells him not to *add*. The whole of Makkan idolatry, as the Qur'an describes it, is an addition problem: God, plus intercessors, plus inherited reverence, plus the desire to keep the crowd's approval. The same is true of the modern forms the sūrah will touch: the man who buys idle discourse (v. 6) has not denied God; he has given God's time to something else, and the one who follows his fathers (v. 21) has not denied God; he has installed another authority beside Him. *Lā tushrik bihi* is therefore aimed at the most common religious failure, which is not atheism but dilution.

The root *sh-r-k* gives *sharīk*, a partner in a business — a co-owner with a share, one whose contribution is counted alongside another's. The sūrah's argument in v. 11 supplies the exact disproof of the claim that idols are partners: they have no share of the exhibit. Al-Jurjānī's famous definition in *al-Taʿrīfāt* has the same shape: *al-shirk huwa ʿibādat ghayri Llāh maʿa Llāh*, "worshipping other than God together with God" — and the jurists of *ʿaqīdah* note that the sin is not the denial of God but the *maʿa*, the "together with," the partnership that leaves God still nominally supreme while transferring practical trust elsewhere. That is why the sūrah's shirk-analysis will reach riya', showing off, in the Prophet's own use of the term:

> *"The thing I fear most for you is the minor shirk." They asked: what is the minor shirk? He said: "riya'."* (Aḥmad, from Maʿqal ibn Yasār; graded ḥasan)

And why the Prophet ﷺ policed the smallest grammatical forms of partnership:

> *"Do not say 'what God wills and so-and-so wills,' but say 'what God alone wills.'"* (Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī, from Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim; graded ḥasan)

Luqmān's prohibition, in the sūrah's own idiom, reaches the sentence one is about to say.

**"Shirk Is a Tremendous Wrong": a Category Error Made Moral**

*Inna al-shirka la-ẓulmun ʿaẓīm.* Grammarians point to the *inna* and the *lām* together — the double emphasis, "shirk is *indeed* wrong" — as marking a statement the sage expects to be argued with. And the predicate is chosen with care: not *kufr* here, not *ḍalāl*, not *ishrāk*, but *ẓulm*, a harm, a wrong, an injustice. The lexicographers' definition — *waḍʿ al-shayʾ ghayra fī maḍīʿih*, placing a thing where it does not belong — gives the meaning: to make an idol a partner of God is to misplace the creature, and by misplacing the creature to wrong *it* (a being made for transcendence is degraded by serving stone), to wrong *oneself* (v. 12 already: ingratitude is against one's own soul), and to wrong *the world* (the created signs are treated as though they had no Signifier, v. 10).

Ibn Kathīr's gloss on the verse captures the logic: "the greatest of the sins is to associate another with God in His worship, since He is the sole Creator — so to give worship to another is to give what is most valuable to one who has no claim on it, which is the essence of wrong." That reasoning matters because it treats shirk as an *ethical* crime, not merely a doctrinal one — and it explains why this sūrah, which contains no legislation and almost no legal vocabulary, begins its wisdom with the same equation the hadith literature uses to rank sins. The famous tradition of the greatest sins, transmitted by Abū Bakrah, is the exact parallel to vv. 13–15 as a set:

> *"Shall I not tell you of the greatest of the major sins?" — three times. They said: "Yes, O Messenger of God." He said: "To associate partners with God, and to be undutiful to parents" — and he was reclining, and he sat up and said, "nay, and false speech and false testimony" — and he kept saying it until we thought, would that he would stop."* (al-Bukhārī 5976; Muslim 87)

Our source makes precisely this connection, and it is the interpretive key to the order of the next three verses: shirk, then parents, then the return to accountability. The Prophet ﷺ sat up to say it. Luqmān says it softly, to one listener, in a house.

**Why a Son Hears This First**

There is a pedagogical reason, and the sūrah's placement of it makes the reason visible. Everything else in Luqmān's counsel — prayer, zakāh-adjacent social responsibility, patience, humility — is a practice, and practices decay the moment their object is disputed. A father who starts with "pray" raises a ritualist; a father who starts with "do not make anyone a rival to God" raises a monotheist who will then find rituals the natural shape of that belief. The order is also the order the sūrah itself keeps insisting on: God's right before anyone's, and no human claim, not even a parent's, can override it (v. 15). The single line that qualifies the entire Qur'anic teaching on family obedience — "*but if they strive with you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, then do not obey them*" — is built on v. 13's prior assertion. Shirk is a tremendous wrong first; therefore parental authority is bounded second.

And the "tremendous" (*ʿaẓīm*) deserves its plain reading. In Qur'anic usage *ʿaẓīm* describes what is great enough to alter the shape of everything around it: the Day of Judgment is a *yawmun ʿaẓīm* (82:19), the Qur'an calls itself a *Qur'ānun ʿaẓīm* (50:1), and the phrase *ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm* — a tremendous punishment — runs through the threat-passages as a standing description of consequences that are not local. Applied to shirk it means: this is not a mistake in a corner of your life; it is a crack in the foundation, and every pious thing built over it inherits the crack. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his analysis of why a sinner's deeds can be voided, returns to 39:65 here — "*If you associate, your deeds will become void and you will be among the losers*" — and the point is not a threat but an observation about load-bearing beliefs: a person's worship offered to a compound of loyalties is worship that has no center, and a life with no center disperses. Verse 13 is where the sūrah's son is given his center, in eight words, with the tenderness of "O my little son" in front of it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:14

> **And We have enjoined man concerning his parents—his mother bore him, weakness upon weakness, and his weaning was two years—give thanks unto Me and unto your parents. Unto Me is the journey’s end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Interrupts the Father**

The most striking feature of verse 14 is a feature no translation can carry: it is spoken in the first person plural by God, in the middle of a father's speech. Verse 13 was Luqmān talking; verse 16 is Luqmān talking again. Between them the sūrah opens its mouth and speaks with a different voice — "*wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi iḥsānā*" — and closes it. The commentators of every school notice the interruption, and al-Zamakhsharī's comment on the passage is the standard one: the parental command is mentioned in God's own words, not in the sage's, to show that it does not depend on the sage's authority. This is a real piece of argument by arrangement. A filial obligation delivered by a father could be heard as a father's interest; delivered by the Creator of fathers, it is a law of the world's design. And there is a second, more delicate effect: Luqmān, who has just told his son never to associate anything with God, does not have to be the one who tells him to honor his parents — because the God who forbade giving Him partners does not require the son to give the father a partner's rights. The two statements belong together and cancel the only confusion the first could produce: tawḥīd does not mean the family is nothing.

**"We Enjoined Man": a Bequest to the Species**

*Waṣṣaynā* is the causative of *waṣā*, and it is the verb of the *waṣiyyah*, the bequest a dying person lays on those he leaves behind (2:180; 4:11–12). Its use here is not ornament: God describes the duty toward parents as a bequest He has laid on the human race, something entrusted to each generation that will be asked whether it delivered. The object of the verb is also pointed: not "the believer" but *al-insān* — human being. The obligation is written into the species, not into the community, and the sūrah thereby aligns filial duty with the obligations of reason and nature; the monotheism that follows (v. 16's mustard seed) is addressed to the son in a covenant, but the mother's claim is prior to the covenant, since it was paid before the child could sign anything.

The qualifier *iḥsānā* is the mode: excellence, beautiful conduct, doing what is beautiful (see 29:69 and our treatment of *muḥsin* at 2:22 in this project). Al-Ṭabarī glosses: "We charged him with virtue toward them — kindness, gentleness, generosity, and a good disposition." *Iḥsān* is deliberately vaguer than "obedience" would have been, because the sūrah's next verse will limit obedience. What cannot be limited is excellence: a person who cannot obey without sinning can still be *muḥsin* to the parent who commanded the sin. Verse 15 will make that precise. The two verses are, in Islamic law's own vocabulary, the foundation of the whole chapter on the rights of parents, and the jurists' rule that "no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator" (transmitted from ʿAlī in Aḥmad's *Musnad*, and the standing maxim of the schools) has its verse in the pairing.

**"Weakness upon Weakness": the Anatomy of What a Body Paid**

*Ḥamalat-hu ummuhu wahnan ʿalā wahin.* The verb *ḥamala* means to carry — a load, a burden on the back — and the noun *wahn* is weakness, infirmity, the state of one whose strength has been spent. The phrase "weakness upon weakness" is transmitted in two directions by our source from Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Saʿdī's chain, and both readings are affirmed by the sūrah's other use of the word: it is either layer upon layer of physical hardship — the weakness of pregnancy on top of the weakness of menstruation, then the weakness of birth, then the weakness of nursing and sleepless nights — or it is the progressive deepening of one condition, the exhaustion accumulating as the months run. Al-Zamakhsharī's image is the sharper for being concrete: *wahnan dhāta wahni*, "a weakness built out of weaknesses."

Theology is present in the detail, because the verse names the *mother* as the one who carried, though the father is equally commanded. The Arabic of v. 14 says *ḥamalat-hu ummuhu* and *arḍaʿat-hu* by implication through *fiṣāl* — the nursing and the weaning are hers — and *al-wālidayn* ("the two parents") appears in the enjoined clause. The disproportion is deliberate, and the Prophet ﷺ explained it in answer to a direct question, in the hadith that stands in both Ṣaḥīḥs at the chapter on parents:

> *"A man came to the Messenger of God ﷺ and said: 'O Messenger of God, who among people is most deserving of my excellent company?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Then your father.'"* (al-Bukhārī 5975; Muslim 2548)

Three for the mother and one for the father, and the exegetes connect it to this verse precisely: the mother's *wahnan ʿalā wahnin* is a claim three bodies deep — her own, the child's carried inside it, and the milk that is her blood's transformation, as the old medicine put it. In the same chapter of Bukhārī there is the account of the man from Yemen who carried his mother on his back around the Kaʿba, saying, "I am her camel, loaded gently, I do not throw her off when she is angry," and asked the Prophet whether he had repaid her: "*lā, wa-la zamṭatan wāḥidah*" — "No, not even one labor-pang." (Transmitted by Aḥmad and al-Ḥākim; judged ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim.) The arithmetic of the debt is stated in this sūrah, and it is stated as unpayable.

**"Two Years": Mercy Written as a Legal Measure**

*Wa-fiṣāluhu fī ʿāmayn.* *Fiṣāl* is from *f-ṣ-l*, to sever, to separate — the same root as *faṣl al-qaḍāʾ*, the judicial verdict, because weaning is a verdict on a dependency: it is the sentence that ends a phase. The two years are not, in the sūrah's grammar, a command to the mother ("nurse for two years") but a description of the natural course, and the legal weight comes from the parallel verse where the same number is issued as a rule: "*Mothers shall nurse their children for two full years, for whoever wishes to complete the nursing*" (2:233), with the fathers' obligation for their food and clothing in the same verse. From these two texts the jurists derived the settled points: two lunar years is the maximum period of obligatory nursing in the majority view; after that, nursing by consent is permitted; and the extended doctrine of *raḍāʿ*, that nursing creates marriage-prohibiting kinship (*maḥramiyya*), is built on them — with the famous report of ʿĀʾishah that five known nursing episodes establish what ten once did (Muslim 1452).

But the sūrah's purpose in naming the number is not legislation, it is measurement of devotion. Two years of the mother's nights, in an ancient world without formula, without a bottle that could substitute, without any way to pass the work to anyone else — and the child's *first two years of life* are thus also recorded as the parents' *two years of life*, given away. Ibn Kathīr notes that the mention of the *fiṣāl* is included to make the son understand that what he received at the age when he was least able to receive it, his mother gave at the age when giving was hardest. There is a piece of developmental psychology in that: the child's earliest needs are the ones he cannot remember, and the parents' earliest labors are the ones no one will ever thank them for. The sūrah solves the problem by writing them into revelation, so that a duty nobody can recall becomes a duty nobody can forget.

**"Give Thanks to Me and to Your Parents": the Double Object**

*An ishkur lii wa-li-wālidayk.* Two prepositional objects share one verb, and the ordering is the point: God first, parents second, and neither object canceling the other. Al-Ṭabarī's transmitted gloss: gratitude to God is realized by praise and obedience; gratitude to the parents by *birr* and upholding the ties of kinship. The conjunction establishes, in a single line, the two great failures of later religious families in every age: the piety that neglects the mother because it is too busy being pious, and the filial devotion that has become a religion — the parent made into the authority one cannot question, the *qiblah* of the family's decisions. Verse 15 exists because the second of these was already happening in Makkah and Madinah. Verse 14 exists because the first is the subtler danger, and it is the reason the thankfulness to parents is commanded *inside* the thankfulness to God rather than as a separate concession.

That the same verb governs both objects also means that the *nature* of gratitude is identical in both: as established in v. 12, *shukr* is to make a favor visible. Toward parents, visibility has a specific content, and the Prophet ﷺ named it as being seen: "*the one who is ungrateful to people is ungrateful to God*" (al-Tirmidhī 1954). A father who works and never thanks, a son who succeeds and never calls — the sūrah calls that *kufr* in the etymological sense: covering up.

**"Unto Me Is the Journey's End": the Guarantee of Fairness**

*Ilaiya al-maṣīr.* The verse ends by moving the settlement out of the household and into God's hands. Two functions. First, reassurance: a son who has borne the two years of weakness, and then borne a parent who never once said thank you, is not owed closure by that parent. The *maṣīr* — the destination of the journey, from *ṣ-y-r*, "to come to be, to turn out" — is where every unreturned kindness is booked; and the sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah's "we will write what they did and their footprints" (46:19) supplies the image of the record: the traces left behind by a life of walking. Second, restraint: the child does not become the parent's judge. God says "unto Me" rather than "and then you may settle accounts," and the phrasing limits even the aggrieved. The entire sūrah, from the mustard seed of v. 16 to the informing of v. 23, runs on this economy: accountability is real, and it is not yours to administer.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:15

> **But if they strive to make you ascribe as a partner unto Me that of which you have no knowledge, then obey them not. Consort with them in the world in a kindly manner, and follow the way of those who turn in repentance unto Me. Then unto Me is your return, and I shall inform you of that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Draws the Line**

Verse 14 made the parents the nearest claim on a human being after God; verse 15 states the limit of that nearness, and it states it in the first person again — "*li-tushrika bī mā laysa laka bihi ʿilm*" — "to associate with *Me*." The pronoun is what makes the logic airtight. A parent's demand becomes illegitimate at the precise moment it competes for what belongs to God alone; it does not become illegitimate because it is unpleasant, inconvenient, or demanding. The condition is specified by *an* plus the verb — *idhā jāhadāhumā* — "if they *strive* against you," *j-h-d*, the same root as *jihād*: exertion, pressure, a sustained campaign. The sūrah is not describing a single wish gently expressed. It is describing coercion: the parent who works on the child, applies pressure, escalates. Only then is the child released. And even then he is released from *obedience*, not from *kindness* — which is the whole jurisprudence of the family in one verse, and why al-Qurṭubī's comment that the verse abrogates nothing but *specifies* is exactly right: v. 14 remains in force; only the claim to be obeyed against God is void.

The phrase *mā laysa laka bihi ʿilm* — "that of which you have no knowledge" — carries the sūrah's epistemology into the household. The same triple formula will appear in v. 20 about the argumenters "without knowledge, without guidance, without a book of light," and in v. 21 the inherited religion is diagnosed as exactly that. What is remarkable is that in this verse the *parent* is the source of the claim without knowledge, and the child is told to weigh it: not "they are old, so obey" but "does this have a warrant you can stand behind?" A revealed text that permits — indeed requires — a child to refuse a parent's demand on the ground that it has no knowledge behind it is installing a criterion of truth above lineage. That is the point of the whole sūrah's argument about idolatry (v. 11) applied to the smallest social unit.

**The Occasion: Saʿd and His Mother**

The reports of the verse's occasion, gathered by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Isḥāq (via Ibn Hishām), al-Wāḥidī, and Ibn Abī Ḥātim, and transmitted in Aḥmad's *Musnad* and al-Ḥākim's *Mustadrak* from Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ — Saʿd ibn Mālik — agree in shape. Saʿd was known for his goodness to his mother; when he became Muslim, she — in the reports, Umm Saʿd bint ʿAwf — swore that she would neither eat nor drink nor shelter from the sun, and said to him: "You think your religion is better than mine? I will not eat, and I will die, and people will say 'the killer of his mother,' and you will never again enjoy life." He refused to leave Islam; she held out; after two or three days she broke her fast when she saw he would not yield, and the verse was revealed, in the telling: "*wa-waṣṣayna al-insāna bi-wālidayhi...*" (31:14–15). Some transmitted variants attach the incident to a second Saʿd, and the reports differ on the details of the exchange; the sound position, taken by Ibn Kathīr, is that the occasion is Saʿd's, and the ruling is general, since the sūrah says *al-insān* and not a name, and the jurists' rule is that "the generality of the wording is taken, not the specificity of the occasion."

The reason the story deserves its place in a commentary is that it is, in a very literal sense, the first case of a situation that has never stopped occurring. The convert whose family applies pressure — hunger strikes, weeping, disinheritance, exile, threats of suicide, and in the worst cases violence — is reading, in this verse, a text that was revealed about exactly that. And the verse's verdict is not "leave them": it is "do not obey, and keep them company kindly, forever."

**"Do Not Obey Them" — and "Keep Them Company"**

Two commands in two clauses, and the second uses the verb of companionship: *wa-ṣāḥibhumā fī al-dunyā maʿrūfan*. *Ṣāḥib* is a companion; *ṣuḥba* is the lived company of friendship, and the sūrah applies it to the relationship of a believer with his polytheist parents — the only instance in the Qur'an where *ṣuḥba* is so used, as the commentators observe. The *maʿrūf*, "what is recognized as right," is the standard term for the measure of conduct that a sound community knows without being told — it is the same word used in 2:233 and 2:236 for a husband's treatment of a wife and in 4:19, so the sūrah places the estranged parents under the same law of decent treatment that governs a divorce: even where a legal bond is ending, the *maʿrūf* survives.

Then the qualifier *fī al-dunyā*, "in the world," and the jurists' inference from it is severe but honest: the worldly obligations continue; the obligations of the other world do not — one does not inherit from, nor is one buried beside, nor does one pray the funeral prayer for, a parent who died in shirk (9:84: "*and never pray over any of them who dies, and never stand at his grave*"). Al-Ṭabarī's summary of the verse's legal content is that the child owes his disbelieving parents "companionship in this world and good treatment," and this is the basis for the whole chapter in fiqh on *ṣilat al-rahim* — upholding kinship — with non-Muslim relatives, including the maintenance of a needy parent. The practice of the Prophet ﷺ fixed it: when Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr's polytheist mother came to visit during the truce of Ḥudaybiyyah and Asmāʾ asked whether she should keep ties with her, the Prophet said, "*Yes, uphold ties with your mother*" (al-Bukhārī 2696; Muslim 1003). And the general rule for all non-combatant relatives and neighbors is 60:8: "*God does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them.*"

The pairing teaches a distinction most communities never learn: the difference between *disagreement with* and *withdrawal from*. To obey is a specific act; to love and keep company is a general stance, and it survives every disagreement. The modern family with its silences, its uninvited relatives, its canceled names — this verse refuses all of it. One may refuse a demand and still answer the phone.

**"Follow the Way of Those Who Turn to Me"**

*Wattabiʿ sabīla man anāba ilayya.* *Ināba* is repeated turning: from *anāba*, to return, to come back to; the *muʾnib* is the one who keeps returning — the sūrah's own usage is with the humble: "*the one who fears God unseen and returns in repentance (munīb)*" (50:33). The verb's shape matters here, because the verse does not say "follow the perfect" but "follow those who turn," which means the way is defined by direction rather than by finish, and a son told to walk that road will not be paralyzed by his own inconsistency. The commentators add a second layer that fits the occasion: Ibn Kathīr transmits from several of the Salaf that "*man anāba ilayya*" here is a reference to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers (Qatādah), and — in the reports tying the verse to Saʿd — that it points specifically to Abū Bakr, in whose company Saʿd entered Islam: al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Isḥāq preserve that reading, and Saʿd himself, late in life, is reported to have said, "I am one of those whom God means: *follow the way of those who turn to Me*," naming Abū Bakr. Whether or not the reference is that narrow, the social instruction is clear: when family authority is broken, the person does not become an authority-free island. He needs a *way*, a path with companions on it — and in a book about a father teaching a son, the sūrah says plainly that a young man who loses his father's guidance must find another, and the one to find is the one of the people who keep returning to God.

Compare 4:115, where the same structure of "following other than the way of the believers" is what defines the exit from the community: the *sabīl* is the criterion. Verse 15's clause is thus also a warning in the other direction. A person may refuse a parent's coercion and then, without noticing, hand his obedience to whoever else offers the loudest certainty. *Man anāba ilayya* — the standard for choosing company is: who is turning to God, not who is arguing loudest.

**"Then to Me Is Your Return, and I Will Inform You"**

The verse ends as v. 14 did, with the transfer of judgment, and now with an added word: *fa-unabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "I will *inform* you of what you used to do." The verb *nabbaʾa* is used in Arabic for news that is significant and previously unknown; the sūrah will use it again in v. 23 for the disbelievers, and in v. 15's own family, both parties will be informed: the child who yielded to pressure, and the parent who applied it. Al-Zamakhsharī's observation on this construction is worth repeating because it disarms a common misreading: the verse does not say "I will punish them." It says "I will inform them," and the informing is itself the settling — which is the same technique the sūrah uses for the father whose son refused him. Nobody in this household is authorized to be the executioner of the other's record. What the believer is given is permission to stop keeping the books, because the Owner of the books has said He will read them out, in full, without error and without the distortions of memory that make family grudges survive for thirty years.

And for the young Muslim whose parents are alive and hostile, the last clause is the quiet promise underneath: the return is *to Me*, not to them. The conflict, real as it is, is not the last word. It is a chapter with an accounting attached — and the accounting is conducted by a judge who knows what lay in the mother's fear, what lay in the father's pride, and what it cost the son to keep both his conscience and his manners intact.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:16

> **“O my son! If it be but the weight of a mustard seed, be it in a rock, in the heavens, or on the earth, God will bring it forth. Truly God is Subtle, Aware.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smallest Weight a Market Knew**

*Innaha in takū mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin.* "If it be only the weight of a grain of mustard." Arabic has several words for "tiny," and the sūrah chooses the one a child in a Makkkan household would recognize: *khardal*, mustard seed, so small that it became the idiom for nothing — the same measure the Prophet ﷺ used in his own teaching ("*the Day of Judgment will be raised upon the heart of a man of the believers, no lighter than a mustard seed for him*," and in the eschatological traditions about the bridge and the scale). A weight, not a size: the verse is already thinking in terms of a balance. The mustard seed is the unit of the measurable minimum, and Luqmān begins at the minimum because the danger in a moral life is never the enormous, obvious act; it is the one that is light enough to be dismissed.

Al-Rāzī, commenting on 21:47 where the same phrase appears — "*We shall set up scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so that no soul is wronged in anything; even if it be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth*" — notes that God's promise to bring forth the mustard seed is what makes the difference between a moral universe and a merely causal one. Causality explains what a deed does; it never accounts for what a deed *is worth*. The sūrah's answer is that weight is real and is registered even when no consequence follows in this world. A kindness that produced nothing still has weight; a cruelty nobody was harmed by — in the sense that nobody noticed — still has weight. Verse 9 already promised that the *waʿd* of God is *ḥaqq*, and v. 16 gives that promise its unit of measurement.

**"In a Rock, or in the Heavens, or in the Earth": the Three Refuges**

The three locations are not a poetic list; the exegetes read them as the three ways a thing can be hidden from every eye, and the choice of the *rock* — rather than "in a house" or "in a chest" — is the deliberate extreme. A mustard seed inside a solid stone is not merely concealed; it is inaccessible, unreachable by any instrument of the age, sealed in a substance whose whole nature is impenetrability. The heavens represent concealment by remoteness; the earth, concealment by depth; the rock, concealment by enclosure. Al-Zamakhsharī and Ibn Kathīr both note that God's knowledge runs through all three, and the phrasing with *aw* ("or") covers the whole space of possibility rather than offering alternatives: wherever it is — inside, above, below — the seed comes out.

Compare the near-identical verse 6:59, which our sūrah's commentators bring forward: "*With Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows what is on the land and in the sea; and there does not fall a leaf but He knows it; nor a grain (ḥabbah) in the darknesses of the earth (aẓ-lumāti al-arḍ), nor anything moist or dry, but it is in a clear book.*" The parallel is exact and instructive: Luqmān's son is told the seed inside the rock; the leaf falling in the dark, the grain in the earth's darknesses, and the "moist and dry" — which is the whole world of living things, in the sūrah's own image — are the same doctrine in another register. And 34:3 adds the third measurement to the set: "*Those who disbelieve claim that the Hour will not come to them. Say: Yes — by my Lord, it will come to you — Knower of the Unseen, and nothing is hidden from Him, an atom's weight (mithqāla dhirrah) in the heavens or on the earth; and there is nothing smaller than that or greater but it is in a clear book.*" Note the last words: the sūrah of Sheba says there is nothing *smaller* than the atom's weight, and the mustard seed of Luqmān is being used, in the tradition's own cross-referencing, as the *human-scale* minimum beneath the theological minimum.

**"God Will Bring It Forth": a Resurrection in Miniature**

*Yaʾti bihā.* "He comes with it" — the verb of "coming" (jāʾa), which the sūrah's readers would associate with the Day itself: "*and your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank*" (89:22), "*the Day they will come forth into the open, with nothing of them hidden from God*" (40:16), "*and the Trumpet will be blown, and behold, from the tombs to their Lord they will stream*" (36:53). The choice is not accidental. What is described in one line is the entire resurrection, shrunk to the scale of a seed. And the object of the verb is a *seed*, chosen for its own natural history: a mustard seed is the paradigm of a buried thing that becomes visible, of a small body that opens and puts forth what was folded inside it. The Qur'an exploits that image constantly — "*He brings forth the sprout from the grain, and brings forth the living from the dead*" (6:95); "*so We brought forth from it growth, and brought forth from it green, from which We bring forth clustered grain, and from the date-palm...*" (6:99) — so that the father's sentence works on two levels at once: nothing is too small to be weighed, and nothing buried stays buried. The seed will come out, and so will the dead, and the same "*bring forth*" applies to both. This is the metaphysics of v. 16 and why it sits exactly here: after three verses about God's right, the parents' right, and the danger of being over-awed by a powerful human being, the son is given the sense that keeps a person honest when he is afraid of nobody — that the small is watched.

The commentators also press the verb for what it says about *reassembly*: a mustard seed scattered, dissolved, ground into nothing, or dispersed across a mile of sand — God "brings it," the object entire. Al-Qurṭubī's gloss on the Day's re-creation in the parallel of 36:78–79 — "*Who will give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He who produced them the first time will give them life*" — is the correct supplement here: the sūrah does not argue that God collects the atoms; it asserts that what has vanished is not distant from Him in any sense that matters.

**"Subtle, Aware": the Two Names That Fit the Case**

*Inna Llāha Laṭīfun Khabīr.* The pairing is one of the most carefully matched in the Qur'an, and its appropriateness is what the sūrah's own commentators point to. *Laṭīf* is an adjective with an unusual double reference: in Arabic *laṭāfah* means fineness, subtlety, and impalpability, and applied to a person's conduct it means kindness, gentleness, courtesy. When the name is applied to God, the lexicographers and theologians hold both: He is *Laṭīf* in that His knowledge reaches the most delicate, invisible particulars, and *Laṭīf* in that He is gentle and subtle with His servants — "*is not He who created all things most Knowing (al-Laṭīf, ʿAlīm)?*" (67:14), used there precisely of the one who hears the secret whisper. Ibn Kathīr, on our verse, transmits the gloss from the early authorities in its standard form: *al-Laṭīf* is "the one whose knowledge penetrates the most minute things," and he adds, on the parallel of 6:103, that God's *latāfah* includes His reaching what the senses cannot — the subtle thing that enters where nothing else can.

*Khabīr*, "Aware," is from *khabr*, news, information, and in its intensive form the name means the One who has knowledge of the inner reality of a thing, not merely of its outside; al-Rāghib and, in the theological tradition, al-Ashʿarī's school, gloss *al-Khabīr* as *al-ʿĀlim bi-bāṭin al-ashyāʾ*, "Knower of the interior of things." Together the two names describe the exact competence the case demands. A mustard seed inside a rock has no outside that reveals it and no interior that is bigger than the outside — it is a thing of pure hiddenness; and the One who sees it is therefore the One whose knowledge is fine enough to be inside it and aware enough to know what it contains. This is why the sūrah's final word about God, in v. 34, will be *Khabīr* again — after the wombs, the future, and the land of one's death.

**The Ethics of the Unobserved**

Now the practical work of the verse, and it is why a father says this to a son. A child is governed, most of the time, by being seen. Luqmān's instruction converts supervision into an attribute of the world: there is no such condition as "unobserved," and so a person's private acts are not exceptions to his public ones. The verse does not say "God will punish you for small things"; it says He will *bring them forth* — which is why it is quoted by moral theologians as the foundation of *murāqabah*, self-watchfulness, the discipline the early Muslims defined as "knowing that the One who knows you never loses sight of you." Al-Ṭabarī and the Sufi commentators alike, including al-Muḥāsibī in his *al-Riʿāyah li-Ḥuqūq Allāh*, built whole chapters of the inner life on this verse and 50:16–18, and the psychological insight is one that modern research on anonymity confirms in its own way: moral conduct collapses, predictably, at precisely the point where a person becomes certain nobody will know, and the strongest predictor of integrity is not fear of being caught but the settled belief that nothing is hidden. Luqmān is installing that belief before he installs a single rule.

For a believer, the same name *Laṭīf* keeps the verse from functioning as mere surveillance. Read the sentence to the end: the One who brings forth the mustard seed is the *Subtle* One, the Gentle One — and in 12:100 Yaʿqūb says, "*My Lord is indeed Laṭīf to whom He wills*," where the meaning is unmistakably kind. The same God who has not missed the seed has, in the sūrah's next breath, given the man who believes and does right "Gardens of bliss" (v. 8). Nothing small that you did is lost, and the record is kept by One whose name includes gentleness. That double force is why this verse is one of the most quoted in Muslim letters on repentance: it is the text that makes a person stop lying to himself at three in the morning, and it is the text that lets him get up afterward.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:17

> **O my son! Perform the prayer, enjoin right and forbid wrong, and bear patiently whatever may befall you. That is indeed a course worthy of resolve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Imperatives, One Life**

The verse is built as a list of commands, and the order is an argument: *aqimi al-ṣalāh*, *muʿruf bil-maʿrūfi*, *wanha ʿan al-munkar*, *waṣbir ʿalā mā aṣābak*. Prayer, then the public duty of moral speech, then patience in the cost of that speech, and then — as a footnote that turns out to be the keel — "that is among the *ʿazāʾim al-umūr*." Al-Rāzī notices that the sequence reproduces the three relations of a moral agent: to God, to other people, and to one's own suffering. A man who has prayer without *amr bi-l-maʿrūf* has a piety that is safe; a man who has *amr bi-l-maʿrūf* without *ṣabr* has a piety that will quit at the first retaliation; and a man who has both without prayer has, in the sūrah's view, nothing to enjoin.

The address is the fourth "*yā bunayya*" — and the repetition itself is pedagogical: Luqmān does not stack the imperatives in one sentence but calls his son again, and again. The Qur'an's teachers do not lecture; they return.

**"Establish the Prayer," Again — Inside a Father's Mouth**

The verb is the same as in v. 4 — *aqim* — "set it up straight," and the echo is one the sūrah's structure depends on. In vv. 2–5 the *muttaqūn* were defined by establishing prayer; now the *muttaqī* is being *formed*, in a home, by the same command from a father. The circle closes deliberately: the description of the virtuous at the start of the chapter and the education of a boy in the middle are the same thing seen from the outside and from the inside. There is a second, subtle link. Verse 4's three marks were prayer, alms, and certainty about the Hereafter; here the three marks are prayer, moral speech, and patience. The substitution of social action for alms and the addition of endurance are not revisions but the same character applied to the harder case: the zakāh of wealth is comparatively easy to legislate, but a person's tongue and his capacity to absorb punishment are the real tests of what prayer has done in him.

Why does a father begin with prayer when the son is a child? The classical answers converge: it is the act that gives the day a structure, the only one that recurs at set times rather than on occasions, and the Prophet ﷺ made the discipline of recurrence a legal principle for parents, in the hadith transmitted in Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī:

> *"Command your children with the prayer when they are seven, and strike them for it when they are ten, and separate them in their beds."* (al-Tirmidhī 407; Abū Dāwūd 495)

The physical discipline here was hedged heavily by the jurists — no striking the face, no severity that marks, nothing that breeds hatred of the act — and the point they extract from the hadith and this verse together is that a child's religion is formed by *rhythm*, not by persuasion. A child who has prayed at the same hours for three years has been taught something about reality before he can articulate it: that the day has a shape, that the shape is not his, and that at the hour of the prayer, whatever else is happening, he stops.

**"Enjoin the Right and Forbid the Wrong": the Social Conscience**

*Maʿrūf* and *munkar* are, literally, "what is known" and "what is not known" — and the terms are the most eloquent in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary because they make knowledge the criterion of the good. Al-Rāghib explains that *maʿrūf* is what is *recognized* by sound intellect and by revealed law as fitting, and *munkar* is what the same faculties recoil from; the pairing occurs dozens of times across the Qur'an, and here in v. 17 it is the *second half of the Qur'an's definition of the best community*: "*You are the best community brought forth for mankind: you enjoin what is known, forbid what is not known, and believe in God*" (3:110). The root is the same as in 9:67, where the hypocrites are described as "*they enjoin the munkar and forbid the maʿrūf*" — the inverse community, and a warning that a society's health is measurable in which direction its speech runs.

The duty is not the right. The jurists' tradition on this verse and 3:104 is famous for its *conditions*, and the conditions are what turn zeal into wisdom: the exhorter must know what he is enjoining; his aim must be the correction, not the humiliation; he must use the gentlest effective means; the harm of his intervention must not exceed the harm it corrects; and the matter must be one of recognized right and wrong rather than of private preference. The famous graduated hadith sets the levels of capacity, and the sūrah's son is being told, at the same time, to use them:

> *"Whoever among you sees a munkar, let him change it with his hand; and if he cannot, then with his tongue; and if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."* (Muslim 78, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

The ordering is important in a way it is usually missed: the verse makes the duty universal and the hadith makes it proportional. A person who cannot act does not fail; he descends a rung. And the last rung — the heart's disapproval — is called *faith*, not "an acceptable substitute," which is the answer to every reader who has felt that private objection is pointless.

Luqmān's context gives the command a specific shape. He is a father speaking of *religion* to a son in a society of idols, which means "*enjoin the right*" in this sūrah begins at home. This is why the classical scholars treated 31:17 along with "*save yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is men and stones*" (66:6) as the proof-text for the family's priority in *ḥisbah*, moral oversight: the reform of a society begins, in the Qur'anic order, with the household, and the reform of a household begins with a father who can say "O my son."

**"And Be Patient over What Reaches You"**

*Waṣbir ʿalā mā aṣābak.* The verb for what befalls is *aṣābaka*, "it reaches you, it strikes you" — the same root as *muṣībah*, calamity — and the object is deliberately unspecified: not "be patient in persecution," though the Makkan hearers would hear that; "be patient over whatever strikes you," which includes the ordinary, undramatic prices of saying what is right: the cold answer, the joke at your expense, the friendship that cools, the job that closes, the relative who stops speaking. This is why patience is not appended but integrated: in this sūrah the moral life has a cost, and the sūrah has just named the two things that pay it — wealth (v. 4's alms) and standing (vv. 17–18).

The Qur'an never asks for endurance without giving a reason for it, and here the reason is the last clause of the verse. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all preserve the double reading of *dhālika min ʿazāʾim al-umūr*, and both readings are load-bearing. Take *ʿazm* as the human act of resolve: "this is among matters that require firm resolve" — the meaning our translation gives ("a course worthy of resolve"). Take it as God's determination, which al-Ṭabarī favors: "this is among the things God has decreed and prescribed," i.e., these are not your inventions to be abandoned when inconvenient but commands that have been settled. In either case the verse answers the temptation at the exact moment it arises: after "be patient," a man's first thought is whether he must. The verse says: it is not yours to negotiate, and it is worth your whole will.

Patience in the Qur'an is not passivity — the sūrah's own combination proves that: the one commanded to be patient is the same one commanded to intervene. Al-Rāzī's definition of *ṣabr*, transmitted through the ethical literature, keeps the formula: "restraining the self from what it dislikes, in accordance with what the intellect and the Law require." It is an act of *holding*, and its opposite is neither complaint alone but *recoil* — abandoning the duty to avoid the cost. The prophetic hadith on the believer's situation gives this patience its economics:

> *"Strange is the affair of the believer; all his affair is good, and that is for no one except the believer: if something pleases him, he is grateful, and it is good for him; and if something harms him, he is patient, and it is good for him."* (Muslim 2999, from Suhayb)

A person whose losses are converted into gain is, in the most literal sense, unbreakable — which is why the verse puts the two commands together: enjoin and be impossible to silence.

**"That Is Among the Matters of Resolve": a Father's Summary**

Read as a whole, verse 17 is the shortest complete account of a religious life in the Qur'an: a vertical discipline you keep daily, a public responsibility you discharge at whatever level your capacity allows, and a willingness to absorb the cost of both — and a statement that this triad is not optional extra. Luqmān's son will go on to hear about walk and voice and humility, but he has been given the load first, in three imperatives and one line of reassurance. A young man who has established the prayer, spoken what he knows to be right, refused to be frightened out of it, and knows the three were settled before he was born, is a person a society can neither bribe nor bully. That, in the sūrah's vocabulary, is wisdom — given, as v. 12 said, to whom God wills.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:18

> **And turn not your cheek at men in scorn, nor walk exultantly upon the earth. Surely God loves not any vainglorious boaster.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Sūrah Turns to Manners Here**

Verses 13–17 gave creed, parents, accountability, and the triad of prayer, moral speech, and patience. Verse 18 changes register entirely: it forbids a look and a gait. The placement is the most psychologically acute moment in the sūrah's curriculum, and the commentators say so in different words. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, in the passage the source alludes to, notes that a man raised to the level of commanding right and prohibiting wrong is in permanent danger of looking down on the people he is correcting — so the sūrah, immediately after the commission, forbids the condescension the commission breeds. Ibn Kathīr transmits a comparable sense from the early exegetes: once you have established prayer and taken up *ḥisbah*, do not suppose yourself above those you address. The structure of the counsel thus enacts the very humility it teaches: the correction of the person who corrects comes without a pause.

This is a law of the whole Qur'an, not a Luqmān special. Wherever revelation gives a community its mission it gives, in the same breath, its danger: "you are the best community brought forth for mankind" (3:110) is preceded by "hold fast, all of you, to the rope of God, and do not be divided" (3:103) and followed by the long, unsentimental passage on the People of the Book, so that the claim to excellence is framed by a warning against the very pride it might encourage. The verse of *iʿtirāḍ*, 25:63 — "*the servants of the All-Merciful are those who walk upon the earth in gentleness (hawnan), and when the ignorant address them, they say 'peace'*" — is the same pairing in a Madinan register. And it is why, in the sūrah's own logic, arrogance is not one vice among others but the failure that un-makes the others: a person who enjoins right while despising people has, by the hadith's definition, already committed *kibr* — "rejecting the truth and despising people" (Muslim 91) — and so his *amr bi-l-maʿrūf* has lost its first half.

**"Turn Not Your Cheek": the Grammar of Contempt**

*Lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-l-nāsi* — do not twist your cheek aside from people. The verb is *aṣ-ṣar* in the intensive form, "to turn away forcefully," and the body part is the cheek, because in Arabic the cheek is the seat of the glance one gives or withholds. To "turn the cheek" is to keep your face pointed where you want it to be, and let the person speaking to you be answered by the side of your jaw. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is admirably concrete: "do not incline your face away from people in arrogance toward them." The two readings recorded by our source (the general command against arrogance in conversation, and the specific report that it refers to a man who used to disdain a companion he had formerly loved) are not alternatives; they describe the same act from the rule and from the case.

What makes the verse deep is the *mīʿat* it forbids — the outward sign — not the inward feeling alone. Contempt is a feeling most people cannot fully control; it can be *managed*, and the sūrah legislates the management. It says: your face is not yours to arrange in dismissal. The jurists and ethicists treated the external act of belittling as a sin in its own right, quite apart from the internal *kibr*, because the external act is what actually severs a person from others; hence also the Prophet's ﷺ prohibition of a man's shunning his brother for more than three days (al-Bukhārī 6077; Muslim 2559), and the rule in the hadith that a Muslim may not refuse to speak to his brother for more than three nights, the better one being the one who initiates the greeting (al-Bukhārī 6237). Luqmān's son is being trained not to convert his opinions into facial expressions, because an expression, unlike an argument, is a permanent message about another human being's worth.

**"Nor Walk Exultantly": the Pathology of a Gait**

*Wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥā.* *Maraḥā* is a gait of swagger, of a man inflated with himself, walking with the loose-jointed confidence of someone who has never had to hurry for anyone. Al-Zamakhsharī remarks on the frequency with which the Qur'an legislates over the body — *lā tamshee maraḥā* here; "*and do not walk upon the earth insolently*" (17:37); "*walk gently*" (31:19) — and the reason is not scrupulosity about locomotion but a theory of the self: the body rehearses the soul. Arabic already knew the phenomenon as a proverb — the proud man's walk is his first confession — and the modern study of "enclothed cognition" and posture feedback has rediscovered the same one-way street: how a person holds and moves changes how he feels about himself, and the two loop. A command about walking is therefore, in this sūrah's psychology, a command about the self.

Verse 17:37, quoted in our source as the closest parallel, supplies the reason in a clause this sūrah omits: "*and do not walk upon the earth in pride, for you cannot split the earth, nor reach the mountains in height.*" The argument is physical before it is moral — the earth you stride on will not open for you, and you will not outgrow the mountains — and its logic applies exactly to a young man being trained to correct his elders: you are walking on ground you did not make, past things taller than you, in a body you did not design. The sūrah's own cosmology (v. 10: mountains cast so that the ground would not sway *beneath you*) is the correct backdrop for this little prohibition. The man who knows the earth is *held* for him cannot stride as though he were the one holding it.

**"God Does Not Love Every Boastful Proud One"**

*Inna Llāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mufakhrin fakhrā.* The predicate is about divine *love*, and the choice matters: this is not "God does not forgive," and not "God punishes," but "God does not love" — the withholding of closeness, which in the sūrah's own scale of goods is the only real loss, since the *muttaqī* was promised, in v. 5, to be "*upon guidance from their Lord*" and the whole chapter is oriented toward being near. The two adjectives are the sūrah's last word on the mechanism of pride: *mufakhkharin* — one who makes a show of how grand he is (the intensive active participle of *fakhkhara*, to boast about one's own status; and, in Arabic usage, to make much of lineage, wealth, and genealogy) — and *fakhūr*, the same quality as settled character. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *mufakhkhar* as "one who praises himself and counts himself great over people," and *fakhūr* as the one whose boasting is habitual. The distinction is a diagnosis of two types: the man who boasts *about* things, and the man whose *posture* is boastful even when he is silent.

Note also the *kulla*, "every": God does not love *any* boastful braggart. In classical Arabic theology the comprehensive negation is the strongest form of the sentence, and Ibn Kathīr uses it here as evidence that the quality, not merely the count, is what is repudiated. The verse does not say "God does not love the one who boasts while being in fact superior" — the objection that pride is justified when the achievements are real. There is no exception in the sūrah for the man who has earned it. Al-Qurṭubī, on the related hadith of the man who asked about beautiful clothes, reports the Prophet's reply that "God is Beautiful and loves beauty; pride is rejecting the truth and despising people" (Muslim 91) — so the aesthetic of excellence is not condemned; only the *use* of excellence as a claim above other human beings is.

**The Environmental Reading and the Limits of It**

Our source records, as a modern application, that some contemporary writers invoke v. 18's gait-language in argument against human arrogance toward nature, understood as an abuse of the vicegerency of 2:30 and 6:165. The reading is legitimate as an extension rather than as the verse's plain sense — Arabic usage ties *masha maraḥan* to human-to-human conduct — but the sūrah's own frame makes the extension natural: the same verses that forbid striding over people describe a world of mountains that were *put there for you*, of animals *scattered* for you, and of a sea in which ships sail by God's blessing (v. 31), and the one who has been given all of it and then walks insolently has simply widened the circle of what he treats as his inferiors. The ethic of *ḥilm* toward the earth is the ethic of v. 18 at a larger scale; and, as our commentary on the previous verse noted, the sūrah is consistent in tying cosmic resources to manners, not to rights.

**What a Father Is Repairing**

Set in its place, verse 18 is not an appendix to ethics; it is the safeguard of everything in vv. 13–17. Monotheism breeds a certain independence, moral speech breeds a certain authority, patience under attack breeds a certain self-respect, and each of those is a seed of arrogance that will grow if nobody trims it. So the sūrah, speaking through a father, reaches for the two least intellectual places to cut: the face and the feet. Do not turn away; do not swagger. A young man who has been told this — and told it in a sentence that ends with "God does not love" rather than "God will punish you" — has been taught more about the interior of religion than a year of doctrine would. And a reader of any age who is honest will notice the verse asking one question that the sūrah's whole treatment of wisdom makes decisive: not "do I believe?" but "whom do I look past?"

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:19

> **And be moderate in thy pace and lower thy voice. Truly the vilest of voices are those of asses.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Commands About Volume and Speed**

*Wa-qṣid fī mashyika wa-ghḍuḍ min ṣawtik.* "And be midmost in your walk, and cut short from your voice." Both verbs are spatial before they are ethical. *Qaṣid* is from *q-ṣ-d*, to aim, to make for the middle of a target rather than its edge; the closely related *qiṣṭ* is the just balance in the scales, and the adjective *muqtaṣid* is the Qur'an's own term for the moderate course: "*then We made some of them inherit the Book: among them such as wrong themselves, among them such as follow a middling course (muqtaṣid), and among them such as are foremost in good deeds by God's will*"] (35:32). To be told to be *muqtaṣid* in walking is therefore to be told to occupy the just middle of a range, not the smallest value of it. And The sūrah thus puts the two commands in the vocabulary of *reduction*, and the reduction it wants is not of energy but of amplitude: neither the fastest nor the loudest, but the man in the middle of himself.

The last clause is the sūrah's joke, and there is no other honest word for it. *Inna ankaral-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu al-ḥamīr* — "the most *nukr* of voices is the braying of donkeys." *Nukr*, from *n-k-r*, is what is strange, odious, harsh to the ear — the same root as *Munkar*, "the not-known," and as *nakīrah*, the conscience. Luqmān has been building a curriculum in speech; he ends it with an animal sound everybody knows, and the effect is disarming: the child will remember the donkey long after he forgets the argument. The classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī — all take *ḥamīr* literally, the domestic ass, and all note that the comparison is to a donkey's *braying*, not to the animal's worth; the point is the sound's two qualities, its volume and its ugliness, and the fact that it is the noise the beast makes when it wants something loudly and cannot care who hears.

**The Donkey, the Ass of Scripture, and Why the Image Is Not Cruel**

Asses are, in the Qur'an, the most human of animals, and they are used for exactly the quality Luqmān is warning against: the combination of utility and noisiness, of being loaded and being loud. Four references constitute the sūrah's own background. In al-Baqarah, the man who passed by a ruined town and wondered how God could revive it is told to look at his donkey — "*and look at your donkey, and We will make you a sign for the people*" (2:259) — and the animal is revived after a hundred years, bone by bone, in front of him: the donkey is, in that passage, the living proof of resurrection. In al-Naḥl, they are counted among the blessings: "*and horses and mules and donkeys, that you may ride them and as adornment*" (16:8). In al-Jumuʿah the most severe reproach in the Qur'an falls on religious people and is expressed with a donkey: "*the parable of those who were charged with the Torah and did not carry it out is like the parable of the donkey carrying a load of books*" (62:5) — a beast with Scripture on its back and no benefit from it, which is the closest the Qur'an comes to describing the pious who are not wise. And in al-Muddaththir the fleeing of the heedful is "*as if they were startled donkeys fleeing from a lion*" (74:50-51).

Against that background, the bray in v. 19 is not an insult to an animal — the donkey in the Qur'an is a workmate, a miracle, and a metaphor for religious people — but a precise description of a *manner of sound*: prolonged, loud, tuneless, and produced while the animal is demanding something. The exegetes note that the sūrah says *ṣawt*, "voice," in the plural sense of "voices," and the comparison is not "you are an ass" but "your voice, when it is the loudest and least necessary sound in the room, is the ugliest kind of thing a throat can make." Al-Zamakhsharī's comment captures the aesthetic and the moral at once: the ass's bray unites *idtirāb*, disorder, with *shiddah*, excess — the two qualities of a raised voice at an undignified occasion.

**"Lower Your Voice" in the Qur'an's Larger Ethics of Volume**

Luqmān's command is one verse in a sustained Qur'anic treatment of the voice as a moral organ. Its sharpest expression belongs to the Madinan community, where volume was not etiquette but a spiritual test:

> *"O you who believe, do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet, and do not be loud with him in speech as some of you are loud with others, lest your deeds become void while you do not perceive it. Indeed, those who lower their voices before the Messenger of God — those are the ones whose hearts God has tested for reverence (taqwā); for them is forgiveness and a great reward."* (Qur'an 49:2–3)

Three things make that passage the commentary to ours. First, it connects volume to the state of the heart — "God tested their hearts *for taqwā*" — exactly as Luqmān's sūrah connects manners to wisdom. Second, it warns that the loud man's *deeds may become void*, a severity that commentators from al-Bukhārī onward note was, after the verse came down, applied literally: it is reported that Thābit ibn Qays, who had been loud, feared his deeds were nullified, and was consoled with the news that he would be in Paradise (al-Bukhārī 4847, 3613). And third, it establishes the *test* character of volume: the way a person speaks to someone he loves and fears is a diagnostic of what he thinks he may get away with. The Prophet ﷺ generalized the diagnostic to the whole of the moral life:

> *"Is there anything that drags people in the Fire on their faces — or he said, 'on their noses' — other than the harvest of their tongues?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2619; also Aḥmad and al-Ḥākim; graded ḥasan)

Our source quotes the near-identical wording in the hadith about the tongue; both versions carry the same claim: the tongue's produce is what is weighed, and a voice is its first product.

**The Middle Path: Neither Sluggish Nor Rashing**

"Be midmost in your walk," read by our source as "neither lazy nor hasty" (Ibn Kathīr), is one of the clearest statements of the Qur'anic *wasatiyya* — the middle — in an everyday act. The same virtue appears in the sūrah's own description of the believers' money: "*those who, when they spend, are neither extravagant nor niggardly, but hold the medium (qasṭan) between them*" (25:67), and in the command about prayer: "*do not call out in your prayer nor be silent in it, and seek between that a path (sabila)*" (17:110) — a verse about volume *in prayer* whose wording is nearly a twin of our v. 19. The ethics is not "less is better"; it is *proportioned*. A man who walks slowly because he is lazy is as far off the command as a man who strides; a man who mutters is not humble, only inaudible. Al-Ghazālī, whose *Iḥyāʾ* has a full book on the ethics of the tongue and a chapter on the manners of walking and sitting, treats both of Luqmān's imperatives as belonging to *al-iʿtidāl*, "being straight and balanced," and notes that the reason the prophets legislate over gait and tone is that these are the two habits through which the interior states leak out fastest and are least consciously faked.

Our source also preserves a second gloss: al-Zamakhsharī's reading of *qṣid fī mashyika* as "direct your walking" — aim your steps, don't drift — which converts the command from tempo to direction. A reader who takes both readings is not confused; he has the two halves of one virtue: go straight, and don't rush.

**Practical: What a Lowered Voice Actually Does**

The verse is, on inspection, advice about power. The loudest person in a room has decided that pressure will work better than content, and he is often right in the short run: noise recruits attention, interrupts, and crowds out. But it costs him the two things Luqmān's whole curriculum is building. It costs him the ability to be heard when it matters — a person who is always at maximum volume has no dynamic range left for the moment of real urgency. And it costs him the *audience's judgment*: the raised voice invites a reply at the same pitch, and an argument about the merits becomes a contest of who can shout last. A lowered voice, by contrast, does three measurable things: it forces the other party to lean in; it makes the speaker's content, rather than his state, the subject of the exchange; and it preserves the speaker's own composure, since a body that is calm slowly persuades the mind that there is no emergency. Every teacher, every parent negotiating with a teenager, every person writing a comment under an article knows the two doors, and this verse puts the choice in front of them at fourteen centuries' distance.

**Why the Sūrah Ends the Counsel Here**

Luqmān's sermon closes on pace and volume, not on the hereafter or the creed, and the reason is that a person's voice and gait are where belief becomes visible. The sūrah began with a man who would not *listen* (vv. 6–7); it ends with a boy taught how to *sound*. Between them are the heavens without pillars, the mustard seed in the rock, the mother's two years, and prayer. Wisdom, in the sūrah's own definition, has arrived at its smallest practical unit: the decibel, and the stride. And it has arrived there in the company of a father who called his son "*my little son*" four times and never once raised his voice to say it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:20

> **Have you not considered that God has made whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth subservient unto you and has poured His Blessings upon you, both outwardly and inwardly? Among mankind are those who dispute concerning God without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Have You Not Considered": the Sūrah's Favorite Verb**

*Alam tara anna Llāha sakhkhara lakum.* The phrase is usually rendered "do you not see," and the sūrah's commentators note that the verb of seeing here means *ʿilma* — knowledge, not optics — since the verse continues with "what is in the heavens," which no one sees in its entirety. It is the fourth time in this chapter that the audience is told to look: the heavens "that you see" in v. 10, the blessings and the subservient cosmos here, the night and the day and the sun and the moon in v. 29, and the ships in v. 31 — a chain of *tara* and *anzala* clauses with nothing abstract in them. Al-Qurṭubī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī both observe that the verb of "considering-seeing" is how the Qur'an conducts its arguments: not "I assert, therefore believe," but "look, and draw the conclusion yourself." It is a text that treats its readers as witnesses, and in a sūrah about wisdom — defined in v. 12 as understanding, not information — that method is the message.

**Taskhīr: the Most Misused Comfort in the Qur'an**

*Sakhkhara* means to render a thing tractable, easy to handle, made to submit without the submitter being consulted; the noun *sukhriyyan* appears in 43:13, "*that you may settle upon their backs and then remember the blessing of your Lord when you have settled upon them, saying: glory to Him who subjected (sakhkhara) this to us, and we were not able to it, and unto our Lord we are returning.*" Two features of the word do the theological work. First, it is about *capacity given*, not capacity earned: "we were not able to it" — the horse, the ship, the harvest, the sky's weather were beyond us, and they became available. Second, the verb's cousin, *sukhriyya*, is the word for "mockery" (43:13's other use is 49:11: "*let not a people mock (yaskhar) another people*"), and the sūrah's own v. 6 used the same root family for those who take God's signs "as a mockery" (*huduwan* — a different word, but the semantic field is identical). A reader who holds both senses together gets the chapter's sharpest irony: everything in the heavens and the earth is *made to submit* to the human being, and the human being alone of all creation *mocks* what submits for his benefit. The stones serve him, the stars run for an appointed term, the sea carries him, and he jokes about the messages that came with them.

The classical doctrine of *taskhīr* also has a limit that must be stated, since a careless reading turns verse 20 into a license. The universe is "subjected to you" — not "given to you to do with as you like." The sūrah itself, at v. 10 and v. 20, presents the subjection as a *niʿmah* (blessing) which is, by definition, a trust with conditions; and 2:30 with 6:165 make the conditions explicit: "*He made you vicegerents (khulafāʾ) on the earth and raised some of you above others in degrees, in order to test you in what He gave you*" — the vicegerency is a *test*, not a title deed. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his treatment of humanity's relation to the rest of creation, repeatedly points out that *taskhīr* implies both gift and accountability: animals and land are entrusted, and 21:105 ("*the earth inherits My righteous servants*") and the prophetic instructions in the chapters on hunting and on the treatment of animals belong alongside these verses. The modern environmental reading our source records for v. 18 fits here as well, and better: a world that was *subjected* to you is not *owned* by you, and the Owner's name for the arrangement is "blessing."

**"He Poured Out His Blessings, Outward and Inward"**

*Wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan.* The verb *ṣabagha* is a verb of liquid: to flood over, to pour widely, to let something cover its object — the same root gives *ṣibghah*, "dye" (2:138: "*the ṣibghah of God, and who is better than God at dyeing?*"). The image is of blessings as water poured on a surface until it runs over: not a distribution but an overflow. Then comes the division, and the sūrah's own tradition gives several complementary ones, all preserved in our source:

Outward and inward as the *channels of apprehension*: blessings received through the senses — health, food, shelter, children, safety — and blessings received through the mind and heart — knowledge, faith, certainty, love of God (the reading our source preserves from al-Ṭabarī and others). Outward and inward as *visibility*: the favors everybody can see, and the favors only God knows — the sins He concealed, the faults He covered, the strength He gave in secret. The report from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī transmitted by al-Ṭabarī runs this way: the outward blessing is the good of this life and the bodily gifts; the inward is what is between a servant and his Lord. And a third reading, also transmitted, makes the outward one *Islam* — the visible religion, the practices and the community — and the inward its *inner reality*, sincerity and the light in the heart. The three are not alternatives; they are the same claim measured along the senses, along conscience, and along faith.

The verse's function in the sūrah's structure is decisive. Verse 12 commanded gratitude; verse 14 grounded it in the mother's body; verse 20 now generalizes it: *all* blessings, not merely the ones you can name, and the ones you cannot name are precisely the ones the verse counts. Compare 14:34, the sūrah's own commentary on the impossibility of the task: "*He gave you of everything you asked Him; and if you count the blessings of God you will not be able to count them.*" Compare 16:18, "*if you count the blessing of God you will not be able to count it; God is Forgiving, Merciful.*" The pairing with forgiveness is deliberate in 16:18, and it teaches the psychology of gratitude: a person who keeps inventory finds God forgiving rather than exacting, and a person who cannot count the gifts has no energy left for counting offenses.

**"They Dispute About God Without Knowledge, Without Guidance, Without an Illuminating Book"**

The second half of the verse pivots to the objectors with a clause that is, in Arabic, nearly verbatim the opening of Sūrat al-Ḥajj: "*and among the people is he who disputes about God without knowledge, and he follows every rebellious devil*" (22:3), and the sūrah of al-Ḥajj continues with the same sequence as ours — 22:4 "*and when it is said to them, follow what God has sent down, he says: nay, we follow what we found our fathers on*" — so that 31:20–21 and 22:3–4 are a matched pair, and the sūrah's commentators treat them as one another's tafsīr. The three negations are not rhetorical piling-up; they are a complete classification of the sources of human conviction, and their absence is total.

*ʿIlm* is knowledge acquired — the demonstrative and the evidential, the reasoned premises. *Hudā* is the guidance that comes from above — prophecy, the sent messenger, the direction that does not have to be discovered. *Kitāb munīr* is the revealed text in its written, luminous form — *munīr*, giving light, illuminating, from *n-w-r*, and the epithet is used of the Book at 4:174 ("*a clear light*") and of the *Burūj*-like passages. The claim is that the objector has no three of the three: nothing he proved, nothing he was told, nothing written to check him. This is the most economical epistemology in the Qur'an, and it is worth noting what it concedes: the verse assumes there *are* valid sources — knowledge, guidance, a book. It does not dispute the possibility of religious knowledge; it demands that the disputant use one. Verse 27 will add the other half: human knowledge is small compared with God's words, and the wise use of a small knowledge is not to argue with what exceeds it.

Al-Rāzī's comment on the placement is subtle: the disputers are mentioned immediately after the blessings because ingratitude and argument are the same symptom. A person who has been *poured over* with benefits and yet disputes the Beneficiary's nature is not confused; he has a motive — *li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīlillāh*, "to lead astray from God's path," which is the sūrah's phrase for the man who bought idle discourse (v. 6), and the link is the verb *ḍ-l-l* itself. The chapter keeps insisting that unbelief is practiced for reasons, and that the reasons are usually about authority, not evidence.

**The Verse as a Summary of the Chapter's Case**

Read in one breath, verse 20 restates everything since v. 10: the created heavens and earth (v. 10) are now *subjected*; the plants and animals are now *blessings*; the wise Book (v. 2) is now the *kitāb munīr* whose absence is counted as a fault; and the man who "takes the signs in mockery" (v. 7) is now the man who "disputes about God" with nothing. The sūrah is tying its own threads before it turns to the last movement, and it does so with a claim about human dignity that the following verses will keep qualifying: everything in the heavens and earth was made for you — and you, in turn, were made for something. That is the sentence Luqmān's education has been building toward, and it is why the counsel of vv. 12–19 sits at the center of a chapter framed by cosmology: the universe is subservient to you, so do not be subservient to anything except the One who subserved it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:21

> **When it is said unto them, “Follow that which God has sent down,” they respond, “Nay, but we follow that which we found our fathers following.” What! Even though Satan is calling them to the punishment of the Blaze?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Refusal in Seven Words**

The verse gives the exchange in two lines, and the economy is itself the analysis. Someone says *ittabiʿū mā anzala Llāh* — "follow what God sent down" — a sentence with no hostile intent in it, an invitation, the most courteous form of address available in the sūrah's register. The reply is not an argument, not even a question: *bal natbiʿu mā wajadnā ʿalayhi ābāʾanā* — "nay, we follow what we *found* our fathers on." The verb *wajada* is the word for finding, not for seeking; the entire ground of the position is discovery, not investigation. The sūrah's exegetes press this: al-Zamakhsharī notes the choice of *wajadnā* over *alafnā* ("we came upon") or *wajjahnā* ("we were directed to") as deliberate exposure of the claim's poverty — a person who only ever *found* something has contributed nothing to its being true.

The formula appears nine or ten times in the Qur'an in nearly this shape, and its repetitions are a deliberate feature of the text's anthropology: 2:170, "*and when it is said to them, follow what God has sent down, they say: nay, we follow what we found our fathers upon* — even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?"; 5:104, "*sufficient for us is what we found our fathers upon* — even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?"; 7:28; 10:78 (Pharaoh's people to Moses and Aaron: "*have you come to turn us away from what we found our fathers upon?*"); 11:62 (ʿĀd to Hud); 14:9; 26:74; 37:69 — "*we found our fathers upon a religion, and we are following in their footsteps*" — and 43:23. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr both comment that the Qur'an repeats the formula precisely because it is the most common and least defensible answer human beings give: the appeal to inheritance.

**The Logical Shape of the Error**

In the vocabulary of argument theory this is the *argumentum ad populum* fused with the appeal to antiquity, and the Qur'an treats it not as a fallacy of form but as a failure of the will to test. The sūrah does not say "your fathers' religion is false"; it says the claim "my fathers held it" is not a *reason* at all. Al-Zamakhsharī's rhetorical question, which is the sūrah's own, is the refutation: what if they were wrong? — and the answer the objectors can never give is that they have not checked. The verse's second half, "*aw wa-law yanudu hum al-Shayṭānu ilā ʿadhābi al-saʿīr*" — "even though Satan summons them to the punishment of the Blaze?" — supplies the second half of the diagnosis, and it is a genuinely difficult clause. The word *wa-law* in Arabic expresses incredulous concession: "what — even if it is so?" — a construction of astonishment, and the sūrah's own parallel (2:170 and 5:104, "even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?") shows the same tone. What is astonishing is not that the man follows his fathers; it is that he follows them *into something he would not follow himself into*. The verse assumes the argumentative context is fair: he does not want the Blaze. So the only explanation available is that the "even if" is aimed at the fact that he is not even consulting his own desires — he has surrendered not just the conclusion but the *right to check*.

**"Satan Is Calling Them": Tradition as an Agent, Not a Neutral Fact**

The verse's most surprising move is to name an agent behind the inheritance. This is why the sūrah's placement matters: after the account of God's subjugation of everything (v. 20) and the pouring out of blessings, a reader might assume that an inherited religion is a private mistake. Verse 21 says it is not private, and it is not neutral. The logic, as the exegetes put it, is that the devil's method is not to invent a religion but to *maintain one already loved* — and the sūrah states it explicitly in two other places: "*and thus We made for every prophet an enemy — the devils of mankind and jinn, some of them inspiring to others the adornment of speech, for delusion*" (6:112); and "*Satan makes them promises and stirs up desires in them, and Satan only promises them delusion*" (4:120, quoted by our source in the commentary on v. 33). The Qur'anic model of bad tradition, in other words, is not that ancestors are evil but that whatever is unexamined is *available* to a force that wants it unexamined.

The phrase "*ʿadhāb al-saʿīr*," the punishment of the Blaze, is one of the most physically vivid of the Qur'an's names for the fire. *Saʿīr* is from *s-ʿ-r*, to kindle, to blaze, and in the same root the *saʿīr* is used for an oven set alight (11:40: "*and the oven (tannūr) boiled over*"), so that the word names a fire with an inside and an outside — a contained, furious heat, not a general glow. The Qur'an's own gloss on the same name is nearby: "*it is a fire whose fuel is men and stones, prepared (uʿiddat) for the polytheists*" (2:24). The verse's terror is not that the fire is hot, but that it is *ready*.

**A Sūrah That Honors Fathers and Refuses to Worship Them**

Any reading that turns verse 21 into a general attack on tradition must contend with the same sūrah's vv. 14–15, which make the kindness and company of parents a duty second only to God's, and with v. 13's insistence that nothing may be made a partner. The Qur'an's position is precise, and it is best stated as a separation of two kinds of authority. Fathers have authority over *gratitude* and *companionship* (vv. 14–15) and none over *belief* (v. 21); and their practical authority yields entirely to a command that cannot be witnessed: "*if they strive with you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, then obey them not*" (v. 15). The Prophet ﷺ operated with the same distinction in practice. He restored the Ḥijr and the Kaʿbah's structure only as far as his people's newly-formed faith could bear: "*were it not that your people are recently from jahiliyyah, I would have ordered the House to be taken down and rebuilt on the foundation of Abraham*" (al-Bukhārī 1583; Muslim 1333) — an admission that inherited practice, even defective, must be handled with the real weight of people's attachment in mind.

That single hadith gives the verse its practical register and prevents two errors simultaneously. The first is the reformer's contempt: the sūrah condemns the *refusal to test*, not the affection that makes inherited practice precious, and a Dawah built on insulting the fathers is, in the sūrah's own terms, a different act from the one here forbidden but a sibling of it. The second is the traditionalist's claim that reverence for the pious past makes testing impertinent: it does not, since the Qur'an has already said of the best of all generations that a man's deeds are voided by association (39:65) and that the criterion is "*what God sent down*," not "what the fathers left."

**The Verse's Contemporary Faces**

Verse 21 is one of the most applicable sentences in the Qur'an precisely because the mechanism it describes is indifferent to content. "We follow what we found our fathers upon" is the sentence of the Muslim born into a culture that has fused custom and revelation, of the secular person who inherits an anti-religious family stance without examining its arguments, of the partisan raised in a political identity he cannot articulate but will defend, of the nation that treats its constitution, its grievances, or its myths as self-authenticating. The sūrah names what makes them all cases of the same disease: the failure to introduce *verification* anywhere between inheritance and conviction. It is why v. 20's triple standard — knowledge, guidance, a book of light — is the positive program of the whole passage: you may inherit, and you must test. A Muslim tradition that has produced within itself the most elaborate science of *testing* reports in the world — the whole apparatus of *isnād*, *jarḥ wa-taʿdīl*, and the criterion for accepting a transmitted report, applied by the muḥaddithūn to the Prophet's own words — has, on the level of method, taken verse 21 seriously: even in religion, *find* is not *know*.

**"Follow What God Sent Down": What Is Being Asked**

Finally, the request that is refused deserves attention, because of its smallness. The objector was not asked to abandon his fathers, to lose his memory, or to change his name. He was asked to *follow what God sent down* — which, in this sūrah, is a Book that has just been described as *ḥakīm* (v. 2), guidance and mercy (v. 3), and the measure by which the heavens and the earth and the blessings can be seen as gifts at all (v. 20). To refuse is to refuse the only offer that would have honored the fathers rather than erased them, because a man who has tested the inheritance and kept it can love it as a choice. This is why Luqmān's own counsel, in the verses just before, is the verse's cure: a son who has been taught to look up at a sky with nothing holding it, to count the two years of his mother's weakness, and to weigh a mustard seed, is a son who will not be told "your fathers said so" by an idol, by a habit, or by himself.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:22

> **And whosoever submits his face to God and is virtuous has indeed grasped the most unfailing handhold, and unto God is the end of all affairs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Submits His Face": the Whole Person in One Body Part**

*Wa-man yaslim wajhahu ilā Llāh.* The idiom is untranslatable in its literalness, and every translator of this sūrah loses the same thing: *aslama al-wajh* — to hand over, to give up, the face. The Arabic *aslama* is the verb of the fifth form's opposite: *salima* means to be safe, *aslama* to deliver something into someone's keeping, so that *islām* in the Qur'an is at once surrender and safety — the safety of the thing delivered. And *wajh*, "face," is not here a body part but a metonym for the self in its entirety, because the face is what a person presents, what is identified, what is turned toward or away, and what one "seeks" when one acts for a purpose. When the Qur'an elsewhere says "*every thing is perishable except His face (wajh)*" (28:88) and "*the face of your Lord will remain*" (55:27), *wajh* is plainly not anatomy but essence-and-direction. The commentators of this verse draw the point: al-Ṭabarī glosses *yaslami dātah wa-khalīqatahu* — "he submits his inmost self and his whole being"; Ibn Kathīr: "he singles God out with his intention and acts, and humbles himself to Him."

To say "his face" rather than "himself" is, then, not a figure of speech but an emphasis on what is at stake: what a person *presents to the world* is what he has handed over, and the sūrah asks which direction it faces. Within this sūrah the verb *aslama* appears only here, and the link that matters is the next verse's *man yakfur*, and the whole of vv. 22–23 is built on the choice between *islām* and *kufr* in the sense established at v. 12's *shukr/kufr* pair. The face is submitted or it is covered.

**"And Is Virtuous" — Iḥsān as the Interior of Islām**

*Wa-huwa muḥsin.* The noun is *iḥsān*, from *a-ḥ-s-n*, "to do what is beautiful, to make things beautiful," and the sūrah's own commentary tradition here (our source's most philosophically interesting note in the passage) makes the pairing the heart of the verse: submission channels and neutralizes the passions *from the outside*, through the disciplines of the Law; virtue dissolves them *from the inside*, so that what the outer discipline was meant to compel begins to arise spontaneously. A person under discipline refrains from what he wants; a person in *iḥsān* has wanted, instead, what he ought. The two are not competing ideals but a single ascent, which is why the Prophet ﷺ, in the most comprehensive hadith in the whole of Islamic ethics, defined them as one ladder:

> *"Islām is that you testify there is no god but God and that Muḥammad is God's messenger, that you establish prayer, give zakāh, fast Ramadan, and make the pilgrimage to the House — and that is islām."* ... *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; and though you do not see Him, He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 8; Muslim 8, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb in the hadith of Jibrīl)

The last clause of that definition — "He sees you" — is exactly v. 16's mustard-seed doctrine and v. 22's "submits his face," and the sūrah's placement of the hadith's content in a Makkan chapter is one more reason to read *muḥsin* here not as "good-deed-doer" generically but as "one whose submission has become interior." Al-Zamakhsharī comments that *iḥsān* in this verse is used in its widest sense, of making one's own soul and one's dealings with God beautiful. In a chapter about a *sage* whose Book was called *ḥakīm* (v. 2), *muḥsin* is the human counterpart to a wise text: wisdom in a book is its rightness; wisdom in a person is the beauty of his inner state.

**"The Most Unfailing Handhold": the Image and Its History**

*Fa-qad istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā.* The image is a rope, a cliff-edge, a handle, a branch — anything a person grips when slipping. *ʿUrwa* is in Arabic the loop of a bucket-rope, the handle of a jug, the knob on a saddle, the ring through which a tether passes; and *wuthqā*, from *w-th-q*, is firmness that does not give: the same root gives *thiqah*, "trust," and *mawthūq bihi*, "one who is relied upon." The phrase *al-ʿurwa al-wuthqā* occurs only twice in the Qur'an: here, and in 2:256, "*whoever rejects ṭāghūt and believes in God has grasped the most unfailing handhold, with no unravelling of it, and God is Hearing, Knowing.*" The earlier verse's context is the same problem as here — the choice between a false authority and God — and the sūrah's own readers, who have just met the man who follows his fathers instead of revelation (v. 21), are now told what the alternative is worth: a grip that cannot come loose. Note also the verb *istamsaka*, "he grasped, he clung to," the intensive form of *m-s-k* — not "he held" but "he took hold of it deliberately, with his whole hand."

What, then, is the handhold? The transmitted answers are complementary rather than contradictory, and our source preserves the range: al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr report "the firm handhold is islām"; others say "the Book of God" (with the Qur'an's own support in 3:103, "*hold fast, all of you, to the rope (ḥabl) of God*"); others, "*the good word*" — "*a good word is like a good tree whose root is firm and whose branch is in the sky... God keeps the believers firm with the word that is steadfast, in the life of this world and in the Hereafter*" (14:24–27); and, in the reports transmitted about the verse's first hearers, the meaning is confirmed by a dream: ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, the Jewish scholar of Madinah who became Muslim, reported:

> *"I saw myself in a garden in which there was a pillar, and at the top of the pillar was a handhold, and I was told to climb it. I said: 'I cannot.' Then a servant came and lifted my garment, and I climbed and grasped the handhold — and I woke with it in my hand."* He told the Prophet ﷺ, who said: *"The garden is islām, and the handhold is the unfailing handhold: you will remain upon it until you die."* (Transmitted by Ibn Isḥāq, Ibn Mardawayh, and in the tafsīr literature; classed as weak by some of the hadith masters, and used by the commentators as a confirmatory report — *shāhid* — not as proof.)

Ibn Kathīr's caution is worth carrying: the dream-report's chain is not strong, but its content is exactly what the verse's plain sense teaches.

The source also records that Shīʿī exegetes — al-Kāshānī and al-Qummī among those cited — read the handhold as the authority of the Imams, on the general principle that the *wuthqā* grip must be a living, sinless guide rather than an abstraction, and this reading has its own internal logic: a handhold must be graspable by real people in real danger. The Sunni majority answers with the sūrah's grammar: *ilā Llāh* — the submission and the grip terminate at God — and with 2:256, where the handhold is what a person grasps *after* rejecting a false claimant to authority. Both readings agree, in any case, on what a handhold is for: not to be admired, but to be held, and both agree on the property that matters, "*lā faṣla lahā*" in 2:256 — no splitting, no breaking, no way for it to come apart under load.

**Why the Sūrah Says "Handhold" in a Chapter About Ships**

The image's placement is more pointed than it looks. Two verses later (v. 31) the sūrah will put its readers on the sea, and in v. 32 the waves will "cover them like awnings," and the drowning will call on God "sincere to Him in religion." Verse 22's handhold is therefore not decoration but the sūrah's own answer to the storm it is about to describe: some people grip God only when the water is over their heads, and their grip is released the moment they step on land. The person of v. 22 has gripped *before* the wave — and the *wuthqā* in the metaphor means the one grip that does not depend on the weather. Verse 32 will complete the contrast in the single word *iqtisād*, the "middling course" of the man who half-believed (v. 32's *fa-minhum muqtaṣid*): a handhold that is held at the point of drowning and released afterward is not a handhold at all.

**"And to God Is the End of All Affairs"**

*Wa-ila Llāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr.* *ʿĀqibah* is the aftermath, what comes out at the end — from the same root as *ʿaqaba*, the heel, the successor, the consequence, and the word used in 3:140 for the "turn" of events in battle and in 8:44 for what God did "so that a matter that had to be done might be done." The claim is a metaphysical one, stated in a single prepositional phrase with no verb at all: the *outcome* of every affair is at God. Al-Rāzī draws out the argument: because the ends of things belong to God alone, submission to any other is *structurally* futile, regardless of that thing's power in the present — and the sūrah has already granted the present its weight, in v. 24 ("We grant them enjoyment a little"). The clause thus serves three functions at once. It consoles the believer who has clung in a society that mocks him (he does not need the world to resolve in his favor, because the *ʿāqibah* is already God's). It warns the disputers of vv. 20–21 (their affair ends where everyone else's does). And it grounds the whole verse: a "handhold" that ends in God cannot unravel, because the only other thing a rope ends at is a point in the water.

This is also why the sūrah attaches the sentence to *islām + iḥsān* and not to *islām* alone. Submission without *iḥsān* is submission made to a judge; *iḥsān* is submission made to a Lord who is Himself the end of all affairs, and the second clause is the difference between a man who clings out of fear and a man who clings because he has understood the arrangement of the world.

**The Verse, Summarized**

Strip the metaphor and verse 22 states the sūrah's whole position in three sentences: hand over the entirety of what you present to the world, and do it to God; make that act beautiful on the inside, not merely correct on the outside; and, in the meantime, remember where everything finishes. The result, in the sūrah's own image, is a person who cannot be pulled off — a handhold with no splitting. It is the strongest thing Luqmān's chapter says about the individual believer, and it is said in the middle of a sūrah of seas, storms, and mustard seeds, so that nobody can mistake it for a promise of ease. What is promised is not calm water. It is a grip.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:23

> **And whosoever does not believe, let not his disbelief grieve thee. Unto Us is their return; then We shall inform them of that which they did. Truly God knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Addressed to the Messenger, and to Every Teacher After Him**

The verse changes address. Verses 20–22 spoke about "them" and "whoever"; v. 23 speaks to a singular "you" — *fa-lā yaḥzunka kufruhū* — and in the sūrah's own idiom that "you" is the Prophet ﷺ first, with every believer who carries the same burden included in the address by extension. What is being commanded is not patience with the disbeliever but the governance of the preacher's own feelings, and the sūrah puts it as a prohibition on grief. The verb *ḥ-z-n* gives *ḥuzn*, sorrow — the heavy, sinking kind, the kind that sits in the chest. The Qur'an's treatment of it is remarkable for its consistency: the word and its cognates occur dozens of times, and where a human being is the subject of a divine command about it, the command is "do not grieve" — to Mary under the palm (19:24), to the Prophet in the cave — "*lā taḥzan inna Llāha maʿanā*" (9:40) — and here. The near-identical command *lā takhāfā*, "do not fear the two of you; I am with you, I hear and I see" (20:46), shows the same divine habit at the head of a mission. Yaʿqūb is the exception that proves the rule: he does not receive the command, but he says, "*I only complain of my sorrow and my grief (ḥuznī wa-ḥuznī) to God*" (12:86).

The Qur'anic psychology is subtle and honest. The command cannot mean "feel nothing" — Yaʿqūb went white with grief (12:84), and the Prophet ﷺ said at the death of his son Ibrāhīm, "*the eyes weep, the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord*" (al-Bukhārī 1303). What is forbidden is the grief that turns a person away from his work: the sorrow that curdles into despair, the hurt that becomes resentment, the mourning of effort as wasted. And the verse gives the reason, in the order of a treatment, with three grounds, each removing one thing the grieving preacher had wrongly assumed was his to carry: their return is to *Us* — you are not the destination; *We* will inform them — you are not the accountant; and God knows what is in breasts — you cannot even see the case properly.

**"Let Not Their Unbelief Grieve You": the Consolations of the Makkan Years**

This verse is one of a family of consolations issued to the Prophet ﷺ precisely in the years of rejection, and the parallel texts are as famous as they are moving: 18:6, "*perhaps you will waste away (kādha) your own soul with grief after them, if they do not believe this discourse*"; 26:3, "*perhaps you will kill yourself with grief, because they will not believe*"; 35:8, "*fa-lā tadhhab nafsaka ʿalayhim ḥasarāt*" — "do not let your soul go out over them in sighs"; and 6:33, "*We know that what they say grieves you greatly — but they do not deny you; it is God's signs the wrongdoers deny.*" That last is the exact commentary on ours, because it tells the grieving preacher the truth about the rejection he is experiencing: the refusal is not about him, and his sorrow, though wholly natural, rests on a misattribution.

The link to the sūrah immediately before this one is also instructive. Al-Rūm ended by releasing the messenger from the result of his preaching — "*you cannot make the dead hear, nor the deaf hear their call when they turn away*" (30:52) — and its last words are the same prescription our verse gives, with a verb of steadiness instead of a verb of feeling: "*So be patient; indeed, the promise of God is true, and let not those who have no certainty disquiet you*" (30:60). The adjacency, which the sūrah's introduction already flags, is therefore exact: al-Rūm ends with "do not be disquieted," and Luqmān's parallel passage ends with "do not grieve." Between them stands a chapter about wisdom, as if to say that the preacher's cure is not a new strategy but a longer education.

**"Unto Us Is Their Return, Then We Will Inform Them"**

The shift to the royal "Us" is deliberate. Verse 22 ended "*wa-ila Llāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr*" — and to God is the end of all affairs — and v. 23 repeats the point in the first person plural, "*ilaynā rujiʿuhum*," so that the listener notices the claim stated twice, for two different audiences: the man clinging in v. 22 needs to know that the ends belong to God; the man grieving in v. 23 needs to know that the returns do too. *Rujūʿ*, "return," is the Qur'an's standard noun for the resurrection, and its most precise statement is the one the commentators pair with such verses: "*From the earth We created you, into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you forth another time*" (20:55) — a triple movement in which the second and third verbs belong entirely to God.

Note also what the verse promises them: not "We will destroy them" but "*thumma nunabbi-ʾuhum bi-mā faʿalū*" — "then We will tell them what they did." The verb *nabbaʾa*, to inform with significant news, was used in exactly this way at v. 15, where the same clause closed the verse about parents who coerce their children. The sūrah's insistence on *informing* rather than *retaliating* is a theological statement, and al-Zamakhsharī's observation on such verses captures its force: to be told, by a narrator who cannot err, exactly what one did — with the reasons, the occasions, and the effects — is itself the punishment. The accused's corrected memory is the court. The same idea appears elsewhere with plainer imagery: "*Read your own book; sufficient is your own self today as a reckoner against you*" (17:14), and "*That is the Day when We will seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to earn*" (36:65; cf. 24:24).

**"God Knows What Lies Within Breasts"**

*Inna Llāha ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr.* *Ṣudūr* is the plural of *ṣadr*, the chest, and in Arabic's psychological vocabulary the chest is the seat of conviction and intention: hence *sharḥ al-ṣadr*, the expanded breast for the receptive soul (94:1; 39:22, "*one whose breast God has opened to islām, so that he is upon a light from his Lord*"), and hence the *ḍayyiq*, constricted breast of the closed one (6:125, "*it is as though his breast were constricted and he were climbing into the sky*"). The idiom is not vague; it locates the failure. The man of vv. 6–7 did not miss the recitation because the acoustics were poor; he missed it because of *dhāt al-ṣudūr*, the contents of his chest — and the sealing described in 30:52 and 30:59 is the consequence of contents, not of ears.

The same formula at 3:119 was addressed to a community tempted to be crushed by the hypocrisy of others: "*you are those who love them though they do not love you, and you believe in the whole Book; and when they meet you they say 'we believe,' and when they are alone they bite their fingers at you from rage. Say: die of your rage! God knows what is in the breasts* — thus does God heal what is in the chests." (3:119, with the clause's closing words paraphrased) Our verse is the Makkan twin of that Madinan one, and in both the point is that a believer's despair is built on partial data: what a person shows you is not what he is, and the verdict will not be a verdict on a performance. The verse also cuts the other way, and a commentary that skipped that half would be dishonest. Nothing here comforts the man whose chest contains malice and whose tongue contains the words of vv. 20–21; the same clause that releases the preacher's grief is the clause that removes the dissembler's cover. God knows — and as Luqmān said two verses back, He brings forth the mustard seed.

**What the Verse Leaves the Reader Holding**

For anyone who has tried to do good among people who preferred not to be helped, v. 23 is a permission slip written by God: you may stop carrying the outcome. And for the person on the other side — the young Muslim laughed at for praying, the employee who will not sign the false document, the son who refused his fathers (v. 21) — the verse relocates the injury: you were not slighted; what was refused was not your opinion but a sent-down sign, and the refusal was received by the One who knew what the chest held at the moment it was said. Verse 24 then adds the last thing a hurt person needs to hear, that the mocker's ease is not a reward: only a provision, "*a little*." With that, the consolation is complete — and the sūrah moves, in the same breath, from the grief of the preacher to the physics of divine patience.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:24

> **We grant them enjoyment a little, then We compel them toward a grave punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Dismantle Envy**

*Numattiʿuhum qalīlan.* The verb *mattaʿa* is not "to bless" and not "to give"; it is to supply *matāʿ*, what a person uses for a while and then leaves behind — a provision with an expiry date printed on it. Al-Rāghib defines *matāʿ* as "what is enjoyed for a short span," and the Qur'an's usage is consistent: "*the world's life is only a provision of delusion (matāʿ al-ghurūr)*" (3:185; 4:77: "*say: the world's enjoyment (mataʿu al-dunyā) is little, and the Hereafter is better for one who reveres God*"); "*leave them to eat and enjoy themselves (wa-yatamattaʿū) and to be lulled by hope; soon they will know*" (15:3); "*enjoy yourselves (tamattaʿū), for truly your journey is to the Fire*" (14:30). The verse 24 sits inside that cluster, and its first word therefore already contains its second: a *matāʿ* is not a gift but a loan, and *qalīlan* — "a little" — is its measure in both quantity and duration.

The point of placing this verse immediately after the consolation of v. 23 is precise. A rejected preacher, a mocked believer, a young man whose family has chosen ease over revelation (vv. 20–21) — the natural and nearly irresistible inference from their success is that they are winning. Verse 24 says: what they have is being *supplied* to them, in small measure, and it says "We" do the supplying. Two things follow, and both are needed. The first is that the prosperity of the wicked is not evidence of divine approval; the second is that it is not unmanaged either — it is granted, measured, and will end. The verse does not say God has withdrawn from the scene; it says He is the one who is *metering*.

**Istidrāj: the Theology of the Uninterrupted Favor**

The Muslim tradition developed a technical term for what this verse describes: *istidrāj* — a gradual drawing-out, from *daraja*, a step or rung; God leads a person up rung by rung, not to raise him but to expose him. The hadith that fixed the term for the whole tradition is this:

> *"When you see God giving to a servant from the world what he loves while he persists in disobedience, know that it is only istidrāj."* Then the Messenger of God ﷺ recited: "*So when they forgot what they had been reminded of, We opened to them the doors of all things, until, when they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly, and at once they were in despair.*" (6:44) The report is transmitted in Aḥmad's *Musnad* and quoted by the exegetes on our verse; the Qur'an's own statement of the mechanism is 7:182–183: "*Those who deny Our signs — We will draw them on gradually (sanastadrījuhum), from where they do not know; and I respite them — indeed My plan (kadī) is firm.*"

The classical discussions of divine justice made the mechanism explicit, because the verse poses a hard question: is "granting enjoyment a little" a *reward*? Al-Zamakhsharī, whose Muʿtazilī commitments require that God not benefit the wicked gratuitously, reads *numattiʿuhum* as *istidraj* and stresses the word "a little" to deny the disbelievers' prosperity any standing as divine favor. Ibn Kathīr, transmitting al-Ṭabarī's reading, simply affirms that God gives them provision in this world "then compels them" to the punishment, without the Muʿtazilī narrowing — and cites 10:69–70 and 16:55 in support. Ibn Taymiyyah's analysis of the Ashʿarī–Muʿtazilī dispute over "the good and the best" (*al-ṣalāḥ wa-l-aṣlaḥ*) bears on the same verse from the other direction: he argues that God is neither obliged to reward the obedient in this world nor obliged to withhold from the sinner, and that worldly abundance and worldly hardship are both, in the sūrah's own terms, *balāʾ* — testing material: "*We will test you with something of fear and hunger and loss of wealth and lives and fruits*" (2:155). A prosperity is a trial exactly as a calamity is, and the sūrah speaks of the past nations in one sentence that could be our verse's own gloss: "*and We gave them enjoyment for a little while, then a severe punishment seized them*" (45:21; cf. 26:206, 29:66). What matters, in every one of these readings, is that v. 24 has removed the one inference that actually corrupts a believer's mind: that God's favor can be read off a bank statement.

**"Then We Compel Them": the Verb, and What It Does Not Mean**

*Thumma naṭṭarruhum ilā ʿadhābin ghāliẓ.* The verb is the intensive stem of *ṭ-r-r/w*, to drive, press, impel: *naṭṭara ilā*, "he drove toward." It is the language of driving cattle, and the same stem is used in the Qur'an of the gathering of the last day: "*and the day when He will gather them all together, and then say to those who associated partners: where are your partners you used to claim?*" (6:22) — a gathering no one walks away from. The careful reading must be that the compulsion here is not the creation of a punishment against an unwilling object but the closing of the road the man has himself been building: nothing is added to him that was not already the shape of his own choices; he is *driven* only in the sense that a current carries the man who refused to swim. This is how al-Qurṭubī reads *naṭṭarra* in such verses — as compulsion effected by withdrawal of ability, not by force against will — and it matters, because a verse that promised punishment imposed from outside would sit badly with the sūrah's own insistence, at v. 23, that God "informs" them of *what they did*.

The adjective *ghāliẓ*, "thick, coarse, heavy," is one of the Qur'an's most physical descriptions of pain, and it occurs at 14:17, of the man whose wealth and self-importance profited him nothing at the end: "*he will come to death from every place, though he is not dying — and beyond that is a punishment ghāliẓ.*" Its lexical sense is of a substance too dense to be absorbed — the opposite of the "fine" (*laṭīf*, v. 16). The sūrah has just named God *al-Laṭīf*, the Subtle; the punishment is called *ghāliẓ*, coarse. The pairing is a piece of rhetoric too exact to be accidental: the One whose knowledge enters the finest hidden grain is the One whose recompense has nothing fine about it — and in a chapter whose whole theme is proportion, the coarse is what is left when a person rejects the proportioned.

**The Little and the Much: the Sūrah's Arithmetic of Time**

*Qalīlan* is the sūrah's own unit of comparison, and readers miss how often it returns. Verses 16 and 34 weigh the smallest things — a mustard seed, what is in a womb, tomorrow's earnings; v. 27 floods an ocean of ink to show that God's words do not run out; and here, "a little" enjoyment. The sūrah is teaching one calculation, and it is the calculation of the Day: "*they will say: how many years did you remain on earth? They will say: we remained a day or part of a day; ask those who count. He will say: you remained only a little, if only you had known*" (23:112–114). The point of v. 24's "a little" is not to minimize the pleasure but to measure it against the alternative in a single scale — the same scale that will hold the mustard seed.

Read this way, the verse is the antidote to the specific sickness of a comparison culture: the sense that the person with more time, money, visibility, or ease has won. The Qur'an's answer is a rate of exchange rather than a sermon about austerity: what they have is *matāʿ*; what they will be driven toward is *ghāliẓ*; and between them is *qalīl*. Any arithmetic that counts the last term as zero is a bad arithmetic, and Luqmān's whole educational program — the seed, the rock, the two years, the lowered voice — is training in counting it.

**What the Verse Does Not Teach**

Two cautions belong in any honest commentary on v. 24. First, it is not a curse on prosperity: the sūrah itself has described God pouring out blessings outward and inward (v. 20) as the ground of gratitude, and the believers are promised *naʿīm* (v. 8), a word from the same family. The verse's target is not enjoyment but *enjoyment mistaken for vindication*. Second, it is not a prediction of a fixed date, and to use it as one — "see, they will be punished soon, watch" — is to do to others what v. 34 forbids: claiming the Hour's timing. The word *thumma* ("then") in the verse belongs to God's sequencing, not to anyone's calendar; the sūrah's only stated timetable is "a little," and its only stated certainty is the destination. Between those two, the verse leaves the believer with a job rather than an opinion: to keep the ledger open without keeping the score.

**A Last Note on the Sūrah's Manner**

It is worth observing how gently the chapter handles its harshest clause. V. 23 told the Prophet not to grieve. V. 24 tells him not to worry either — not because the disbelievers will be spared, but because their case is already being administered by a power that does not lose track of a seed. That is why this sūrah never needs to shout; and a reader who has been with Luqmān since v. 13, where a father lowered his voice to teach a son, should notice that the God of the chapter has been doing the same: the *ʿadhāb al-ghāliẓ* is stated in six words, and the *Gardens of bliss* in four.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:25

> **And wert thou to ask them, “Who created the heavens and the earth?” they would surely say, “God.” Say, “Praise be to God.” Nay, but most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse About the Answer, Not the Question**

The verse is built as a thought experiment — *wa-laʾin saʾaltahum* — "and if you were to ask them" — followed by a predicted reply and then a response. What is unusual is that the argument is over before it begins: the question is asked, the answer is supplied in advance, and the sūrah never waits for the reply. That is the whole strategy of this chapter's final movement. The Meccan idolaters are not being argued into theism; they are being shown that their theism does nothing for them. Al-Rāzī, on this family of verses, notes that the Qur'an repeatedly treats the opponents as *muqirrin* — concede-ers — and directs its force not against their creed about God but against their conduct toward Him. The verse's real content, then, is not the syllogism but the last words: "*bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn*" — "nay, most of them do not know." The people who give the right answer about creation do not *know*, and the sūrah's claim is that knowing something about God and knowing God are different states.

The same question, with the same predicted answer, recurs seven or eight times, and the repetition is a deliberate feature of the Qur'an's method of confrontation with inherited religion: 29:61, 63, 66; 10:31; 23:84–89; 39:38; 43:9, 87; 31:25. In 43:87 the formula includes the sūrah's own verdict in the sharpest form — "*And if you ask them, who created them, they will say: God. Then how are they deluded?*" — where the "how" (*fa-annā yuʾfakūn*) is an exclamation of astonishment, and it is the same *fa-anna* construction that Luqmān's audience had heard at 43:87's earlier verse. In 29:61–63 the same concession is followed by the detail our sūrah only implies: they concede that God created, and yet they take masters besides Him. The Qur'an states the psychology of that combination in one plain line elsewhere: "*They worship besides God that which neither harms them nor benefits them, and they say: these are our intercessors with God*" (10:18) — the association is not a failure of cosmology but a bid for support.

**"Say: Praise Be to God": Two Ways to Read a Thank-You**

*Qul: al-ḥamdu li-Llāh.* The response is not "correct!" nor "then why do you worship others?" but a formula of praise — and the commentators read it in two complementary registers. Al-Ṭabarī's is the primary: praise belongs to God alone for the existence of the created order, so that your concession has already testified to what we require of you; and it is also praise that God is *praised* even by those who misdirect their thanks. Ibn Kathīr adds a second note: the phrase is a *taʿajjub*, an expression of wonder, as in 17:1, "*subḥāna alladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi*" and as in the *ḥamdala* one utters at a remarkable thing — the way a man says "all praise to God" when he hears an astonishing answer. In our source's words: "the Prophet is then told to say, *Praise be to God*, meaning that only God should be praised for the existence of the created order, not those who had nothing to do with bringing it forth, and that no being other than God should be worshipped."

Both registers are needed, because the two are doing different jobs. The first establishes the *sūrah's* rule about gratitude — the entire chapter's argument (vv. 12, 14, 20, 31) — praise is owed to the Giver, and to no one else, because no one else gave. The second is the Prophet's own emotional instruction, and it teaches a preacher how to handle a concession: he is told to *praise God*, not to gloat. This is a text that regulates the moment of victory in an argument. A debater who has just extracted a concession from his opponent will be tempted to press the advantage; the sūrah tells him instead to turn the admission into a *ḥamd*. Verse 23 told him not to grieve over their refusal; v. 25 tells him not to exult over their agreement.

**Why the Concession Proves More Than They Intended**

The logic deserves to be spelled out, because this verse is the hinge of the sūrah's whole argument against *shirk*. The concession "God created the heavens and the earth" is not a neutral piece of theism; in the Qur'an's usage it carries, by implication, everything that the act of creation consists in: the pillars-less sky (v. 10), the ballasted earth, the animals, the rain, the growth — and therefore the *taskhīr*, the subjection of all that to human beings, and the blessings poured out "outward and inward" (v. 20). If the sūrah's preceding verses are in the reader's ear, the answer "God" is a verdict: the one who did all that is the one who is praised, and the idols of vv. 11 and 25 have, by the concession itself, been assigned exactly zero. That is why our source glosses the praise formula with both halves: praise belongs to God *and* nothing besides Him should be worshipped. The second half is not an extra command; it is the first half's consequence.

This is also why the closing words, "*most of them do not know*," are so withering. The sūrah does not say "most of them do not believe." It says they do not *know* — *lā yaʿlamūn* — and the distinction is the sūrah's own: in v. 20, the argumenters were "without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating book," and in Luqmān's world, *ʿilm* is precisely what would connect a true premise to a true life. A person can store a correct sentence about God and still be in the state of *jahl*, because he has not let the sentence do what sentences are for. The verse is therefore a theory of the gap between profession and knowledge, and it is why the whole sūrah keeps insisting on *yaqīn* (v. 4), *ʿilm* (v. 15, 20, 25, 27), and *khabīr* (v. 34): knowledge is what the mustard-seed God can see inside the rock.

**"Most of Them": the Sūrah's Honest Quantifier**

An overlooked word here is *akthar*, "most." The Qur'an does not say "all of them," and its commentators note the softening as an instance of the sūrah's justice: some of them *do* know, and in fact the sūrah is being recited in a city where, within a decade, most of them would. The same sentence, *bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn*, appears at 29:63 and 43:55 as well, and it is a signature of the Qur'an's realism about populations: it diagnoses majorities without foreclosing individuals, and the principle that governs the difference between them is the one the Qur'an states most bluntly: "*whom God guides, he is the guided one; and whom He sends astray — for him there is no guiding friend*" (7:186; 13:33; 18:17). A "most" is a real number, not a final verdict, and the sūrah leaves the door open because it expects people to walk through it.

**The Verse's Place in the Chapter's Architecture**

Verses 25–26 (our source treats them together, and we will take v. 26 separately while preserving the pairing) mark the sūrah's turn from the argument about *shirk* to the argument about *independence*. Verse 25 wins the creation question; v. 26 will state what follows from it — that everything in the heavens and earth belongs to God, and that He is *al-Ghanī al-Ḥamīd*. Verse 25 is therefore the courtroom moment and v. 26 the sentence. Read as a pair with the earlier creation-argument (vv. 10–11), the movement is a ring: at v. 11 the challenge was "show me what they created," which is addressed to the wrongdoers' *ability*; at v. 25 the challenge is "who created," which is addressed to their *conscience*, and the second is the stronger — the sūrah does not need to prove the idols are powerless, only to make the worshipper say it himself. That is why this sūrah is called *al-ḥikmah* in miniature: it argues by extracting the opponent's own premises, exactly as a father does with a son, asking not "do you accept my authority?" but "who made the thing you are proud of?"

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:26

> **Unto God belongs whatsoever is in the heavens and on the earth. Truly God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Ends the Case**

Verse 25 extracted a concession; verse 26 is the concession's consequence, and it is stated as a fact about ownership rather than as an argument. *Lillāhi mā fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — "to God belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth." The Arabic uses no verb at all: a prepositional phrase and a relative clause, and the reader supplies "belongs," "owns," "is in the power of." That verbless form, *jumla ismiyya*, is the Qur'an's preferred construction for permanence — the same device behind "God is the Living, the Self-Subsisting" and "their abode is the Fire" — and here it says that God's ownership is not an event with a date but a standing condition of everything. Al-Zamakhsharī, who is attentive to these choices, notes in his commentary that such clauses set up a contrast with what human beings imagine they hold: your idols are *among* what belongs to Him, not a party to it, and the man who entrusts himself to them has given a share of what is already somebody else's.

The same formula, in the same position of a chapter's close, appears at 2:284, "*to God belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth*," which is the verse that concludes al-Baqarah's longest stretch of legislation with the claim that everything, including what is in the breasts, is already His, and at 5:120, "*to God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them*"; at 3:109 and 24:42, "*and to God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth, and to God is the return*." The last is closest to our pair: the ownership clause followed by the return clause, exactly as vv. 22–23 of this sūrah have it. The sūrah has been saying, in a different idiom all along, the thing the juridical language of *mulk* says: nothing in the world is ownerless, and nothing is owned by its user.

**Two Names, One of Them the Chapter's Anchor**

*Wa-inna Llāha huwa al-Ghanīyyu al-Ḥamīd.* The pairing was already given at v. 12, at the head of the Luqmān section, and the source notes the deliberate echo: "*regarding the divine names al-Ghanī al-Ḥamīd, see 31:12c.*" This is a structural fact about the chapter, not an accident of diction: the sūrah's longest unit is bracketed by the same two names, and the reader who has followed the argument from v. 12 to v. 26 arrives back at them with the content filled in. At v. 12 they proved that gratitude to God is a favor to the self (v. 12's "*whoever is ungrateful — his Lord is Ghanī, Ḥamīd*"); at v. 26 they prove that *shirk* is not merely wrong but incoherent, because the partners are possessions, and the Possessor does not require the tribute of the owned.

*Al-Ghanī* deserves the technical unpacking the scholars gave it, because in ordinary Muslim speech it has been flattened to "rich." Its root *gh-n-y* means to be free of need of a thing, and the noun *ghinā* is opposed to *faqr*, indigence, in the specific sense of *istighnāʾ*: not "having much" but "not requiring." The Qur'an uses it this way of human beings too — "*if the two of them wish, God will reconcile them; God is Ghanī, Knowing*" (4:130) — where "rich" would be nonsense and "not in need of the outcome" is exactly the point. Al-Qurṭubī, on this verse and on 2:263, transmits the summary: God is *Ghanī* of the worship of His servants and *Ḥamīd* in Himself whether or not He is thanked, and both halves are required, since the first makes human worship unnecessary to God and the second makes it non-arbitrary: He is *Ḥamīd*, praiseworthy, so that praise given to Him is not flattery but accurate description.

That the sūrah's audience were idolators whose gods were *asked for rain, for victory, for intercession* makes the names polemical rather than abstract. The logic is one line: if God needed nothing, then every intermediary is either (a) able to give something God withholds, which is impossible, or (b) able to oblige God, which is a contradiction of *ghinā*; and in both cases the intermediary is a possession of the same Owner. Al-Rāzī draws the conclusion with the jurist's bluntness in his treatment of *tawassul*: what can be commanded is not what can be worshipped; and only what is *ghaniyy* over all things is worthy of *ibādah*.

**"The Praised": Praise as Fact, Not Petition**

*Ḥamīd* — and in the sūrah's own cosmology, the word is a description of the world rather than a request for it. Everything praises; verses 17:44 and 57:1, 59:1, 61:1, 62:1, 64:1 (all quoted in our commentary on v. 12) establish that the heavens, the earth, and all between glorify Him, and 22:18 extends the choir to "*the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the moving creatures, and many of mankind*." The one exception named — *wa-kathīrun wa-ḥaqqa ʿalayhi al-ʿadhāb*, "and many, but the punishment has become due for them" — is exactly the population of vv. 6–7 and 20–21: not beings who do not praise, but beings who refuse to join in the praising everything else is doing. So when Luqmān's audience is told "*al-Ḥamīd*," the claim is not that God is complimentary but that He is *constantly and universally praised*, by the sky that stays up, the mountains that hold, the plants that give, and the animals that carry — and that the one species capable of deliberately *withholding* praise is thereby the most anomalous thing in the inventory.

**The Verse's Function as a Seal**

Set at this point in the chapter, v. 26 does three closing jobs at once, and this is why the source groups it with v. 25 as one unit. It closes the *creation-argument*: everything visible belongs to God, so the argument of vv. 10–11 needs no addition. It closes the *polemic against inherited religion*: the "fathers" of v. 21 owned nothing, which is the sūrah's quiet and devastating answer to ancestral authority — you cannot inherit a deed to what belongs to God. And it closes the *gratitude-argument* of vv. 12 and 20: gratitude has no other object, because no other hand has anything to give. A reader who has been following the mustard-seed thread (v. 16), the subjection thread (v. 20), and the two-years thread (v. 14) will notice that v. 26 gathers them into a single sentence of ownership: your mother's two years, the seed in the rock, the earth's stability, the ships and the sun, were all *His* property lent to you and to her.

Finally, the pairing of the two names answers a question a listener might be left with after v. 25: if God's rights are absolute and He needs nothing, does worship matter at all? The sūrah's own theology has already said yes — in v. 4's prayer, v. 17's command, v. 22's handhold — and v. 26 explains why: because *Ḥamīd* makes the praise real rather than needed, and because a human being made for thankfulness (v. 12) achieves precisely what he is when he gives it. *Ghanī* says the praise does not profit God; *Ḥamīd* says it is not therefore a waste. Between those two words is the entire ethics of worship in Islam, and the sūrah has placed the pair here as its lock.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:27

> **And if all the trees on earth were pens, and if the sea and seven more added to it [were ink], the Words of God would not be exhausted. Truly God is Mighty, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Own Parable of the Unfathomable**

*Wa-law anna shajarata fī al-arḍi aqlāmun wa-al-baḥru yamdadduhu min baʿdihi sabʿatu abḥurin nafidat kalimātu Llāh.* A reader should let the arithmetic of this sentence fail before he moves on. Take every tree on the planet; assume, per impossible, that each becomes a pen. Take the sea — in Arabic *al-baḥr*, the mass of water, the salt ocean — and pour it as ink. Then add seven more seas. Now write. The verse's verdict is one word: *nafidat* — "would run out." The pens snap, the ink is gone, the trees are used up, and the Words of God are not finished. The sūrah is not describing a large quantity; it is performing an act of *reductio* against the only model of mind its audience had: the human mind, which writes, runs out of ink, needs a new quill, and forgets.

This hyperbole is not the Qur'an's one experiment of its kind. The almost identical image stands in Sūrat al-Kahf, at the very place where Moses asks to learn from al-Khiḍr, and the pairing is the sūrah's own commentary: "*Say: if the sea were ink for the words of my Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of my Lord were exhausted, and We would bring the like of it as a reinforcement*" (18:109). Our source's cross-reference to 18:109 is therefore exactly right, and the difference is instructive: there, one sea plus its like; here, the ocean plus seven more, and pens added to the ink. Luqmān's version is the larger because the sūrah's claim is larger — not about knowledge of one hidden matter but about *kalimāt Allāh*, God's words entire.

**What Are "the Words of God"?**

The commentators offer three glosses, and the verse is stronger for holding all three.

First, *kalimāt* as *creative commands*: His word to a thing is "*kun fa-yakūn*" — "Be, and it is" (36:82; 2:117; 19:35; 40:68), which is precisely the association our source makes. On this reading the verse says: the possible creations of God are inexhaustible, because the divine utterance does not consume material; the supply of what *can be made* is not bounded by what has been made. Second, *kalimāt* as revealed speech — the Book, its stories, its laws, its names, and the inexhaustibility of its sense: al-Ṭabarī reports from the early authorities that no scholar has ever exhausted the sūrah's meanings, and al-Qurṭubī's famous remark, quoted again and again in the science of the Qur'an, is that from this verse "one knows that the meanings of the Qur'an are not exhausted." Third, *kalimāt* as knowledge — what God knows, including "the words" by which He orders and names everything. Ibn Kathīr, on 18:109, transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss in the sense of "knowledge," and al-Zamakhsharī notes that the verse is meant to make the hearer unable to conceive of any limit.

Al-Rāzī's synthesis is the most useful: the words of God are inexhaustible because they are not *things*, they are *acts of a power*, and a power whose essence is not composite is not diminished by what it produces. That is the reason the sūrah seals the verse with "*ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm*": the Mightier One's capacity has no reservoir that drains; the Wise One's utterances have no waste-product. Whatever a reader learns from the verse about God's knowledge, the two names teach him that it is neither limited nor indiscriminate.

**The Occasion: "You Have Been Given Little — and Much"**

The reports of the verse's occasion, gathered by al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī in *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*, and Ibn ʿAbbās's transmitters, all revolve around one difficulty, and it is a good one. A group of the People of the Book — in the Madinan setting of several versions, a deputation that included rabbis — asked the Prophet ﷺ about 17:85: "*and you have not been given of knowledge except a little*." Did that verse describe them or the Arabs? He said: "Both." They objected: have we not been given the Torah, "*a clarification of all things*" (16:89)? The reply in the transmitted reports: in relation to God's knowledge, what you have is little — and enough. They pressed further: but God says "*whoever is given wisdom has been given much good*" (2:269). How can "little" and "much good" belong to the same thing? And in the report in al-Wāḥidī, it was for that objection that 31:27 came down — with a different group of Makkan disbelievers in another version, saying "what Muḥammad reports will soon run out" (al-Rāzī's and our source's note).

The verse's answer is not a correction of either number; it is a change of scale. "Little" and "much" are both true, because one is measured against *the ocean* and the other against *thirst*. Two liters is nothing and everything, depending on whether the question is "how much water is in the world" or "will you survive the crossing." The commentators extract exactly that harmonization: the knowledge a human being is given is small compared with God's, and enormous compared with what a human being needs — so that both 17:85 and 2:269 are plain statements, not a contradiction, and 31:27 is the verse that tells you which yardstick is which.

There is a second edge, sharper, in the occasion: the dispute was between two religious parties about the size of their libraries. A revealed text that ends such a quarrel by declaring both libraries drops of water is not taking either side; it is relocating the standard. This sūrah, which has been arguing from the sky, the seed, and the sea, here finishes the argument in the one register that humbles scholars as well as merchants.

**"Seven Seas": the Idiom, and Why Literal-Mindedness Loses**

Our source records that *sabʿatu abḥur* is read by the majority as *not literal* but as the Arabic idiom for an unbounded quantity, and there is a solid lexicographical reason: the Arabs used "seven," "seventy," and "a thousand" for the large and incalculable ("*sabʿūna ḥisāban*" and the *khumūl* of indefinite numbers; al-Farrāʾ and the grammarians note the device in the Qur'an, e.g. 2:261 "*like a grain that sprouted seven ears, in each ear a hundred grains*" — where a total is being made deliberately ungraspable). Al-Zamakhsharī comments that the intent is *al-taqrib ilā al-afhām bi-l-mubālagha* — bringing the meaning home to minds by pushing past the imaginable. A reader who instead works out how many seas would be needed misses the sentence's operation entirely: the number is the sound of the arithmetic failing.

**What the Verse Teaches About Intellectual Pride**

The verse is, in its place in the chapter, the cure for the vice of v. 20's *mujādala*, arguing without knowledge. The sūrah has already said the disputers have no knowledge, no guidance, no book; v. 27 now says that even the possessed and the guided — prophets included, in the reports of v. 17:85 — have "little," so that *quantity* of knowledge is not the measure of a person at all. That is a real and uncomfortable proposition for a civilization, ours very much included, that has made the accumulation of information the axis of prestige. Luqmān's sūrah defines wisdom twice, in v. 2 as an attribute of the Book and in v. 12 as a divine gift whose first output is *thanks*; both definitions are re-stated in v. 27: the wise person knows that the sea of what he does not know is what he is standing in, and — the crucial half — that knowing it does not paralyze him, because *Ḥakīm* guarantees that what he has been given was given *for* him: a pen-sized share, sized to the hand, which is enough to write the thing he was made to write.

There is a further, quieter point about ink and trees. The instruments the verse imagines are *the earth's own vegetation* — the very trees whose "noble kinds" were made to grow in v. 10, out of the sent-down water, as signs. The sūrah takes the same natural world it has just presented as evidence and turns it into the stationery. Everything is material for two uses: to be written on, and to be read as a writing. That double role, in a chapter named after a sage, is the definition of the Muslim theory of the physical world: creation is not merely a thing to use and not merely a thing to admire; it is the page.

**"God Is Mighty, Wise": Why the Two Names Close the Verse**

The pairing *ʿAzīz Ḥakīm* with an image of inexhaustibility answers the question the verse would otherwise raise: *why* should a human being not despair of learning enough? *ʿAzīz* says: the supply belongs to an undiminished power, so a person's limits are not evidence of a thin God; *Ḥakīm* says: and what is given you is given with a purpose, so your limits are not an oversight. The sūrah's whole theology of *yaqīn* (v. 4) depends on that. A man who grasps that his knowledge is a drop and that the drop was measured out on purpose can work at his drop all his life without either arrogance or despair — and, in v. 28, he can also believe in a resurrection without asking how long it takes.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:28

> **Your creation and your resurrection are as naught but a single soul. Truly God is Hearing, Seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Answers the Question Nobody Asks Out Loud**

Verse 27 said God's words do not run out. Verse 28 removes the only remaining excuse for doubting: that even an inexhaustible speech might still be *slow*, that raising billions of bodies is a task too large for one act. *Mā khalqukum wa-lā baʿthukum illā ka-nafsin wāḥidah* — "your creation and your raising are nothing other than a single soul." The verse places two events separated by the whole of human history and by every human life — the first creation and the resurrection — in a single clause, and says they are, from the divine side, one act no bigger than making one person. The *illā* is the negation of exception, not of existence: *nothing but*, as a man says "his answer to that was nothing but a laugh." The grammarians of the verse note the ellipsis: "your creation is like the creation of a single soul, and your resurrection like its resurrection," and that the comparison is one of *ease*, not of size — al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah both state the point in the plainest possible terms: nothing is more alike to God than what He wills, and the magnitude of the thing is not a property that burdens the will.

This is not a minor piece of theology. It is the direct antidote to the intuition that the resurrection is a *project* — a vast reassembly that is conceivable in principle but hard to believe in. The Qur'an's answer throughout is that difficulty is a property of beings with limited power who work in time, and that God's acts are not sequenced performances but a single will with its object. "*His command, when He intends a thing, is only that He say to it: Be — and it is*" (36:82; cf. 2:117, 16:40, 19:35, 40:68). "*Our command is nothing but one, like the twinkling of an eye*" (54:50). "*and the Trumpet will be blown, and behold, from the tombs to their Lord they will stream*" (36:53), "*the Day when the caller will call from a near place*" (50:41–42). The pairing of these texts with our verse is the reason the sūrah's own commentary tradition reads v. 28 as reaffirming the previous one: "*this verse reaffirms the idea that God's power would not and could not be exhausted (v. 27) by the process of creation or resurrection*."

**"A Single Soul": the Human Being as the Unit of the Universal**

Why *nafs wāḥida* rather than, say, "a single bird" or "a grain"? Two reasons, one of them a piece of the sūrah's own argument. First, because a human being is the largest thing the audience had to be shown as easy to make: the whole passage about parents, nursing, growth, the two years (vv. 14–15) has just walked through the *labor* of a single human life, the labor that belongs to mothers and fathers, not to the Maker — and v. 28 says: even that, in the making, was nothing. Second, because of what the sūrah elsewhere says about the "single soul": "*O mankind, reverence your Lord who created you from a single soul, and created from it its mate, and spread from the two many men and women*" (4:1; cf. 6:98; 7:189; 39:6). Adam is not here a myth of origin so much as a statement about the *kind* of being humanity is: one family with one beginning — and if there was one beginning, then a return is a continuation of the same single act, not a new and harder one. Our source notes the shift in context, and the shift is what gives the verse its moral force: the same *nafs wāḥida* language that grounds human unity in 4:1 grounds human accountability here.

There is a third, structural reason. In Luqmān's sūrah, the "single soul" of v. 28 sits between the *seed* of v. 16 and the *wombs* of v. 34 — the smallest unit of what grows, and the hidden place where it begins. The sūrah keeps insisting that the unit is where the miracle is. It is easier, in the sūrah's logic, to make one person than to explain one person; and it is the explaining that has never been finished by anyone, seed or no seed.

**"God Is Hearing, Seeing": Two Names That Convert Power into Accountability**

A verse about ease could have ended with "*Inna Llāha ʿalīm*" — knowing. It ends instead with "*Samīʿun Baṣīr*," and the two names of perception do the real work of the sentence. The logic is exactly this: if raising everyone is nothing to God, then nobody escapes it; and if nobody escapes it, then the question is not whether He can reassemble you but what He will find when He does — and what He finds will be what you *said* and what you *did*, because the names that close the verse are the names of the two senses in which a life is manifest. *Samīʿ*: hearing, and thus the witness of words — the promises made, the lies told, the prayer mumbled, the "O my son" of Luqmān and the "we follow what we found our fathers on" of v. 21. *Baṣīr*: seeing, and thus the witness of deeds — the gait forbidden in v. 18, the voice lowered in v. 19, the alms of v. 4 that somebody's need was watching.

The supporting verses for the pairing "Hearing, Seeing" are the ones the sūrah's commentators bring forward: "*God is Hearing, Seeing*" at 4:58, 4:134, 22:61, 22:75, and 58:1, where the last of them is the most relevant — "*God has heard the words of her who pleads with you concerning her husband and complains to God; and God hears your exchange. Indeed, God is Hearing, Seeing*" (58:1), a woman's private complaint, in a room where nobody else was listening, registered by the same two names. That is also why the sūrah's own theology of *iḥsān* (v. 22's "He sees you," in the hadith of Jibrīl) keeps recurring here: the resurrection is not a mass event for a God who has forgotten the details, but the public disclosure of what *Samīʿ Baṣīr* already privately knows.

**What the Verse Refuses, and What It Does Not Require**

Two cautions belong in the treatment, because verses like this one are over-read in opposite directions. First, the verse is not an argument that the resurrection is *probable*; it is a statement that it is *easy*, and it is addressed to people who granted the Creator's power and doubted the manner (36:77–79: "*He proposes for Us a parable and forgets his own creation; he says: who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated? Say: He who produced them the first time will give them life, and He is Knowing of every creation*"). That is the correct context: the objection is not "there may be no God" but "dust cannot come back," and the answer is that dust coming back is not a bigger act than dust being made. Second, the verse does not require a reader to picture the mechanics: the Qur'an nowhere tells us how a body is reconstituted, and the sūrah's own v. 34 will place the *where* and *when* of death outside human knowledge. The demand is not for a theory of resurrection but for a settled practical orientation to the fact of being re-accounted.

**For the Reader Who Believes the Resurrection and Still Procrastinates**

That last point is where v. 28 has its force in a life. A person's real attitude to the resurrection is revealed by how he treats *small* and *postponed* acts, and the sūrah's claim is that postponement is a category error: the delay is in him, not in the doing. Nothing is queued; nothing is queued *to* Him. And because creation and raising are a single act, each human life is already, from the divine side, complete — its beginning and end in one knowledge, which is also the meaning of the sūrah's next-but-one verse: "*and He is Aware of whatever you do*" (v. 29). A man who takes that sentence literally does not become morbid; he becomes prompt, because promptness is exactly what a person acquires when he stops assuming there is slack in the arrangement of the universe.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:29

> **Hast thou not considered that God makes the night pass into the day and makes the day pass into the night, and that He made the sun and the moon subservient, each running for a term appointed, and that God is Aware of whatsoever you do?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Yūlijī": the Verb for Something Being Let In**

*Alam tara anna Llāha yūlijī al-layla fī al-nahāri wa-yūlijī al-nahāra fī al-layl.* The verb is *walaja*, "to enter," in the causative: God *causes* the night to enter the day, and the day to enter the night. The image is not of two things alternately appearing but of one thing being *inserted into* the other, and the commentators take it in exactly that sense: the night's increase is the day's decrease, and vice versa; what one loses the other gains, and the loss and the gain are the same act. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss, transmitted by every later commentary, is the plain one: "He lengthens this by shortening that, and shortens this by lengthening that." Ibn Kathīr's note is the same and adds the practical observation that the phenomenon is the one every traveler and every fasting person measures by: the shadow, the call, the length of a Ramadan day in winter and in summer.

Two further applications are recorded in the tafsīr and both are affirmed by our source: the *daily* exchange, at each sunrise and sunset, and the *seasonal* one, the year's slow tide of long days and short. al-Zamakhsharī and the later commentators allow the verse to carry both, and the double reference is what makes the argument so economical: the same God regulates the clock and the calendar, the small turn and the great one, and a person who has noticed either has been shown the same thing.

The nearest cousin of our verse is the Madinan one on *yukawwir*, in Sūrat al-Zumar: "*He wraps (yukawwiru) the night over the day and wraps the day over the night*" (39:5) — where the verb is the one used for winding a turban or rolling a cloak, and a minority of modern readers have seen in it a suggestion of a rounded surface. The classical exegetes did not build cosmology on the word; al-Rāghib glosses *takwīr* simply as the wrapping of one thing over another, and the responsible commentary treats 39:5 as an image of *continuous overlap* — night and day not swapping places but winding over one another, which is exactly what happens at a terminator on a sphere. The linguistic observation is sound; the verse's purpose is unchanged in either reading, and it should be said plainly that sign-verses of this kind are not a physics textbook and do not need to be one in order to do their work.

**Subservient, Not Divine: the Polemic Inside the Cosmology**

*Wa-sakhkhara al-shamsa wa-al-qamarā.* The verb is the sūrah's own from v. 20 — *taskhīr*, subjection — and it is doing more than describing usefulness. In a world where the sun and the moon were worshipped, feared, read as omens, and addressed as rulers of fate, the sūrah's claim is that they are *made tractable*: they serve; they are not served. The polemic is explicit elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the same two bodies appear in a verse about prostration: "*Do not prostrate to the sun or to the moon; prostrate to God, who created them, if it is He you worship*" (41:37) — and in the sūrah of Luqmān's own audience: "*and among His signs are the night and the day, and the sun and the moon*" (41:37's preface, 41:36–37), and "*He has subjected the sun and the moon to you*" (14:33), "*they are subservient by His command*" (16:12). The Qur'an's cosmology is an anti-astrology from start to finish, and this is why the phrase *kullun yarī ilā ajalin musammā* — "each runs to a term appointed" — is the decisive half of the sentence. A being whose motion has an appointed end is not a god; it is a clock with a date.

The hadith literature turned the same claim against a specific superstition of the Prophet's own day, when a solar eclipse coincided with the death of his infant son:

> *"The sun and the moon are two of God's signs; He strikes awe into His servants by them. They do not eclipse for the death of anyone or his life. If you see it, then pray."* (al-Bukhārī 1044; Muslim 901)

The verse in front of us is the theoretical ground of that sentence: bodies that run to a term are signs, not agents, and the correct response to their disturbance is not divination but prayer — that is, turning from the sign to its Sender, exactly as Luqmān's cosmological passages (vv. 10, 20, 29, 31) keep doing. And the "term appointed" has an eschatological sense the commentators note: in one report the phrase refers to the day when they are folded up and stopped (cf. 75:8–9, "*when the light is dimmed and the sun and moon are joined*" — *wa-jumiʿa al-shamsu wa-al-qamar*, a verse that speaks of them being *brought together* as an end); and the tradition of the Hour includes the sun rising from the west as the sign after which belief no longer profits a soul (Muslim 157). The same two bodies that prove God's orderliness in verse 29 are the ones whose arrest will announce that the terms of everything have run out.

**"A Term Appointed": the Universe With a Deadline**

The expression *ajal musammā* — a named, fixed, specified term — appears throughout the Qur'an for the lifespan of individuals, of nations, and of the world (7:34: "*and when their term comes, they will not remain behind an hour, nor will they precede it*"; and cf. 39:5, already quoted). Applied to the sun and moon, it makes a metaphysical claim the audience could check against nothing: these things will *stop*, and not because they ran out of fuel, but because they were given a term. This is the sūrah's own answer to the most natural form of idolatry, which is reverence for what seems permanent. The Arabs stood in front of the sun and felt small; the verse tells them that the sun is small, in the only way that counts — it has a deadline, and so does everything that does not die. Al-Rāzī draws the general rule from our verse: whatever has a term is preceded by non-existence at its end, and whatever can be described as "running to a limit" is, in the strongest sense, a *thing* rather than a *lord*.

There is a psychological benefit in the same phrase for the man being taught wisdom, and it is why this clause sits in a chapter about a father's counsel: everything you will ever worry about has a term. The deadline that makes the sun unworthy of worship is the same property that makes an oppression, an empire, an illness, or a humiliating week in the office *finite*. Luqmān's son, who has been told to be patient (v. 17) and not to grieve, is handed a cosmology in which nothing hostile is eternal and nothing friendly should be trusted absolutely.

**"And God Is Aware of Whatever You Do": the Odd Ending**

Readers regularly miss the abruptness of the final clause. The verse has been describing an astronomical arrangement, and it ends with surveillance of *your* deeds. But the abruptness is the point of the sūrah's method, and the precedent is Qur'anic and repeated: the subjection of what is in the heavens and the earth in 45:13 is followed by "*therein are signs for a people who reflect*"; the stars, the night, and the day in 6:97–98 end with "*thus do We detail the signs for a people who know*"; and the sun and the moon of this sūrah's own v. 16 were closed by "*Truly God is Subtle, Aware.*" Three things are accomplished by the turn.

First, it prevents the verse from becoming mere astronomy: the signs are not a museum; they are an argument with a conclusion, and the conclusion is that a moral order is being administered by the same hand that keeps the day and night in their appointments. Second, it supplies the sūrah's *reason for the precision*: the sun never says "today I will be late"; the reason a universe can run on exact terms is that the One who set them is *Aware* of every particular, including the one you thought nobody noticed. Third, the verse thereby connects to v. 16's mustard seed and v. 34's "God is Knowing, Aware": the *Laṭīf Khabīr* who fetches a seed from inside a rock is the *Khabīr* who watches the deeds the sun watches over.

That is also, in the sūrah's psychology, the connection between time and responsibility that most readers feel the most. The alternation of night and day is the mechanism by which human life is measured, and it is the thing that makes "yet" and "already" meaningful. A person who has considered that the day is *inserted* into the night by a command has been given, in the same clause, the ground for the chapter's whole ethic: your days are not yours to spend however you like, because they are being counted by an Awareness that does not lose the thread.

**The Verse as Devotional Practice**

Finally, this verse is recited at the hours of its own content. The five daily prayers are assigned by the sun's position — its decline, the lengthening shadow, the sunset's red, the night's darkening, and the dawn's true light (17:78: "*establish prayer at the decline of the sun until the darkness of the night, and the Qur'an of the dawn*"; 11:114; 24:58; 30:17–18, in the sūrah just before this one: "*glory to God when you reach the evening and when you enter the morning, and His is the praise in the heavens and the earth, and in the afternoon and when you enter noon*"). Luqmān's audience and every congregation after them are being taught the verse by their bodies: the sun's *taskhīr* sets their hours, and their hours are their accountability. The verse's abstraction is therefore the most practical kind: whoever prays on time has believed 31:29 whether or not he has ever analyzed it.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:30

> **That is because God, He is the Truth, and whatsoever they call upon other than Him is false, and God is the Exalted, the Great.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"That Is Because": the Clause That Ties the Cosmos to a Conclusion**

*Dhālika bi-anna Llāha huwa al-ḥaqq.* The demonstrative *dhālika* — "that" — has been running through the whole second half of the sūrah as a connector, and here it does its largest work. Our source records both readings and their sense: it refers back to everything just mentioned (the night and day, the sun and moon, the running to a term — the position of al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī and the majority), *or* it opens a fresh asseveration, "know for certain that God is the Truth." In the second reading, the phrase is an elliptical command — as in "*dhālika* by God, who has no god but He" — and the verse becomes an imperative of knowledge rather than a sentence of inference. But either reading makes the same claim, and both are needed: the universe's orderly running *is* the evidence, and *that* is what must be known.

The force of the *bi-* of cause is worth spelling out, because it is the sūrah's answer to a question that a reader of vv. 20–29 may have begun to form: why should all this prove anything? Because the order of the world is not an additional fact beside God; it is *the way the Truth appears*. The night entering the day does not point to a designer by analogy alone; it is the visible shape of a statement: God is *al-Ḥaqq*. That is a much stronger claim than the design argument usually makes, and it is why Luqmān's cosmology ends, twice, not in an inference but in an identity.

**"God Is the Truth": the Name and Its Three Senses**

In ordinary Arabic *ḥaqq* means "true" as opposed to "false," a property of statements. Applied to God it acquires, in the sūrah's own commentary tradition, three senses at once, and the tradition's treatment of them is a compact piece of philosophical theology.

It means first that *His speech is true* — no lie, no error, no broken promise (v. 9 already: "*God's promise in truth*"). It means second that *He is real in the fullest sense*: the *ḥaqq* is what is *thābit*, established, self-subsisting, as against *bāṭil*, what is vain, sinking, and hollow — the same opposition the Qur'an makes at 13:17, "*so as for the foam, it passes away as scum, and that which benefits people remains on the earth; thus does God set forth parables*," and at 17:81, "*truth has come and falsehood has vanished; indeed, falsehood is ever bound to vanish.*" And it means third, and most precisely, that He is *the Only Real*: in the vocabulary of the philosophers, *wājib al-wujūd*, the Necessary, whose non-existence is not even conceivable, against all else that is *mumkin*, contingent, existing only because it is being held in existence. Our source puts this last sense in the commentary on the verse directly: God is "the Ultimate Reality beyond and behind all of creation," and "all of the things to which anyone ascribes ultimate power or upon which anyone calls for intercession are entirely dependent upon God, possessing no reality in and of themselves."

That third sense does the polemical work of the second half of the verse, and it is what makes *bāṭil* here a terrifying word rather than a polite one: "*that which they call upon besides Him is bāṭil.*" The idols are not false gods who are in fact powerless; they are *unreal*. The grammar of the sūrah's argument, from vv. 10–11 through v. 30, is therefore a graduated exposure of the same thing: the idol was first shown to have no *share in creation* (v. 11), then no *ownership of anything* (v. 26), and now no *being* (v. 30). To pray to it is not an error of priority but a transaction with a nonentity.

**"The Exalted, the Great": Two Names Against Two Illusions**

*Wa-anna Llāha huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr.* The pair is chosen for the enemies it answers. *ʿAliyy*, from *ʿ-l-w*, is high — in rank, in place, in condition — and the Qur'an's usage includes "the Most High" in the sense of above any share in what He created: "*He is the Most High (al-Aʿlā) over His servants*" (6:18, 6:61), "*glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High*" (87:1). What it denies is *proximity of degree*: the idols are not merely lower gods, they are of a different order, and no ladder of being connects them to Him. *Kabīr*, from *k-b-r*, is the root of *kibr*, which the sūrah has already legislated against in vv. 18–19 — and in the sūrah of al-Ḥajj the two names appear together in an address to the pilgrims: "*so that they may witness benefits for themselves and mention the name of God over what He has provided them of livestock; ... your God is one God; then whoever hopes to meet his Lord, let him do a righteous deed*" (22:37, "*their flesh and their blood reach God not, but your God-consciousness reaches Him*"), where *al-Kabīr* is the name that makes the whole cult of sacrifice meaningful — and where "God is Great" is the pilgrim's *takbīr*. The *Allāhu akbar* is the name in use. The sūrah's claim is that God's greatness is the one thing in the universe that is not comparative: nothing is "bigger than" Him in any respect, and so every claim of the great ones of Makkah — their lineage, their sanctuary, their wealth, their gods — is a *kibr* that has no object.

Ibn Kathīr's gloss on the pair is the devotional one: He is *ʿAliyy* in His essence above His creation, and *Kabīr* in that nothing is greater than Him; and al-Qurṭubī adds that the two names are here a rebuttal of the *ʿuluww* of the idols' claim, since whatever is called upon "from below Him" cannot be exalted at all.

**The Truth That Has an Object**

There is a subtlety in the verse that the classical rhetoricians noticed: the sūrah does not say "God is true" (*ṣādiq*), but "God *is* the Truth" (*huwa al-ḥaqq*) — with the definite article and the pronoun of separation, in a structure that makes *al-ḥaqq* not a quality but the whole predicate, and even, in the reading of some, the exclusive: He and nothing else is the Real. This is why the same formula recurs at 22:62 ("*that is because God is the Truth, and that which they call upon besides Him is falsehood, and God is the Exalted, the Great*" — our verse's near-twin), at 24:25, and at 31:30, and why the sūrah of al-Ḥajj, with which our sūrah shares both the *mujādala* language (22:3–4 / 31:20–21) and this verse, places the formula in a context of *pilgrimage*: the ritual of the ḥajj is the enactment of the claim that only the Exalted-Great is called upon, and the sacrifice's meat never reaches Him — only *taqwā* does (22:37). The cosmological statement of Luqmān and the liturgical one of al-Ḥajj are, in other words, the same sentence addressed to the sky and to the altar.

**The Verse and the Reader's Actual Worship**

Strip the argument and ask what v. 30 asks. It asks what a person *calls upon* when the practical pressure arrives — the thing he addresses in hope, the thing whose approval he cannot bear to lose, the thing he trusts to provide. The sūrah has already defined the idols of its own audience as *mā yadʿūna min dūnihī*, "what they *call upon* besides Him," and the verb of calling is the operative one, since in Qur'anic usage *duʿāʾ* is worship: "*your Lord said: call upon Me and I will respond to you; indeed, those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell humbled*" (40:60), and "*do not call upon, besides God, that which neither benefits you nor harms you, for if you do, then you are of the wrongdoers*" (10:106). To call upon money, upon a person's opinion, upon an institution, upon a substance, upon a lucky arrangement of stars, is to have already decided, in practice, that they possess *ḥaqq* — reality with leverage. Verse 30 says they do not; and says that in the same sentence in which the sun and moon, the most impressive "real" things the audience could see, are named as running to a term they did not choose (v. 29). If those are not gods, nothing else is.

The practical residue of the verse is not a metaphysical puzzle but a freedom. A person who has believed that God alone is the Real stops being frightened by what is not, and stops needing what is not — and this is exactly the temperament the sūrah has been building from v. 13 onward: the monotheist who can bear to be laughed at (v. 7), refuse a parent (v. 15), lower his voice (v. 19), and lose the argument (v. 23), because the only verdict that exists is already settled in a being who is not in competition with anything.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:31

> **Hast thou not considered that the ships sail upon the sea by God's Blessing, that He may show you His signs? Truly in that are signs for all who are patient, thankful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sea as the Sūrah's Last Classroom**

The fourth and final "*alam tara*" of the chapter (vv. 10, 20, 29, 31) takes the reader off the land. *Alam tara anna al-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-niʿmati Llāh* — "do you not see that the ships run on the sea by God's blessing?" The sea is, in the Qur'an's imagination, the one place where a human being is most visibly *not* in charge: nothing to hold, nothing to build on, and every hour owed to conditions a person cannot produce or predict. Luqmān's argument has been ascending through the ordinary — sky, mountains, animals, plants, rain, sun, moon — and it ends on the ship because a ship is the summit of *human* making: it is timber, rope, sail, and skill, and it is still, in the sūrah's one phrase, "*bi-niʿmatihi*," by His blessing.

The noun *fulk* is one of the Qur'an's most remarkable words because it is a *collective singular* — a ship, or a fleet, the same form used of Noah's vessel and of the ships the Quraysh saw putting in at Shuʿayba — and its root *f-l-k* means to split, to cleave: a ship is the thing that *splits the water*. The image the sūrah has chosen is thus already a miracle of engineering described in the vocabulary of cutting: the sea is a solid to everything else, and a cleft has been made in it for you. Compare the sūrah's own cousin verse, in al-Shūrā: "*the ships running in it like mountains*" (42:32) — where the comparison is to *aʿlām*, the peaks and landmarks of the earth — and al-Raḥmān's "*the large-flowing (al-jāriyāt) in the sea like a flag*" (55:19–24). In every case the awe is the point, and in this sūrah the awe is expressly *for the same reason* as the sky's: "*li-yuriyakum min āyātihī*" — so that He may show you some of His signs.

**"By God's Blessing": the Cause, and the Trust**

The prepositional phrase does real theological work. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both read *bi-niʿmati Llāh* as the answer to a specific error — the sailor's (or the engineer's, or the pilot's) sense that the ship moves "by the wind, by the hull, by my handling." The verse does not deny the chain of causes; the sūrah elsewhere praises *asbāb*: "*and prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war*" (8:60), and Noah is told to *build*: "*and construct the ship under Our eyes and by Our inspiration*" (11:37). What the phrase denies is that the chain is self-sufficient — hence the Qur'an's own formulation of the arrangement in the duʿāʾ of the ark, which the Muslims have recited as a boarding prayer ever since: "*Board it; in the name of God is its running (majrāhā) and its anchorage (mursāhā); indeed my Lord is Forgiving, Merciful*" (11:41). Al-Ṭabarī transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that the words "*bismillāhi majrāhā wa-mursāhā*" are what Noah said, and the Prophet ﷺ taught the community that whoever says them on boarding is saved (in the transmissions collected in the *ʿamal al-yawm wa-l-laylah* literature).

This is exactly the doctrine of causes that the later tradition summarized in a proverbial hadith, when a man asked whether he should tie his camel:

> *"Tie her and trust."* (al-Tirmidhī 2517, from Anas; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Al-Ghazālī's treatment in the *Iḥyāʾ* (the Book of Tawḥīd and Reliance) presses the same point against the two one-sided people: the man who refuses causes, trusting in God while leaving the sail furled, and the man who trusts in causes and forgets the Sender of the wind. God "has made everything with its cause, and the causes are not what bring the effects about but what God has decreed them in," he argues, and the practical consequence is a peculiar mental state he calls *sukūn* at the moment of danger: the believer uses every means with total diligence and places no weight on them at all. The ship in v. 31 is the classical illustration, because the sea is where the arrangement is most visible: competence is necessary, and never sufficient.

**"So That He May Show You His Signs": the Purpose of the Physical World**

*Li-yuriyakum min āyātih.* The purpose clause is the sūrah's own claim about nature's function: the world shows. Note the *min*, "some of": an ordinary partitive, so that the sentence is careful — He shows you *some* of His signs here. The sea's signs are not the whole curriculum, and the reader is meant to go on reading the other text, the Book of v. 2, with the same eye. The classical ethic of nature follows from this: nothing in the created order is *only* itself; a ship is a ship and, if you are looking properly, an *āyah*. That is why the same word has run from v. 2 (the verses of the Book) to vv. 10, 20, 29, and 31 (the signs in the world): Luqmān's sūrah has one continuous doctrine of legibility, and its readers are being trained to read both texts with one faculty.

**"Signs for Every Patient, Thankful One"**

*Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr.* The two words are intensive patterns — *ṣabbār*, extremely patient; *shakūr*, extremely thankful — and the sūrah's commentators note that the pairing names the two postures a human being needs for the two conditions he will actually meet. Ibn Kathīr, quoted by our source, glosses: "steadfast in affliction, thankful in ease," and the sūrah of Abraham states the same two-part definition of the person the signs are legible to: and al-Shūrā uses the same formula about the heavens, the creatures that He scattered through them, and the ships running by His command: "*indeed in that are signs for every steadfast, thankful one*" (42:29–33). Al-Rūm, the sūrah immediately preceding Luqmān, used the identical phrase about the ships and the storm — "*the ships running in the sea like mountains; if He wills, He stills the wind so they become motionless on its back. Indeed in that are signs for every steadfast, thankful one*" (30:46–47) — and Luqmān's v. 31 is that passage's twin. The continuity between the two sūrahs is thus not just thematic but verbal, and it tells us the *quality of reader* the sūrah is addressing: not the merely intelligent. The sea will disclose its signs only to a person with two dispositions, and one of them is the capacity to stay composed when it is dangerous, the other the capacity to remember who to thank when it is safe.

That is the sūrah's whole theory of evidence in one line. The universe is not hidden from human beings; it is hidden from *impatient and ungrateful* ones, because the sign requires a patience that will not skip to conclusions and a gratitude that will not stop at the mechanism. A sailor who only ever notices the waves will not learn anything; one who only ever notices that he arrived will not learn either.

**The Traveler's Duʿāʾ, and the Modern Ship**

The practice the verse generated is instructive, and the Prophet ﷺ supplied its words. When he set out on a journey he said:

> *"O God, We ask You, in this journey of ours, for righteousness and God-consciousness, and for a deed that pleases You; and We ask You to make it easy for us and to shorten its distance for us. O God, You are the Companion on the journey and the Successor over the family. O God, I take refuge in You from the hardship of the journey, from the sorrowful sight, and from an evil turn in wealth and family."* (Muslim 1342, from Ibn ʿUmar; and he ﷺ, on returning, added "*āyibūna, tāʾibūna, ʿābidūna, li-rabbinā ḥāmidūn*" — "we return, repenting, worshipping, and praising our Lord.")

The wording is a commentary on v. 31 by the Prophet himself: the traveler asks for *birr and taqwā* rather than for speed, for ease rather than for certainty, and he names what he fears as the *change* — in family, in health, in what he owns. There is also the hadith that the journey is "*qaṭʿatun min al-ʿadhāb*" — "a cutting-off, a fragment of torment" (al-Bukhārī 1804; Muslim 1818) — the traveler is deprived of his bed, his food, his routine, and his defenses, and the sūrah's point is that this deprivation is the *sign*: it is the ship that reminds a human being what the whole of life looks like from outside, and this is why it sits at the end of the sūrah's cosmology. A reader who has never seen a ship in a storm has nonetheless been in one — of illness, of a market, of a family — and the two names *ṣabbār shakūr* are the two things that carry a person through, with the sea as their classroom.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:32

> **And when waves enshroud them like awnings, they call upon God, devoting religion entirely to Him. Then when He has delivered them safely to land, some of them take a middling course. And none reject Our signs, save all who are perfidious, ungrateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Experiment the Sūrah Runs on the Human Heart**

Verse 31 showed the ship running by God's blessing; verse 32 shows it in danger, and the sequence is an experiment with a prediction. *Wa-idhā ghashiyahumu al-mawju ka-l-ẓulali daʿawū Llāha mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — "and when the wave covers them like awnings, they call upon God, sincere to Him in religion." The sūrah is not saying "they pray in a storm," a piece of obviousness. It is making a claim about the structure of the self: that under the condition where every created help has been removed, the human being's religion becomes *pure* — and that the purity does not survive the rescue. This is one of the Qur'an's most repeated passages for that reason, and its fullest statement is in Yūnus:

> *"It is God who enables you to travel over the land and the sea, until, when you are in the ships and they sail with them by a fair wind, and they rejoice in it, a storm-wind comes upon them and the waves come at them from every side, and they suppose that they are encompassed — they call upon God, sincere to Him in religion: 'If You save us from this, we shall surely be of the grateful.' But when He had saved them to the shore, if they go astray — only the one who wrongs himself goes astray."* (Qur'an 10:22–23; cf. 6:63–64, 17:67, 29:65–66, 30:33)

**"Like Awnings": the Image of the Enclosed**

*Al-ẓulal* is the plural of *ullah*, a shade, an awning, a canopy held over a head — the word used in the Qur'an of the cloud-shade of the travelers in 2:57, of the shade of Paradise (4:57; 7:41), and of the heat of the Fire, "*cut off for them are garments of fire, and boiling water will be poured over their heads*" (22:19) — a canopy of the same shape, descending. Applied to waves, the image is not of water *above* a ship only, but of a ceiling closing over it: the sea rising to form a roof, so that what surrounds them is not merely dangerous but *sealed*. The same sense appears in 29:65 with the verb *ghāshiyahum*: "the waves have covered them." The point is the removal of alternatives: no land visible, no help reachable, no argument left. Al-Rāzī notes that the sūrah has chosen the precise condition in which the heart is stripped of its partners — not because the danger makes men logical, but because it makes them honest, and the honesty is involuntary.

This is why the verse's middle claim — *mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — is used in the kalām literature as evidence for the doctrine of the *fiṭrah*: that the acknowledgment of one ultimate rescue is original, and polytheism is an acquired overlay. The supporting text is the Prophet's ﷺ statement about children and their parents:

> *"Every child is born on the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658)

The storm, on this reading, does not teach a new idea; it removes the acquired one. Even the Makkkan pilgrim, who had carved a hundred names into his stone, found only one to say when the water was over the gunwale.

**"Some of Them Take a Middling Course": the Sūrah's Diagnosis of the Average**

*Thumma idhā anjāhum ilā al-barri fa-minhum muqtaṣid.* The word for the post-rescue state is *muqtaṣid*, from *q-ṣ-d*: one who takes the middle, who moderates, who comes to a balance. It is a term with an honorable history in the Qur'an — in 35:32 it names the middle class of the community that inherits the Book, between the self-wronging and the foremost in good deeds — and here it names the same *position* but with a different content: not the moderate believer, but the man who has *partially* kept his resolve. The classical glosses preserved in our source (al-Zamakhsharī, and the reading in 17:67's "*most of them are ungrateful*") make the sense explicit: the vow made in the waves is not repudiated; it is *thinned*, and this is the sūrah's most unsparing and most useful line, because it describes not apostasy but the ordinary drift of a sincere intention into a compromise.

The Qur'an's own name for this condition appears in al-Ḥajj: "*and among the people is he who worships God on one side (ʿalā ḥarf); if good befalls him, he is content with it, and if a trial befalls him, he turns on his face*" (22:11) — worship held at one edge, ready to fall off. And the man of 22:11 is the same man as Luqmān's *muqtaṣid*: the person whose God-consciousness has no ballast of its own and depends on whether the weather cooperates. Verse 4 of this very sūrah has already described its opposite: "*those who are certain (yūqinūn) of the Hereafter*" — and the difference between the certainty of a believer and the certainty of a drowning person is that one of them persists after the water is gone.

The application is not far to seek. The resolve made at the bedside of the dying, in the plane's turbulence, in the month of Ramaḍān, in the week after a loss — the whole of Muslim pastoral practice exists to answer one question: what will you do when the wave has passed? The Prophet ﷺ answered it with a rule about the middling man's tendency to drift, in a hadith that is itself an instruction to be *muqtaṣid* in the good sense:

> *"Take the right course and come close, and know that no one will be admitted to Paradise by his deeds."* They said: "Not even you, Messenger of God?" He said: *"Not even I, unless God envelops me with His mercy; and speak to people with gentleness (wa-lin).*" (al-Bukhārī; and the parallel, "religion is easy, and none makes it hard but it overcomes him — so take the right course and come close (saddidū wa-qāribū)," in al-Bukhārī 39, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr)

The hadith's force in our verse's light: the *middle* is not automatically the fault — the fault is a middle that was set during panic and quietly renegotiated in safety.

**"No One Rejects Our Signs but Every Perfidious, Ungrateful One"**

*Wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā kullu khattārin kafūr.* The verb is *juḥūd* — denial *with recognition*, the refusal to acknowledge what one has seen, and it is the sūrah's own diagnosis of the hearer of vv. 6–7, who "turns away as if he had not heard." The two nouns that follow are the sūrah's characterological verdict, and the pair is worth pausing on. *Khaṭṭār* is from *khiṭāra/khaṭr*, and in the intensive form it denotes, as our source records, "one who betrays, stabs in the back, and breaks every promise made" (Ibn Kathīr's gloss) — a person whose covenant-keeping is habitually *toward himself*: he re-selects his obligations as it suits him (al-Rāghib connects *khiṭār* to *khayr*: choosing, in the sense of taking the best for oneself). *Kafūr* is the ingrate, from the root of covering, established at vv. 12 and 14: he *covers over* the favor he has just received, converting a rescue into an incident, and a gift into an entitlement.

The claim is therefore not that the man who fails to keep his vow lacks evidence — he has just *been* the evidence; he has stood in the place where the signs are undeniable. It is that he has a *character* problem: perfidy and ingratitude. This is one of the most honest moves in the sūrah's apologetics, and the commentators read it exactly this way (al-Ṭabarī: the *kufr* here is "ingratitude for the blessing," not denial of the Creator), because it refuses to convert the history of belief into a contest of arguments only. A man may see, and then *not wish* what he saw to be binding, and then arrange a life that does not require him to remember — and the sūrah calls that what it is: ungratefulness, and the breaking of a promise made at the moment when it was easiest to make one and hardest to keep.

The pairing also closes the sūrah's argument from gratitude. The Book was "guidance and mercy for the virtuous" (v. 3); the universe was "blessings outward and inward" (v. 20); the ships sail "by God's blessing" (v. 31); and a man who has been drowned, saved, and then shrugs has, in the sūrah's account, rejected not a proposition but a *kindness* — and that is a fault of the will. It is why, in this chapter, unbelief is described mostly by verbs of aversion and purchase (vv. 6–7) rather than by verbs of error.

**The Verse as a Mirror**

The last thing to say about v. 32 is that the sūrah gives its readers no comfortable place to stand. The man in the waves is *everybody* — even the idolaters of Makkah were monotheists for an hour; and the man who steps onto land and thins his vow is, by the sūrah's own *fa-minhum* ("*some* of them"), not all of them, so that the chapter's verdict on the middle is balanced by the possibility of the other middle, the *muqtaṣid* of 35:32 who keeps going. The verse asks one question and it is addressed to the reader rather than to the historical Makkans: after the wave, what did you promise, and what is left of it now?

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:33

> **O mankind! Reverence your Lord and fear a day on which no parent will avail his child aught and no child will avail his parent aught. Surely God's Promise is true. So let not the life of this world delude you, nor let the Deluder delude you concerning God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address That Widens**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nās.* For the third time in this sūrah the address broadens to all of humanity (vv. 20 and 33; cf. also the general "*yā ayyuhā al-nās*" passages that dominate Makkan sūrahs), and here the widening is the point. The chapter has travelled from the Wise Book and its three marks (vv. 2–5), through one father's speech to his son (vv. 13–19), through the cosmos addressed to a particular people in a particular dispute (vv. 20–31), and now the address opens onto the entire species, because what follows is a sentence that no family, no culture, and no century can be exempted from. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the rhetorical *taʿjīb* — the astonishing force — in the choice of "*fear a day*," and the later commentators observe the idiom: a *day* cannot be feared in itself; the day is feared because of what is *in* it, and the ellipsis makes the hearer's imagination do the rest.

Two commands stand at the head, and the pairing is the sūrah's own summary of the religious life: *ittaqū rabbakum wa-ḥāfū yawman* — "revere your Lord, and fear a day." *Taqwā* is the continuous guarding, described already at 2:2 and 31:3 as walking as though the path were dangerous; *khawf* is the specific recoil from the awful thing. One is the steady state, the other the moment of clarity, and the sūrah requires both — which is why the "Deluder" at the end of the verse is described in the act of attacking not God's existence but *the connection*: "deluding you *concerning God*."

**The Day Where the Family Ends as a Unit**

The clause that gives the verse its force is the one about parents and children: *lā tajzī wālidun ʿan waladihi wa-lā mawlūdun huwa ʿānīhū shayʾan* — no parent answers for his child, no child for his parent, in anything. The verb *ajzā*, with *ʿan*, is the legal term for *satisfying a claim in place of another*, for standing in somebody's stead and discharging their debt. The sūrah is thus not describing emotional distraction, though that too (80:34–37: "*the Day a man flees from his brother, his mother and father, his consort and his children — for every one of them that Day will have business enough*"), but the collapse of *vicarious satisfaction*: no substitution, no proxy, no family credit, no inherited salvation.

The echo into the sūrah's own family-material is a deliberate ring. Verse 14 established the claim of the parents: the mother carried, nursed, and paid two years of her life, and the son was commanded to gratitude and *iḥsān*; verse 15 established the limits of that claim. Now v. 33 says that the claim does not extend into the next world as a *power*: the parent who sacrificed for the child cannot use the sacrifice as leverage, and the child who honored the parent cannot use the honor as a transferable credit. Al-Qurṭubī draws the practical conclusion for both parties, and it is a hard, clean piece of pastoral reasoning: *birr al-wālidayn* is owed and rewarded in this world and in the next on the parent's own account, and a parent's faith is not a child's faith. The family is the school of love and duty; it is not the mechanism of salvation.

The verse belongs to a family of near-identical texts, each of which specifies a different excluded resource: 2:48 and 2:123 ("*guard against a day when no soul will answer for another, no parent will be answered for on behalf of his child, nor a child on behalf of its parent in anything*"), 2:254 ("*before there comes a Day in which there is no buying, no friendship, and no intercession*"), 6:70, 39:7, 44:41 ("*no one will answer for anyone in anything*"), 53:38 ("*that no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another*"), and 82:19 ("*and what will convey to you what the Day of Sorting is? The Day when no soul shall have power for a soul in anything; and the command, that Day, is all of God's*"). What all of them share is the *individualization* of the person, which is one of the Qur'an's deepest anthropological assertions: "*And you have come to Us individually as We created you the first time*" (18:48; cf. 6:94, 19:80, 34:46). The sūrah has said, in v. 16, that the mustard seed of *one* soul comes forth; here it says that the family, which was the sūrah's central social unit, will not come between the soul and its reckoning.

**"God's Promise Is True": the Guarantee Restated**

*Inna waʿda Llāhi ḥaqq.* The clause is a quotation of v. 9, where the promise of the "Gardens of bliss" was guaranteed, and its reappearance here is not repetition but re-application: there it supported a promise of mercy, here it supports a warning, and the same *ḥaqq* covers both. This is the sūrah's own answer to the question of whether God's words have one standard for reward and another for threat. Verse 9's pairing of *ʿAzīz* and *Ḥakīm* explains the arrangement: the promise is true because it is not negotiable and because it is not arbitrary. Al-Rāzī's note on such verses — that the *waʿd* of reward is *ḥaqq* in the sense of obligatory-by-mercy while the *waʿīd* of threat remains under the free will of the Forgiving — is a matter on which the schools differ, and it should be stated here as one of the positions rather than as the verse's plain sense: the Muʿtazila held that the threat must be executed (to fail would be *khalaf*, a defect in speech), the majority of Sunnī theologians held that God may forgive whom He wills without breaking His word, since His word about the wrongdoer is conditional on their persistence (4:48: "*God does not forgive that partners be ascribed to Him, and He forgives what is besides that for whom He wills*").

The same verse, read against the background of intercession, requires the balancing note that the sūrah's own tradition supplies. The Qur'an does not deny intercession; it denies *independent* intercession: "*who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?*" (2:255), and "*there are many angels in the heavens whose intercession avails nothing at all, until God permits it to whomever He wills and is pleased with*" (53:26). And the Prophet ﷺ's own statement in the authentic traditions attaches a positive intercession to the one who is being warned here:

> *"My intercession is for the people of the major sins among my community."* (al-Tirmidhī 2433; Abū Dāwūd 4739, from Anas ibn Mālik; graded ḥasan by some of the hadith masters)

Verse 33 therefore does not abolish hope by isolating the individual; it abolishes the *network* as a substitute for the self. The person who will intercede is not a father or a tribe but the One who gives permission to whom He wills — and the verse's real target, as its own context makes plain (vv. 20–21, 25), is the assumption that a connection, a lineage, a sanctuary, or a saint is a protection against consequence.

**"Let Not the Life of This World Delude You, nor the Deluder Concerning God"**

*Fa-lā taghurrannakumu al-ḥayātu al-dunyā wa-lā yaghurrannakum bi-Allāhi al-gharūr.* The root *gh-r-r* means to veil, and by extension to deceive by hiding the consequence; the sūrah has used its noun already in the phrase "the enjoyment of delusion" (*matāʿ al-ghurūr*, 3:185; 4:77), and here it gives the world itself and a named agent, *al-gharūr* — the Deceiver — as the two sources of the same delusion, the second working through the first. Our source preserves the sense of the second phrase exactly: the Deceiver is Satan, whose method is described in 4:120 — "*he makes promises to them and stirs up desires in them, and Satan only promises them delusion*" — and in 14:22, where at the end he says to the people of the Fire: "*God promised you a promise of truth, and I promised you and I failed you; and I had no authority over you except that I called you and you responded to me; so do not blame me but blame yourselves.*"

The most valuable single word in the verse is the preposition: *bi-Allāh* — "concerning God," "about God," "with respect to God." The commentators of this verse, including the Sufi exegetes, draw out the nuance: Satan does not, at this stage, deceive a person about whether God exists; he deceives him *about who God is*. He makes forbearance look like approval ("God did not punish me, therefore He is pleased"), and mercy look like permission ("He is Forgiving, so it does not matter"), and the delay of judgment look like absence of judgment. Al-Ṭabarī records the verse's plain sense in the transmitted glosses: *al-gharūr* is Satan, and the delusion "*about God*" is the presumption that His forbearance is approval. The tradition's moralists made the same point in the aphorism long current in the ascetical literature — "*do not look at the smallness of the sin, but at the greatness of the One you have disobeyed*" — and the Qur'an itself supplies the diagnosis of the opposite disease in 39:53, where the command to the despairing and the presumptuous alike is the same: "*Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; indeed God forgives sins all together. Indeed, He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.*"

The two statements have to be held together, and the reason is that *ghurūr* works in both directions at once. Hope is not delusion; the hadith qudsī is explicit against the man who thinks his sin is bigger than God's mercy: "*Son of Adam, as long as you call upon Me and hope in Me, I forgive you for what is in you and I do not care; and if your sins were to reach the clouds of the sky and you asked My forgiveness, I would forgive you and not care.*" (al-Tirmidhī 3540, from Anas ibn Mālik; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). What the sūrah forbids is not hope but *the substitution of hope for conduct* — the use of "He is Merciful" as a permission slip, which is precisely what the verse's own context, the mockery and the purchased distraction of vv. 6–7, looks like in practice.

Against this the sūrah places its third clause, "*God's promise is true*": the delusion about God is defeated not by fear alone but by a correct description. He is forgiving — and He is the One whose promise about consequence is *ḥaqq*; both statements are equally His nature, and the Deluder's method, in this verse, is to hold one of them in front of the other.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Summons**

Read in the sūrah's structure, v. 33 is the chapter's final public argument, and it gathers everything it has taught. *Revere your Lord*: the *taqwā* of vv. 3–5. *The day on which no parent avails*: the family of vv. 14–15, and the mustard seed of v. 16, and "unto Me is the journey's end" of v. 14. *God's promise is true*: the *waʿd* of v. 9. *Let not the world delude you*: the *matāʿ* of v. 24 and the idle-purchase of v. 6. Luqmān's sūrah has been teaching a man how to be wise; verse 33 tells the whole human race what wisdom finally consists in: holding, at every moment, that the connections which sustain a person in this life will not carry him in the next, and that the One whose promise is true is also the One of whom the Devil is trying to give a false account. What remains, in v. 34, is the sentence that closes the chapter and, in effect, the entire sūrah's epistemology: five doors that do not open — and the One who holds the keys.

## Sūrah Luqmān 31:34

> **Truly with God lies knowledge of the Hour, and He sends down the rain and knows what lies in wombs. And no soul knows what it will earn on the morrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die. Truly God is Knowing, Aware.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Locks, Two Keys Only in God's Hand**

*Inna Llāha ʿindahu ʿilmu al-sāʿah, yunazzilu al-ghaytha, wa-yaʿlamu mā fī al-arḥām; wa-mā tadrī nafsun mā taksibu ghadan, wa-mā tadrī nafsun bi-ayyi arḍin tamūt. Inna Llāha ʿalīmun khabīr.* The sūrah closes with a list, and the list's shape is the argument. Three items are stated as God's knowledge — the Hour, the rain, what is in the wombs — and two as the negation of human knowledge — tomorrow's earning, the land of death. The sūrah does not say five times "God knows" (which would be doctrinally true and rhetorically flat); it says three times that He knows and twice that *you do not*, and the alternation places the reader's own ignorance inside the sentence rather than beside it. The five together are, in the sūrah's tradition, called *mafātīḥ al-ghayb*, the keys of the Unseen, and the hadith that fixed the number is transmitted in both Ṣaḥīḥs, in the tafsīr chapter on our verse:

> *"The keys of the Unseen are five: God — none knows what is in the Hour except God, and none knows what is in the wombs except God, and none knows what will be tomorrow except God, and none knows in what land it will die except God, and none knows when it will rain except God."* Then the Prophet ﷺ recited: "*Indeed, God — with Him is the knowledge of the Hour; He sends down the rain and knows what is in the wombs...*" (al-Bukhārī 4697, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar; and cf. 6:59: "*and with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He*")

That the Prophet ﷺ *recited the verse as the answer* is itself the point of the report: the text is not about a set of facts but about a limit placed for a reason.

**Why These Five**

The commentators ask why these particular five out of everything that is unknown, and the answers converge on one design: they are the five anxieties of an embodied creature. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah each note that they cover the *end of the whole* (the Hour), the *means of livelihood* (rain, and with it the harvest, the trade, the survival of the year), the *future person* (what is in the womb — its sex, its span, its state, in a society that had no instrument for any of it and enormous stakes riding on the answer), the *next day's outcome* (what a person will do, gain, or befall), and the *place of dying* (which land will be the last one). Every human being, in any century, lives inside those five questions; a farmer, a pregnant woman, a merchant, a traveler, and a man afraid of death between them hold the whole of our species' foreboding. The sūrah's claim is not that these questions are uninteresting; it is that they are *closed to forecasting* and *open to trust*.

There is a second, subtler reason for the rain's presence between the Hour and the wombs, and the sūrah's own theology supplies it: rain is the Qur'an's standing figure for resurrection ("*and We sent down from the sky blessed water, so We grew gardens with it and the grain of the harvest... and God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and He made for you hearing, sight, and hearts, that perhaps you would be thankful*" — 16:65, 78?). The accurate pairings are 50:9–11 — "*and We sent down from the sky blessed water, so We grew gardens with it and the grain of the harvest, and the palm-trees... a provision for the servants, and We gave life thereby to a dead land; thus is the coming-forth (al-khurūj)*" — and 16:65 with 16:78: "*God sends down water from the sky and gives life with it to the earth after its death*" and "*God brought you out of your mothers' wombs knowing nothing, and He made for you hearing, sights, and hearts, so that perhaps you would be thankful.*" Both halves of the verse's five — rain and wombs — are the Qur'an's standing images for being *given* life one does not choose. So the verse's order is: the End, the means of life, the hidden beginning of a person, the unknown next act, the unknown last place. Life and death are braided through all five, and each item is an instance of the same thing: beginnings and endings belong to God.

**"What It Will Earn on the Morrow": the Verse Inside the Dispute on Freedom**

*Wa-mā tadrī nafsun mā taksibu ghadan.* The word for what the soul will do is *taksibu*, from *kasb*, earning — and the choice is not incidental, because in the sūrah's own idiom a *kasb* is what a person *owns by doing*, the same verb family used for the record: "*for it is what it earned, and against it what it earned*" (2:286), and "*today every soul is recompensed for what it earned (kasant)*" (40:17). The verse, in other words, does not say "what will *happen to* you tomorrow"; it says what *you* will do. And it says that you do not know it.

This makes the clause one of the quiet load-bearing texts in the discussion of divine decree and human freedom. The Muʿtazila read the whole vocabulary of *kasb* as evidence that the servant is the genuine author of the act; the Ashʿarīs read it as evidence that the act is created by God and "acquired" by the servant; and the tradition of the salaf, which Ibn Taymiyyah restates at length in his *Darʾ Taʿāruḍ*, treats the verse as evidence of neither party's extreme but of the arrangement: the act is *yours* — which is why it is written down, weighed (v. 16), and testified by limbs — and its tomorrow is *not knowable by you*, which is why you cannot use it as an excuse in advance ("I could not help it; it was written") nor as a plan ("tomorrow I will repent"). Both abuses are answered by the same sentence, and both are answered without any appeal to mystery about God's side: the verse never says God does not know what you will earn. It says *no soul knows*. Divine foreknowledge and human freedom are, in the sūrah's presentation, both affirmed and both out of reach of the person's own calculation.

**"In What Land It Will Die": Death, Location, and Flight**

The last unknown is spatial and, for the sūrah's audience, concretely practical: *bi-ayyi arḍin tamūt*. Death was imagined as something a man might meet at a ford, in a pass, in a plague-town, in a foreign market — and the sūrah says that the map contains no such information about you. Our source preserves the reply that made the doctrine livable: the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, "*if God wills to take the soul of a servant in a particular land, He places in that servant a need for it, so that he does not cease until he arrives there*" (transmitted in the tafsīr sources on this verse; and after making the statement, in some accounts, he recited this verse). The logic is not fatalism about travel but a denial of geography as a defense: you cannot out-arrange your end, and you cannot out-run it either — a point the Qur'an makes elsewhere with total directness: "*wherever you are, death will overtake you, even if you are in fortified towers, rising high*" (4:78), "*say: if the death from which you are fleeing will surely overtake you*" (62:8), and "*and He it is who takes you by night and knows what you have committed by day*" (6:60). Al-Qurṭubī's comment on our verse belongs to the same register: the land of one's death is one of the five things no creature can know, and the person who supposes otherwise is, in the sūrah's phrase, "in manifest error" (v. 11).

The pastoral application, drawn by al-Tustarī in the quotation our source preserves, is the sūrah's own last word on the individual soul: "*no soul knows what it will earn on the morrow — that is, what will be counted for it and against it in what has been measured out from the Unseen. So be wary by upholding the remembrance of Him and crying out to Him for help, until He Himself takes care of your affair.*" The sentence is not "live in terror"; it is the discipline of *preparedness without presumption*, which is why this verse is the natural end of a chapter about wisdom. A sage who cannot tell his son what tomorrow holds can tell him what to do with it.

**The Occasion: a Bedouin's Four Questions**

The reports of the verse's occasion, in al-Wāḥidī and the tafsīr collections, describe a man — in the version our source transmits, a bedouin — who came and asked the Prophet ﷺ about the Hour and when it would be, and then added: "our land has dried up: when will the rain come? And I left my wife pregnant: what will she bear? And I know the land I was born in, but in which land am I going to die?" (cf. the variant in al-Qurṭubī where the questioner is a man of the Quraysh, and the report that the verse had already come down and the Prophet simply used it as the answer). The four questions map the verse exactly: the Hour, the rain, the womb, the land of death — with "what will I do tomorrow" implicit in the fifth. Two things follow from the pairing. First, the verse is a *reply to a person*, not a doctrinal tablet: it is what revelation says when a human being asks for the information he thinks he needs. Second, the answer is not "you shall not know, and stop asking"; it is a re-direction — the four things you asked about are the four you do not need in order to be ready, and the one thing you do need (that the Hour is *real*, that God is *ʿAlīm Khabīr*) is the only one that was given.

The Qur'an's other statements on the same refusal should be read with it, because they show that the withholding is consistent and deliberate: "*They ask you about the Hour, when it will come to rest? Say: its knowledge is only with my Lord; none will disclose it at its time. It is heavy in the heavens and the earth; it will not come to you but suddenly*" (7:187); "*They ask you about the Hour, as if you were keeping track of it. Say: its knowledge is only with God; but most people do not know*" (79:42–44); "*Indeed, those who dispute concerning the Hour are in deep error*" (42:18); and the hadith of Jibrīl, in which the Prophet ﷺ, asked "when is the Hour?", answered with the sentence the whole ummah memorized:

> *"...the one asked about it is not more knowledgeable than the asker."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8, in the hadith of ʿUmar)

**"Knowing, Aware": the Sūrah's Last Two Words**

The chapter ends as it began, and the ring is exact. Verse 2 announced *āyāt al-kitāb al-ḥakīm* — the signs of the Wise Book, the knowledge God chose to hand over. Verse 34 closes with *ʿAlīmun Khabīr* — the knowledge He did not. The sūrah of wisdom thus brackets human life between two facts about knowing: a revelation that tells us what we cannot find out for ourselves, and a mystery that keeps what we do not need. Nothing in between is left to chance; the same *Khabīr* who knows the wombs is the One who brings forth the mustard seed (v. 16), and the reader who has been following this sūrah verse by verse has been taught both halves of the definition: God has told us enough, and God has not told us everything.

The practical theology of that pair is the last and greatest thing Luqmān has to give his son. A life organized around the five unknowns is unlivable: the man who needs to know when he will die cannot plant a tree, and the man who needs to know what he will earn tomorrow cannot repent today. The sūrah's answer is to move the certainty from the *schedule* to the *person*: not "when," but "who" — He is *ʿAlīm*, knowing, and *Khabīr*, aware of the inner reality of every particular. The hour will come; the rain will fall; the child will be born; the tomorrow will write itself; the dying will find its land — and in every one of those there is one subject who is never surprised. That is a sufficient foundation for serenity, and for responsibility; and it is why this chapter, which is called after a sage, ends by teaching that the sage's defining virtue is not foreknowledge but *trust under a limit*.

**What a Ending Does to a Chapter**

The commentators on the *khātimah* (closing verse) of sūrahs note that a Makkan chapter's ending usually re-establishes either the Hour, the mercy, or the divine names, and Luqmān does all three in one line by making *khabīr* the last word — a word of *knowledge that is intimate* — so that the chapter about a father's tenderness ends with a name of God's care. The reader who came from the *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt* of v. 1 — letters whose meaning was reserved with God — has travelled through thirty-three verses to arrive back at reserved knowledge, and the ring is the sūrah's final lesson: revelation is not the elimination of mystery but the ordering of a life inside it. Wisdom, in Luqmān's chapter, is what a person becomes when he accepts both halves at once: he acts as if everything he does is registered, and he sleeps as if none of it is his to control.
