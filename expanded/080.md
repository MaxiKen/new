# Sūrah ʿAbasa (Chapter 80) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After a Frown**

Sūrah ʿAbasa takes its name from its opening word — "He frowned" — and in that single choice of title lies one of the most quietly remarkable facts in the Qur'an. The community that memorizes, recites, and names its chapters preserved, at God's own prompting, a word that recalls a moment of correction directed at the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself. Generation after generation of believers has opened this sūrah by reciting the record of their Prophet's momentary lapse in courtesy, because God willed that the lesson be permanent. This is not scandal; it is divine pedagogy of the most deliberate kind. The Qur'an names other chapters after the Prophet's states as well — al-Muzzammil, "The One Enwrapped," and al-Muddaththir, "The One Cloaked" — but those are names of endearment, words spoken to him in intimacy. ʿAbasa is the one title that carries a correction inside it. Muslims have always understood this as a mercy: the Book that teaches humility begins by practicing it upon the best of mankind, so that no teacher after him could ever say "correction is beneath me." A religion whose scripture keeps its own Prophet's lesson alive in its chapter titles has built humility into its very table of contents.

The sūrah is also known by other names that map its contents. It is called al-Ṣākhkhah, "The Piercing Cry," after verse 33, where the deafening blast of the resurrection shatters history in two. It is called al-Safarah, "The Scribes," after verse 15, where the honored pages rest in the hands of the angelic writers. Each name is a door into the chapter: enter by ʿAbasa and you enter by the lesson of a face; enter by al-Ṣākhkhah and you enter by the terror of the end; enter by al-Safarah and you enter by the majesty of the Book. That a short sūrah of forty-two verses should need three names is a sign of its density — like a room with three doors, each opening onto the same floor.

**Where the Sūrah Stands and When It Came**

ʿAbasa is an early Makkan sūrah, revealed in the years when the Muslim community was a small, poor, and mocked band in Makkah, before the migration to Madinah, before the state, before the numbers. Its Makkan character is visible on every page: the verses are short and struck like hammer-blows; the argument runs from creation to resurrection; the audience is being called from idolatry and arrogance to the worship of the One; and there is almost no legislation, because a people must first know whom they worship before they can be taught how to live. It is counted among the last third of the Qur'an, in the region the scholars call al-Mufaṣṣal, "the divided ones," where the verses are short and the sūrahs come in quick succession like beads on a string.

It stands immediately after Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt, and the classical commentators noted how deliberately the two are joined. Al-Nāziʿāt ends with the question of the Hour — *"They ask thee of the Hour: when is its anchoring? Whereunto does its mention reach thee? Unto thy Lord belongs its term"* (79:42–44) — and then addresses the Prophet: *"Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it"* (79:45). ʿAbasa opens by showing what that warning office costs and how it must be conducted: whom to attend to, whom not to court, and what the message actually is — a reminder in honored pages — before it too ends where al-Nāziʿāt ends, at the Hour itself, described in even more piercing colors. The two sūrahs are a matched pair of panels: the mission of the warner on one side, the Day he warns of on the other, and between them the Book that connects the two.

**The Man Behind the First Ten Verses**

The occasion of the first ten verses is among the most securely reported events in the literature of the Qur'an's revelation, transmitted from ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās through the channels of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Isḥāq, and the compilers of the occasions of revelation, and echoed by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr in their commentaries. The Prophet ﷺ was engaged in earnest conversation with a gathering of the chiefs of Quraysh — the reports name men such as ʿUtbah and Shaybah sons of Rabīʿah, Abū Jahl, and Umayyah ibn Khalaf — hoping, as any bearer of a message must hope, that if men of influence accepted the truth, those who followed them would follow them into it. While he was so engaged, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm, a blind believer, came near and repeated his request: "O Messenger of God, teach me, guide me," seeking the Qur'an, seeking instruction. The Prophet ﷺ frowned at the interruption and turned away, keeping his face and his effort toward the notables. The verses then descended:

> *"He frowned and turned away, because the blind man came to him."* (Qur'an 80:1–2)

Who was this blind man whose coming reshaped the ethics of every teacher after him? Ibn Umm Maktūm was among the earliest to embrace Islam — one of the first handful in Makkah — a man of Quraysh stock through his mother's line, poor, sightless, and devoted. He is the answer to a question the sūrah forces upon every reader: whom does God send to correct His Prophet? Not a chief, not a scholar of the later academies, but a blind man whom the world overlooked. After the revelation, the Prophet ﷺ would honor him whenever he came, saying:

> *"Welcome to him on whose account my Lord rebuked me."* (recorded in the commentary literature from the reports of the Companions; cited by al-Qurṭubī)

His later life is the fruit of the lesson he was used to plant. He was one of the first two Muslims to migrate to Madinah ahead of the Hijrah, where he taught the Qur'an to the community — the sīrah and ṭabaqāt literature records his name among the first teachers of the Book. He and Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ, the freed Abyssinian, were the two voices that gave the call to prayer in Madinah; al-Bukhārī records the Prophet's ﷺ instruction about the two dawn calls, in which Bilāl would call first in the darkness and Ibn Umm Maktūm would call at the edge of dawn:

> *"Bilāl gives the call in the night, so eat and drink until Ibn Umm Maktūm gives the call."* (al-Bukhārī 620; Muslim 1092, from ʿĀʾishah)

The blind man whom one moment of history might have dismissed became the alarm-clock of an entire city's fasting. More than once the Prophet ﷺ left Ibn Umm Maktūm in charge of Madinah when he went out on campaign — a blind man governing the capital in the Prophet's absence, an appointment that was itself a living commentary on this sūrah. When the verse of exemption was revealed — *"not equal are those of the believers who sit still... and those who strive... save those who are disabled"* (4:95) — it was Ibn Umm Maktūm's case that occasioned it, for he asked whether he must stay behind though he was blind. Yet even then he would not accept exemption as exclusion: he asked to be stood between the battle lines with the standard, saying, "I am blind and cannot run away." He was found among the martyrs of the great battle of al-Qādisiyyah in 14/636, still clutching the flag. The sūrah that begins with a blind man being overlooked ends, in history, with a blind man dying beneath the banner he would not drop. God does not merely rebuke neglect of the humble; He writes their biographies in gold.

**The Delicate Question: Was the Prophet Rebuked for a Sin?**

Because the opening verses correct the Prophet ﷺ, the commentators took care to explain precisely what kind of correction this was. Muslim theology affirms the Prophet's protection from sin (ʿiṣmah) in conveying the revelation, and the classical scholars — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah among them — converged on a subtle and satisfying account. The frown was not a transgression of any command; no verse had yet forbidden it. It was the choice of the weightier-looking of two goods — the hope of winning powerful men to the faith — over a plain duty already owed to a seeker who had come. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah of al-Andalus expressed the principle memorably: the scholars have said that the Prophet ﷺ was not blamed here for a sin, but for preferring the better of deeds to what was better than it — choosing one good while a greater good, already at his elbow, waited. On this reading, the verses are not the indictment of a crime; they are the sharpening of a standard. God trains His Messenger the way a master trains a master-craftsman: not by punishing failures, but by refining judgments until even his pauses are perfect. The Qur'an shows this same pattern of gentle, public correction elsewhere — *"O Prophet, why dost thou forbid what God has made lawful to thee, seeking the pleasure of thy wives?"* (66:1); *"May God pardon thee! Why didst thou give them leave...?"* (9:43) — and in every case the correction itself becomes a lesson for the community, which is precisely why the occasions were preserved rather than smoothed away. A scripture that hides its prophet's corrections teaches the infallibility of men; a scripture that records them teaches the sufficiency of God's mercy.

A weaker line of reports tried to shift the rebuke onto some other figure — a companion who neglected a poor man while entertaining a rich one — but the mass-transmitted account from ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās has carried the field from the earliest generations, and the sūrah's own grammar (the singular "thou" addressed throughout) marks the Messenger as the addressee. The tradition's consensus is itself a lesson in intellectual honesty: the scholars did not rewrite an uncomfortable text; they read it closely and found in it wisdom rather than fault.

**Four Movements, One Escalating Argument**

The sūrah is built like a rising staircase, and its architecture rewards attention:

First, verses 1–10: a lesson in ethics drawn from a living incident — whom the message is for, and the radical equal­ity of the seeker's claim upon the teacher.

Second, verses 11–16: the nature of the message itself — a reminder, written, honored, exalted, purified, in the hands of noble scribes. Having corrected the messenger's aim, God shows him the dignity of what he carries.

Third, verses 17–23: the case against man's ingratitude — argued not by threat but by anatomy: you were a drop; He proportioned you, eased your way, will die you, bury you, and raise you; and you have not even fulfilled what He commanded.

Fourth, verses 24–32: the argument from the dinner table — rain poured, earth split, grain, vine, olive, palm, garden, fruit, pasture — a complete agricultural cycle marshaled as evidence.

Fifth, verses 33–42: the conclusion the whole argument has been driving toward — the Piercing Cry, the flight from one's own kin, and the two kinds of faces into which all humanity resolves: radiant and laughing, or dust-darkened and ruined.

The method is worth naming: the sūrah moves from etiquette to cosmology, from a frown to the end of the world. This is not disproportion; it is revelation's way of saying that the smallest social gesture and the greatest cosmic event are on one continuum of meaning. God does not separate, as modern thought often does, the "private" from the "ultimate": how you treat the man who interrupts you is a rehearsal for how you will stand when the heavens split.

**The Sūrah of Faces**

Read whole, ʿAbasa is a sūrah about faces. It opens with a frown on a face — ʿabasa — and a face turned away — tawallā. It ends with faces that shine and laugh and faces that are caked with dust and veiled in darkness. Between these poles, the blind man who could not see a face comes seeking the One who sees all faces. The trajectory is unmistakable: the face you wear in your fleeting moments of impatience is being trained, by this Book, for the face you will wear forever. And there is a quieter symmetry that the attentive reciter will hear: the sūrah's first villain, so to speak, is the man who *deemed himself self-sufficient* (istaghnā, v. 5), and its great reversal comes when *every man's own affair shall suffice him* (yughnīhi, v. 37) — the same Arabic root g-h-n-y framing the two edges of the chapter. The one who thought he needed nothing, and the Day when nothing but one's own record will do: between those two sufficiencies lies the whole human situation.

**How to Read What Follows**

What follows treats each of the forty-two verses as an independent discourse, in strict order, exactly as the verse-by-verse method requires. Verses the source commentary grouped together — the kinship flight of verses 34–36, the botanical list of verses 27–32, the paired faces of verses 38–39 and 40–41 — are here separated and given full individual treatment. The Arabic is opened word by word where it repays it; the Qur'an is quoted against itself where one passage illumines another; the hadith is cited with its collection; the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, Ibn ʿAbbās and the early exegetes — are given their voice; and every verse is pressed for the logic of its teaching and its bearing on ordinary modern life. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is short; a short sūrah fully unfolded is a longer labor than a long one merely summarized. The sūrah that taught a Prophet not to neglect a single blind man will not, in this commentary, have any of its own forty-two verses neglected in turn.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:1

> **He frowned and turned away,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Word of Arabic, a Whole Scene of the Heart**

The sūrah opens with a single verb and no named subject: *ʿabasa* — "he frowned." The root ʿ-b-s describes the drawing together of the brow, the hardening of the face that happens when displeasure, distaste, or preoccupation takes over; from the same root comes ʿabūs, a scowling, ill-natured face. It is one of the most bodily words in the Qur'an: it names not a thought, not a statement, but the face itself changing — the muscles of the brow contracting, the eyes narrowing, the whole countenance closing like a door. And it is followed instantly by a second verb of the body: *tawallā* — "he turned away," from the root w-l-y, to turn, to take another direction, to withdraw one's orientation. The two verbs together compose a complete drama in eight Arabic syllables: first the face closes, then the body follows. That order is itself the lesson. Before any word is spoken, before any act is chosen, the face has already broadcast the verdict, and the body has already acted on it. Sin and courtesy alike begin as posture; the tongue only later signs what the face has already declared.

**Why the Qur'an Does Not Name the Frowner**

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say "the Prophet frowned" or "Muhammad turned away." The subject is hidden — an unnamed "he," and the reader is left to complete the sentence from the occasion of revelation. The classical commentators observed this reticence and found it eloquent. God, as it were, lowered His voice even while correcting His beloved. It is the manner of a wise parent who disciplines a child without ever announcing the child's fault to the street: the correction is precise, but the dignity is preserved. Al-Qurṭubī and others note how the divine address stays gentle throughout — the whole passage is framed as instruction, not accusation, and within a few verses God will say, in effect, "you are not even accountable for the outcome" (v. 7). At the same time, the unnamed subject performs a second function for every later generation: whoever reads this verse while wearing the authority of a teacher, a parent, an imam, a counselor, a manager — anyone at whom others come seeking — is invited to place his own name into the sentence. The hidden subject is hidden precisely so that no reader can hide from it. The verse becomes a mirror in which each teacher sees his own face.

**The Frown Was Not a Sin — and the Lesson Is Greater Because of It**

The occasion of revelation, transmitted from ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās through the early exegetical literature, sets the scene: the Prophet ﷺ was pleading with the chiefs of Quraysh, hoping that their conversion would open the door for their followers, when Ibn Umm Maktūm, the blind believer, pressed near with his repeated request for teaching, and the Prophet ﷺ knit his brow and kept his face toward the notables. The theological question this raises was squarely faced by the tradition: if the Prophet ﷺ is protected from sin, what is being corrected here? The answer the scholars converged upon — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Ibn Kathīr — is that nothing forbidden had occurred. No command of God had yet been violated; the frown was the natural cost of a strategic hope — winning the influential was itself a good. What the verses correct is the ranking of goods: the powerful men were a *hope*, while the blind man was a *duty already standing in front of him*. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah put it in a phrase the later tradition never improved upon: the Prophet ﷺ was not blamed for a sin, but for choosing the lesser of two good actions when the greater was within reach. This is a far more searching standard than "avoid sin," and it explains why the Qur'an preserves the incident. A religion that only policed crimes would produce law-abiding mediocrity; a revelation that refines the allocation of attention itself — which seeker gets your face — is training excellence.

The Qur'an contains this same pattern of tender public correction elsewhere, and the pattern itself is doctrine. In Sūrah al-Taḥrīm:

> *"O Prophet! Why dost thou forbid what God has made lawful to thee, seeking the pleasure of thy wives? And God is Forgiving, Merciful."* (Qur'an 66:1)

And in Sūrah al-Tawbah:

> *"May God pardon thee! Why didst thou give them leave before it was clear to thee who spoke the truth?"* (Qur'an 9:43)

In each case the correction is real, is preserved, and becomes a permanent lesson for the community — while the address remains clothed in mercy ("May God pardon thee"). ʿAbasa is the earliest and most celebrated instance: God does not say "you did wrong"; He simply states the fact — the frown, the turning — and lets the following eight verses supply the perspective that turns fact into education.

**A Later Verse Proves the Training Worked**

The most beautiful commentary on ʿabasa is found in a verse revealed years later, in Madinah, when the community had become a state:

> *"It was by the mercy of God that thou wast lenient with them; hadst thou been harsh and hard of heart, they would have scattered from around thee. So pardon them, and ask forgiveness for them, and take counsel with them in the affair."* (Qur'an 3:159)

Here God names the very quality whose absence verse 1 records — harshness of face and heart — and declares that its absence was His own mercy placed in the Prophet. The trajectory from ʿAbasa to Āl ʿImrān is the arc of a training completed: the young messenger whose brow once knitted at an interruption becomes the statesman whose gentleness is itself a divine gift and the precondition of the community's survival. The Qur'an thus documents its own pedagogy across two decades. And history sealed the training in the Prophet's own reported habit toward the man at the center of the incident: he would spread his cloak for Ibn Umm Maktūm and greet him with the words, "Welcome to him on whose account my Lord rebuked me." No resentment at the correction — rather, a lifetime of honoring the occasion of it.

**The Universal Frown: Every Teacher's Hidden Default**

Strip away the sacred history and the verse still names a universal mechanism. Every person who carries something valuable to give — knowledge, help, access, approval, faith — faces a constant temptation of attention: to spend the face on those whose response will be profitable, prestigious, or pleasant, and to withhold it from those who need it most. The frown is rarely planned. It is the automatic face of a mind that has ranked its audiences by return-on-investment: the wealthy prospect gets the open face, the awkward questioner gets the brow, the repeated petitioner gets the turned shoulder. Modern institutions have professionalized the very tendency this verse corrects: the donor is escorted to the front, the gatekeeper screens the "unimportant," the follower count decides whose message is answered. The Qur'an's first word on evangelism, education, and leadership is a word against allowing the calculus of status to govern the muscles of one's face. And it identifies the beginning of the whole failure at its true, tiny source — not a policy, not an ideology, but a frown that lasted a second. Great ethical failures are usually small facial expressions that hardened into habits, then into systems. God intervenes at the first millimeter, because that is where correction is cheapest and mercy is most easily received.

**The Face as the First Sermon**

There is a final, quiet teaching in the choice of ʿabasa as the sūrah's opening word: the face is the first text anyone reads. Before a teacher opens his mouth, the seeker has already read his countenance; before the message is preached, it has been *faced*. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Do not consider any kindness insignificant, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face."* (Muslim 2626, from Abū Dharr)

The reverse is implied with equal force: do not consider any unkindness insignificant — even a frown. In the divine accounting that this sūrah opens, a face is not a private possession but a public trust, the first act of hospitality or the first act of expulsion that every human being administers dozens of times a day. The sūrah will end, thirty-nine verses later, with faces that shine and faces that are darkened — the eternal faces. It begins with a face that frowned for a moment and was corrected forever. The distance between those two kinds of faces is the distance time puts between a mistake that can still be mended and a state that can no longer change; and the whole space of that distance is the mercy of a God who corrects His beloved while there is still time to turn back.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:2

> **because the blind man came to him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Causal Link: The Coming Itself Is the Charge**

The second verse supplies the reason for the frown with stark economy: *an jāʾahu al-aʿmā* — "because the blind man came to him." The particle *an* is causal: the frown happened *because* this man approached. The Qur'an does not say the blind man importuned too insistently, or interrupted rudely, or did anything at all blameworthy; the entire weight of the sentence falls on a single, irreproachable fact — he *came*. That is his whole action. The verse thereby establishes one of the foundation principles of religious and moral duty: the arrival of a seeker is itself a claim. A person who comes to you for knowledge, for guidance, for help, for a hearing, constitutes — by the mere fact of coming — a right upon your attention, prior to any assessment of his rank, usefulness, or convenience. The frown was the refusal of a claim the man had already established simply by drawing near. Al-Ṭabarī brings out this reading plainly: the rebuke is not for a wrong done to the man's person, but for the wrong done to the *meaning* of his coming — for treating as an interruption what God had sent as an appointment.

**Why the Qur'an Describes Him by His Blindness**

The verse calls him *al-aʿmā* — "the blind man" — with the definite article: not "a blind man" but *the* blind man, the known one. The choice is deliberate at several levels. First, it preserves the historical particularity of the occasion: everyone in the early community knew precisely who was meant — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm, one of the first Muslims. Second, it makes his disability the very badge of his honor in the Qur'an: he enters revelation not by name but by his condition, and thereby every blind person for the rest of history owns a share of this verse — God ennobled the condition rather than the clan, in a chapter that simultaneously corrected the courting of clan prestige. Third, and most searching, the description sets up the sūrah's own irony, which the commentators loved to unfold: the man who could not *see* was the one who *came* to the light, while the men of sight — the chiefs being courted — refused to come at all. Physical sight was on the side of the sitters; spiritual sight was on the side of the comer. The Qur'an makes the point explicitly elsewhere, in words that read like a commentary on this very scene:

> *"Have they not traveled through the land, that they might have hearts by which to understand or ears by which to hear? Truly it is not the eyes that grow blind, but it is the hearts within the breasts that grow blind."* (Qur'an 22:46)

Ibn Umm Maktūm is the walking proof of 22:46: sightless eyes, seeing heart.

**The Irony of the Frown That Could Not Be Seen**

There is a tender divine irony in the incident that the verse quietly encodes. The frown — the entire fault being corrected — was wasted on its recipient: a blind man cannot see a scowl. Ibn Umm Maktūm never knew, in the moment, that the Prophet's ﷺ brow had knitted; he could not have taken offense from a face he could not perceive. The frown therefore harmed no one's feelings. And yet God corrected it — because, as the commentators observe, the moral quality of an act does not depend on whether the victim notices. The lesson is aimed at the actor, not the injured: a kindness concealed is still kindness, and a discourtesy unnoticed is still discourtesy, because both are first facts about the *self* that performs them before they are experiences for others. God saw the frown that the blind man could not, and that is the standard: live, and teach, and give, as one standing before the sight of God rather than before the sight of men — *"though you conceal it or make it public"* (Qur'an 2:77) applies to faces as much as to funds. It also means that God is, in the deepest sense, the advocate of those who cannot advocate for themselves: the unnoticed, the unseen, the invisible poor at every door. He registers on their behalf the slights they never even perceived.

**He Came — The Direction of Movement as a Moral Fact**

Hold the two verbs of approach side by side across the passage. In verses 5–6 the self-sufficient notable is the one to whom the Prophet ﷺ must *attend* (taṣaddā) — the messenger leaning toward him. In verse 8 the striver is the one who *came to thee* (jāʾaka yaʿṣā) — the seeker crossing the room. And here in verse 2 the blind man *came to him*. The direction of physical movement is itself a map of spiritual reality: the hungry walk toward the spring; the full must be sought out and persuaded. This is why the verse dignifies the act of coming with a causal clause of its own. In the Qur'an's moral physics, drawing near to God and to His teaching is never a trivial act — it is the external sign of an internal motion that God honors and answers. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever travels a path seeking knowledge, God will make easy for him a path to Paradise."* (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah)

Ibn Umm Maktūm's short walk across the gathering, stumbling toward the voice he wanted to hear, was a journey of exactly this kind — and God answered it not with a gentle human welcome only, but with ten verses of His own eternal speech, and then with the honor of a lifetime: the Prophet's ﷺ greeting whenever he came — "Welcome to him on whose account my Lord rebuked me" — the two dawn calls of Madinah raised by his voice beside Bilāl's, the governorship of the capital in the Prophet's absences, and at last the standard at al-Qādisiyyah found gripped in his dead hand. The man who came was never again allowed to feel unwelcome.

**The Persistent Requester**

The reports of the occasion add a detail that colors the verse: Ibn Umm Maktūm did not merely appear — he repeated his request, calling, "O Messenger of God, teach me, guide me," more than once, while the Prophet ﷺ was occupied. This persistence is worth pausing on, because it is the pattern of nearly every earnest seeker in every age. Those who most need teaching are often those least trained in the etiquette of waiting; their need makes them repetitive. The worldly eye sees an interruption repeating itself; God's eye sees an appetite He loves — for the Prophet ﷺ said that God is more merciful to His servants than a mother to her child, and that He *loves* the importunate asker. The Qur'an praises the relentless in another register entirely:

> *"And when My servants ask thee concerning Me — truly I am near; I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me."* (Qur'an 2:186)

The verse before us trains every bearer of knowledge to answer repeated need as God answers repeated prayer: not with a frown at the third request, but with recognition that need that returns is need that is real.

**Who Are Today's Blind Men at the Door?**

The application of the verse to modern life is almost uncomfortably direct. Every community has its Ibn Umm Maktūms: the person whose questions are awkward or repeated; the newcomer with no standing and no connections; the disabled, the poor, the socially toneless, the ones who "always seem to come at the wrong time"; in a mosque, the convert who asks basic things after the sermon; in a university, the student without polish who haunts office hours; in a workplace, the junior colleague whose request competes with the important meeting. Verse 2 establishes that their coming is not an interruption of the work; in God's accounting, their coming *is* the work — the appointment was made by Him before either party knew it. The chiefs of Quraysh, meanwhile, were a hypothesis: *perhaps* they would believe. The blind man was a certainty: he believed already and hungered for more. The frown was the victory of the hypothesis over the certainty. Every teacher since is being asked, in the two words *an jāʾahu al-aʿmā*, to reverse that ranking — to let the certain seeker standing near outrank the speculative convert being courted far off — while never ceasing, as verses 5–7 will themselves insist, to offer the message to the far off as well.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:3

> **And what would apprise thee? Perhaps he would purify himself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Qur'an's Rhetoric of "What Would Make You Know?"**

The third verse shifts from narration to address: *wa mā yudrīka* — "and what would apprise thee?" The verb *adrā* means to cause to know, to bring something within another's ken; the construction *mā yudrīka* is a recognized rhetorical device of the Qur'an, used when a reality lies beyond the addressee's means of knowing, and it usually introduces something of great weight: *"And what will make thee know what the Night of Decree is?"* (97:2); *"And what will make thee know what the Crushing Blow is?"* (101:3). Here the unknown is not a cosmic mystery but a human interior: the potential of the man standing in front of the Prophet ﷺ. God is saying, in effect: you could not know — no external inspection could tell you — what this blind man might become through the teaching you are withholding. The device functions both as rebuke and as instruction. As rebuke, it exposes the limits of the Prophet's ﷺ strategic calculation: he was ranking his audience by what could be *seen* — status, influence, the visible levers of Meccan society — while the truly decisive facts about any human being, the facts God weighs, are precisely the invisible ones. As instruction, it establishes the epistemological humility on which all true teaching rests: no teacher ever faces a mere visible person; he faces an unseen interior whose future only God knows. The lesson generalizes instantly: because you cannot see what anyone might become, you must treat every seeker as the field of an unknown harvest.

**The Grammar of Hope: "Perhaps"**

The verse does not assert that the blind man *will* purify himself; it says *ʿasā* — "perhaps he might." This little word is one of the most humane in the Qur'an's vocabulary. It is the grammar of hope deliberately held open — and the Qur'an uses ʿasā constantly to frame the outcomes that God invites His servants to reach for: *"Perchance your Lord will have mercy upon you"* (17:8); *"Perchance they may be guided"* (2:186, of even the heedless). What the word does here is define the teacher's proper stance toward every student: to see him not as he is, but as he *perhaps* may become. The Prophet ﷺ embodied this optic in his own dealings, in ways the Qur'an itself records. After the battle of Uhud, when the fortunes of many seemed ruined and some had fled, God commanded forgiveness toward them:

> *"And it was by the mercy of God that thou wast lenient with them... so pardon them, and ask forgiveness for them, and take counsel with them."* (Qur'an 3:159)

He saw men not as their worst day, but as their possible repentance — and history vindicated him in person after person: ʿUmar, once the Qur'an's most violent enemy, became its most towering champion; Khālid ibn al-Walīd turned from leading the ranks against the Muslims to leading their armies. Verse 3 of ʿAbasa is the seed of that entire pedagogy: the possibility framed by *ʿasā* is the space in which teaching does its work.

**Yazzakkā: Purification as Growth**

The verb the verse attaches its hope to is *yazzakkā* — "he would purify himself," from the root z-k-w, whose field of meaning covers both *cleansing* and *growing*. The same root gives *zakāh*, the purifying alms, and *zanā* in its ancient sense of flourishing. This doubleness is not accidental; in Qur'anic psychology, purity and growth are one process. To be purified is not merely to have something removed (dirt, sin, distraction); it is to be put into the condition in which growth becomes possible — as a gardener "purifies" a seedbed not to leave it empty, but so that something may grow in it. And what is the instrument of this purification? The context of the next verse will say: *reminder* — being reminded, such that the reminder benefits him. The Quran's own word for itself is *dhikr*, remembrance, and its whole theory of moral change is contained in this verse's chain: a human being forgets his origin, his Lord, and his end; the reminder re-places what forgetting displaced; and the re-placed knowledge works itself out as purification — the reordering of the soul around what it now remembers. The commentators note the reading-variants here: *yazzakkā* (he purifies himself, reflexive) and *yazzakā* (he is purified, or accepts purification) — a small difference the scholars happily allowed to coexist, since the truth embraces both: the reminder enables the purification, and the man must also take hold of it himself.

**Blindness, Purity, and the Qur'an's Great Leveling**

There is a historical poignancy in hoping that *this* man — blind, poor, easily ranked at the bottom of every Meccan social ledger — might purify himself and rise. The Qur'an had already, in these early years, declared the radical revaluation on which verse 3 rests: worth is not inherited from the tribe or read off the body, but *achieved* in the unseen. The Meccans, by contrast, had a body-and-lineage anthropology: the blind, the lame, the poor were, in their view, either ill-favored by the gods or useless to a patron god of prosperity — this very argument was thrown at the Prophet ﷺ: *"Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon some great man of the two cities?"* (43:31). God's answer unfolded across the early sūrahs and culminates here in miniature: the great man of the two cities was standing in front of them, and he was blind. The sūrah's two portraits — the self-sufficient notable of verses 5–7 and the striving, fearful man of verses 8–10 — will complete the revaluation: purity, fear of God, effort, and receptivity are the new currency, and the old currency of status simply does not convert. The Prophet ﷺ was taught the same lesson in the same years by the Qur'an's insistence on the poor believers' presence at his gatherings:

> *"And keep thy soul patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face; and let not thine eyes pass beyond them, desiring the adornment of the life of this world."* (Qur'an 18:28)

Verse 3 of ʿAbasa is that command in narrative form: the eyes that had passed beyond the blind man are turned back toward him, and told, in the word ʿasā, what they had failed to see.

**The Teacher's Impossible Knowledge and Sacred Hope**

For anyone who teaches, counsels, parents, or leads, this verse quietly relocates the whole enterprise. The temptation revealed by the frown of verse 1 was, at bottom, an epistemological error: believing that one could tell, from visible markers, who was worth teaching. Verse 3 denies the premise. The teacher does not know — *cannot* know — which child in the classroom, which convert at the door, which repeated questioner on the phone, carries inside him a purification that one more hour of patience would unlock. Since the knowledge is impossible, the only rational policy is universal availability of the face. This also dissolves the despair that afflicts every teacher and every parent — the sense of wasting effort on the apparently unreachable. God's verdict on that arithmetic is the word *perhaps*. The results of teaching are not visible at the moment of teaching; the seed-sower of the Qur'anic parable does not see the seven hundredfold:

> *"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is as the likeness of a grain that sprouts seven ears; in every ear is a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 2:261)

What the sūrah's third verse adds to the parable is the personal, face-to-face form of the same truth: the grain that sprouts may be the blind man everyone assumed was barren ground. Teach him anyway — *perhaps* he will purify himself; and if he does, no social strategy ever devised by man will have matched the return on that one act of attention.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:4

> **or be reminded, such that the reminder might benefit him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tadhakkur: Remembering as an Act Performed Upon Oneself**

The fourth verse completes the sentence of hope begun in verse 3 with a second possibility: *aw yadhdhakara* — "or he would be reminded." The verbal form here is what Arab grammarians call the fifth form, the *tafaʿʿul* pattern, which carries a reflexive, effortful sense: not merely to remember, but to *bring remembering upon oneself* — to set oneself to recall what has slipped. Ibn Umm Maktūm was already a believer; what he needed was not conversion but *reminding* — the fresh delivery of truths already entrusted to him, deepened, repeated, applied. The Qur'an's word for its own message is exactly this: *dhikr*, remembrance. And the sūrah will say so explicitly in a few verses — *"Truly it is a reminder"* (80:11). The chain the verse now constructs is the whole Qur'an's theory of moral formation: *reminder offered → self-reminding performed → benefit received*: *fa-tanfaʿahu al-dhikrā* — "such that the reminder might benefit him." The reminder itself is only the external half; the human act of yadhdhakara — taking the reminder into oneself — is the hinge on which benefit swings. The same conditionality is stated as a general law elsewhere in the Book:

> *"And remind — truly the reminder benefits the believers."* (Qur'an 51:55)

**Why Humanity Needs a Religion of Reminder**

Behind this verse stands the Qur'an's sober anthropology: the human being is a forgetful creature. The Book says of the first man himself, *"And We made a covenant with Adam before, but he forgot"* (20:115); and it says of mankind generally that *"man was created weak"* (4:28) and *"anxious"* (70:19) — a creature whose knowledge of the highest things is real but flickering, always present in principle, always in need of rekindling in fact. On this diagnosis, religion is not primarily the delivery of new information but the *reactivation* of buried knowledge — which is why the Qur'an so often calls itself dhikr, and why its recurring command is not "learn!" so much as "remember!" (*fa-dhkurū*). The insight has been confirmed by every serious tradition of moral psychology: human beings do not fail for lack of principles nearly so often as they fail from *inattention to* principles they fully possess. A distracted surgeon knows anatomy; the harm comes from the lapse of attention. The entire rhythm of Islamic practice is built as an answer to this diagnosis: the five daily prayers are five re-orientations, the Friday sermon a weekly re-collection, Ramadan an annual re-grounding, the dhikr of the tongue a continuous hum beneath the day. Verse 4 is the charter for all of it: the reminder must keep coming because the forgetting keeps coming.

**The Benefit Is Conditional — and That Is the Respect**

The subtlest word in the verse is the little *fa-* in *fa-tanfaʿahu*: the reminder benefits *once* the self-remembering occurs. The benefit is not automatic. The Qur'an is explicit that the same words fall differently on different soils:

> *"God has sent down the fairest discourse, a Book consimilar, paired in promises and warnings... And those who are guided — He increases them in guidance and grants them their reverence."* (Qur'an 47:17–18)

And conversely:

> *"And when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that haply you may receive mercy."* (Qur'an 7:204)

Receiving depends on the posture of the receiver. What makes this conditionality profound rather than disappointing is what it implies about human dignity: the reminder does not override the person. It invites; it does not compel. God could have made faith an inescapable impression, but He willed a creature who must *take* the reminder for it to work — *lā ikrāha fī al-dīn*, "no compulsion in religion" (2:256), is the legal echo of this verse's grammar. Ibn Umm Maktūm's hunger made him exactly the kind of listener in whom the reminder's benefit was near-certain — which is the final irony of the frown: the interruption was, in God's estimate, the most promising teaching opportunity in the room. The Qur'an will draw the general conclusion a few verses later: the reminder is for *whoever wills* to remember (80:12).

**The Prophet's Own Ministry Described by God as Reminding, Not Compelling**

The sūrah's understanding of the prophetic office as reminder-ministry was revealed with equal clarity in other sūrahs of the same period, and nowhere more precisely than in Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah:

> *"So remind — thou art only a reminder. Thou art not a master over them."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

The two verses together relieve every caller of two crushing burdens that do not belong to him. First, the burden of *omnipotence over hearts*: results are not his to manufacture; the reminder's benefit is conditional on a freedom he does not control — *"Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but God guides whomsoever He wills"* (28:56). Second, the burden of *elite-capture*: if the office is reminder, then the audience is *whoever comes to be reminded* — and God decides who comes. The man sent to the chiefs of Quraysh discovered, through ten verses of correction, that his true constituency was standing behind him, asking to be taught. The same relief belongs to every teacher, parent, and preacher: sow the reminder faithfully, make the face available to whoever comes, and leave the yield to the One who made the soil.

**Repeated Reminders Are Not Redundant — They Are the Design**

Because the verse's benefit runs through *yadhdhakara*, the faithful teacher learns not to measure a reminder by its novelty. The greatest mistake of the sophisticated — in every age — is to disdain repetition: "we have heard this before." The Qur'an itself is the most repetitive great book in world literature, and unapologetically so, because its Author knows the machinery of the human heart better than the heart itself: what is known but not *present* to consciousness does not steer behavior; only what is *remembered now* does. The Prophet ﷺ institutionalized this understanding in his own preaching, as Ibn Masʿūd reported:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ used to take care to preach to us at intervals, fearing that we should grow weary."* (al-Bukhārī 68; Muslim 2821, from Ibn Masʿūd)

Note the balance in the Prophet's ﷺ practice: he repeated enough to keep the reminder alive, but spaced it enough to keep it heard — a master's calibration of exactly the process verse 4 describes. For the modern believer, the verse dignifies the felt experience of hearing "the same" khutbah, the "same" verses, the "same" counsels year after year: they are not the same, because the listener is not the same, and the moment when an old verse finally *benefits* — the day the illness, the grief, or the midlife reckoning makes a lifetime-heard verse suddenly open like a door — is the day this verse's chain completes itself in him: reminder offered, self-remembrance performed, benefit received.

**An Everyday Portrait of the Verse at Work**

Picture the verse's two persons in any modern setting. In one chair sits the self-sufficient listener — the one who has decided, perhaps unconsciously, that he has nothing to receive; words fall on him like rain on glass, and slide away. In the other sits the striver — the anxious parent at a counseling session, the new Muslim at a study circle, the exhausted believer who has come to the mosque specifically because life has broken his self-sufficiency. The same sentences are spoken to both; only one is changed by them. Nothing about the words differed; everything about the receiving differed. That is *fa-tanfaʿahu al-dhikrā* — the reminder benefits *him*, the one who yadhdhakara. The verse is thus both a comfort and a warning to its reader: it comforts every teacher whose fidelity looks fruitless in the short term, and it warns every listener that the outcome of hearing is, in the end, his own act — the reminder can only ever meet him halfway.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:5

> **As for him who deems himself beyond need,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Istighnāʾ: The Feeling of Needing Nothing**

With verse 5 the sūrah pivots from the blind man to his opposite number, and the pivot word is one of the most important moral diagnoses in the Qur'an: *man istaghnā* — "him who deems himself beyond need." The root is gh-n-y, wealth, riches, self-sufficiency; the tenth-form verb *istaghnā* means to *find oneself sufficient*, to conclude — rightly or wrongly — that one has enough and needs no further supply. The form itself carries the psychology: this is not simply being wealthy; it is the *verdict one passes on oneself*: "I am covered." And the verse's context tells us what realm the verdict has been extended into: not merely food and shelter, but *guidance* — the man who feels he has no need of what the Prophet ﷺ is offering. The chiefs of Quraysh, per the occasion of revelation, are the immediate referent: Utbah, Shaybah, Abū Jahl, and their peers — men whose storehouses, alliances, and standing convinced them that their lives were already complete, and that the Messenger's message was a beggar's message, fit for beggars. But the Qur'an frames the type generically — *whoever* deems himself beyond need — because the condition it names is portable across every century and every tax bracket.

**The Twin Verse in Sūrah al-ʿAlaq**

The early Makkan sūrahs return to istighnāʾ with striking insistence, and no parallel is closer than the one in the very sūrah most Muslims count among the first revealed:

> *"Nay! Truly man rebels in that he sees himself self-sufficient (raʾāhu istaghnā). Truly unto thy Lord is the return."* (Qur'an 96:6–8)

Read 96:6–8 alongside 80:5 and the full anatomy emerges: *istighnāʾ* is not a financial state but a spiritual reflex — the moment a creature concludes he is self-sufficient, he begins to *transgress* (ṭagha), because the brake of dependence has been released. Why should dependence act as a brake? Because every human being knows, at some level, that he did not make himself, did not choose his century or his genes or his heartbeat's next contraction; the sense of being *held* by Another is the standing reminder that his powers are loans. Istighnāʾ is the amnesia that cancels the reminder: the loans are mistaken for property, the tenancy for ownership — and character follows the accounting. This is why the Qur'an can trace injustice to wealth-illusion in one step: *"Nay, man rebels when he sees himself self-sufficient."* Pharaoh, the Qur'an's archetype of the type, says it outright: *"I am your lord most high"* (79:24), and *"there is no god but me, so worship me"* (28:38).

**The Deed Is a Delusion: Everything We Have Is On Loan**

The moral logic of the verse becomes airtight the moment one inspects the claim of self-sufficiency against the facts of the human condition. A man who deems himself beyond need is making a statement about himself that reality refutes at every joint. He did not generate his existence; he cannot halt his aging; he cannot veto a single cell's mutiny; his intelligence was given, his language was inherited before consent, his sight and hearing arrive and depart on a schedule he never saw. Even his wealth — the usual ground of the feeling — is a temporary arrangement of things he will leave to others, in a pattern the Qur'an describes with devastating economy: *"And spend of that whereunto He has made you successors"* (57:7). The Qur'an's favorite mirror for istighnāʾ is the harvest that hides its own dependence:

> *"Know that the life of this world is only play, and diversion, and adornment, and boasting among you, and rivalry in wealth and children — like a rain whose growth pleases the tillers (al-kuffār)..."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The Arabic for tillers here, *kuffār*, is from the same root as *kufr*, disbelief — because a farmer *covers* seed with soil; and the disbeliever, by a divine pun the Qur'an savors, *covers* the Giver beneath the gift. The self-sufficient man is thus not merely ungrateful; he is factually mistaken — the covering has worked on his own eyes. The Qur'an asks the question that ends the discussion: *"Did He not find thee an orphan, and shelter thee?"* (93:6).

**The Spiritual Disease That Is Not Owned by the Rich**

It is essential to the verse's justice that the sin it names is a *self-assessment*, not a net worth. The Prophet ﷺ warned that this disease does not check bank statements at the door; it can ride a camel of any load:

> *"Richness is not in abundance of goods; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051, from Abū Hurayrah)

And its inverse — the poverty that is actually wealth — the Qur'an stamps on its very pages: the people of al-Rūm are praised as *"the poor of the earth"* (2:273? rather 30:25's context), and the Prophet ﷺ's community is told plainly that God chose the weak and humble to shame the strong. A billionaire who lies awake knowing all of it is a trust can be a servant in the truest sense — Solomon, ruler of a kingdom no modern state has matched, stood in his palace and said, *"This is of the bounty of my Lord, to try me whether I am grateful or ungrateful"* (27:40). And a penniless student can be a spiritual pharaoh if his learning has convinced him that he has no need of being taught. Istighnāʾ lives in the mirror, not the wallet; hence the verse's wording — not "the rich man," but "the one who *deemed* himself beyond need." The verb indicts the verdict, not the portfolio.

**Why the Sūrah Names This Man Second**

Notice the architecture of vv. 5–10: it is a deliberate two-panel portrait. Panel one (vv. 5–7): the self-sufficient one, whom you attend — though you are not responsible if he never purifies. Panel two (vv. 8–10): the striver, fearful, who came to you — from whom you are diverted. The panels are set in deliberate inversion, each phrase of one answered by a contrary phrase of the other, so that the misallocation stands exposed in the structure itself. The sūrah is not saying the notables should never be addressed — the Prophet ﷺ continued to call the mighty to God all his life, and the Qur'an commands it. The correction concerns *priority of presence* when the two audiences compete for the same hour: the one who comes seeking has, by his coming, an immediate claim; the one who must be courted has a standing offer. Modern religious and institutional life reproduces the Meccan scene with uncanny fidelity — the flagship program for the influential donor, the "VIP outreach," the professional whose calendar is structured entirely around prospects, while the walk-in, the caller, the needy member is triaged into invisibility. Verse 5 names the gravitational pull that causes all of it — the company of the self-sufficient feels like leverage, and leverage feels like the mission. The next two verses will dismantle that equation entirely.

**The Only True Ghanī**

Finally, the verse hides a door into one of the Qur'an's most tender doctrines. The root gh-n-y, used here of man's laughable self-estimate, is one of God's own names: *al-Ghanī*, the Self-Sufficient, the Rich beyond need — *"O mankind, you are the poor toward God; and God, He is the Rich, the Praiseworthy"* (35:15). The verse's quiet claim is therefore this: there is exactly one Self-Sufficient Being in the universe, and every creature who reaches for that title is engaged in a kind of identity theft. The tragedy of istighnāʾ is not merely that the self-sufficient man is wrong; it is that he is wrong in the direction that costs him everything: by refusing to be a needy servant before the only Ghanī, he ends up needy indeed — and the sūrah will seal the irony thirty-two verses later, when on the Day of the Piercing Cry *"every man's own affair shall suffice him"* (80:37) — *yughnīhi*, from this same root gh-n-y. The man who *deemed* himself self-sufficient (istaghnā) will discover that the only real sufficiency (*ighnāʾ*) on that Day is one's own record. The root that named his delusion will name the truth that replaces it.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:6

> **to him dost thou attend,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taṣaddā: Leaning the Whole Self Toward**

The verb God chooses for the Prophet's ﷺ engagement with the self-sufficient chiefs is *taṣaddā* — "thou attendest," from the root ṣ-d-w/ṣ-d-y, which carries the sense of turning and stretching toward a thing, giving it one's face and full presence; the form is intensive and voluntary: you set yourself toward him. The picture the Arabic draws is almost physical — a man angling his entire body, gaze, and energy in one direction, at the cost of everything outside that arc. That is exactly what the occasion describes: the Prophet ﷺ, face toward the notables, back and shoulder turned to the blind man — leaning into the conversation that might win Makkah. Al-Ṭabarī glosses the word with exactly this sense of deliberate orientation: to make something one's qiblah, so to speak, for the hour. It is worth letting the force of the verb land: taṣaddā is not a sin-word. It is an attention-word. The sūrah's genius is to show that the theater of moral choice is often not the deed but the *direction of attention* — and that attention, spent anywhere, is spent *from* somewhere else. The blind man standing outside the arc of the Prophet's ﷺ leaning was not the victim of an aggression; he was the casualty of an allocation.

**The Motive Was Noble — and the Correction Still Stands**

Justice to the Prophet ﷺ requires hearing, with the classical commentators, what his leaning was *for*. This was not a lapse into flattery or love of power. The reports of the occasion say he was calling the chiefs to Islam and hoping for their belief; the strategic prayer behind the hope was the one that any responsible leader would pray — if these men entered, their followers would enter with them, and a door of mass-guidance would swing open that no other door could replace. The Qur'an itself records how sincerely the Prophet ﷺ longed even for individuals to be saved: *"Thou wouldst nearly kill thyself with grief over their footsteps, lest they believe not this discourse"* (18:6); and God had to gentle him: *"Perhaps thou wilt kill thyself with grief that they will not be believers"* (26:3). So verse 6 does not indict a corrupt motive; it corrects a *calculus*. And here is where the verse becomes permanently useful, because most of the misallocated attention in the world — in religious work, education, philanthropy, even parenting — flows from motives exactly this defensible. The danger of a good strategic motive is that it makes misallocation *feel like duty*. The teacher who gives all his office hours to the gifted students who will make his program famous; the imam whose hospitality is indexed to donor capacity; the parent whose face lights for the accomplished child and dims for the struggling one — each is leaning toward the future he can *see*, and each needs the same correction delivered to the Prophet ﷺ: the future you can see is not the only future there is, and the claim in front of you is not suspended while you pursue it.

**God's Counter-Economics: The Overlooked Are the Lever**

What makes the correction more than a matter of etiquette is the economic history that followed it. God's implicit argument in vv. 5–10 is that the Prophet's ﷺ strategy rested on a false estimate of where guidance's leverage actually lay. The chiefs, courted for their influence, almost to a man died in unbelief or fought the message to their last breath; the blind man, neglected for one moment, became a teacher of the Qur'an, the voice of Madinah's dawn, a deputy of its governor, and a martyr with the standard in his hand. Islam's history repeated this pattern so consistently that it must be read as a law of the religion's spread: the community was built not by the capture of elites but by the fidelity of the overlooked — the slaves, the poor, the foreigners, the young. The Quraysh themselves named the pattern with contempt and thereby documented it: *"Is it these upon whom God has bestowed favor among us?"* (6:53). And God answered through His Prophet ﷺ in the two sūrahs that bracket this one historically — commanding him never to expel the humble seekers to please the mighty:

> *"And drive not away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face. Naught of their account falls upon thee, and naught of thy account falls upon them, that thou shouldst drive them away and become of the wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 6:52)

Verse 6 of ʿAbasa is thus not merely about one hour in one gathering; it is about a whole theory of influence. The world's theory says: win the strong, and the weak will follow. God's observed economics in history says: honor the hungry, and the strong will one day come asking — as indeed the masters of Makkah came, years later, as suppliants into the faith of the servants they had mocked.

**The Gravity of Status and the Discipline of the Face**

There is also a plain psychological law embedded in the verse that every human being lives under: status exerts gravity. Faces of power draw attention without effort; faces of need repel it just as silently. Sociologists measuring conversational gaze find it within minutes; every waiter knows which table "matters." The Qur'an does not deny the gravity — it legislates against its autopilot. The Prophet ﷺ, corrected once in Makkah, spent a lifetime demonstrating the counter-gravity, and the reports of his gatherings show a man who arranged his own attention by criteria no status-meter could read: he gave his lap to his grandchildren in assembly, turned fully toward whoever spoke to him, and was never described as receiving the poor with less face than the rich. The Companion ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, a man who had known the highest councils of the world before Islam, was asked later what the Prophet ﷺ was like and said words to this effect:

> *"I have never seen anyone give me his full attention as he did; as though I were the most important person in the room — so completely did he listen to me."* (the famous report of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ's impression, preserved in the sīrah literature)

That full-turning was not charisma; it was obedience to the lesson of this sūrah, practiced until it became second nature.

**Attending to the Powerful Is Not Forbidden — It Is Reframed**

Precision matters, because the verse has been misread as forbidding outreach to the influential. It does not. The Prophet ﷺ kept calling kings and chiefs; the Qur'an praises those of wealth and power who are just. What vv. 5–7 govern is the *order of operations* when presence is contested, and the *independence of the message from the audience's favor*. The next verse will make the independence explicit: your attending to the self-sufficient man carries no obligation for him to purify, and no fault of yours if he does not. In other words: court the powerful as *recipients of the message*, never as *lords of the message*; offer them the reminder with the same face you offer the beggar, and never let their presence purchase the neglect of the beggar's claim. The moment the messenger's face begins to function as a market — attention sold to the highest bidder — the message itself is devalued, for everyone watching can see that what is being preached is a Lord whom the preacher does not actually fear. An audience takes its estimate of the message from the messenger's estimate of his audiences. That, in the end, is what verse 6 protects: not only the blind man's claim, but the credibility of the Book in whose name the face was turned.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:7

> **though thou art not answerable, should he not be purified.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Double Negative That Carries a Whole Theology**

The seventh verse releases the Prophet ﷺ — and every teacher after him — from a burden he was never assigned: *wa mā ʿalayka allā yazzakkā* — "and it is not upon thee that he should not be purified." The Arabic is a beautifully compressed double negative: literally, "it is not incumbent upon you that he not purify himself" — that is, his refusal to be purified is not laid to your account. The construction assumes the very outcome the Prophet ﷺ was working to prevent: the self-sufficient man may hear every word of the invitation and remain exactly as he is — and if he does, the ledger of that outcome is his, not yours. The verse thereby completes the correction begun in v. 6 with a liberation: stop *leaning on* his conversion as if it were your responsibility to force; attend, convey, remind — and then release the result to God. Al-Ṭabarī draws the principle out plainly: the messenger's duty is the *delivery* (balāgh) and the *clarification* (bayān); the *acceptance* (qabūl) is a separate act, performed by a separate will, in a freedom God Himself chose to create and refuses to override.

**The Qur'an's Own Commentary on This Release**

That this is the Qur'an's settled doctrine — not a local consolation — is proven by how many times and how emphatically it is stated across the revelation, in both Makkan and Madinan sūrahs, in nearly identical words:

> *"Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but God guides whomsoever He wills; and He knows best those who are rightly guided."* (Qur'an 28:56)

> *"It is not for thee their guidance; rather God guides whomsoever He wills."* (Qur'an 2:272)

> *"So remind — thou art only a reminder. Thou art not a master over them."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

The first of these was revealed, the commentators report, over the Prophet's ﷺ aching for his beloved uncle Abū Ṭālib — the man who had shielded him from the clans of Makkah his whole mission long, and who nonetheless died clinging to the religion of his fathers. If ever a soul's non-acceptance could have been charged to insufficient love in the messenger, it was that one; and God's answer was verse 28:56: guidance belongs to Me. ʿAbasa 80:7 was the first installment of that answer, and its placement is surgically precise: it stands directly between the portrait of the courted notable (vv. 5–6) and the portrait of the neglected striver (vv. 8–10), teaching that the messenger is free from the *outcome* of the first precisely so that he can be free *for* the duty of the second. The teacher who has released results can never again justify neglecting one seeker in the name of securing another.

**What This Verse Is Not: A License for Indifference**

The verse must be read with both hands, because each half balances a danger. Read alone, "you are not answerable for his purification" could curdle into an alibi: *why preach to the hard cases at all?* The Qur'an forecloses that reading everywhere else it speaks — the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to convey to *all*, gently, wisely, with beautiful counsel (16:125); he is never permitted to write anyone off as beyond the reach of mercy, for *"God guides whomsoever He wills"* keeps every door formally open, and He forgives all things (39:53). What is removed from the messenger is *accountability for the other's choice*; what is never removed is the *duty of the offer*. The distinction is the entire ethics of daʿwah in one line: exhaust the offer, release the outcome. In the modern language of responsibility ethics: one is responsible for one's *effort*, not for the world's *response*. This is also, incidentally, the only stable psychological foundation for work whose success rates are heartbreaking — teaching, medicine in its losing battles, parenting prodigals, relief work, advocacy. The alternatives are two kinds of collapse: despair (blaming oneself for others' refusals) or burnout-driven coercion (trying to force the purification the verse says is not one's to force — in a parent's dictatorship, a teacher's contempt, a preacher's manipulation). Verse 7 forbids both by relocating the accountability: the offer is yours; the acceptance is his; the outcome is God's.

**A Word About the Reading Variants**

The classical commentators preserved a small and instructive difference in the recitation of this verse. In the reading of the ḥafṣ tradition the verb is *yazzakkā* — "purify himself," reflexive, in parallel with v. 3 — while another canonical reading gives *yazzakā* — "be purified," or "accept purification" — shifting the accent slightly from the man's own inner work to his *receiving* of purification from the reminder. Ibn Kathīr records both without alarm, and the two readings illuminate each other: purification in the Qur'an is neither a self-generated achievement nor a passively received implant, but the meeting of a divine offer with a human taking-hold. *Yazzakā* guards against the Pelagian error (man purifying himself by his own bootstraps); *yazzakkā* guards against the fatalist error (man waiting for purification as one waits for weather). The Qur'an's full doctrine holds both: *"He it is who purifies whom He wills"* — and the servant's taking of the means is itself part of what He wills.

**The Freedom of the Messenger and the Freedom of the Message**

There is one more yield from this verse, easily missed: the sheer *dignity* it gives to unbelief's possibility. God could have made acceptance inevitable; He made it free. That freedom is what makes the whole scene of vv. 1–10 intelligible — and what gives a Teacher the right to be gentle. The Prophet ﷺ could court the chiefs without despair and welcome the blind man without condescension, because neither man's soul was his to own; he was the courier of an offer, not the custodian of wills. And the message itself gains something from that arrangement: an offer freely refusable is an offer that can be freely accepted — faith coerced would be reportage; faith chosen is worship. The Qur'an stakes this explicitly:

> *"Had thy Lord willed, all who are on the earth would have believed together. Wouldst thou then compel men, until they are believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

Verse 7 of ʿAbasa is that cosmic arrangement stated in pastoral terms: teach the self-sufficient man, love his guidance, grieve his refusal — and lay the outcome where it belongs, at the door of the only One who owns hearts. The messenger who has truly internalized this verse becomes unblackmailable by results and unpoisoned by flattery — free to turn, at last, toward the man who came striving, which is precisely where the sūrah turns next.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:8

> **But as for him who came to thee striving earnestly,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivot of the Sūrah: "But As For Him Who Came"**

Verse 8 completes the two-panel portrait with its second and beloved half: *wa ammā man jāʾaka yaʿṣā* — "but as for him who came to thee, striving." The construction mirrors v. 5 exactly — *ammā man... fa-anta...* — so that the two men are grammatically weighed in the same scales, phrase against phrase. The self-sufficient one: you leaned toward him. The striver: he came to you — and (as v. 10 will state with a sting) you were turned away from him. The symmetry is the argument: identical syntax, inverted realities. Everything the Qur'an wants corrected is visible in the grammatical mirror itself. And with the word *jāʾaka* — "came to thee" — the sūrah quietly establishes its social doctrine once more: the movement of the seeker toward the source is the honored movement; the man who crosses the room with a need for God outranks, in the economy of this Book, every man who must be fetched.

**Yaʿṣā: The Verb of Straining Toward**

The participle *yaʿṣā* is a lexical treasure. Its root, ʿ-ṣ-w, means to strive, strain, and press eagerly toward something with the whole body's desire — the Arab lexicons illustrate it with the image of a child straining and wriggling to reach something it wants, and with the camel hastening with lowered head toward the watering place. It is a word of *bodily* eagerness: not calm interest, but leaning, pushing, unable-to-wait wanting. Ibn Umm Maktūm did not stroll into the gathering; he pressed in — blind, navigating by voice, repeating his request, physically insistent in exactly the way the root describes. The Qur'an rarely uses physical handicap as a moral badge; here it does. The man whose eyes could not aim his body still aimed it, by effort, at the one place on earth where the revelation was being taught. That is yaʿṣā.

A linguistic warning belongs here, because the word is a trap for the careless reader: *yaʿṣā* in this verse is *not* the more famous verb ʿaṣā, "to disobey" (root ʿ-ṣ-y), though the two are written with the same consonants in unpointed script. The early Arabic homograph conceals two opposite worlds: the ʿ-ṣ-y of rebellion, and the ʿ-ṣ-w of eager striving. The context — and the canonical recitation — point decisively to striving: the man who came was not disobeying; he was *straining*. Some of the earlyexegetes even paused on the irony of the shared spelling: in the same letters that can spell "disobedience" the Qur'an chose "eagerness," as though to mark how near the raw material of rebellion lies to redeemed desire when it is aimed aright.

**The Two Marks of the True Seeker**

Set verses 8–9 side by side and they deliver a complete phenomenology of the genuine student: *yaʿṣā* — striving, energetic, pressing in; *wa huwa yakhshā* — and while fearing, reverent. Two marks, neither sufficient alone. Effort without reverence becomes ambition — the career student, the credential collector, the one who consumes knowledge as power. Reverence without effort becomes passivity — the one who "respects" sacred knowledge so deeply that he never actually struggles to acquire it. The Qur'an's seeker has both: the straining of the camel and the hush of the worshipper; heat in the feet and awe in the chest. The classical scholars of Islamic learning enshrined this pairing in the very etiquette of study: the student travels (effort) and sits humbly (reverence); he questions (effort) and never to compete (reverence); he memorizes through the night (effort) and begins with the basmalah (reverence). The Prophet ﷺ made the promise attached precisely to the effort-side:

> *"Whoever travels a path seeking knowledge, God will make easy for him a path to Paradise."* (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah)

And he named the reverence-side the very condition of benefit:

> *"He is not of us who does not honor our elders, show mercy to our young, and recognize the right of our scholars."* (al-Ḥākim, from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit; graded ḥasan by the tradition's scholars)

**Ibn Umm Maktūm's Striving Read Backward Through His Life**

The word yaʿṣā was, in the event, a prophecy. Everything known of Ibn Umm Maktūm's later life confirms that the striving of that afternoon in Makkah was not an exception but a character. This was a man who left his city as one of the first two emigrants to Madinah — undertaking, blind, a migration that sighted men were refusing. He taught the Qur'an to Madinah's community in the mosque of the Prophet ﷺ himself. He raised the call to prayer beside Bilāl, twice a day at dawn's edge, for years. He governed Madinah in the Prophet's ﷺ absences. And when the verse of exemption excused the disabled from fighting, he refused the ease of it — he asked instead for the standard, with the argument his blindness itself supplied: "I am blind and cannot run away." Al-Qurṭubī preserves the account of his end: found among the fallen at al-Qādisiyyah, his hand still around the flag. Striving — ʿaṣw — is the through-line of the whole biography. The verse thus teaches by example what the hadith teaches by precept: the value God places on the person of *effort*. Even his one great disadvantage became, in his own deployment of it, an argument for usefulness — "place me between two rows; I cannot flee" is perhaps the most quietly devastating sentence of self-assessment in Islamic history: a man converting his liability into a qualification.

**The Seeker's Claim and the Teacher's Face**

Finally, the verse establishes the practical ethics that the first ten verses exist to teach. When a person comes *striving* — visibly eager, pressing, perhaps slightly too insistently for polished taste — the verse instructs the bearer of knowledge to recognize in that very insistence the mark of the soil the reminder will benefit. Social polish and spiritual hunger are different qualities, and the teacher must not price the second by the first. Every experienced teacher knows the type: the awkward, intense, repetitive student who is quietly the most serious person in the room. The temptation to find such a person tiresome is precisely the temptation v. 8 was revealed to disarm — for the original "tiresome" interruption is now, fourteen centuries later, one of the most beloved figures of the prophetic biography, his every subsequent honor flowing from the divine correction recorded here. The sūrah's counsel can be stated in one sentence: *when someone comes straining toward the truth you carry, drop the ledger of status you are holding, and turn your face fully around* — the next verse will describe what such a seeker brings with him, and the verse after will name the fault of the face that does not.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:9

> **while fearful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yakhshā: The Fear That Elevates**

The second mark of the true seeker, named in a single Arabic participle: *wa huwa yakhshā* — "and he, while fearing." The root kh-sh-y denotes fear mixed with reverence — a fear that does not flee its object but draws near to it carefully, as one fears a king one loves, or as one walks carefully in a temple one adores. It is the fear of *standing before what is ultimate*, and the Qur'an reserves it for a particular class of person with a famous verse:

> *"Truly, those of God's servants who fear Him are the learned (al-ʿulamāʾ)."* (Qur'an 35:28)

The claim is quietly enormous: khashyah is the *product* of knowledge — the more one truly knows, the more precisely one fears, because fear here is not terror of the unknown but awe of the accurately known. Ibn Umm Maktūm, blind, poor, and early to the faith, is depicted by this word as already in possession of the very thing the scholarly life aims at: a heart that trembles in the right place. The contrast with the other panel is exact and deliberate. The self-sufficient chief of vv. 5–7 fears nothing — his wealth has anesthetized him; the blind striver fears God — and his fear is his credential. In the Qur'an's economy, that single asymmetry outweighs every asymmetry of the world's economy.

**Two Fears, Two Masters**

The scene of the sūrah puts two kinds of fear on display without naming the second, and the reader is meant to see them both. The chiefs of Quraysh feared loss — of face, of following, of the ancestors' religion's social order; that fear kept them seated in their pride, unable to admit that a message carried by the poor had claims on them. Ibn Umm Maktūm feared God — and that fear made him cross the room, humble himself, repeat his request, and endure a frown he could not even see in order to obtain what his soul needed. The Prophet ﷺ taught the choice of fears to his Companions in a single counsel:

> *"Fear God wherever you are, and follow a wrong with a good that will erase it, and treat people with beautiful character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, from Abū Dharr; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The chiefs could not risk Makkah's opinion; the blind man risked a frown from the one man in the city he most revered. The psychology of the two fears is one of the oldest and most stable observations in moral experience: fear of men shrinks the soul to the size of its audience, while fear of God frees it from every audience. One fear made cowards of the sightless-hearted; the other made a hero of the sightless-eyed.

> *"Fear God wherever you are, and follow a wrong with a good that will erase it, and treat people with beautiful character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, from Abū Dharr; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

**Fear as the Doorway, Not the Destination**

Theological honesty requires the balance the tradition itself always kept: fear (khawf) in Islam is one wing of a bird whose other wing is hope (rajāʾ), and the bird flies on both. The Qur'an describes its ideal servants as calling on their Lord *"in desire and in fear"* (7:56), and the Prophet ﷺ saw a man so terrified he could not pray and taught him — and through him everyone — to balance: "Say: O God, I ask You for steadiness..." Yet the sūrah's placement of khashyah here is exact: fear is the *entry* posture of the seeker, and the divine curriculum knows why. Before the remedy can be received, the disease must be felt; before the reminder can benefit, the heart must admit its exposure. This is not unique to religion — every serious undertaking begins in a salutary fear: the medical intern's fear of error makes them careful; the pilot's checklist-reverence keeps passengers alive. Fear is the mind's acknowledgment of stakes. What the Qur'an adds is the *object* at which such fear aims most truthfully: not the examiner one can fool, but the One from whom nothing is hidden — *"He knows what deceives the eyes and what the breasts conceal"* (40:19). And for the believer who lets that fear in, the trajectory of the sūrah itself shows where it ends: the last movement of ʿAbasa displays faces *"shining, radiant, laughing, joyous"* (80:38–39). Fear walked in the door first; laughter sits at the end of the table. The tradition summarized it: the one who does not fear on the road does not arrive — and the one who does not hope at the end despairs of arriving.

**The Fearful Seeker as the Teacher's Easiest Duty**

The verse also completes the practical instruction to the teacher begun in v. 8. The striving (yaʿṣā) supplies the seeker's energy; the fear (yakhshā) supplies his receptivity. Together they describe what every bearer of knowledge should scan for in a face: the person who both *wants* to learn and *trembles* at what learning touches is the person for whom the reminder's benefit is nearest — vv. 3–4 said exactly this: he would purify himself, or be reminded, *and the reminder would benefit him*. In other words, the sūrah's two portraits are not arbitrary; the second man is drawn as the living embodiment of the conditions of the first paragraph's hopes. The blind man *was* the "perhaps he would purify himself" of v. 3 — the perhaps made flesh, standing right there, waiting. And the Prophet ﷺ was, for one moment, attending to the man in whom those hopes had no purchase, while the man in whom they had every purchase stood behind him. That is the entire tragedy of vv. 1–10 in one sentence, and v. 9 is its hinge: the one who came *fearful* was the one the Book had already declared teachable.

**A Modern Iconography of Khashyah**

Translate the word into the present and it loses none of its edge. Khashyah is the opposite of the default modern posture before knowledge: the scrolling consumer of content, sampling wisdom like a buffet, criticizing what cost nothing to approach. The fearful seeker of v. 9 stands before sacred knowledge the way one stands before a living Teacher rather than a dead screen: with the heart's shoes removed. In practical terms, the verse's person can still be recognized today — the student who is silent long enough to hear the whole answer; the questioner whose voice slows down in the presence of what is greater than his problem; the reader of the Qur'an who pauses, as the Book itself commands at its own recitation: *"And when the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent, that haply you may receive mercy"* (7:204). What khashyah adds to the modern mind's model of learning is the dimension without which sacred knowledge does not *function*, however well it is *stored*: the awareness that some truths change the one who receives them, and must therefore be received on one's feet, hat in hand, heart awake. The sūrah's blind man received a frown and kept his fear of God intact; the reader of the sūrah is invited to inherit both — his khashyah, and, through the correction the incident purchased, a teacher's face that turns all the way around.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:10

> **from him thou art diverted.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tatahallā: The Grammar of Self-Distraction**

The final stroke of the portrait is its most stinging: *fa-anta ʿanhu tatahallā* — "from him thou art diverted," or with the intensive Arabic form fully rendered: "thou lettest thyself be drawn away from him." The verb is reflexive-intensive: not a passive "you were distracted" that would scatter the blame onto circumstances, but a form that keeps the agency — awkwardly, personally — in the second person: *you* busied yourself away from him. The root l-h-w/lahw is the Qur'an's standard word for play, diversion, idle amusement — the very word in *"the life of this world is but play and diversion (lahw)"* (6:32; 29:64; 47:36). The choice is quietly devastating. The Prophet's ﷺ engagement with the Quraysh chiefs was, in his own intention, high strategic work — the most serious of outreach; and God names the *form* of it, measured against the claim of the seeker standing behind him, with the vocabulary of diversion. What looked like the main task was, from the vantage of the man God had sent, the lahw — the thing one allows to pull the face away. The verse thereby delivers a doctrine of priorities sharp enough to cut in every century: an activity is not classified by its self-description but by what it displaces. The nobler the errand, the more easily it launders the diversion; nobody is diverted from the sick by crime — it is always by something defensible.

**The Completed Chiasm: Two Men, Two Treatments, One Misplacement**

Now the full architecture of vv. 5–10 can be read at a glance, and the early commentators delighted in its mirror-work:

- The self-sufficient one — who did *not* come, and *cannot* be purified by your effort (v. 7) — receives your attending (v. 6).
- The striver and fearer — who *did* come, and is the very man the reminder would benefit (vv. 3–4) — receives your turned shoulder (v. 10).

Every term is misplaced by exactly one position: the obligation has been given to the option, and the option has been given the obligation's face. The Qur'an does not add a single word of accusation; it lets the two panels stand in their frames and lets the reader feel the disproportion unaided. This restraint is itself teaching: the most effective correction is often not a verdict but an arrangement of facts — here, ten verses in perfect symmetry — after which the accused supplies his own verdict. It is how God corrected His Prophet ﷺ, and it is the model of correction for every teacher, parent, and leader since: arrange the truth so it can be seen, and leave the conscience its work.

**What Was Actually Lost in the Moment**

For all its brevity, the phrase ʿanhu — "from him" — invites a meditation the sūrah has engineered: what, concretely, was lost when the face turned? Not, as we noted at v. 2, the man's feelings — he never saw the frown. What was lost was the *hour of teaching*: the Qur'an that would have been recited to him, the verses he would have carried away, the purification and benefit of vv. 3–4 placed on hold. Ibn Kathīr and the occasion-literature emphasize exactly this: the man was asking for Qur'an — "teach me, recite to me" — while the Prophet's ﷺ effort was bent on men who were asking for nothing. The economics of the moment are the economics of every moment of teaching since: attention is the only currency a teacher actually spends, and its allocation is the entire ethics of the profession. One might say the sūrah's first ten verses are a treatise on the moral theology of *presence*: God does not merely judge what His servants do; He judges whom their faces were turned toward while they did it — for the face turned toward one human being is, for that moment, the face of God's hospitality turned toward him, or the back of it.

**The Divine Bookkeeping of Micro-Gestures**

Stand back and consider what the first ten verses establish about God's attention: He noticed a frown that lasted seconds, in a private gathering, directed at a man who could not perceive it — and He answered it with revelation. Nothing in the moral universe, the sūrah implies, is beneath the register of heaven; there is no gesture so small that it is unrecorded. The Qur'an says this in its own voice elsewhere: *"Not an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth escapes Him, nor anything smaller than that or greater, but it is in a manifest Book"* (34:3; also 10:61). And the reverse doctrine, the encouraging twin: no kindness is too small to be seen and rewarded — the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face."* (Muslim 2626, from Abū Dharr)

A religion in which a frown is weighed is a religion in which a smile is weighed — and both are found in the scale. This is far more psychologically serious than it first appears. Human beings calibrate their behavior to what they believe is being *observed*; the sūrah's doctrine of total divine observation recalibrates everything: the moment when no one important is watching becomes the most honest measure of character, and the treatment of the person who cannot see your face becomes the truest reading of your face. Ibn Umm Maktūm could not see; God could. The verse asks every reader the question the scene poses: how do you treat the person whose only witness is heaven?

**The Turn That History Made**

The correction, as the reports record, was received whole. The Prophet ﷺ never again is described as turning from Ibn Umm Maktūm; the record runs the other direction — the spread cloak, the warm greeting ("Welcome to him on whose account my Lord rebuked me"), the muezzin's post, the deputy-governorship of Madinah, and the community's permanent reverence for the man of this incident. And the Companions drew the universal lesson immediately: the early Muslims were famously indistinguishable in their gatherings between the wealthy and the enslaved — a leveling so offensive to Meccan custom that the chiefs made it an explicit condition for sitting with the Prophet ﷺ: drive away these wretches, they demanded, and God answered with Sūrah al-Kahf's command (*"Let not thine eyes pass beyond them..."* — 18:28) and Sūrah al-Anʿām's (*"Drive not away those who call upon their Lord..."* — 6:52). ʿAbasa 80:1–10 is the narrative seed of which those commands are the law: the community that resulted — in which Bilāl the freedman and Abū Bakr the merchant stood in the same row — was the visible fruit. The verse's final word to every later reader is the one with which the section closes: *fa-anta ʿanhu tatahallā* — thou lettest thyself be diverted from him. It is the last of ten verses in which the word "thou" appears; from the next verse to the sūrah's end, the address broadens to mankind. The lesson of the face has been delivered; the lesson of the Book, the drop, the rain, and the Piercing Cry now begins.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:11

> **Nay! Truly it is a reminder—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā: The Qur'an's Sound of the Braking Mind**

Verse 11 opens with *kallā* — "Nay!" — one of the most distinctive words of the early Makkan revelation. It is a particle of repulsion and redirection: it pushes away what has just been said or assumed, like a hand raised against a mistaken thought. It appears over thirty times in the Qur'an, overwhelmingly in the later (revealed-earlier) Makkan sūrahs — al-Nabaʾ, al-Nāziʿāt, al-Muṭaffifīn — always at a hinge where a false assumption must be broken before a truth can be set in place. Here the scholars differ over precisely what is being negated, and the classical options are three. Al-Ṭabarī leans toward reading kallā as turning from the whole scene of vv. 5–10: enough of that misallocation — the matter is otherwise than you were arranging it. Others read it as breaking the thought that the Prophet ﷺ might be free to allocate his effort by the world's criteria at all — nay, the truth is different. And a third line, which al-Qurṭubī records, notes that some reciters and scholars read the word here not as repulsion but as affirmation — in the sense of "truly" (*bal* in the reading attributed to some of the Kufans for certain occurrences, and noted for this very verse by Ibn Kathīr) — so that the sentence becomes "Truly, it is a reminder," a solemn attestation rather than a rebuke. The two readings do not fight; kallā both breaks the old frame and sets the new one, and the verse stands either way as the announcement: the subject is now the message itself.

**Innahā Tadhkirah: "It Is a Reminder" — The Message Defines the Mission**

What is the "it"? The feminine pronoun *innahā* — "truly it" — has its antecedent debated with care by the commentators, precisely because the answer defines the Prophet's ﷺ whole office. Al-Ṭabarī's principal reading: the pronoun refers to the sūrah itself — these very verses are "a reminder." Others: to the Qur'an as a whole; others again: to the exhortation just delivered in vv. 1–10 — the lesson of the blind man being itself a reminder to be carried to mankind; and still others, looking ahead, to the honored pages of v. 13 — the ṣaḥīfah, a feminine noun, standing for the revealed writ as such. The convergence beneath the options is what matters: whatever the pronoun's exact antecedent, the message is defined by its function, and its function is *reminding*. Not philosophizing, not legislating first of all, not entertaining — reminding. The Qur'an's most frequent self-designation is exactly this word-family: *dhikr*, remembrance — as when God swears by the Book itself:

> *"Ṣād. By the Qur'an possessing the reminder (dhī al-dhikr)."* (Qur'an 38:1)

And as He promises its preservation in terms of the same name:

> *"Truly We sent down the Reminder, and truly We are its Guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

A reminder presupposes that the truth being delivered is, at some level, already the hearer's own — forgotten, overlaid, buried, but not foreign. The Qur'an's anthropology is that God's lordship and the soul's need of it are not news to the human being so much as *old news mislaid*: *"So set thy face to the religion, upright — the nature (fiṭrah) of God upon which He created mankind; no change in God's creation"* (30:30). The reminder is the reactivation of the fiṭrah, and this is why its tone, throughout the Book, is so often "remember," "recall," "take heed" — and why its surest condemnation falls not on ignorance but on the covering-over of what one knew: *"And they rejected them, though their souls were certain of them, in wrongdoing and insolence"* (27:14).

**The Reminder as Answer to the Incident**

Place the definition against the scene it answers, and the verse's pastoral genius shows. The Prophet ﷺ had been dividing mankind by his own strategic estimate — worth the face here, not worth it there — and God replies by naming the one thing every human being has in common: everyone is a forgetter; therefore everyone is a candidate for the reminder. The self-sufficient chief is not outside the reminder's brief — he too once knew, and covers; the blind striver is not the reminder's only rightful audience — he is simply its readiest one. The correction of vv. 1–10 thus culminates not in a new ranking of audiences (striver above chief) but in the dissolution of the whole ranking-game: the message is a reminder, and the reminder'sAddressee is man as man. The Prophet's ﷺ office is to be its courier to all comers, with the only priority the sūrah established — the one who comes asking is the one the hour belongs to. Ibn Kathīr, reading this verse against the occasion, draws exactly this conclusion: the verses teach the Messenger that his task is *reminding and conveying*, while guidance's outcome rests with God — as the Qur'an says plainly in the sister-sūrah of the same period:

> *"Thou dost not govern them; but God guides whomsoever He wills."* — and, in Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah's perfect parallel: *"So remind — thou art only a reminder."* (Qur'an 88:21)

**Why "Reminder" Is Also a Name of God**

There is a final dimension the tradition never let pass unremarked: the word dhikr names not only the Book but the act of worship it commands — and, by extension, the entire life of consciousness of God that Islam calls *the life of dhikr*. The Qur'an orders: *"Remember God with much remembrance"* (33:41), and the Prophet ﷺ was asked which deed is best and answered:

> *"That your tongue stay moist with the remembrance of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3375, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Verse 11 stands at the head of all of this: it tells the believer what the Book is for — not a relic to be ornamented, not a document to be litigated, but an instrument of remembering, to be recited until its picture of reality becomes the mind's default picture. And in the same breath it tells the believer what he is: a creature whose highest act is not to invent but to *recall* — whose prayers begin with recitation, whose week is punctuated by the Friday reminder, whose very last rites, at the graveside, are the reminding of the dead man's God to the living. A religion that defines itself as reminder has made peace with the truth about its adherents: they will forget, they will forget again, and the mercy is that the reminder will keep returning. That is what the next verse will formalize — the reminder is left open, "for whoever wills to remember" — and what the verses after that will ground in the highest imaginable cosmology: pages honored, exalted, purified, in the hands of scribes noble and pious.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:12

> **so let whomsoever will, remember it—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-man Shāʾa: The Reminder Is Offered, Never Forced**

The twelfth verse completes the sentence begun in v. 11 and, in nine Arabic words, states the entire doctrine of religious freedom: *fa-man shāʾa dhakarahu* — "so let whomsoever will, remember it." The reminder has been sent; the door of it stands open; who walks through is left to the walker. The sūrah that began with God's meticulous concern for the treatment of one seeker now guards the seeker's other, equally sacred right — the right to refuse. These are the two panels of the Qur'an's religious liberty: no one who comes may be turned away (vv. 1–10), and no one who stays away may be compelled (v. 12). The Prophet ﷺ is the custodian of both halves — open door and open leave — and the two together define what Islamic daʿwah is at its root. The great Madinan charter-verse says the second half in legal language:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion; righteousness has become distinct from error."* (Qur'an 2:256)

And ʿAbasa 80:12 is its Makkan seed: the reminder is an offer, addressed to the will.

**The Grammar of the Masculine Pronoun — A Doorway Into Meaning**

The commentators noticed a grammatical subtlety here that becomes a window onto the verse's scope. In v. 11 the reminder is called *tadhkirah* — a feminine form — yet the pronoun of v. 12, *dhakarahu* ("remember it/him"), is masculine. Al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and others set out the resolutions: either the masculine pronoun reaches forward to the *ṣuḥuf* ("pages") of v. 13 and its implied Book — the written Qur'an being masculine in its names (*qur'ān, dhikr, kitāb*) even when a feminine epithet (tadhkirah, ṣaḥīfah) was used a moment before; or — the reading some of the early exegetes preferred — *dhakarahu* means "remember **Him**," that is, God: let whoever wills remember his Lord. The ambiguity, as the classical scholars were happy to note, is not a defect but a gift, for the two readings converge in the life of faith: to remember the Book rightly *is* to remember God — *"Recite what has been revealed to thee of the Book, and perform the prayer; truly the prayer restrains from indecency and wrong — and the remembrance of God is greater"* (29:45). And the grammatical detail carries the doctrine: whichever way the pronoun leans, the remembering is left to the one who wills.

**The Twin Verse in Sūrah al-Muddaththir**

The Qur'an states this exact principle once more, in nearly identical words, in a sūrah of the same early period — and its twin is worth setting beside it, because the twin adds the balancing truth:

> *"Nay! It is truly a reminder! So let whoever wills remember it — in honored pages, exalted and purified, by the hands of scribes, noble and pious."* (Qur'an 74:52–56, abridged to the parallel clauses) — where the very next verse continues: *"And they will not remember unless God wills; He is worthy of reverence, the Lord of forgiveness."* (Qur'an 74:56)

Read 80:11–12 beside 74:54–56 and the Qur'an interprets itself on its most contested question — the relation of human will to divine will. ʿAbasa gives the invitation's face: *whoever wills, let him remember*. Al-Muddaththir gives the same truth's depth: *they will not remember unless God wills*. The tradition refused, century after century, to amputate either half. Those who magnified human will alone fell into the ancient error that a creature's choice could be sovereign against its Maker; those who magnified divine will alone dissolved human responsibility and made the invitation of the prophets — and this very verse — meaningless noise. The Sunni settlement, articulated variously by al-Ashʿarī, al-Māturīdī, and with special clarity by Ibn Taymiyyah, held the marriage of the verses: God creates the willing and the deed; man truly wills and truly acts; the command addresses the human will as real, and the treasury of guidance's effect lies with the Real. An analogy the scholars used in various forms: a father who has cooked the feast, set the table, and opened the door says to his children, "whoever is hungry, come and eat." The invitation is genuine, and no child at that table is forced; and yet nothing on the table — not the food, not the appetite, not the strength to walk to the chair — exists except from the father's provision. The verse's "whoever wills" and the twin's "unless God wills" are the two hands of one invitation.

**The Dignity and Weight of Being Free**

Because the verse lodges remembering in the will, it transforms the moral situation of every hearer of the Qur'an. A compelled remembrance would be worthless — the value of dhikr lies precisely in its being freely chosen; and the liability of refusing it lies in the same freedom. The Qur'an presses this repeatedly: *"Indeed, We have guided him upon the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful"* (76:3); *"And shown him the two ways?"* (90:10); *"Indeed, We created man in the best stature, then returned him to the lowest of the low — save those who believe and do righteous deeds"* (95:4–6). The picture is of a creature standing at a junction with the map in hand. Modern readers may recognize the structure as the precondition of every meaningful human act: love that is programmed is not love, faith that is forced is not faith, and a God who wanted worship worth having had to create wills that could withhold it. Verse 12 is thus one of the Qur'an's most dignifying verses about man — it treats the reader as the free author of his remembering — and simultaneously one of its most sobering, for the same freedom makes refusal *his*. The sūrah's later verses will trace the consequences of both uses of the will: the one who "will not remember" becomes the *kafarah* and *fajarah* of v. 42; the one who wills becomes the radiant, laughing face of vv. 38–39.

**The Open Door as a Standing Policy**

Practically, the verse has always functioned as the constitution of the Muslim community's handling of its own message. The muezzin calls — and whoever wills comes; the mosque's door is unlatched; the Qur'an is recited aloud in assembly precisely because "whoever wills" cannot will to enter a door he has never seen open. The teacher is forbidden (by vv. 1–10) to shut the door on the seeker, and forbidden (by v. 12) to drag anyone through it. The modern reader, living amid both religious coercion and religious consumerism, can take the verse's balance as a diagnostic: any community that turns away the inconvenient seeker has violated the first panel; any community that abandons inviting because "it's up to them anyway" has violated the second. The Qur'an's policy is maximum offer with zero compulsion — the Prophet ﷺ summed up his own office in the same terms: convey on My behalf, even a single verse. The reminder's spread through history has followed exactly the verse's shape: offered freely, received variously, honored by some, ignored by many — and carried nonetheless, on to the next open will, and the next, until the end of time.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:13

> **on pages honored,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣuḥuf: The Reminder Is a Written Thing**

Having declared the message a reminder for whoever wills, the sūrah now grounds that reminder in a physical, describable form: *fī ṣuḥufin mukarramah* — "in pages honored." The word ṣuḥuf is the plural of ṣaḥīfah, from the root ṣ-ḥ-f, whose core meaning is to flatten, spread out, smooth — a ṣaḥīfah is a spread-flat sheet, a page, a scroll. The Qur'an uses the word for revelation in several registers: for the scriptures of the earlier prophets — *"Ṣuḥuf of Abraham and Moses"* (87:19; cf. 53:36) — and for the heavenly archetype of writ itself, as the classical commentators note here, linking the verse to the Preserved Tablet: *"In a Preserved Tablet (Lawḥ Maḥfūẓ)"* (85:22). The interpretive options for this verse are carefully laid out by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī: the pages are the Qur'an alone; or the writ of every prophet; or the mother-record of all revelation, the Tablet from which every scripture descends. What all readings share is the assertion that the reminder is not an abstraction, a mood, a philosophy drifting in the air — it is *written*. It has pages. It can be held, copied, carried, recited from, transmitted across centuries without dissolving. At the very moment the sūrah has insisted the Prophet's ﷺ task is only to remind and God's to guide, it anchors the reminder in a fixed text — the two doctrines together producing the distinctively Muslim synthesis: free hearts, fixed words. The reminder persuades whom it will; the pages, meanwhile, change not.

**Why Writing Matters: The Verse's Quiet Doctrine of Scripture**

That God chose to describe His final message as pages — in an Arabia of celebrated oral poets, before printing, before mass literacy — is a fact of the first importance for everything Islam became. The Qur'an's earliest descriptions of itself are insistently textual: it is a *kitāb*, a book — *"This is the Book in which there is no doubt"* (2:2) — and its first revealed command concerned reading and the pen: *"Read! And thy Lord is the Most Generous, who taught by the pen"* (96:3–4). The community took the cue with a seriousness unmatched in religious history: the Prophet's ﷺ scribes wrote revelation down as it came; the first caliphs gathered the written sheets (the ṣuḥuf — the very word of our verse — is what the collected leaves of the Qur'an were called before and during their compilation into one muṣḥaf); and the Islamic civilization that followed became, within a century, the most book-producing and manuscript-copying civilization of its age. The ʿUthmānic muṣḥaf stands today in near-identical recension from Indonesia to Morocco — the practical fruit of verses like this one, which taught the community to think of its message as *pages*, worthy of every care that honored paper deserves. When, in the tenth century, Cairo's al-Azhar and Baghdad's libraries held hundreds of thousands of volumes, they were the institutional descendants of *ṣuḥufin mukarramah*.

**Mukarramah: The Honor Is in the Handwriting of the Source**

The pages are called *mukarramah* — honored, ennobled, from the root k-r-m, nobility and generosity. What makes paper honored? Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr answer with one voice: the honor flows entirely from what the pages carry and from Him who sent them — the sheets of the Qur'an are intrinsically ordinary (parchment, ink) and functionally the most honored objects on earth, precisely because the divine speech rests on them. The Qur'an states the principle of its own rank elsewhere in exactly this vocabulary:

> *"Truly it is a noble Qur'an, in a hidden Book, which none touch save the purified."* (Qur'an 56:77–79)

And the Prophet ﷺ attached the same honor to the bearers of those pages, in the ḥadīth that will crown the discussion of vv. 15–16: the proficient reciter is *"with the scribes, noble and pious."* The logic of karamah here repays a moment's thought. In Meccan society, honor (karam) was the property of lineage and loot — the aristocracy of the chiefs the Prophet ﷺ had just been gently corrected for courting. Now, in the space of three verses, God relocates honor: away from faces born to rank, onto pages written by heaven and the noble scribes who bear them. The sūrah thus completes in cosmology the lesson it taught in etiquette: worth is not read from the tribe; it is written on the page. A religion whose sacred objects are honored sheets cannot, without self-contradiction, despise the humble human being who memorizes them — and the faith that emerged in Makkah made its first honored class precisely out of those the city had dishonored: Bilāl the freedman and Ibn Umm Maktūm the blind, the community's first reciters.

**The Preserved Tablet Reading: Pages Before Paper**

For the commentators who take the pages as the Preserved Tablet, the verse opens the doctrine of the Book's pre-existence — the Qur'an as descended, portion by portion, from a writ that preceded the world. The hadith of the divine decree gives the backdrop in a report al-Bukhārī and Muslim both carry from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr, in which the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"God wrote the decrees of created things fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth — and His Throne was upon the water."* (Muslim 2653)

The doctrine of the Lawḥ places every revealed page within a single architecture of writing: God writes the decree of creation; God writes the record of deeds (as the angels' pages, 43:80; 82:10–12); God wrote the Torah, the Gospel, and the Qur'an; and the final Book announces itself in glory — *"a blessed Book We have sent down to thee, that they may contemplate its signs"* (38:29). Human writing of revelation is thus always second penmanship: the transcription of a text that already exists above. This is why Muslims treat the physical muṣḥaf with the etiquette the verse implies — pure hands for the honored pages, no careless handling, no placing beneath other things, no disrespectful storage — not because paper is divine, but because the written reminder is the guest of the paper, and the host honors his guest.

**From Honored Pages to Honored Lives**

There is a final, homely application that Muslim devotion has always drawn from this verse: as the reminder is honored by what it carries, so is the human being. The heart that takes the reminder in becomes, in the Qur'an's own idiom, a bearer of something — *"God sent down the fairest discourse, a Book consimilar... guidance for the godfearing"* (39:23) — and the believer's own life becomes, as it were, additional pages on which the reminder is transcribed: pages of conduct, of speech, of treatment of the poor and the seeker, written daily. The sūrah's movement has been deliberate: it corrected the misuse of a face (vv. 1–10), then named the message (vv. 11–12), and now shows the message's lodging — honored pages in noble hands. The unstated question the sequence puts to every reader is simply this: whose hands, and whose life, will carry the pages next? The verse's two companion-clauses — *exalted and purified* (v. 14) and *in the hands of scribes, noble and pious* (vv. 15–16) — will now unfold the full dignity of what it means to hold, and to be, a carrier of the reminder.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:14

> **exalted and purified,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Marfūʿah: Elevation as Protection and Rank**

The fourteenth verse hangs two participial adjectives on the honored pages: first *marfūʿah* — "exalted, raised high," from the root r-f-ʿ, to lift, elevate, raise in rank. The word does double duty, and the commentators unpack both sides of it. In the first sense, the pages are high in station: raised above ordinary speech, above the claims of any human author, above every text that merely human ambition produced — as the Qur'an says of itself in a sister-verse of the same early period: *"in a hidden Book" (56:78) — and as God asks elsewhere: "With what discourse after God and His signs will they believe?"* The reminder is not one voice in a market of voices; it is the discourse before which markets fall silent. In the second sense, al-Qurṭubī and al-Bayḍāwī note, the elevation is protective: the pages are *out of reach* — lifted beyond tampering, beyond the reach of every hand that would grab and rewrite them. The image is of a document held high, not thrown on the table where anyone might mark it. Both senses converge in the doctrine this verse teaches a community that would carry a scripture through empires, persecutions, and centuries: the Book is above its bearers — never their possession to edit, always their possession to obey.

**Muṭahharah: Purity on the Side of the Text**

The second adjective is *muṭahharah* — "purified," from the root ṭ-h-r, purity, cleanliness, ritual soundness. The classical commentators give the word three linked applications here, and each is a doctrine. First, the pages are pure *in their content* — Ibn Kathīr: free from any addition or deficiency; nothing false has entered the text, and nothing true has fallen out of it. Second, they are pure *from assault* — al-Qurṭubī: guarded from being assailed by the disbelievers, from being corrupted by hostile hands. Third — the sense that has governed Muslim ritual practice ever since — they are pure *in their handling*, and this the Qur'an states explicitly in the parallel that every commentator on our verse cites:

> *"None touch it save the purified."* (Qur'an 56:79)

The Book itself, in other words, declares its own ritual boundary: it is approached in a state of purity (the ritual ablution, wuḍūʾ, for the muṣḥaf's formal touch, in the majority school's practice) — not because God's words need protection from dirt, but because the approaching human being needs to remember *what* he is approaching. The etiquette of handling the muṣḥaf is the page-level version of the sūrah's opening lesson: if even a moment's inattentive face toward a blind seeker required heaven's correction, how much more the manner of hands toward heaven's own pages.

**The Great Promise Attached to These Two Words**

Taken together, *marfūʿah* and *muṭahharah* are the Qur'an's self-description of its textual integrity, and God attached to that integrity the most unqualified preservation promise in scripture:

> *"Truly We sent down the Reminder, and truly We are its Guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

Muslim intellectual history has understood these verses as forecasting exactly what happened, and what did not happen. The Qur'an's transmission became the most rigorously verified textual tradition of the pre-modern world: the oral-memorial channel (the ḥuffāẓ, memorizers whose recitation was checked letter by letter against the written) running parallel to the written channel (the muṣḥaf copied under community verification), each correcting the other across fourteen centuries. In every generation there have been millions who carried the whole of it in memory — a fact without parallel for a book of its length — and the recitations from Morocco to Malaysia agree down to the vowel. The comparison the tradition itself drew: the earlier prophets' communities guarded their books with human hands alone, and the texts' histories show the results; the final Book announced that its Author would guard it, and its textual history reads as the receipt of that announcement. Non-Muslim textual scholars contest details of this narrative, but the believing reader of ʿAbasa 80:14 stands on the verse's own ground: elevated out of reach, and purified from corruption.

**Elevation and Purity as a Program for the Book's Bearers**

The verse has always been read as more than information about pages — it is a job description for the community of the pages. Whatever the Book is, its bearers must become. If the pages are *marfūʿah* — raised above tampering — then the bearer's relation to the Book must be one of reverence rather than revision: the believer does not stand over the text as its editor, but under it as its student; the modern temptation to rework the Book to fit the century is precisely the lowered hand this verse lifts the text away from. And if the pages are *muṭahharah* — purified — then the bearer's own purification is the other half of the Book's plan, for the reminder benefits, as v. 4 has said, the one who *takes it in*; and the Qur'an names its own function as the purifier: *"He it is who sent upon His servant the Criterion"* (25:1) — the furqān that separates, in the soul of its hearer, what belongs to God from what belongs to falsehood. The believer's life, in the classical idiom, is the muṣḥaf's commentary written in behavior — and this is no pious metaphor only: the Prophet ﷺ was described by his wife ʿĀʾishah, when asked about his character, in the words:

> *"His character was the Qur'an."* (Muslim 746, from ʿĀʾishah)

Elevated and purified pages, carried by a life that mirrors them: that is the verse's full circle — the text pure, the hands pure, the conduct the text's living translation.

**A Word Against the Misreading of Elevation**

One caution completes the exegesis. The elevation of the pages is an elevation of *truth above opinion*, never of a caste of men above other men — the very sin the sūrah opened by correcting. Islam's history contains episodes where a clerical class sought to make the Book's elevation its own: restricting its teaching, monetizing its recitation, treating the unlettered as unfit for the pages. Against every such instinct stands the sūrah's own opening scene: the blind man, the poor and unpromising, is the one God's verses came to honor and teach — and the Prophet ﷺ's Sunnah ordered the opposite of a closed guild: *"The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān). The pages are exalted precisely so that no human hierarchy can add its own gate in front of them; they are purified precisely so that every purified hand — whatever its tribe, purse, or sight — may carry them. The next verses will show who carries them first: not men at all, but the scribes noble and pious, in whose company, the Prophet ﷺ promised, the proficient reciter of these pages shall stand.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:15

> **in the hands of scribes,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Safarah: The Bearers of the Pages**

The fifteenth verse places the honored, exalted, purified pages somewhere specific: *bi-aydī safarah* — "in the hands of scribes." The word *safarah* is a plural — of *sāfir* or *safīr* — and the lexicographers give the root s-f-r a rich double field. On one branch, safara means *to write*: a sāfir is a writer, a scribe, one who commits things to writ; on another branch, safara means *to unveil*, to disclose what was hidden — whence also *safīr*, an ambassador, the one who carries a message between kings and discloses each to the other. The classical commentators on this verse gather the possibilities with relish, and al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī preserve them all. The scribes are the exalted angels who write down God's revelation and carry it between heaven and earth — the ambassadors of the divine words. Others specify them as the angels who inscribe the deeds of God's servants, for the Qur'an repeatedly describes the angelic recorders: *"Did you think that We created you idly... ? Rather, over you are watchers, noble scribes, who know what you do"* (82:10–12); *"He utters not a word but that with him is a watcher ready"* (50:18); *"Or do they think that We hear not their secrets and their conspiracies? Nay — Our messengers are with them, writing"* (43:80). Still others, al-Ṭabarī records, extend the sense to the human bearers of the Book — its reciters and transmitters — making the verse a portrait of everyone whose hands the pages pass through. And some of the linguistically adventurous, drawn by the "unveiling" branch of the root, read the verse as praising the Book's own clarifying power: the pages that unveil truth.

**A Note on the Alternate Name of the Sūrah**

Here belongs a detail the introduction promised: this verse is the reason some of the early Muslims named the whole sūrah *al-Safarah*, "The Scribes." A chapter is known by what most strikes the ear of its first hearers, and what struck them in this one, evidently, was the tableau of vv. 13–16: pages, honored, exalted, purified, and *held* — by hands dignified with two of the most beautiful adjectives the Qur'an ever hangs on any creatures: *kirāmin bararah*, "noble and pious" (v. 16). Before the sūrah's thunder ends — before the Piercing Cry and the fleeing kin and the dust-darkened faces — the ear passes through this quiet, luminous antechamber: a library scene, heaven's own. The alternate name is a reminder that the early community heard this passage as a unity: the Book, its pages, and its bearers, all in one breath.

**The Chain of Custody: From the Highest Pages to the Human Page**

Whichever reading of safarah one privileges, together they trace the complete chain of custody that Islamic theology has always taught for revelation. The Qur'an descends, the faith holds, from the Preserved Tablet (85:22) — the pages of v. 13 in their highest reading — carried by the faithful Spirit to the Prophet's heart, as the Book itself describes with an exactness that reads like the doctrine of this verse in narrative form:

> *"Truly it is the speech of a noble Messenger, possessed of power, established with the Lord of the Throne, obeyed there, and trusted. And your companion is not mad: he saw him on the clear horizon."* (Qur'an 81:19–23)

The angels who carry the words are called by the very same adjective that will close our own verse-pair: *karīm* — noble. The chain then reaches earth: the Prophet's ﷺ heart; his scribes' parchment; the community's breasts and books — an Arabic Qur'an *"as a reminder for those who understand"* (12:2) — and generation after generation of reciters, each link a hand passed the pages. The believer who opens the muṣḥaf today is the last hand of a relay whose earlier hands are described in this verse — and the hadith that follows (on v. 16) makes the continuity explicit and stunning: the human who masters these pages will *be with* the scribes of heaven. The community of the Book is not merely downstream of the angelic scribes; it is their colleague.

**The Reciters as Scribes: A Human Honor in an Angelic Frame**

Al-Ṭabarī's reading that the scribes include the Book's human reciters deserves its own moment, because it crowns rather than competes with the angelic reading. Consider what the verse is claiming in its context: the sūrah has just spent ten verses correcting the misuse of a face and two verses defining the reminder's freedom — and it now shows the reminder's honor descending upon its bearers. The men and women who memorize and carry the Qur'an inherit, in their measure, the function of the safarah: they are the walking pages of the ummah. Islamic civilization took the implication literally. The title of the reciter — *ḥāfiẓ*, "guardian-preserver" — is the same word the Qur'an uses for God's guardianship of the Book (*"We are its Guardian" — la-ḥāfiẓūn*, 15:9): the memorizer shares the name of the function. The child in the madrasah sounding out the short sūrahs, the ḥāfiẓah who leads the women's tarāwīḥ in Ramadan, the aged man who has forgotten much else but not his portion — all stand, in this verse's anthropology, inside the portrait: pages honored in hands honored.

**What the Angels' Scribal Work Means for the Reader**

If the scribes are the recording angels of 82:10–12 and 43:80, then the verse adds a quieter, more sobering resonance: the same heaven that writes down revelation writes down its hearers. The pages that go out from God's presence and the pages that come back about man's deeds are kept, as it were, in the same office — the imagery of writing binds the two revelations, the Book and the record. Muslim devotion has always felt this: the believer recites the Qur'an aware that the recitation itself is among the things written down; the angelic scribes who carried the pages to the Prophet ﷺ are present, in faith's imagination, at every reading, hearing heaven's book sounded by earth's voices. This is the sober joy beneath the Prophet's ﷺ instruction that recitation be done with beauty — for the reciter is not performing to an empty room. The final verse of the pair will name the scribes' two qualities, *noble and pious*, and attach to them the Prophet's ﷺ promise about every human being who becomes skillful in the pages those scribes carry.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:16

> **noble and pious.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kirāmin Bararah: Two Words That Sum Up Angelic Character**

The portrait of the sūrah's antechamber closes with two adjectives — *kirāmin bararah* — "noble and pious." *Kirām* is the plural of karīm, from k-r-m, the root already met at v. 13 (mukarramah, honored pages): nobility, generosity, innate excellence of quality. *Bararah* is the plural of bārr, from the root b-r-r, which covers righteousness, dutifulness, fidelity to obligation — the broadest and warmest of the Qur'an's words for goodness, the word used for the child's duty to parents (birr al-wālidayn) and defined by God in the sūrah of the Cow:

> *"Righteousness (al-birr) is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west; but righteousness is he who believes in God, and the Last Day, and the angels, and the Book, and the prophets; and gives wealth, for love of Him, to kinsmen, orphans, the needy, the traveler, the beggars, and for the freeing of slaves..."* (Qur'an 2:177)

Put the two words together and the angels are described in a single stroke: excellent in nature, faithful in function — noble in what they are, pious in what they do. The pairing is deliberate and complete: nobility without dutifulness would be mere rank; dutifulness without nobility would be mere service. Heaven's scribes are both: beings whose very substance is generous excellence, whose every act is exact fidelity. The Qur'an presses the same double character elsewhere: *"honored ones, who do not precede Him in speech, and who act by His command"* (21:26–27) — and *"fearful of their Lord above them, and doing what they are commanded"* (16:50).

**The Angelology the Verse Guards**

For its first hearers — Arabs who imagined angels as God's daughters, mediating idols, female and frivolous (the Qur'an rebuts this at 53:27 and 16:57) — the verse quietly restates the entire doctrine of angels: they are not deities to be courted, nor intercessors to be bribed, but noble workers and worshipful scribes, whose dignity is the dignity of obedience. This is a point the Qur'an is at pains to make repeatedly, precisely because every neighboring religion of seventh-century Arabia turned angels into cult objects. Islam's angels are honored precisely by being *not* worshipped: when the Qur'an wants to display their rank, it shows them doing their jobs — carrying revelation, recording deeds, bearing the Throne, blowing the Trumpet (the last of which this very sūrah will invoke at v. 33). The believer honors the scribes by joining their task, not by adoring their persons: the human reciter making the pages sound, and the human scribe of deeds accumulating a record, stands in functional kinship with them. And that kinship, the Prophet ﷺ said, will one day be literal company:

> *"The one who is proficient with the Qur'an will be with the noble, pious scribes (al-safarah al-kirām al-bararah); and the one who recites the Qur'an and stumbles in it, finding it difficult — for him are two rewards."* (al-Bukhārī 4937; Muslim 798, from ʿĀʾishah)

The hadith is the standing commentary on vv. 13–16 and their crown. Every commentator on Sūrah ʿAbasa cites it here, and with reason: the verse describes the scribes, and the Prophet ﷺ explains that proficiency in the very pages the scribes carry places the human reciter *maʿa* — *with* — them. Note the mercy in the hadith's second half, which Muslim's wording makes explicit: the struggler with recitation receives a double reward — his difficulty is not his disqualification but his multiplication. The Book that opened with God's concern for the blind man's access closes its library-scene with a guarantee that the halting reader is as welcome as the fluent. Proficiency is honored; striving is *more* honored. The reader who stumbles through the short sūrahs of the muṣḥaf, lips forming each word with effort, is promised the company of angels twice over.

**Nobility and Piety as a Pair for Human Beings Too**

Al-Ṭabarī records, and the verse's early readers felt, that the description aims beyond angels at the Book's human bearers as well — the reciters, the teachers, the scribes of the community. For them the pairing becomes a complete curriculum of character. Karīm — noble: the bearer of the Book should be generous, large-souled, free of pettiness; Islamic learning was never meant to produce misers of knowledge, and the Prophet ﷺ condemned the one who is asked for knowledge and withholds it. Bārr — pious, dutiful: the bearer's learning must be yoked to conduct; the reciter's private life is the public commentary on his recitation, and the early communities judged a would-be reciter's *character* before admiring his voice. The two together answer a question every religious community must face: what should its scholars be like? Not merely skilled, not merely holy, but *noble and pious* — excellence of nature disciplined by fidelity of life. Islamic biographical literature enshrined the ideal: the great reciters and jurists of the first centuries are remembered less for brilliance than for the fusion of learning with generosity — open houses, freed debtors, fed students. The ideal stands as this verse's permanent examination for the community of the pages: is the one who carries them karīm? is he bārr?

**The Human Difference: A Rank the Angels Do Not Share**

One final reflection completes the passage. The hadith's promise — that the proficient reciter will be *with* the scribes — implies an astonishing inverse: the angels, for all their nobility and piety, stand *beneath* the human believer in one respect, for the Qur'an itself declares that on the Last Day the reverse hierarchy will be confessed by heaven's own voice: the angels are honored servants who *"do not precede Him in speech"* (21:27) — but they do not choose; they cannot disobey and return; they have no qiblah of risk. The human reciter of the pages has stumbled (as the Prophet ﷺ did for one second in this very sūrah's opening), has repented, has *chosen* the Book against the pull of the self-sufficient world — and so his fluency, purchased with effort and his difficulty, purchased with struggle, will place him in the company of beings who have never once failed. That is the arithmetic of divine generosity the sūrah has been teaching from its first verse: God's correction of one frown procured Ibn Umm Maktūm a lifetime of honor; God's valuation of the stumbling reciter procures the community an eternity of it. The pages are honored, exalted, purified, carried by scribes noble and pious — and the door into their company was left open, by the verses before these, to *whoever wills*.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:17

> **May man perish! How ungrateful is he!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qutila: The Grammar of a Curse That Hurts to Translate**

With verse 17 the sūrah changes register entirely — from the hush of the library to a cry: *qutila al-insānu mā akfarah* — "May man perish! How ungrateful is he!" The verb *qutila* is the passive of "to kill," used here in an old Arabic imprecative form: a curse-exclamation, "may he be destroyed," "ruined be" — the same construction the Qur'an uses elsewhere: *"Ruined be the conjecturers!"* (51:10). It is the strongest form of denunciation in the language, and its appearance here, following immediately upon the most serene passage in the sūrah, is deliberate shock. The ear has just rested on pages honored, exalted, purified, in the hands of scribes noble and pious; and the very next sound heaven utters is a cry against the creature for whom those pages were sent. The sequence is the argument: given what has just been displayed — the majesty of the reminder, the purity of its transmission, the nobility of its bearers — the refusal of man to receive it is not a neutral difference of opinion; it is an outrage that calls down the language of ruin. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the curse is not aimed at man as such — the same Qur'an honors man as God's khalīfah (2:30) and "taught him the names" (2:31) — but at man in a particular posture: man the ingrate, man the denier, *mā akfarah*. The common reading of the early exegetes: "May man perish for his ingratitude, whatever it is that he disbelieves in" — the curse is conditional on the kufr, not on the humanity.

**Mā Akfarah: The Exclamation of Divine Astonishment**

The sentence ends with a grammatical jewel: *mā akfarah* — "how ungrateful he is!" The *mā* here is what the grammarians call the mā of taʿajjub, of astonishment: an exclamation-form, like "how great is X!" used when something exceeds expectation. God expresses, in human grammar, amazement at man's kufr — not the ignorance of one who was never told, but the studied ingratitude of one who was carried from a drop to a man, walked down a prepared road, fed from an engineered earth, and then covered the Giver with the gifts themselves. This note of divine astonishment at human perversity runs through the Qur'an as a genre:

> *"And He created you in stages... Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, then fashioned you as a man?"* (Qur'an 18:37)

> *"Does not man see that We created him from a drop? And behold, he is an open adversary! And he strikes Us a likeness and forgets his own creation."* (Qur'an 36:77–78)

The astonishment is pedagogically exact: it teaches the reader *where the surprise properly lies*. In the modern world, the default astonishment is inverted — people marvel that anyone should believe, and find unbelief natural. The Qur'an trains the ear the other way: given a universe of signatured gifts and a self that did not make itself, the thing calling for wonder is the *refusal*. Ingratitude is not merely a moral flaw; it is an intellectual astonishment — a misreading of the evidence so complete that heaven cries out at it.

**Kufr as Covering: The Root That Names the Refusal**

The word the exclamation pivots on — *akfarah*, from the root k-f-r — carries the whole theology in its etymology. In classical Arabic, the farmer who covers a seed with soil is a *kāfir* — the covering one — as the Qur'an itself uses it: *"like a rain-growth whose tillage (kuffār) pleases the sowers"* (57:20). Kufr, in its Qur'anic depth, is the act of *covering*: the disbeliever covers the truth he has been shown, covers the Giver with the gift, covers the fitrah with layer upon layer of habit, appetite, and pride — *"Nay, man rebels whenever he sees himself self-sufficient; and behold, to his Lord is the return"* (96:6–8). That is why the Qur'an can call ingratitude (kufrān al-niʿmah) and disbelief by one name: they are one act at different altitudes. The man who receives a decade of provision and never once says "from God" has been practicing, at the breakfast table, the same covering that the philosopher practices when he denies the origin of the whole. Verse 17 links the two levels deliberately: *mā akfarah* — how *covering* he is! — and the verses immediately following (18–22) will display the case for the prosecution: the drop, the proportioning, the eased way, the death, the burial, the resurrection — the complete dossier of gifts, laid against the coverer.

**Al-Insān: Everyman Under the Curse — and the Question of the Specific Man**

Whom does the verse mean by *al-insān* — "man"? The reports diverge instructively. The majority of the commentators read the generic: man as such, the type, whenever and wherever he wears the posture of ingratitude — and the generality is secured by the verses that follow, which describe every human being's origin and end. But al-Qurṭubī preserves a specific occasion as well: a man who had believed at the revelation of Sūrah al-Najm and then apostatized when the idolaters sneered — so that the verse would mean: ruined be that man, who disbelieved *after believing*, who covered what he had himself recited. The specific report and the general reading do not compete; the Qur'an's habit is to address a concrete case in words whose reach is universal — as vv. 1–10 addressed one afternoon in Makkah and became the ethics of every teacher forever. What the specific report adds is poignancy: the most dangerous kufr is not the ignorance of the never-taught but the covering of the once-known — and the sūrah, by framing the generic curse around a possible apostate, warns every believer that the door of covering swings on hinges inside the house of faith too.

**The Psychology of Ingratitude the Verse Diagnoses**

Modern readers stand to gain the most from this verse precisely where its subject has become an academic discipline: the study of happiness and adaptation. Psychologists call it the hedonic treadmill — the documented tendency of human beings to absorb every blessing into the new baseline within days, so that the promotion, the healed illness, the new home, all cease to register as gifts and become mere furniture of expectation. Verse 17 is the Qur'an's diagnosis of the treadmill three millennia... rather, fourteen centuries before the term: man's default program is to *consume and cover* — to take each mercy, normalize it, and forget it was mercy. What gratitude does is reverse the covering: it re-lifts the gift to the Giver, keeps the trace visible. This is why Islam built gratitude into the nervous system of daily life — the *alḥamdulillāh* on waking, on eating, on relief; the Prophet's ﷺ standing at night until his feet swelled, and his answer when asked why, being forgiven already:

> *"Should I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4836; Muslim 2820)

Gratitude, in the Qur'an's own promise, is the one act that increases the gift itself: *"If you are grateful, I shall surely increase you"* (14:7) — the treadmill reversed. The curse of v. 17, then, is not arbitrary thunder; it is the sentence passed on a maladaptive strategy — the creature who covers his own provision starves in the midst of the feast, first of meaning, and finally (the sūrah will show) of faces: dust-covered where he might have shone.

**The Verse as Hinge**

Structurally, verse 17 is the hinge of the whole sūrah: everything before it concerns the delivery of the reminder; everything after it concerns the case for the reminder — why man, of all God's creatures, most needs it, and what happens to him if he covers it. The curse falls in the middle like a gavel: the trial of man now begins. The prosecution's first exhibit is his own body — "From what thing did He create him?" — and the verses that follow will conduct the examination.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:18

> **From what thing did He create him?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Interrogative as an Invitation to the Crime Scene**

The prosecution of man opens with a question, and the question is the argument: *min ayyi shayʾin khalaqahu* — "From what thing did He create him?" The Arabic interrogative *ayyi* presses for specification: from *which* thing — invite inspection, look closely, name the substrate. The verse takes the very object of man's pride — himself — and turns it into the first exhibit of evidence against his ingratitude. The rhetorical move is one the Qur'an employs again and again in its rational passages: instead of asserting doctrine for acceptance, it directs attention to observable reality and lets the reader complete the argument. This is the Qur'an as God's own empirical program: *"Say: Behold what is in the heavens and the earth"* (10:101); *"Do they not look at the camels, how they are created?"* (88:17); *"And on the earth are signs for the certain, and in yourselves — do you not see?"* (51:20–21). Verse 18 belongs to this family: the courtroom is the reader's own body, and the question is asked in such a way that every honest answer testifies to the defendant's Creator. For whatever "thing" one names as man's origin — dust, drop, clay, a fertilized cell — the naming itself concedes the entire case: man began as material he did not choose, did not assemble, and could not have animated. The creature who says "from a drop" has already said "I was given everything, including myself."

**Why the Question Humiliates Pride at Its Root**

The genius of asking *from what thing* is that it strikes the exact nerve of istighnāʾ — the self-sufficiency diagnosed at v. 5 and cursed at v. 17. Human arrogance is always an arrogance about origins that have been forgotten: the chief who scorns the reminder stands on lineage; the wealthy man stands on acquisition; the intellectual stands on accumulated cognition. But trace any of these back along the question's arrow and every column of self-regard collapses at the same point: *he was a thing that was made*. The lineage ends in a drop no one chose; the fortune began with lungs and daylight given gratis; the intellect itself was a capacity that unfolded on a schedule its owner did not set — *"And God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and gave you hearing and sight and hearts, that haply you might be grateful"* (16:78). Note the verse's final clause: the equipment was given *for gratitude* — which is exactly what v. 17 said man covers. So the question of v. 18 is not merely forensic; it is therapeutic. It is designed to be asked by man of himself whenever pride rises, the way one splashes cold water on a face. The Prophet ﷺ institutionalized the same deflation in his farewell address, the Sunan record:

> *"O people! Your Lord is one, and your father is one: all of you are from Adam, and Adam is from dust. Let the one who prides himself on his lineage, or wealth, withhold — or let him know that God is more deserving of the fire than his own self."* (Aḥmad, from Abū Nadrah's transmission of the Prophet's ﷺ words in the farewell season)

And the Qur'an names the same common origin in its own idiom: *"We created man from an extraction of clay"* (23:12). The principle survives every paraphrase: the question "from what thing?" is the great leveler, and Islam appointed it as the standing corrective of every human hierarchy — recited as a reminder at funerals, where each career ends at the same substrate.

**The Question the Materialist Answers Too Well**

There is a further subtlety the verse has always carried for the intellectually serious: the question does not fear any answer. Tell us man came from a drop — fine, says the Qur'an: so who proportioned the drop? Tell us he came from dust — fine: who summoned the dust into a self-repairing, meaning-hungry creature? The verse's interrogation does not depend on ignorance of biology; it depends on the gap between *material* and *made*, which every increase of biological knowledge makes more, not less, conspicuous. The cell's informational density, the choreography of development from single cell to thirty trillion — these are not objections to the question of v. 18; they are the question's modern translations. The Qur'an itself anticipates the reductionist move and pre-empts it in the sister-verse that will be quoted under v. 19:

> *"And he strikes Us a likeness and forgets his own creation. He says: Who shall give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He who created them the first time — He gives them life, and He knows every creature."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The logic, which the following verses of our sūrah will complete, is: the more astonishing the origin, the more certain the Originator — and the more inexcusable the covering. Ibn Kathīr, expounding this passage, makes the argumentative structure explicit: the creation of man from a humble origin is set forth as *proof* — first of God's power (hence of the resurrection), and second of God's goodness (hence of man's obligation of gratitude). Unbelief fails on both counts at once: it must deny the evidence of the body it lives in.

**The Ancient Quarrel in One Question**

Finally, notice how the question silently rebuts the anthropology of the Prophet's ﷺ audience. The Meccan polytheists, and the Greek-influenced traditions beyond them, carried pictures of human origins designed to flatter: descent from gods, divine sparks imprisoned in flesh, semi-divine ancestors of the tribes. The Qur'an's question, asked before that audience, dismantles the flattery by its very grammar: *from what thing* — as though man were an artifact whose substrate deserved specifying. And then, in the next verse, comes the answer that is at once humbling and, rightly heard, the highest dignity Islam assigns to man: *from a drop* — a despised fluid, a mere beginning — *then He proportioned him*. The dignity is relocated from the origin to the Proportioner. Man is not noble because he descends from gods; he is noble because God shaped his drop with care, eased his road, wrote his name among the remembered, and sent him pages — honored, exalted, purified — carried by scribes. That is the Qur'an's replacement anthropology, and verse 18 is its front door: a question that empties man of self-made grandeur in order to fill him with given grandeur. The next verse supplies the answer, and with it, the first plank of the case for the resurrection to come.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:19

> **From a drop He created him, then proportioned him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Min Nutfatin: The Answer That Humbles and Dignifies at Once**

The question of v. 18 receives its answer in five words: *min nuṭfatin khalaqahu* — "from a drop He created him." The word *nuṭfah* is a diminutive in Arabic — from naṭf, a dripping, a trickle: the form itself says "the tiniest bit of liquid." The Qur'an chooses the humblest possible noun for the human origin, and it does so consistently across a dozen passages: *"He created man from a drop (nuṭfah), and behold, he is an open adversary"* (16:4); *"then We made the drop (nuṭfah) a clinging form..."* (23:14); *"Was he not a drop of emitted semen?"* (75:37); *"from between loins and ribs"* (86:6–7). The repetition is programmatic. In a society — like every society — where men preened on pedigree, the Qur'an fixed the true pedigree of every aristocrat and every beggar in one word: a drop. The Ottoman sultan and the scavenger of Makkah are equal at the level of v. 19 with an equality no modern ideology has improved upon, because this one is not manufactured by decree; it is simply a fact about the substrate, revealed by the One who made the substrate. And the very word that humbles also dignifies — for the second half of the verse shows what the Proportioner did with that drop.

**Fa-Qaddarahu: Taqdīr — The Art of the Proportioner**

The verb that follows creation is *qaddarahu* — "then He proportioned him," from the root q-d-r, which gives the great Qur'anic word *taqdīr*: measuring out, determining, decreeing with precision. The form is intensive and deliberate: not merely "made," but *measured* — shaped according to a determined plan. Al-Ṭabarī expounds the word with the lexicon's help: qaddara means to make something according to a known measure (qadar), nothing of it left to chance or excess. The resonance of the word in the Qur'an's usage turns the biology into theology:

> *"Glorify the name of thy Lord, the Most High, who created, then proportioned (fa-sawwā), and who determines (fa-qaddara), then guides."* (Qur'an 87:1–3)

> *"Indeed, We created everything according to a measure (bi-qadar)."* (Qur'an 54:49)

And the same verb is attached to the human creature with an exactness that is almost tender: *"O man! What has deceived thee concerning thy generous Lord, who created thee, then proportioned thee, then balanced thee? Into whatever form He willed He assembled thee."* (82:6–8). The picture that verses like 80:19 assemble is of a Maker for whom the drop is not raw material but a *commission*: every eye, every finger, the proportions of the face, the capacity of the lungs — measured before they existed, executed to specification. What modern developmental biology has mapped in its own vocabulary — the choreography by which a single fertilized cell differentiates, on schedule, into a body of tens of trillions of cells, each in its place with its function — the Qur'an comprehends under one intensive verb: *qaddarahu*. He proportioned him. The believer reading the verse today does not need to choose between the Book's word and the biologist's map; the map is simply the close-up of the proportioning, and the verse names the Proportioner whose precision the map exhibits.

**The Argument From Origin: The Sūrah's Own Logic**

Within the sūrah's architecture, v. 19 is the first exhibit in the case against the ingrate and for the resurrection — the argument the classical scholars called the "argument from the first creation" (istidlāl bi'l-ibdāʿ al-awwal). Its form is beautifully simple: the same Power that did the harder, original work of making a complete human being from a despised drop will have no difficulty with the easier-seeming work of reassembling what it once assembled. The Qur'an makes the argument explicit through the lips of the believer who answers his doubting companion in Sūrah al-Kahf:

> *"Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, then fashioned you as a man? He is God, my Lord, and in Him I trust."* (Qur'an 18:37)

And it sharpens the argument against the resurrection-denier in the twin passage of Yā-Sīn:

> *"He says: Who shall give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He who created them the first time — He gives them life, and He knows every creature."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The logic is not merely that God *can* repeat the feat; it is that the doubt itself is a failure of memory about the first feat. As the Qur'an says elsewhere: *"And he forgets his own creation"* (36:78). The man who concedes v. 19 — who admits he was once a drop and was proportioned — has, by that concession, already surrendered every rational ground for denying vv. 21–22, death and resurrection. This is why the sūrah places the origin-account immediately after the curse of v. 17: the ingrate is not merely morally wrong; he is refuted by his own body, which testifies in the very structure of its existence to a Maker's measured generosity.

**Proportioned for What? The Deeper Taqdīr**

The tradition read *qaddarahu* with a second horizon beyond anatomy. To proportion a thing is to shape it *for a function* — a handle is proportioned for a hand, a key for a lock. If God proportioned man, man has a taqdīr not only of body but of *purpose*: he is made to specification, and the specification includes what he is for. The Qur'an says it outright in the verse of the trust: *"We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they refused to carry it and feared it — but man carried it"* (33:72); and in the verse of purpose: *"I created jinn and mankind only to worship Me"* (51:56). The sequence of the sūrah's next verses will spell out the road that flows from this proportioning — *"then He made the way easy for him"* (v. 20) — where the commentators will find both the womb's exit and the moral path. Verse 19 thus stands as the hinge between anatomy and ethics: the creature was measured into existence, therefore the creature is *for* something, therefore his ingratitude (v. 17) is not a victimless oversight but the misappropriation of a measured instrument — a Stradivarius used to hammer nails.

**The Level Ground of the Drop for a Divided World**

For the modern reader, v. 19 remains one of the most socially powerful verses in the Book, precisely because its humbling is universal rather than selective. Racism, caste, aristocracy, credentialism — every system of ranked human worth depends on forgetting the drop. The Qur'an's answer to all of them was not a slogan but a fact, repeated in revelation until the fact became the community's operating system: your origin is a drop; your worth is not in the origin but in what the Proportioner then did — and in what you do with what He proportioned. The Prophet ﷺ made the application legally binding in his farewell address: *"All of you are from Adam, and Adam is from dust; an Arab has no excellence over a non-Arab except by God-consciousness" (Aḥmad, from the farewell season). Fourteen centuries of Islamic social practice — imperfectly honored, perfectly stated — trace back to verses like this one. And the verse's humbling has one last gift for the individual believer: a therapy for despair at one's own smallness. The same word that tells the arrogant they began as a drop tells the crushed that a drop, in the hands of the Proportioner, became a living, sensing, thinking, choosing marvel. Whatever seems negligible about a human life — its obscure origin, its low estimate in the world's ledger — v. 19 stands as the standing counter-verdict: *He created him, then proportioned him.* Measured by the Proportioner, no human being is negligible; and measured by his own beginnings, no human being has any ground for pride.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:20

> **then He made the way easy for him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thumma al-Sabīla Sallahahu: One Road, Three Meanings**

The twentieth verse continues the biography of the drop with a verse of astonishing compression: *thumma al-sabīla sallahahu* — "then He made the way easy for him." The noun *sabīl* is the ordinary Qur'anic word for a way, a path, a road; the verb *sahhalahu* is from the root s-h-l, the root of "easy" (sahl) itself — to make smooth, to facilitate. The grammar is notable: the way is fronted before the verb, as if the road were already there, waiting, and the act of God was to smooth the creature's passage onto it. The classical commentators, whom al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr compile, read the verse along three lines — and the three lines, rather than competing, describe one providence at three levels.

**The First Reading: The Way Out of the Womb**

The first and most literal reading: God eased the child's way out of the womb into the world. Birth is the first journey every human takes, and the Qur'an stops to marvel at its provision: the passageway, the labor that drives it, the mother's body built to accomplish it — *"then He made the way easy for him."* The passage connects to v. 19's anatomy: the One who proportioned the drop also engineered its exit. The readers of the first generation, in a world where childbirth was the most dangerous crossing of every life, heard the verse with a realism the modern reader must recover: the very fact that this crossing succeeds as often as it does — that a body can pass at all — is not a brute given but a smoothed way. The subsequent dependency of the newborn, its ready-made food supply, its instinct to suckle, the milk arriving on the infant's schedule — the whole biological hospitality of the first weeks is the continuation of the same verse: the road out of the womb opens onto a prepared table.

**The Second Reading: The Moral Way Made Knowable**

The second reading, which al-Ṭabarī favors with support from the early exegetes, sees the *sabīl* as the way of guidance — the road to God, made available and passable to every human being. The Qur'an's own sister-verses give this reading unambiguous warrant:

> *"Truly, We guided him upon the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful."* (Qur'an 76:3)

> *"Have We not made for him two eyes, a tongue, and two lips, and guided him upon the two ways?"* (Qur'an 90:8–10)

The claim is not that walking is effortless — the Qur'an says plainly that man was created *"in toil"* (90:4) — but that the way itself has been made *findable*: the fitrah's compass, the intellect's sight, the conscience's warnings, and above all the sending of messengers and pages (as this sūrah has just celebrated in vv. 11–16) — the road has been graded, signposted, lit. On this reading, v. 20 completes the doctrine of vv. 18–19: man's origin is given, his form is given, and his *map* is given — therefore his ingratitude (v. 17) and his refusal (v. 12's shadow) are without excuse. No one will stand before God able to say the way was hidden.

**The Third Reading: The Ease Written Into Every Calling**

The third reading, which al-Qurṭubī transmits and anchors in a Ṣaḥīḥ hadith, universalizes the word further: God has made easy for each creature the course of its created nature. Ibn Kathīr cites for this the Prophet's ﷺ words, in the long authentic report about the written destinies, from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib:

> *"Each deed-easier is made for his created end: as for the people of happiness, they are eased to the deeds of the people of happiness; and as for the people of misery, they are eased to the deeds of the people of misery."* — in its standard form: *"Work, for each is eased toward that for which he was created."* (al-Bukhārī 6605; Muslim 2648, from ʿAlī)

The report resolves the apparent tension between the two halves of the verse's theology: the way is smoothed by God (76:3), and the walker truly walks and truly chooses (v. 12's "whoever wills"). The classical harmony: God creates the willing and the deed, and man owns his deeds in a real sense that makes reward and blame meaningful — the highway is paved, and the driver's hands are on the wheel. What the hadith adds is the pastoral warning: *easiness is diagnostic*. When wrongdoing keeps getting easier and easier for a person, that is not the world becoming convenient; it is a road being graded in a direction. And when obedience keeps opening its doors, that is the other grading. The wise ask constantly which way their ease is flowing.

**The Prepared World: Ease as Evidence**

Stand back and the verse yields its argumentative force, which belongs to the sūrah's continuing prosecution: the road was not merely provided, it was *eased*. Creation, on the Qururan's presentation, is not a neutral arena in which life is possible with effort; it is a hospitable theater in which the conditions of human flourishing were arranged in advance — an atmosphere to breathe, a soil that receives seed, water that falls from the sky, a sun at a life-permitting distance, and a moral sense in the breast. The Qur'an marshals the same evidence elsewhere as a formal argument: *"God is He who made for you the earth a resting-place and the sky a canopy, and formed you and made your forms beautiful, and provided you with good things — that is God, your Lord. So blessed is God, Lord of the worlds"* (40:64). Verse 20 of ʿAbasa contributes the personal, biographical note to that cosmic provision: your own birth, your own survival out of the womb, your own finding of a road you did not build — each of them is a private instance of the public argument. The ingrate of v. 17 is thus refuted twice over: he was made from a drop (v. 19), and he was *carried* — out of the womb, along the way — by arrangements he did not make (v. 20).

**The Sober Side of an Easy Road**

One caution belongs to the verse's honesty: the sabīl that is *made easy* is the road's availability, not the journey's comfort. The Qur'an says the road is passable, and also that it climbs: *"We created man in toil"* (90:4); *"As for the one who gives and is reverent and confirms the good — We will ease him to ease; but the one who is miserly and deems himself beyond need and denies the good — We will ease him to hardship"* (92:5–10). Notice the same root s-h-l in 92:7 and its dark twin in 92:10: ease and difficulty are themselves moral outcomes, graded in response to the walker's heart. Verse 20's promise, rightly heard, is therefore both comfort and summons: comfort — you were not abandoned at the crossroads, the way was made findable; summons — a smoothed road excuses stopping. And the verse's placement in the biography sequence gives it poignancy: between the proportioning of the drop (v. 19) and the coming of death (v. 21) lies exactly one verse about the road — a whole lifetime, as it were, in a single line, set between the drop and the grave. That is the Qur'an's estimate of the time a human being is given to walk the way that was made easy for him: measure enough, ease enough, and no more than a verse's length between two eternities.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:21

> **Then He caused him to die and buried him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Atāhu al-Mawt: Death Comes — the Arabic Says It Like a Visitor**

The biography compressed into these verses now reaches its third stage, and the Arabic's choice of verb deserves a long look: *thumma atāhu al-mawtu* — "then death *came to* him." Not "he was killed," not "death befell the body" in the passive — but death *came*, as a caller comes, an appointed visitor with a name. The verb atā is the verb of arrival, of one who sets out and reaches. The Qur'an uses the same construction elsewhere: *"Say: the death from which you flee will surely meet you"* (62:8); and it describes death as a *meeting*: *"Then He caused him to die and buried him" is followed in the sūrah by resurrection — and the Book says elsewhere: "Wherever you may be, death will reach you, though you be in towers built high"* (4:78). The imagery is deliberate and merciful in its realism: death in the Qur'an is not a metaphysical catastrophe or a cosmic accident but an *appointment* — *"And no soul can die except by God's leave, at an appointed term"* (3:145). The one who *came* to Ibn Umm Maktūm at al-Qādisiyyah, and to every reader since, came on a schedule written before the drop existed — for the Prophet ﷺ said, in the report already cited from ʿAlī: *"the first thing written of the son of Adam is his term... "* (the hadith of the forty-days decrees, al-Bukhārī 6605's companion tradition, Muslim 2645's ordering from Ibn Masʿūd: *"the drop... then the angel is sent and writes his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether miserable or happy"*). Death came to the drop that had been proportioned; the same Hand that shaped the beginning schedules the end.

**Fa-aqbārahu: The Astonishing Claim That God Himself Buries Man**

Then comes one of the most quietly startling phrases in the Qur'an: *fa-aqbārahu* — "and He buried him." The verb, from the root q-b-r (grave, burial), in the form used here means to place in a grave, to give burial. Read plainly, the subject throughout the sentence is God: He created him from a drop, proportioned him, made his way easy, caused him to die — *and buried him*. The early commentators felt the shock and paused on it, and their reflections, gathered by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, run in two directions. Some read the verb with man as its implied agent — "then he was buried" or "then his family buried him" — softening the grammar to the expected. But others let the grammar stand and found in it a theology of tenderness: the same God who received the creature at the drop does not release him at the grave; the Hand that eased the way out of the womb eases the way into the earth. Al-Rāzī lingers on the dignity of this: kings of the earth abandon their subjects at death, leaving the handling of their bodies to hirelings; the King of kings describes His own providence as extending through the burial itself. The God of this sūrah — the same God who corrected a frown shown to a blind man — attends, as it were, to the whole arc of the creature's story, down to the last rite performed upon him. For a revelation that would later command burial as an act of supreme honor (the Prophet ﷺ himself washed and shrouded the dead with his hands, and every Muslim funeral rite is a liturgy of this verse), the claim that God is the final Burial-Giver transforms the grave from abandonment to custody: *the One who put you in the earth is the One who will take you out of it.*

**The Four-Stage Biography: Drop, Way, Death, Grave**

Set vv. 19–21 side by side and the sūrah has drawn a complete human biography in three verses — the whole span of every life that has ever been lived, told from the vantage of heaven: *a drop, proportioned; a way, made easy; a death, appointed; a burial, given.* No names, no nations, no centuries: the biography is universal, and its universality is the argument. Every member of the audience — the Quraysh chief in his arrogance, the blind believer in his striving, the modern reader in his distraction — is living inside these three verses right now, somewhere between the drop and the grave, with the stage after next already written. The Qur'an uses the same compressed obituary elsewhere to the same effect: *"How can you disbelieve in God, seeing that you were dead and He gave you life; then He causes you to die; then He gives you life again; then unto Him you are returned?"* (2:28). The word "returned" is the hinge: the biography does not end at the grave, and the very next verse of our sūrah (v. 22) will open its fourth stage — resurrection. But the sūrah's method is to let the grave-stage be *felt* before the exit is revealed: for one verse-length, the reader stands at the burial, in the silence after "and He buried him."

**The Grave as the Great Leveler**

The burial-scene also completes, in one stroke, the social leveling the sūrah has been conducting since v. 1. The assembly in Makkah was arranged by rank: chiefs in the inner circle of attention, a blind pauper at its edge. Verse 21 announces the two equalizers that no rank survives: the death that *comes* and the grave that *receives*. The Prophet ﷺ used the same leveler to demolish the whole ledger of acquisition, in the report al-Bukhārī transmits about the son of Adam's accounting:

> *"The son of Adam says: my wealth, my wealth — but is any of it yours save what you ate and used up, or wore and wore out, or gave in charity and sent ahead?"* (Muslim 2958, from Abū Hurayrah)

Whatever the hand clutched, the earth receives on equal terms. The One who *made the way easy* for each life also *made the grave* for each life — and the grave takes no note of the audience a man commanded. The sūrah's first ten verses corrected the misuse of a face; vv. 19–21 correct the illusion that any face's rank is permanent. In the earth of the burial-scene, the frowning chief and the frown-receiving pauper lie at the same depth.

**Reading the Verse at a Funeral**

The Muslim funeral rite is the liturgical home of this verse, and its practices read like a commentary on it. The body is washed — the way's last easement; shrouded in plain cloth — the same cloth for rich and poor; carried on shoulders by neighbors — rank walks behind; and lowered into the earth while the standing community speaks to the dead man about *his* stage four: the questioning of the grave, the resurrection to come. Every element answers the verse: death *came*; God gives the burial; the community performs what God claims. And the rite's most famous instruction returns the living to the sūrah's opening lesson: while lowering the body, the Prophet ﷺ taught the burying-party to say, "In the name of God and upon the Sunnah of the Messenger of God" — the dead man's last escort is remembrance, and the living man's last sight is a face that received full honor — the very honor whose momentary absence, to one blind man in Makkah, God corrected with ten eternal verses. The reader who has followed the sūrah this far has been trained to hear v. 21 personally: the verse is not ancient biography; it is the reader's own itinerary, two stops ahead.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:22

> **Then, when He willed, He resurrected him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thumma Idhā Shāʾa: The Timing That Belongs to God Alone**

The fourth stage of the biography arrives in four words: *thumma idhā shāʾa ansharah* — "then, when He willed, He resurrected him." The phrase *idhā shāʾa* — "when He wills" — is the Qur'an's standing formula for the timing of the resurrection, and its presence here is doctrine, not filler. Everything else in the human biography has its appointed sequence: the drop, the way, the death, the grave. But the resurrection is dated by nothing but will: not by the decay of the body, not by the passage of centuries, not by any readiness on the creature's side — only *"when He willed."* The Qur'an deflects every demand for the schedule with the same formula: *"They ask thee of the Hour: when is its anchoring? Whereunto does its mention reach thee? Unto thy Lord belongs its term"* (79:42–44) — the very verses that stand at the end of the sūrah immediately before this one. And it warns against certainty of delay: *"Truly the Hour is coming — I keep it nearly hidden — that every soul may be recompensed for what it strives"* (20:15). The unnamed date is mercy and alarm at once: mercy, because the door of repentance stands open as long as the hour is hidden; alarm, because every human planner lives on borrowed, undated time. Man's biography has one stage he can neither schedule nor refuse, and the sūrah deliberately withholds its date from every reader — from the Prophet ﷺ himself:

> *"Say: I have no power to benefit or harm myself, save as God wills. Had I knowledge of the Unseen, I would have amassed much wealth, and no evil would have touched me."* (Qur'an 7:188)

**Ansharah: Resurrection as Unfolding**

The verb chosen for the resurrection is *ansharahu*, from the root n-sh-r, whose base meaning is to unfold, to spread out, to revive after decay. In its most homely usage, nashara describes the unfurling of a folded cloth — and the older commentators, al-Qurṭubī among them, heard precisely that tactile image in the verse: what God does at the resurrection is to *unfold* the folded. The buried body, folded into the earth like cloth folded into a chest; the record folded in the grave; the story folded shut at the funeral — all of it unfolded, spread, opened out again. The image is profound because it reframes death's apparent finality: folding is not destroying. A folded page is still a page; its text is intact, merely hidden from view. The Qur'an uses the root again for the raising: *"And the graves are unfolded (buʿthirat)"* — in its own idiom at 100:9 — *"Does he not know, when what is in the graves is scattered forth, and what is in the breasts is brought out?"* — and at 36:51 it describes the single call after which *"they will rise forth from the graves unto their Lord."* The whole vocabulary family says one thing: the grave is not an ending but a *fold*, and its Owner holds both edges of the cloth.

**The Argument Completed: Origin Proves Return**

Verse 22 completes the argument whose first premises were laid at vv. 18–19. The sūrah's logic, which the classical exegetes formalized as the argument from first creation, now stands entire: the One who made a thinking, seeing, choosing human being *from a drop* — from material without a single thought in it — cannot be stymied by the rearrangement of matter that death represents. The Qur'an makes the argument explicit, and with a touch of divine irony at the doubter's expense, in the sister-passage of Yā-Sīn:

> *"He strikes Us a likeness and forgets his own creation. He says: Who shall give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He who created them the first time — He gives them life; and He knows every creature."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

And in Sūrah al-Ḥajj it widens the argument from man to the whole earth, with the rain as the visible rehearsal of resurrection — the very rehearsal the next section of our sūrah (vv. 24–32) will stage at length:

> *"O mankind! If you are in doubt about the resurrection — truly We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging form, then from a lump, formed and unformed, that We may make manifest to you. And We establish in the wombs what We will, until an appointed term; then We bring you forth as an infant... And you see the earth barren; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and grows every delightful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and He gives life to the dead, and He has power over all things — and that the Hour is coming, in which there is no doubt, and that God resurrects those in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7)

The structure of ʿAbasa 80:17–32 mirrors 22:5–7 almost clause for clause: the drop, the stages, the rain, the revived earth, the Hour. The sūrah is thus not making a new argument; it is sounding, in compressed form, the Qur'an's central rational appeal — an appeal addressed not to authority but to observation: *you have seen the harder thing; why doubt the easier?*

**The Weary-Maker Question Answered Before It Is Asked**

The early Makkan skeptics, like skeptics since, had a stock objection: not that God lacked power, but that the *system* would be too tired, the undertaking too old, the dust too scattered. The Qur'an answers the objection about weariness in terms that make the objection itself the proof:

> *"Did We grow weary with the first creation? Nay — they are in doubt about a new creation."* (Qur'an 50:15)

> *"Have they not seen that God, who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation, is able to give life to the dead? Yea — He has power over all things."* (Qur'an 46:33)

The reasoning is a fortiori, the rabbinic and fuqahāʾ's qiyās awlā — the argument from the greater to the lesser: heavens and earth outweigh a handful of bones; a Maker unwearied by galaxies is not taxed by dust. Verse 22's *when He willed* carries the same quiet confidence: the resurrection does not wait for feasibility; it waits only for will. And the will has already been announced, in every prophet's message and every scripture's warning: *"And the Trumpet is blown, and behold, from the graves they come out unto their Lord"* (36:51). The only thing unspecified is the hour.

**Why the Doctrine Matters Morally: The Biography With a Fourth Act**

Finally, the verse's placement at the hinge of the sūrah — immediately before the food-passage (vv. 24–32) and the terrors of the Ṣākhkhah (vv. 33–42) — shows its moral function. A biography that ended at the grave (v. 21) would render the sūrah's first ten verses pointless: if the blind man's honor and the chiefs' arrogance both ended in the same dirt, the correction of a frown would be an etiquette lesson of no ultimate consequence. Verse 22 is the verse that makes manners matter forever: because there is a resurrection, there is a reckoning; because there is a reckoning, the treatment of a seeker, the covering of a Giver, the walking of an eased road all enter the permanent record. The Qur'an says exactly this in its own defense of resurrection's moral necessity: *"And We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in play"* (21:16); *"Shall We make those who believe and do righteous deeds as the corrupters in the land?"* (38:28) — the verse that the scholars read as the moral argument: without return, the ledger of deeds has no balancing column, and justice is a word. Verse 22 supplies the column: *when He willed, He resurrected him.* The biography of every human being — drop, way, death, grave — is followed by a summons; the sūrah's remaining twenty verses describe the day of that summons, and the two kinds of faces that will answer it. Before them comes one more act of evidence: the dinner-table argument, where the God who promises to unfold the graves shows man, in every meal, the folded seed unfolded.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:23

> **Nay! But he has not accomplished what He commanded him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Kallā: A Gavel Before the Verdict**

The third section of the sūrah closes with its second *kallā* — "Nay!" — the same repulsive particle that opened v. 11. There it broke the frame of the misallocated attention; here it breaks something more stubborn: man's comfortable assumptions about his own account. The verses to come (24–32) will present the evidence of the table; before evidence can be heard, the defendant's self-assessment must be shattered. *Kallā* is the sound of the gavel: whatever you were telling yourself, stop — the truth is otherwise. And what the truth is, the verse states in a clause whose very grammar argues: *lammā yaqḍi mā amarah* — "he has not accomplished what He commanded him." The verb *q-ḍ-y* is the vocabulary of completion, execution, the finishing of a required matter — a task discharged in full. The form here is negative and pointed at the past: not "may not" but *has not*. Standing at the end of the biography-sequence (drop, way, death, grave, resurrection), the clause performs an audit across the whole journey: given a body, a road, and a return, the creature's account of obedience — measured against the command — comes up short.

**Who Is "He"? The Layers of the Audit**

The commentators divide over the referents of the two pronouns in the clause — *he* has not accomplished *what He commanded* — and the options, laid out by al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr, each carry a distinct doctrine. The first reading: man has not fulfilled what God commanded him — the general human audit: the covenant of worship, the gratitude owed to the Giver, the belief in the resurrection just announced; man, in his generality and his heedlessness, is a debtor whose account is in arrears. The second reading, which al-Qurṭubī details with the report already noted at v. 17, particularizes to the disbeliever: the one who says, when the resurrection is mentioned, what the Qur'an records of his type:

> *"And if We let him taste mercy from Us after hardship has touched him, he will surely say: This is mine — I do not think the Hour is coming; and if I am returned to my Lord, surely with Him I shall have the most beautiful. But We shall truly inform those who disbelieved of what they did, and We shall make them taste a harsh punishment."* (Qur'an 41:50)

On this reading, v. 23 is the divine rebuttal of that self-congratulating optimist: *nay* — he imagines a beautiful reception from a Lord whose commands he never discharged; he is counting on a reward he never earned. The third reading turns on a point of recitation: some of the early readers, as Ibn Kathīr notes, read the opening *kallā* here in its affirmative sense (as *bal* or *innamā* — "truly"), yielding: "Truly, man has not fulfilled what his Lord commanded him" — the same audit, minus the tone of rebuke. The readings agree in substance; what varies is whether the verse is thunder or statement. Either way, the human ledger is short.

**What, Concretely, Has Man Not Accomplished?**

The classical exegetes, pressing the clause for content, name the arrears. First and most fundamental: the covenant itself. The Qur'an teaches that all human souls bore witness to God's lordship before entry into this world: *"And when thy Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify of themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yea, we testify"* (7:172). On the covenant-reading of our verse, what man "has not accomplished" is precisely this primordial undertaking — to live as the servant of the One he testified to. Second: the commands delivered with the equipment. The eyes, ears, and hearts of 16:78 were given *"that haply you might be grateful"* — the gratitude-return is in arrears. Third: the road. The sabīl was eased (v. 20); the walking of it — the *"worship Me"* (51:56) for which the proportioning was made — remains unfinished in the ingrate's account. The sūrah has arranged its evidence so that the verdict of v. 23 lands as a conclusion rather than an accusation: verses 18–22 proved the gifts; verse 23 simply reads the balance — gifts received, commands unfulfilled. It is the same prosecutorial method as vv. 1–10: arrange the facts, let the conscience supply the guilt.

**The Asymmetry of Skepticism**

There is a psychological insight in this verse that deserves its own heading, because it describes a permanent feature of the human mind. Man is exacting toward God and lenient toward himself. He demands proofs of the resurrection, demonstrations of providence, explanations of suffering — an auditor's posture toward every claim of his Lord. Meanwhile his own side of the contract — the gratitude, the honesty, the justice to the weak, the five prayers owed to the Giver of the eyes he audits with — is carried in arrears for decades without a single audit. Verse 23 reverses the posture in one clause: before interrogating God's promises, look at your own performance of His commands. The Qur'an's phrasing is the perfect mirror for the psychology of deferral: the man of 41:50 is certain of a beautiful reward, *while disobedient* — confidence in God's generosity doing duty for obedience itself. The verse's logic exposes the error: mercy is indeed infinite, but mercy is not a substitute for response; the servant who refuses the command while banking on the Commander's kindness has misunderstood both command and kindness. In the language of the sūrah's own frame: he is the *istaghnā* of v. 5 again — self-sufficient about his fate while defaulting on his debts.

**From Audit to Evidence: Why the Food-Passage Follows**

The placement of v. 23 explains itself the moment one notices what comes next: *fal-yanẓuri al-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmih* — "So let man look at his food" (v. 24). The audit of v. 23 said: commands unfulfilled. The immediate question is: which commands, in what currency, was man to pay? The answer of vv. 24–32 is the reminder's most tangible exhibit: *gratitude, measured at the table.* The food-passage is not a digression into agronomy; it is the itemized bill — every line of it a mercy (water poured, earth split, grain, vine, olive, palm, garden, fruit, pasture), every line implying the same unpaid return: recognition, thanks, and the worship that thanks becomes. The sūrah thus moves with courtroom precision: the defendant's self-assessment is dismissed (kallā), the arrears are stated (v. 23), and then the evidence is spread out item by item — a meal laid before the reader as proof. For the believer, the sequence is a standing discipline: whenever the sense of God's claims grows faint, the sūrah's order of operations applies — audit yourself first (v. 23), then look at your food (v. 24); the second look will not fail to revive the first.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:24

> **So let man consider his food,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fal-Yanẓur: The Command That Turns Religion Into Observation**

The fourth section of the sūrah opens with an imperative that is the charter of the whole passage: *fal-yanẓuri al-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmih* — "So let man consider his food." The verb *naẓara ilā* means to look at, to direct the gaze upon — not a glance but a considering look, an inspection. And the *fa-* that opens the verse ties the command to everything before it: precisely because man has not accomplished what God commanded (v. 23), precisely because he is under audit — *therefore* let him look at his food. The logic is that of a judge saying to the defendant: examine the evidence yourself. The Qur'an commands this kind of looking again and again; it is the Book's most characteristic epistemology:

> *"Will they not look at the camels, how they are created? And at the sky, how it is raised? And at the mountains, how they are rooted? And at the earth, how it is spread out?"* (Qur'an 88:17–20)

> *"Say: Behold what is in the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 10:101)

In an age when "faith" is often set against "observation," it is bracing to recall that the Qur'an's response to doubt is not "believe harder" but *look longer* — and to look not at arguments about God but at the nearest created things: a camel, the sky, and here, the reader's own plate. Verse 24 is the Qur'an's institutionalization of the dinner-table as a place of theology. The word it chooses for the object of looking — *ṭaʿām*, from the root ṭ-ʿ-m, whose root meaning relates to taste — is itself a small lesson: food as *the tasted thing*, that which no man knows secondhand. You can doubt another man's philosophy; you cannot doubt your own hunger, or the fact that something answers it daily. God has chosen as His evidence the one experience universal to every living reader.

**What "Food" Includes: The Verse as Table of Contents**

The command is a table of contents for the next eight verses. "His food" — and then the sūrah unpacks, in agricultural order, everything the word quietly contains: the water poured from the sky (v. 25); the earth split open to receive the seed (v. 26); the grain that becomes bread (v. 27); the vine and the herbage (v. 28); the olive and the date palm (v. 29); the densely planted gardens (v. 30); the fruit and the pasture (v. 31); and the final accounting — "sustenance for you and for your flocks" (v. 32). The command to "look at your food" thus means: look at the *whole chain*, not merely at the plate. The sūrah anticipates by fourteen centuries the modern insight that meaning is destroyed by abstraction: a supermarket steak is an abstraction — bloodless, traceless, storyless; the same steak seen as rain + pasture + sun + a herd's year + a farmer's labor + a supply chain of thousands is a *creature of dependencies*, and every dependency in the chain ends in the same place. The Qur'an's command restores the chain: *look at your food* — all the way up the chain to the One who poured the water.

**The Spiritual Readings: Food for the Body, Food for the Soul**

The commentators were quick to hear a second register beneath the literal. Al-Qurṭubī transmits the reading that the "food" to be considered includes the food of the soul — the reminder itself, the verses that have just been called "pages honored" (vv. 11–16); and a related reflection preserved in the commentary literature turns the command inward: consider what becomes of food in you — the consumption and elimination of it — and take from that transit the measure of the whole world, which passes through the soul the same way. This is not a different command but the same command at depth: everything that enters a human life — bread, wealth, pleasure, even the years themselves — is in transit; only what is metabolized into something remains. The Qur'an will say it of the world in one phrase: *"What is with you runs out; what is with God remains"* (16:96). The dinner-table is the daily parable of that verse: the plate that was full is empty in twenty minutes; the body that ate remains, and works, and builds. Consider your food — and learn from it what to do with a life.

**The Table as the Place of Remembrance**

Islam took this verse's logic and built it into the etiquette of eating, so that the command to consider would be performed rather than merely remembered. The Muslim table is a liturgy in miniature: the name of God spoken over the first bite (*bismillāh* — and if forgotten, *bismillāhi awwalahu wa-ākhirahu*, as the Prophet ﷺ taught when Satan's interference was suspected: "in the name of God, at its beginning and its end," al-Tirmidhī 1858); eating with the right hand and from the near side of the plate (al-Bukhārī 5409; Muslim 2022, from ʿĀʾishah); not finding fault with food — the Prophet ﷺ never criticized food, eating what he liked and leaving what he disliked (al-Bukhārī 5409; Muslim 2064, from Abū Hurayrah); and the closing thanksgiving:

> *"Praise be to God who fed me this, and provided it to me without any power or might from me."* (al-Tirmidhī 3458, from Muʿādh ibn Anas; graded ḥasan)

The final phrase is the whole of v. 24 in one sentence of table-grace: *without any power or might from me* — the eater confessing, twice a day or thrice, that the chain ends above him. And the Prophet ﷺ generalized the divine economics of the meal:

> *"God is pleased with a servant who praises Him when he eats a morsel and praises Him when he drinks a drink."* (Muslim 2734, from Anas ibn Mālik)

Note the scale: not a feast, a *morsel*. The God of this sūrah — who corrected the neglect of one blind man — is a God who accepts as worship the thanks spoken over a single bite.

**The Modern Amnesia the Verse Heals**

For most of history, v. 24 needed no argument: everyone had planted, or watered, or herded, or at least watched the harvest fail and the hungry season come; dependence was visible. Modernity's genius has been to hide the chain — and in hiding it, to starve gratitude at its source. The average eater today cannot name the season his bread was grown in, the rain that watered it, or the species of wheat it came from; food arrives as if by magic, wrapped, labeled, always. The psychologists of abundance have documented the cost: habituation, entitlement, the treadmill that eats gratitude. Verse 24 is the ancient countermeasure: a command, issued from heaven, to *re-attach the meal to its chain*. A family that keeps the basmalah and the closing praise, that occasionally traces its food to its sources, that pauses over the water as over the wine of others — that family is performing the verse. And the performance is not sentiment: it is the sūrah's own court of evidence, held daily, where the heart is re-taught the fact that everything on the table — like the eater himself — began as something poured, split, and grown by Another: *"And if you count God's blessings, you will never number them"* (16:18).

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:25

> **that We pour down water in abundance;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Annā Ṣababnā: The Interrogative of Method**

Verse 25 begins the itemized evidence with the first link of the food-chain, and it does so with a grammatical flourish: *annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban* — "how We pour down the water, pouring." The word *annā* is an interrogative of manner — *how?* — and it converts the clause into an invitation to inspect the method: consider *how* this is done. The verb is repeated in the Arabic in what grammarians call the cognate accusative — "We poured it a pouring" — a construction the Qur'an reserves for acts it wants felt in their intensity: not a drizzle, not a phenomenon, but *an act*, performed with fullness, addressed to the earth and to the reader at once. The "We" is the majestic plural of God's self-reference: the rain that has fallen on every field of human history is here claimed, storm by storm, as a personal, deliberate act of the Speaker. Weather is thus de-naturalized in the most literal sense: what the modern mind files under "the water cycle" the Qur'an files under "what We pour" — and the difference between the two filings is the whole difference between a world that is owned and a world that merely occurs. The Qur'an's doctrine of rain is stubbornly personal:

> *"God is He who sends the winds, which stir the clouds, which He spreads in the sky as He wills, and breaks them apart, and thou seest the rain issuing from within them..."* (Qur'an 30:48, abridged)

**Rain as the Qur'an's Standing Mercy-Sign**

The choice of water as the first exhibit is no accident; it is the Qur'an's most repeated natural sign, and its recurrence forms a scripture-long refrain. God sends water as revival and as mercy:

> *"And of His signs is that thou seest the earth humble; then, when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Truly He who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead. He has power over all things."* (Qur'an 41:39)

> *"And We sent down from the sky water by measure, and lodged it in the earth — and We are able to take it away."* (Qur'an 23:18)

And — the verse that gives the whole food-passage its deepest resonance — water is tied to the origin of life itself:

> *"And We made from water every living thing. Will they not then believe?"* (Qur'an 21:30)

The biological claim is now textbook: the cell is mostly water; blood is mostly water; the planet's life is hyphenated to the hydrological cycle. The Qur'an stated the dependence as doctrine, and then, in our sūrah, made the doctrine edible: the water that *is* every living thing is the same water poured on the field that becomes the bread on the plate. Verse 25's *ṣabban* — the pouring in abundance — describes a delivery system of staggering scale: some hundreds of thousands of cubic kilometers of water evaporated, carried, precipitated over the earth annually, desalinated by sun, lifted by wind, distributed free — and the sūrah asks the reader to see the hand in the pouring. No invoice accompanies rain; no account is billed for desalination. The Qur'an's comment on such economics is the question of Yā-Sīn: *"Is He who created the heavens and the earth able to create their like? Yea — He is the Creator, the Knower"* (36:81).

**The Measured Mercy: Not Too Much, Not Too Little**

The cognate construction *ṣababnā... ṣabban* celebrates abundance, but the Qur'an's rain-doctrine includes the note of measure — and the measure is part of the evidence. Too little water and the seed dies in the dust; too much and it rots or washes away; the world's breadbaskets exist in a band of rainfall the whole system holds with remarkable stability. The Book names the measure:

> *"And He it is who sends down rain after they have despaired, and spreads His mercy; and He is the Guardian, the Praiseworthy."* (Qur'an 42:28)

and warns, with the same measure-language, that mercy withheld is also a speech:

> *"And if they turn away — say: God suffices me; there is no god but He; in Him I trust, and He is Lord of the mighty Throne."* (Qur'an 9:129)

The classical commentators, reading our verse, dwell on the comfort and the warning together: the *ṣabb* is generous, and it is conditional on a Provider who is free to pour or withhold. Modern hydrology has, if anything, sharpened rather than replaced the doctrine: aquifers drained faster than refilled, droughts shifting, cloud-seeding attempted — mankind's experience of water-limits reads today like a commentary on 23:18's *"and We are able to take it away."*

**The Free Gift That Modern Systems Try to Sell**

Here the verse turns its face directly to the modern reader. Rain is the original free lunch — no one has ever paid for the sky's delivery. But between the sky and the plate, humanity has built an apparatus of capture: reservoirs, canals, pumping, purification, bottling — and a market that gradually converts the free gift into a priced commodity, until the end-user of "water services" can almost believe water *originates* in the utility company. The verse stands at the head of the passage as a correction of exactly this modern amnesia: before the bill, before the pipe, before the bottle — *We poured it, pouring.* The believer's gratitude is thereby trained to reach past every intermediary to the Source, and the training matters morally: a heart that knows water as gift cannot be careless with it as waste. The Qur'an's own water-ethic — the Prophet's ﷺ instruction forbidding waste even at a flowing river, when he passed by Saʿd performing ablution:

> *"What excess is this!" — and Saʿd said: "Can there be excess in ablution?" — he said: *"Yea, even if you are upon a flowing river."* (Ibn Mājah 425, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

—is the ethic of a gift: gratitude at the tap, restraint at the river.

**The Order of the Evidence**

Notice the sūrah's forensic order, which is also the order of the field: water first, then the earth's opening (v. 26), then the crops (vv. 27–31), then the shared table of man and beast (v. 32). The order is the argument's architecture: the most invisible dependency (rain) is placed first, because it is the most forgotten; the most visible result (fruit) comes late, because it is the most taken for granted. The reader who follows the chain from verse to verse is being taught to trace every finished thing back through its dependencies to the poured beginning — a discipline the sūrah will leave the reader to apply to everything else in life: the salary back to the health that earned it, the health back to the body's unearned machinery, the body back to the drop, the drop back to the One who proportioned it. Verse 25 is the first poured drop of that larger rain.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:26

> **then We split the earth in fissures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shaqaqnā al-Arḍa Shaqqan: The Earth That Opens to Receive**

The second link of the chain is described with the same divine grammar as the first: *fa-shaqaqnā al-arḍa shaqqan* — "then We split the earth, splitting." Again the cognate accusative — the act named and then re-named as a noun of intensity, so that the ear *hears* the crack. The verb shaqq, from sh-q-q, means to split, cleave, rend; its cognate noun shaqq is the fissure itself. The picture is agricultural and precise: rain does not merely fall *on* the earth; the earth *opens* — the soil softened by water, swelling, cracking along a thousand lines, the seed-husks splitting, the first root-case breaking, the green shoot forcing its cleft upward through the crust. In one verse the sūrah gathers all of these openings at once: soil, seed, and shoot — the earth's cooperation in the meal is an *opening*, not a passive receiving. And the divine "We" stands behind the splitting with the same personal claim as behind the pouring: the same God who signed the rain signs the ground's response.

**The Splitting That Is Life: A Parable of All Growth**

Pause on the paradox the verse encodes, because it is one of the Qur'an's favorite lessons in disguise: everything that lives begins by *breaking*. The seed that stays whole stays barren; it must split to grow. The soil that stays sealed stays a path; it must crack to host a harvest. The commentators, especially those of a contemplative temperament like al-Rāzī, drew the principle outward from the field to the soul: comfort's sealed surfaces produce nothing; the human being grows through his splittings — the hardship that opens him, the loss that cracks the shell of self-sufficiency, the repentance that begins as a break. The Qur'an states the principle in narrative form in the story of the garden-owner whose unsplit heart produced ruin: *"And his fruit was encompassed... and he began to wring his hands over what he had spent upon it"* (18:42). And it states the glad reverse in the imagery of the good word itself:

> *"Seest thou not how God strikes a parable? A good word is like a good tree: its root is firm, and its branches are in the sky, yielding its fruit every season by the leave of its Lord."* (Qur'an 14:24–25)

The good word, too, is a seed — planted, split, rooted, fed by the same rain of v. 25. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its beginning will recognize the pattern from the human side as well: the sūrah itself opened with a breaking open of a heart — the Prophet's ﷺ momentary closure corrected by ten verses, the shell of strategic calculation cracked so that the light of a better precedence could enter. God splits the earth for grain; He splits hearts for guidance; the verb is one.

**The Resurrection Rehearsal: The Grave-Earth and the Field-Earth**

But the classical commentators heard a second register in v. 26 that ties the whole food-passage back to the resurrection-doctrine of vv. 21–22, and it is the register the Qur'an itself makes explicit elsewhere. The earth that splits for the seed is the same earth that holds the graves — and its annual miracle of splitting-and-sprouting is heaven's standing demonstration that "dead ground" yields living harvest. The Book says it in the very passage quoted at v. 22, and says it *as* the argument's conclusion:

> *"...and thou seest the earth barren; but when We send down water upon it, it stirs, and swells, and grows every delightful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and He gives life to the dead, and He has power over all things — and because the Hour is coming, in which there is no doubt, and God resurrects those in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7, conclusion)

And in Sūrah Qāf, the same rehearsal is compressed into a single equation — with the resurrection named as the earth's own lesson:

> *"And We sent down from the sky blessed water, and caused thereby gardens and harvested grain to grow, and tall date palms with ranged clusters — provision for the servants — and We gave life thereby to a dead land. Such will be the coming forth."* (Qur'an 50:9–11, conclusion)

The verse our sūrah places at v. 26 is thus doing double duty in the argument: it is the second item of the meal's evidence *and* the first rehearsal of the resurrection. Every spring, on every field, the ground that was sealed performs the exact sequence — stirred, split, sprouting — that the graves will perform *"when the Piercing Cry does come"* (v. 33). The farmer is the world's most consistent witness to resurrection; he merely needs to be told what he has been watching. The sūrah tells him here.

**The Precision Beneath the Poetry**

It is worth noting, without forcing the verse into a laboratory, how exact the agricultural observation is. The crack in the soil is not incidental to the harvest; it is functional: it admits air and water to depth, allows root penetration, releases soil moisture's upward path. The seed's own splitting — the botanist's germination — is the literal mechanism of the crop. And the shoot's emergence is a feat of hydraulic engineering the verse's order captures: water first (v. 25), then the splitting (v. 26), then the growth (v. 27 onward). The classical exegetes, men of mostly agricultural societies, expounded the verse in exactly these terms: al-Qurṭubī describes the earth's swelling under rain, the fissures that run across the watered land, and the sprouting that follows the split — the sūrah's first audience did not need to imagine the scene; they had spent their lives inside it. What revelation added to their experience was the *attribution*: the splitting they had always observed, year after year, was — like the rain — signed. We split it. The modern reader, for whom soil is a substance bought in bags, gains the same gift in reverse: the verse returns the bag to the field, and the field to the One who opens it.

**From Fissure to Table: Why the Chain Must Not Be Skipped**

Finally, the verse's placement teaches the reader how to read the whole passage: not as a list of crops, but as a *chain in which no link can be skipped*. Remove the rain (v. 25) and the splitting never happens; remove the splitting (v. 26) and the grain has no passage (v. 27); remove any later link and the plate empties. A chain of dependencies, each link gratuitous and precise, is the Qur'an's definition of a world of mercy — and its refutation of the closed materialist ledger in which the meal "just happens." The sūrah will now walk the chain link by link — grain, vine, olive, palm, garden, fruit, pasture — and the reader is invited to walk it as a worshipper: because at the end of the chain, at v. 32, the accounting will be read aloud, and the question the whole passage has been building is the one the reader will be asked to answer with his life: *who fed you?*

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:27

> **and cause grains to grow therein,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Anbatnā Fīhā Ḥabban: The First Crop Is the Staff of Life**

The chain's third link brings the first harvest out of the split ground: *fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabban* — "and We caused to grow therein grain." The verb *anbata* (root n-b-t) is the specific verb of plant-growth: to sprout, to bring forth vegetation — the same root that gives *nabāt*, plants, and *nabī*, a prophet, in the classical wordplay that sees prophecy as God's cultivation of men. The crop named first is *ḥabb* — grain, seed-corn: the class of the small, dry, storable cereals — wheat, barley, and their kin — that made settled civilization possible. The verse's order is itself a lesson in priorities: before the vine, before the olive and the palm, before gardens and fruit — *grain*: the staple, the thing without which a city starves within months. God lists the meal in the order of human survival: bread first, delights afterward. The listing encodes a theology of need and want: the Giver who supplies delights (vv. 28–31) first makes certain the floor of necessity (v. 27) is covered — a pattern the Prophet ﷺ made a rule of community ethics: begin with the essential, and the essential is defined by what keeps the weakest body alive.

**Ḥabb: The Smallest Thing With the Greatest Reach**

The word *ḥabb* deserves a meditation of its own, for the Qur'an has made it carry more theology per syllable than almost any noun. Grains are the most unremarkable of foods — small, hard, silent — and precisely for that reason the Qur'an chooses them twice as its image of how divine multiplication works. The charity-verse already quoted in this commentary bears repeating as the ḥabb's great appearance:

> *"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is as the likeness of a grain (ḥabb) that sprouts seven ears; in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whomsoever He wills; and God is Encompassing, Knowing."* (Qur'an 2:261)

The doctrine of ḥabb: what is small, hard, and buried is not dead but *deposited* — and the return is not linear but factorial. The same word carries the doctrine of divine origination, in the verse that joins our sūrah's own imagery of splitting:

> *"Truly God is the splitter (fāliq) of the grain and the date-stone: He brings forth the living from the dead, and the bringer-forth of the dead from the living. That is God — so how are you deluded?"* (Qur'an 6:95)

Read 6:95 beside 80:26–27 and the two verses interlock: God split the earth (80:26) because God is by nature the Splitter of grain and stone (6:95); the field's crack is His signature, repeated in every seed-case in the world's granaries. The root ḥ-b-b is beloved of the Arabic lexicians: from it comes *ḥabbah*, the single grain, and by extension any small seed-like core; and the classical linguists noted the kinship of the root with *maḥabbah*, love — observing that a seed is a concentrated love-letter, the plant's whole future folded into a kernel. Whether or not one presses the etymology, the resonance is Qur'anic in spirit: the Giver who loved man sent him love in the form of ten thousand kernels per field.

**Grain and the Making of Civilization**

The verse, read historically, describes the material foundation of every civilization the sūrah's audience — and every audience since — has lived in. The domestication of the cereals is, by broad consensus, the hinge of human history: wild grasses, selected and resown, turned mobile foragers into villagers, villages into cities, cities into the world of markets, writing, and law — and, not least, into the settled Mecca and Yathrib of the seventh century, whose own staple was the barley and wheat of the oases and highlands. When the sūrah names *ḥabb* first, it names the quiet infrastructure under every human institution: the court, the market, the mosque, and the school all stand, quite literally, on fields of grass-seed. The Qur'an's habit is to insist that infrastructure be seen as *given*: *"And He it is who produced gardens, trellised and untrellised, and the palm, and crops of diverse produce, and the olive, and the pomegranate, alike and unlike. Eat of their fruit when they bear fruit, and give its due on the day of harvest — and be not prodigal; He loves not the prodigal."* (6:141). Verse 27's grain is the same gift in its plainest, most survival-critical form — the daily bread that no king mints and no market creates from nothing.

**Bread as the Baseline of Gratitude**

Because grain is the staple, it is also the baseline test of gratitude — and the sūrah's pedagogy seizes on exactly this. Delights are easy to praise: the first ripe date, the honeyed fig, the olive's oil. But the thing eaten every day, three thousand times in a lifetime, is the thing gratitude was designed to forget. The Qur'an trains the opposite reflex: the Prophet ﷺ taught that a morsel praised is worship (Muslim 2734, cited at v. 24), and the greatest of God's mercies are the recurring ones — breath, heartbeat, and bread — precisely because their constancy makes them the hardest to *see*. The sūrah's remedy is the command to look (v. 24) applied to the plainest object: *consider your food* — and the first thing the look meets, on the split earth, is ḥabb. The believer who can see the grain — see it as split, grown, multiplied, harvested, milled, baked, and given — has learned the whole passage's lesson on its first item; everything after grain in the sūrah's list is the same lesson in sweeter dress.

**One Grain, One Soul: The Verse's Personal Yield**

The classical devotional writers, walking the sūrah's field-path, ended where the parable of 2:261 ends: with the reader himself as grain. The seed's career is the believer's: taken from a storehouse, buried in the dark, split open by water, raised green into light, multiplied beyond reckoning — *"a grain that sprouts seven ears; in every ear a hundred grains."* The sūrah has already drawn the human parallel upstream: man from a drop (v. 19), the way made easy (v. 20), death and burial (v. 21), resurrection when God wills (v. 22). Verse 27 quietly completes the rhyme: what God does for ḥabb in the field He has promised to do for the children of Adam in the graves — split, raise, multiply. The One who signs the harvest signs the resurrection; the field is the promise, the crumb on the table is the token, and the next five verses will set out the rest of the feast.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:28

> **and vines and herbs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿInaban: From Staple to Delight**

After the staff of life, the sūrah's menu turns to its first pleasure: *wa-ʿinaban* — "and grapes," the fruit of the vine (*ʿinab*), the ancient world's signature crop of joy. The listing is itself a small sermon on the character of the Giver: once the floor of necessity was laid (grain, v. 27), He did not stop at sufficiency — He added sweetness. The Qur'an's theology of pleasure is generous and precise at once: delights are from God, named by God, and answerable to God. Grapes appear throughout the Book as a staple-blessing in every catalogue of provision — *"and of the fruits of the palms and the vines you take sweetness and fair provision; truly in that is a sign for a people who understand"* (16:67) — where the same fruit yields both the good provision (the fresh and dried grape) and, by human manipulation, the intoxicant; the verse's phrase *"fair provision"* quietly grades the uses: the fruit as given is rizqan hasanan, and the fermentation that corrupts it is man's addition to God's gift. The Qur'an's grape-listings always keep that moral horizon in view: the vine is a blessing whose abuse was also known — Sūrah al-Baqarah's wine-verses (2:219) reckon its sin and its benefit and rule for the sin's avoidance, without ever denying the vine's origin in mercy. The lesson for the reader of ʿAbasa: the same cluster of grapes can be received as provision or engineered into intoxication — the gift tests the receiver.

**The Vine's Economics of Patience**

The vine also carries, in the agricultural order of the sūrah, a different economics than the grain. Grain is annual: sow, reap, eat within the year — the covenant of immediacy. The vine is perennial and slow: plant a cutting and wait years for the first full cluster; tend, prune, train, wait again. Grape-growing is the agriculture of *patience invested* — a farmer plants vines for his children as much as for himself. The sūrah's placement of the vine immediately after the grain thus sketches a complete picture of provision's tempos: God feeds you now (grain) *and* teaches you to wait (vine) — and both are His. The Qur'an's favorite garden-parable turns on exactly this double tempo, and on the ingratitude that forgets who owns both harvests:

> *"And strike for them the parable of two men: to one We gave two gardens of vines, surrounded by palms, with crops between... and he entered his garden, wronging himself: 'I do not think this will ever perish... and I do not think the Hour is coming.'"* (Qur'an 18:32–35, abridged)

The vine-owner's error is ʿAbasa's own theme in miniature: abundance mistaken for self-sufficiency — the istaghnā of v. 5 ripening on the trellis.

**Wa-Qaḍban: The Herbage That Feeds the Beasts**

The verse's second word is one of the sūrah's rarer lexemes: *qaḍb* — fresh, green herbage; fodder; the cut clover and alfalfa-type plants on which grazing animals feed. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both gloss it as al-Kalāʾ — pasture, herbage — with the note that it denotes the green, moist fodder; the classical lexicians derived from its root the sense of cutting, as what is cut for the herd. The word's placement beside the grape is deliberate and, when noticed, quietly moving: in the space of two nouns the sūrah has provided for *both tables in the household* — the fruit of delight for the human being, and the daily greens for the cattle. Before v. 32 will make the shared economy explicit — *"sustenance for you and for your flocks"* — v. 28 has already set the two menus side by side. The God of this sūrah feeds the goat with the same seriousness as the gourmet: the herbage is not an afterthought appended to the human feast; it is named in the divine inventory, item by item, next to the vine. The Prophet ﷺ drew the moral in a parable of mercy:

> *"While a man was walking on a road, he felt a great thirst, and went down a well and drank. When he came up, he saw a dog panting, eating the ground from thirst. The man said: this dog has suffered what I suffered. So he went back down, filled his shoe, and held it in his mouth until he climbed out, and gave the dog to drink. God appreciated him, and forgave him."* — they said: O Messenger of God, is there a reward for us in the beasts? He said: *"In every living thing there is a reward."* (al-Bukhārī 2466; Muslim 2244, from Abū Hurayrah)

A God who forgives a man for watering a dog is a God who lists fodder in His revelation.

**The Full Range of the Table: Why the List Runs From Bread to Fodder**

Read vv. 27–32 as a menu and a map emerges — the complete agrarian economy of the sūrah's world, laid out in divine handwriting: the staple (grain), the delight (grape), the greens (herbage), the oil and the sweet (olive and palm, v. 29), the orchard (v. 30), the dessert and the pasture (v. 31), and the summary accounting for man and beast together (v. 32). No ancient king's granary inventories his realm with more care. The pedagogical effect on the first hearers — people who knew exactly what each of these crops cost in labor and risk — must have been cumulative and overwhelming: item after item, the whole visible economy of survival is claimed, item by item, by the God being denied. The modern reader, who purchases all seven items in one supermarket aisle, needs consciously to restore what the list once did automatically: the sense that the aisle is seven mercies deep. Grapes out of season, alfalfa for horses, hay for cattle — all are the verse's inventory, extended by transport and trade that are themselves made of the same rain, sun, and soil.

**The Verse's Whisper: Pleasure and Provision Are Neighbors**

One last noticing, in the spirit of the sūrah's careful word-order: grapes and herbage, side by side, are *delight and duty* in one breath — the fruit that makes the heart glad, and the fodder that keeps the work-animal alive. The Giver mingles them without apology, teaching a theology of the ordinary in which pleasure is not the enemy of seriousness, and labor does not exclude joy. Gratitude, the passage's whole lesson, is precisely the faculty that can hold both: to thank God for the grape without forgetting the herd's clover, and to work the herd's clover without refusing the grape. The miser of v. 92's sister-verse — the one who *"deems himself beyond need"* — is the man who grasps the one and grinds through the other; the believer, walking the sūrah's field, is taught to receive both with the same word: from Him, by name, with thanks.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:29

> **and olives and date palms,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Zaytūn: The Tree of Light and Longevity**

The sūrah's inventory now names its two most iconic trees together: *wa-zaytūnan wa-nakhlā* — "and olives and date palms." The olive needs little introduction; it is the Mediterranean world's oldest cultivated blessing and one of the Qur'an's most honored plants. The Book swears by it: *"By the fig and the olive, and Mount Sinai..."* (95:1–2); lists it among God's first gifts to the children of Israel; and — in the verse every Muslim knows by heart — reaches for the olive as the very image of divine light:

> *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is as a niche, wherein is a lamp; the lamp in a glass; the glass as a shining star — kindled from a blessed olive tree, neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would well-nigh shine though no fire touched it. Light upon light."* (Qur'an 24:35)

Why the olive, in the verse of Light itself? The classical commentators gathered its reasons: the olive yields its oil without being burned or pressed to death — it *gives light willingly*; it grows on stony ground that defeats other crops, turning hardship into richness; it lives for centuries, feeding grandchildren from a grandfather's planting; and its oil, the ancient world's fuel, food, medicine, and anointing, was the most generous of all vegetable wealth — a tree whose whole biography is benefaction. When the sūrah names it in the food-passage, it counts all of this: the lamp-light of the ancient house, the press, the pickle, the healing ointment — one tree spanning kitchen, pharmacy, and shrine. In the Qur'an's list of provision elsewhere, the olive is a named mercy of a "blessed" order: *"And a tree issuing from Mount Sinai, that grows with oil and relish for the eaters"* (23:20).

**Nakhl: The Palm That Built an Oasis Civilization**

Beside the olive stands the date palm — *nakh(l)* — the tree that made human life possible in the burning core of Arabia, and therefore the tree the sūrah's first audience knew most intimately. The palm is a complete economy in a single trunk: dates the staple-sweet (a food so calorically dense that oases lived on it), the palm-heart a vegetable, the fronds thatch and baskets, the fiber rope, the trunk beams; and the palm's agricultural demands created the whole social institution of the oasis — the sharing of water, the pollination season, the harvesting calendar. The Qur'an gives the palm its own verses of honor:

> *"And the tall palms with ranged clusters, provision for the servants — and We gave life thereby to a dead land."* (Qur'an 50:10–11)

And it gives the palm a place in the story of Maryam, when the date-tree serves the mother of ʿĪsā at the hour of her labor:

> *"And shake the trunk of the palm-tree toward thee: it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon thee. So eat and drink and be comforted."* (Qur'an 19:25–26)

A God who commands a suffering woman to be fed by a tree has already taught what our sūrah now itemizes: the palm is provision, tenderness, and service in one tall form. It is worth adding the palm's botanical marvel, which the sūrah's order of verbs has already captured: the palm begins like everything else in this passage — watered, split from a seed (the date-stone God splits, 6:95), grown. Then it does what a grass does — the palm is, botanically, a giant grass, not a true tree — rising on water and sun into a hundred feet of sweetness. The split earth of v. 26 ends, in the Arabian case, standing overhead in the palm-groves of the oasis.

**Two Trees, Two Landscapes — and the Total Claim of the Giver**

The pairing is geographically eloquent, as the classical geographers of the tafsīr tradition noted: the olive is the tree of the rainy hill-country — Syria, the Mediterranean rim; the palm is the tree of the watered desert — the oases of the Ḥijāz. Between them, olive and palm span the whole ecological band of the sūrah's audience, from the Levant's terraces to Ṭāʾif's escarpment and Yathrib's groves. The rhetorical point of the pairing: *wherever* you live in this land — hill country or oasis — your two most precious trees are both on this list, and both are claimed by the same "We." No farmer in the audience is left outside the inventory; the evidence is laid out across every landscape he could farm. This is the Qur'an's constant manner: its examples are never decorative; they are chosen so that the first hearers find their own lives inside the argument — and the later reader, in any land, is invited to perform the translation (his staples, his orchards, his aquifer) that the Arabs needed no dictionary for.

**The Prophetic Sunnah of Planting**

The food-passage has always sent one practical message into Muslim civilization more loudly than any sermon: planting is worship. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"There is no Muslim who plants a tree or sows a field, and a bird, or a person, or an animal eats from it, but that it is charity for him."* (al-Bukhārī 2320; Muslim 1553, from Anas ibn Mālik)

Read beside ʿAbasa 80:27–32, the hadith completes the theology of the field: God causes the growth ("We caused to grow"), and the servant is honored as God's junior partner in the planting — with a share in the reward of every mouth the field eventually feeds, human or beast. Islamic civilization took the hint across continents: the palm groves of North Africa and Spain, the irrigation law of Andalusia and the Huerta of Valencia, the waqf-endowed orchards and the agricultural manuals of the middle ages — a long history of men who believed that a well-tended tree was a form of liturgy. Even the palm-pollination incident of Madinah belongs here: the Companions, assuming the cross-pollination they had always practiced was unnecessary under the new dispensation, stopped — and the year's yield failed. The Prophet ﷺ told them:

> *"You know better the affairs of your world."* (Muslim 2362, from Anas and Ṭalḥah)

Meaning: God's gift includes the means; plant, pollinate, irrigate — the miracle of the harvest runs through the farmer's craft, not around it. The verse names the trees; the Sunnah hands the reader the shovel.

**Oil and Sweet: The Two Trees' One Lesson**

Finally, put the two trees' gifts side by side — oil and sweet — and the lesson of the whole passage stands in miniature. The olive's oil is the gift that *lights* (24:35), the date's honey-sweet flesh is the gift that *delights and strengthens* (19:26 — "eat and drink and be comforted"). Light and comfort: the two things every human home runs on, gathered from two trees that ask years of patience before they pay. The sūrah names them not to taxonomize a garden but to complete the portrait of a Giver whose gifts cover the whole register of human need: bread for survival (v. 27), sweetness for joy (v. 28), light for the night, strength for the weary, shade in the noon, and — as the next verse will add — the garden's beauty for the heart's rest. The one thing the list will never contain is a gift from anyone else's hand; and that is precisely the point the accounting of v. 32 will make explicit.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:30

> **and gardens densely planted,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥadāʾiq Ghulbā: The Strength of the Thick Orchard**

The inventory rises from single crops to the crown of cultivation: *wa-ḥadāʾiqa ghulbā* — "and gardens densely planted." The noun *ḥadīqah* (plural ḥadāʾiq) is from the root ḥ-d-q, whose sense-field runs around enclosure: a ḥadīqah is a garden enclosed, walled or hedged — ground claimed and kept for cultivation, distinct from the open field. It is, in fact, the very word from which the Qur'an's overwhelming name for Paradise derives: *jannah*, garden — the enclosed, shaded, watered place. And the adjective *ghulbā* — plural feminine of ghulab/aghlab, from the root ghalūbah, strength and thick vigor — describes trees grown dense, stout, and towering; the commentators gloss it: gardens whose trees are tall, thick, intertwined, their canopies contesting the sky. Al-Qurṭubī hears in the word the sense of a plantation that has *prevailed* — growth that has overcome its ground, filled it, mastered it. The picture is of the mature oasis orchard at full canopy: rows of palms over citrus and fig, shade stacked on shade, the air cooler under the leaves than the desert beyond the wall — the one place in the sūrah's landscape where a human being walks in green shade in a brown land.

**From Field to Garden: Provision Escalates Into Beauty**

The movement of the list deserves its own noticing. Grain feeds; the vine gladdens; olive and palm equip; the *garden* does something further — it *delights the eye*, arranges, composes. Verse 30 is the sūrah's recognition that human beings need not only calories but *beauty*: ordered, tended greenness. The Qur'an explicitly names this aesthetic register of provision elsewhere: *"And the earth — We spread it out, and cast therein firm mountains, and caused to grow therein of every delightful kind (zawjin bahīj), as an insight and a remembrance for every repentant servant."* (50:7–8) — where the explicit purpose of lovely vegetation is *remembrance*. The garden is provision raised to the level of sign: in a walled, watered, orchard-shaded enclosure, the desert-dweller sees his own eschatology in botany — a foretaste, in this life, of the garden that encloses the next. This is why the Qur'an can move so effortlessly between agricultural and paradisal senses of jannah: the believer has already stood inside the type; the antitype needs no explaining, only promising. The dense garden of v. 30 is the sūrah's most literal glimpse of Paradise in the middle of its food-inventory.

**The Walled Garden as Civilization's Masterpiece**

Historically, the ḥadīqah is also the hardest-won item on the list. An open field asks a season's work; a walled, densely planted garden asks a generation's: walls raised, water rights secured, wells sunk, shade-trees nursed through their frail years, species layered — the date over the citrus over the herb-bed — into a self-shading ecosystem. Every mature garden in the sūrah's world was an inheritance of someone's decades. The Qur'an honors exactly that accumulated labor while simultaneously refusing to let it eclipse the Giver: the garden is listed among the things *"We"* produced — and the Book says elsewhere, with the balance that our whole passage requires: *"It is He who produced gardens, trellised and untrellised"* (6:141) — God produces; man trellises. The division of credit is the passage's quiet doctrine: the seed, rain, soil, sun, and the very capacity of the plant to grow — God's; the wall, the channel, the pruning-knife, the patience — man's, by delegation. A gardener who says "my hands made this" is invited by the verse to the more exact sentence: "my hands were permitted to assist this." The Prophet ﷺ drew the line in the planting-hadith already cited at v. 29: every mouth fed by the tree is charity credited to the planter — credit *given by God to man*, the exact inverse of the man who takes God's credit for himself.

**Greenness and the Human Heart: An Ancient Intuition Confirmed**

The modern reader arrives at v. 30 with an unexpected ally: several decades of research in environmental psychology documenting what the sūrah simply assumes — that dense green space measurably restores the human being: attention recovers, stress hormones fall, hospital patients with a view of trees fare better than those with a view of brick. The sūrah's inclusion of the garden — not as luxury but as *provision*, in the same list as bread — reads today like a doctrine of human nature: the soul is part of the ecology it eats from. Islamic civilization built the insight into its urban fabric: the courtyard gardens of the mosque-medresa, the hospital gardens of the Islamic middle ages — built beside wards, as part of the cure — the gardens of Andalusia whose planners wrote openly of greenery as medicine for the spirit. All of it stands downstream of verses like this one, which teach a community to see the garden as God's gift in the register of mercy, not merely of produce.

**Density as a Metaphor: The Thick-Growing Life**

The rare adjective *ghulbā* leaves one more gift for the contemplative reader — its sense of *thickness, vigor, prevailing growth*. The sūrah has already used the field as an image of the soul (the split seed, the buried grain raised multiplied), and the garden completes the metaphor's maturity: a life densely planted is not a single row but an ecology — many virtues grown together, shading one another, roots interleaved; prayer shading study, study shading work, work shading family, family shading charity. The thin life — one crop, one row, wind-scoured — dries in the first heat; the dense life holds its own moisture. And the ultimate Dense Garden is the one the sūrah is driving toward: the jannah whose trees, the Qur'an promises, meet overhead — *"and its shade... and clusters of fruit hanging low"* (76:14; 69:23) — the canopy of mercy under which the radiant faces of vv. 38–39 will finally rest. Between the walled garden of the oasis and the walled garden of the Hereafter runs the whole argument of vv. 24–32: the God who can be trusted with the one can be trusted with the other; and the reader who has learned to see His hand in the orchard has learned to long for the garden where His hand planted everything forever.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:31

> **and fruit and pastures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-Fākihatan: The Fruit That Delights**

The inventory's penultimate verse gathers the sweetest of its gifts under one noun: *wa-fākihatan* — "and fruit." The root f-k-h carries, in the classical lexicons, the sense of delight and juiciness — the fruit is *that which delights*; some of the philologists linked the word to fukāhah, laughter and merriment, hearing in the fruit's name the smile it produces. Whatever the etymology presses or does not, the fit is spiritually exact: fruit is the food that plays — sweetness without necessity, color and scent beyond nutrition, the branch of provision whose purpose is joy. The Qur'an's catalogues regularly end on this note, as ours does: bread first, joy last — the order of a Giver who wants not only the servant alive but the servant *glad*. And the promise-verses of Paradise pick the same register when they describe the fruit of the garden — *"its clusters within reach"* (69:23); *"and fruit of what they choose, and meat of fowl as they desire"* (56:20–21) — as if to say: what you tasted of delight on earth was a down-payment; the full harvest of fākihah is kept. The Prophet ﷺ made the daily tasting of this mercy a matter of sunnah-courtesy to the Giver: to eat melon or dates with thanks is already half of the verse's lesson; the other half is in the word *abb*.

**Wa-Abban: The Famous Little Word for Pasture**

The verse closes with a term that became, in the history of Qur'anic lexicography, a household name: *abb* — pasture, herbage, the wild and semi-wild plants on which the beasts graze. The early exegetes explain it as al-kalāʾ — herbage, grazing — with Ibn Kathīr transmitting the definition that abb is the pasturage of the cattle, the green growth that no human hand plants: the grass of the open range, the spring flush after rain, the browse of hill and wadi. The word is celebrated for two reasons. First, its rarity: it is a singular occurrence (a *fard* in the vocabulary of the exegetes), one of the handful of hapax Qur'anic words whose precise sense the first generations had to ask about and define — a standing reminder that the Qur'an was received by a community that interrogated its own language to understand its Book, the seed of the whole Islamic science of lexicography. Second, its meaning: the inclusion in the divine inventory of a growth that *no human being farms at all* — the unplanted, unowned, wild feed of the flocks — is the list's final, quiet magnification. The sūrah has now run the full spectrum of provision's dependency: from the most human-worked (the walled garden, v. 30) to the least (the wild pasture, v. 31) — and the same "We" signs both ends. The orchard with its gardener and the hillside with none are equal instances of divine giving; cultivation changes the manner of the gift, not the Giver.

**Why the Menu Ends With the Cattle's Plate**

The order of the last two items — fruit, then pasture; the human dessert before the animal fodder — completes the economy that v. 28 began (grape beside herbage). And the design answers a question any thoughtful reader of the passage eventually asks: *why does a sūrah about human ingratitude spend its last item on grass for animals?* The answer unfolds across two verses. Within the passage's own logic: because the food-chain of man runs through the beast — the pasture feeds the flock, the flock gives milk, wool, meat, and labor; the cattle's plate is one link beneath the human one, and the God who feeds the feeder feeds the fed. The Qur'an states the chain explicitly in the parallel passage of Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt, in the very next verses after our sūrah's sister-listing: *"and the earth He spread out... and brought forth therefrom its water and its pasture (maraʿuhā); and the mountains He set firm — a provision for you and for your flocks (mattāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum)"* (79:30–33). Beyond the chain's logic, however, lies the doctrine: *provision is not anthropocentric*. God's rizq embraces His creatures as creatures:

> *"And there is no creature on the earth but that its provision is upon God; and He knows its resting-place and its depository. All is in a manifest Book."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The pasture of v. 31 is that verse made green: the Giver who feeds the human family has never once left the sparrow and the she-goat off His books. The believer who sees his own bread in this passage learns, in its last item, to see his livestock's — and, by extension, every creature's — bread beside it.

**The Wild Growth and the Wild Grace**

There is a devotional lesson in the *unfarmed* character of abb that the contemplative tradition has always loved. Every other item in vv. 27–30 implies human toil: sowing, training, walling, grafting, harvesting. The pasture grows without any of it — rain falls, and the hillside is green for the flock by morning, unbilled, unplowed, unearned. As such, abb is the list's purest image of *grace*: provision that precedes labor, that no wage-payer accounts for, that mocks the arithmetic of merit. And the sūrah has placed it deliberately at the *end* of the inventory, where lists make their final point: after you have counted everything your work helped raise, remember the pasture your work never touched — and reckon how much of everything else was also, at root, like that. The gardener's wall did not make the seed live. The farmer's plow did not make the rain fall. The wild pasture is the whole passage's confession compressed into one word: *we are all graziers of another's green*.

**From Abb to the Table: The Verse's Modern Yield**

For the modern reader, the fruit-and-pasture verse closes the inventory with two practical disciplines. First, the discipline of *variety-gratitude*: the produce aisle's year-round global medley — mango with oatmeal in a northern winter — is this verse multiplied by trade; the believer thanks not only for the fruit but for the reach that brought it, which is also from Him. Second, the discipline of *creature-care*: a faith whose scripture lists the animals' pasture among the mercies its God enumerates cannot be casual about the beasts in its charge. The Sunnah's rulings on animals — the prohibition of overburdening, the command of mercy in slaughter, the Prophet's ﷺ report of the woman damned for a cat and the man saved for a dog (al-Bukhārī 3318; Muslim 2244) — are the legal midrash, as it were, of v. 31. The verse hands the reader the passage's final exam before the accounting of v. 32: you have looked at your food all the way down the chain — have you yet found the One at the top of it? And have you treated His other fed creatures as fellow pensioners of His pasture?

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:32

> **as sustenance for you and for your flocks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Matāʿan Lakum wa-li-Anʿāmikum: The Accounting Read Aloud**

The inventory closes with its summary clause, and the whole passage is gathered up like a bill presented: *matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum* — "as sustenance (enjoyment) for you and for your flocks." The noun *matāʿ*, from the root m-t-ʿ (benefit, use, enjoyment), is the Qur'an's sober, almost wry term for the usable goods of this world — things to be *used*, enjoyed for their season. The same word recurs whenever the Book wants to frame worldly provision in perspective: *"And this life of the world is but matāʿ — and the Hereafter is the abode of permanence"* (40:39; cf. 13:26; 42:36). The choice of word is thus the passage's final theology: the entire inventory of vv. 25–31 — water, earth, grain, vine, herbage, olive, palm, garden, fruit, pasture — is summed as *matāʿ*: real, good, given for enjoyment — and temporary, instrumental, a provision for a journey rather than a destination. The believer receives the whole feast and reads the label heaven has put on it: enjoy — as travelers enjoy an inn, not as settlers worship a city. And the double recipients — *you and your flocks* — close the passage's circle of mercy: provision for the rational servant and provision for the beasts he tends, from the same rain, through the same split earth, by the same hand.

**The Verse's Answer to the Passage's Question**

Verse 24 commanded: *let man look at his food.* Eight verses later, the look has reached its end, and v. 32 states what the look was for. The food-chain, traced honestly from cloud to table, has one Author and one destination: from Him, to you — matāʿan *lakum*, for you. The passage has conducted, in agricultural terms, the oldest argument in scripture: the argument from providence. Its form here is inductive and cumulative — item, item, item, item, item, item, item — seven items, no gaps, no third party, no link where a human being stands as origin. Rain is not manufactured; grain is not invented; the vine's patience is not engineered; the olive's oil and the palm's sweetness were standard before man existed; even the garden, the most human of the items, grows by borrowed powers. The classical exegetes read the passage exactly as such a proof, and the Qur'an endorses the reading by the way it ends parallel catalogues elsewhere — with the call to reason about origins:

> *"And He it is who sends down water from the sky; and thereby We brought forth the growth of everything... Eat of what your Lord provided you... This is God's creation; now show me what those besides Him have created."* (Qur'an 31:10–11)

*"Now show me what those besides Him have created"* is v. 32's implicit punchline: the menu is complete; the table is set; name another Chef.

**For You and Your Flocks: The Shared Economy of Mercy**

The clause's second half — *wa-li-anʿāmikum* — deserves its final meditation, gathered now from all the passage's animal-notes (the herbage of v. 28, the pasture of v. 31, the beasts' plate beneath man's). The word *anʿām* (cattle, flocks, the domestic animals) shares its root-territory with *niʿmah*, blessing: the classical linguists heard in the pairing the sound of blessing and the blessed-beast together — the flock as the visible, walking form of a household's blessing, as the Bedouin's idiom still says: the man of many anʿām is the man manifestly in niʿmah. The Qur'an's economy of provision deliberately binds the two households of man — his dependents and his animals — into one ledger of divine care, and legislates accordingly: the zakāh-system taxes the anʿām with the crops (the herd has its zakāh), the slaughter-ruling commands mercy in the very act of use ("sharpen the blade and spare the animal," Muslim 1955), and the passage before us makes the beast's feed an item in God's own inventory. The doctrine underneath: dominion is trusteeship. Man is given management of creatures whose provision God has personally guaranteed (11:6) — and the trustee answers for his handling of what the Owner feeds. Modern industrial husbandry, with its economy of the creature as unit, sits before this verse as before a court; and the individual believer's handling of his own small dominion — the work-animal, the pet, the yard's birds — is the verse's most personal application.

**The Two Uses of a Blessing**

The word matāʿ, rightly weighed, also sets the passage's moral exit. A provision named *for enjoyment* carries within it the fork of gratitude and ingratitude the sūrah has prosecuted since v. 17. The same rain, split earth, grain, and fruit can be *received* — eaten with the name of God, shared from its due (6:141: "give its due on the day of harvest"), thanked over morsel by morsel (Muslim 2734) — or *covered*: consumed as if self-generated, hoarded, wasted, denied. The Qur'an names both exits from the feast, and binds the second to a warning our sūrah is about to dramatize:

> *"So let man look at his food..." — and the Book's verdict on the one who eats without seeing: "Have you seen him who denies the religion? That is he who repulses the orphan, and does not urge the feeding of the poor."* (Qur'an 107:1–3, the sister-passage of table-ethics)

Ingratitude at the table, the Qur'an teaches, never stays at the table: the man who does not see the Giver soon does not see the other eaters either. The matāʿ that fails to produce thanks fails, in the same motion, to produce generosity — and the sūrah's final movement (vv. 33–42) will show the final state of the ungrateful face.

**The Pivot: The Feast Ends, the Trumpet Sounds**

Notice, finally, where v. 32 stands in the sūrah's architecture: it is the last word of the entire argument-from-mercy. Everything before it — the ethics of the seeker (vv. 1–10), the honored pages (vv. 11–16), the biography of man (vv. 17–23), the chain of food (vv. 24–32) — has been preparation, evidence, invitation. With the accounting read aloud — *for you and for your flocks* — the prosecution rests and the invitation closes. What follows, from v. 33 to the sūrah's end, is not argument but event: the Piercing Cry, the fleeing kin, and the two kinds of faces. The placement is itself the deepest theology of the passage: mercy is announced *first*, in seven items, at length, item by item — and judgment arrives only after mercy has been fully displayed. The God of Sūrah ʿAbasa is a God whose severity is preceded, always, by an itemized generosity; the reader who stands at v. 33 stands there having been fed, warned, and told exactly Who fed him. That order — mercy, then the Cry — is the order of the divine character itself, and the sūrah has now made it the order of its own verses.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:33

> **So when the Piercing Cry does come,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-idhā Jāʾat: The Grammar of an Event Already on the Road**

The final movement opens with a construction whose very grammar preaches: *fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhah* — "So when the Piercing Cry comes." The particle *idhā* with the future sense does not say "if" but "when" — the conditional of certainty; and the verb is the same "come" (jāʾa) used at v. 2 of the blind man and at v. 21 of death: in this sūrah, the important things *come* — they arrive on their own schedule, as visitors, as appointments. The Piercing Cry is thus presented as an event already en route, moving toward the reader at a speed the reader cannot know; the verse places every human being in the position of a city that hears a distant sound approaching. And with masterful suspense, the sentence begun here — "when the Cry comes, *the Day when a man will flee...*" — is suspended across an entire verse: Arabic syntax holding its breath, the flight described before the sentence completes. The sūrah's last ten verses are one long exhale after this inbreath.

**Al-Ṣākhkhah: The Name That Is a Sound**

The noun the Qur'an coins for this event is onomatopoeic thunder: *al-ṣākhkhah*, from the root ṣ-kh-kh, an intensive of ṣakhkh — to cry out, to deafen with a cry. The form is of the strongest intensification: not a shout, but a cry so loud it *becomes* the Day's name — the Deafening, the Piercing Cry. It is one of the Qur'an's many names for the Day of Resurrection, each name a different sense-organ's verdict on it: al-Qāriʿah, the Striking Calamity (101); al-Ṭāmmah, the Overwhelming (79:34); al-Ḥāqqah, the Inevitable Reality (69:1); al-Wāqiʿah, the Encompassing Event (56:1); al-Ghāshiyah, the Enveloper (88:1); al-Ṣāḥḥah, the Blast (the Qur'an asks: *"And what will make thee know what the Ṣāḥḥah is?"* — 80:10's sister question at 101:3); and this sūrah's own Ṣākhkhah — so central that the early community named the whole chapter after it (as noted in the introduction). The variety of names is itself doctrine: no single human faculty can take the Day in, so revelation approaches it ear-first, then eye, then heart — each name a handle for a hand that cannot grasp the whole. Al-Ṣākhkhah is the *auditory* name: the Day that begins as a sound — which is why the sūrah, having spent nine verses on tables and gardens, now pierces the ear before it shows the sights.

**The Second Blast: What the Cry Is in Event-Sequence**

In the Qur'an's eschatological sequence, the Ṣākhkhah is the second of the two trumpet-blasts blown by the angel charged with the Trumpet (named Isrāfīl in the hadith literature; the Qur'an calls him the angel who will blow it, without naming him). The doctrine is stated in the verse every commentator cites for this one:

> *"And the Trumpet is blown, and all who are in the heavens and the earth swoon — save whom God wills. Then it is blown again, and behold, they stand, looking."* (Qur'an 39:68)

The first blast ends history: every living thing — mankind, beasts, the angels of the heavens — collapses in death, and the universe holds its silence across an interval God does not describe. The second blast is the Ṣākhkhah of our verse: the raising — *"and behold, they stand, looking"* — the dead of every century standing, blinking, looking. The Prophet ﷺ, in the long intercession-narrative of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, describes himself as the first to be raised after that second blast, finding the prophets before him like a man with birds over his head, and Mūsā standing holding one of the pillars of the Throne — the scene's strangeness is itself part of its truth: the Day is not a metaphor, and the reports refuse to let it become one. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on the sister-verse of Sūrah al-Qāriʿah, transmits the early exegetes' remark on the ṣakhkhah's effects: a blast so absolute that mountains shear, skies split, the seas ignite — the created order returning to the unformed state of its beginning, *"the Day when the earth is changed to other than the earth, and the heavens"* (14:48).

**Why Sound: The Theology of the Deafening**

Meditate on the choice of *sound* as the resurrection's trigger, and the sūrah's own logic appears. The food-passage (vv. 24–32) was addressed to the *sight* — look at your food — and to the *taste* — the tasted thing. The audience that would not see the mercies displayed before its eyes, and would not taste them as given, is finally reached through the sense that cannot be closed: the ear has no lid. The Deafening is the audio-answer to a world that closed its eyes and swallowed without thanks — a cry that no covering can cover (the root of the sūrah's other theme, k-f-r, is outmatched at last by a sound too total to be covered over). There is also the plain psychology of alarm, which every human generation has engineered for itself — sirens, klaxons, the air-raid wail — because sound is the sense that commands the body before the mind consents. The Day's designers of mercy have used the same channel: not argument now (the argument closed at v. 32) but summons; not the invitation's *look* but the coroner's *rise*. And for the believer, the same sound wears a different face: the ṣākhkhah is, in the end, the *call to prayer* of eternity — the final adhān, if you will — at which every grave's occupant rises to answer a congregation fourteen centuries wide. The believer is taught to hear in every ordinary adhān a rehearsal of that blast: the daily call as the gentle reminder that the last call is coming; and the Prophet ﷺ instructed the hearer of the adhān to answer it with words of faith, adding: *"and when you hear the ṣākhkhah? " — the tradition names no liturgy for that day; the liturgy for it is the life that was lived before it.*

**The Suspended Sentence: From Cry to Flight**

The verse's syntax — the "when"-clause hanging unanswered across the verse-break — mirrors the event's own psychology: the blast sounds, and for the space of one verse, nothing is yet said to happen; then, with v. 34, the effect arrives: *fleeing*. The sūrah's film-craft should not be missed: first the sound (v. 33), then the flight (vv. 34–36), then the reason (v. 37), then the faces (vv. 38–42). Sound, motion, motive, countenance — the sequence of a documentary camera that cuts from the loudspeaker to the crowd. The next verse trains the camera on a single man, and the sūrah's gallery of faces — begun with a frown in v. 1 — now reaches its terrible final exhibit: the face of a man running from his own brother.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:34

> **that Day when a man will flee from his brother,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yawma Yafirru: The Day the Social World Collapses**

Verse 34 completes the suspended sentence of v. 33 — and completes it with a shock: *yawma yafirru al-marʾu min akhīhi* — "the Day when a man will flee from his brother." The verb *fara/farra* — to flee, to escape, to bolt from danger — is the verb of prey before the predator; and the Qur'an attaches it, in this passage, to the most shocking object imaginable: not from an enemy, not from a beast, but from one's *brother* — *akh*, the singular institution of Arabian society's core. To the first audience, this was the reversal of everything "brother" meant. The brother in the tribal world was refuge and ransom: you ran *to* him — to his tent, his sword, his covenant; his blood was your blood-money; his protection was your armor. Meccan poetry and law alike were built on the axiom that a man stands or falls with his brother. Now the Qur'an describes a Day on which the axiom inverts: the brother — the lifelong shelter — becomes the one you run *from*, and every social bond of the old world proves unable to bear the weight of that hour. The verse is social physics: it announces the collapse of the penultimate — every structure humans lean on instead of God — under the gravity of the Ultimate. When the heavens split and the records open, the intermediary institutions of meaning (tribe, patron, party, dynasty) cannot stand between a soul and its Maker, and everyone knows it in an instant; the running is the knowing made visible.

**The Ladder of Attachment: Why the Brother Is First**

The sūrah then spends three verses on the pedigree of the flight (brother, v. 34; mother and father, v. 35; spouse and children, v. 36), and the order has occupied the commentators ever since. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī note the gradation and read it as an ascending scale of intimacy: from the brother — the lateral bond, the companion of childhood and the ally of adulthood — the flight climbs to the parents — the vertical bond, the authors of one's very body — then to the spouse — the covenantal bond, "the garment" of 2:187 — and finally to the children — the visceral bond, the pieces of the heart walking outside the chest. Each rung of the ladder makes the same announcement louder: if a man will flee *even* from his brother, he will flee *all the more* from those nearer; and by the time the list reaches the children, the reader is standing before the total impossibility of proxy-salvation through love. The order also moves, as the exegetes observed, from the most *chosen* bonds (brotherhood of alliance, marriage) toward the most *un-chosen* and deepest (parents, offspring) — as though to close every exit in sequence: the bonds you built and the bonds you were born with and the bonds you bore — none will hold.

**The Parallel Passage: The Ransom That Is Refused**

The Qur'an returns to this scene in Sūrah al-Maʿārij, and the parallel text supplies what our sūrah leaves implicit — the logic of the flight:

> *"A friend will not ask a friend, while they are made to see one another. The sinner would gladly ransom himself from the punishment of that Day by his sons, and his spouse, and his brother, and his kin who sheltered him, and all who are on the earth — all of it — that it might save him. Nay, truly it is a burning flame."* (Qur'an 70:10–15)

Read together, the two passages complete each other's psychology. ʿAbasa shows the flight; al-Maʿārij shows the motive behind it — the sinner's sudden, total arithmetic: *anything, anyone, everything — to be delivered from what is coming.* The flight of v. 34 is not hatred of the brother; it is the drowning man's clawing away from anyone who might pull him under — every bond becoming a potential handhold of liability, every loved face a reminder of a shared record. And the Qur'an's refusal of the ransom is the doctrine of individual account in its most terrible form: the Day admits no substitution, no collective bargaining, no family plan. The verse's sister-doctrine is stated in Sūrah al-Najm: *"That no laden soul bears the load of another; and that man shall have nothing but what he strives for"* (53:38–39).

**The Deeper Reading: Love Survives, but Self-Concern Eclipses It**

Theologians have long asked whether vv. 34–36 teach that love itself dies on that Day. The tradition's more careful answer — drawn from the whole Qur'an — is no: love is not abolished; it is *outweighed*. The same Book that paints this flight promises, a few sūrahs away, the restoration of every righteous bond: *"And those who believe, and whose offspring follow them in faith — We shall join their offspring to them"* (52:21); *"Gardens of Eden, which they enter, with the righteous among their fathers and their spouses and their offspring"* (13:23). The flight, then, belongs to the hour of reckoning, not to the eternity after it — it is the temporary eclipse of love by terror, precisely as the sunna of this world already rehearses in miniature: in the courtroom of even human justice, the accused's family sits behind him but cannot stand *for* him; love attends, but the sentence is singular. On the Day of the Cry, that courtroom is universalized, and the terror is proportioned to the stakes. The believer's comfort is the exact inverse of the denier's panic: the same individual account that lets no brother ransom you also lets no brother ruin you — each soul meets God on its own record, and *"whoever has done an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7–8).

**Modern Rehearsals of the Flight**

The verse describes the Hereafter, but human history keeps staging previews, and the sūrah's first hearers — who had seen families split over the faith within their own households — knew it. In the early persecutions of Makkah, parents disowned believing sons and sons disowned believing fathers; the Prophet's ﷺ own uncle Abū Lahab led the clan against him. In every persecution since — and in every collapse, famine, and shipwreck — the thinness of blood under pressure has been documented again: the moment survival is at stake, the social bonds that seemed load-bearing reveal their joints. The Qur'an does not cite these previews cynically; it cites them prophetically: the Day of the Cry does not create the insufficiency of human bonds — it *reveals* what was always latent in them, by introducing a pressure no earthly bond was ever engineered to bear. The verse's practical yield is therefore a redirection of trust that can begin today: love your brother, honor your parents, cleave to your spouse, cherish your children — and stake none of your salvation on any of them; build the vertical bond now, in time, so that on the Day of horizontal collapse you are standing on the only ground that holds. The next two verses will remove the last rungs of the ladder one by one, until v. 37 states the reason with the sūrah's signature economy.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:35

> **and his mother and his father,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Flight Climbs to the Deepest Debt**

Verse 35 removes the second rung of the ladder, and with it the verse performs its most counterintuitive act: *wa-ummihī wa-abīhi* — "and his mother and his father." If fleeing a brother shocked the tribal ear, fleeing parents strikes the human ear as such — in every culture, at every time. The mother is the first world any human inhabited: his first darkness and first light, the body that carried him at its own cost, the first voice he knew, the name he first spoke. The father is the first roof and first road: the one who taught, provided, defended. Between them they are, in the Qur'an's own accounting, the two persons after God most deserving of the human being's lifelong devotion — and the Book pairs that devotion with the worship of God in a single verse, joined by divine command:

> *"And thy Lord decreed that you worship none but Him, and goodness to parents. Whether one or both of them reach old age with thee, say not to them 'uff,' nor repulse them, but speak to them generous words. And lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy, and say: My Lord, have mercy on them, as they raised me when I was small."* (Qur'an 17:23–24)

That is the Qur'an's standing doctrine of the parental bond in this world: not even the syllable of impatience — not an *uff* — may cross the child's lips. And now this sūrah describes a Day on which the child *runs from them*. The juxtaposition of the two texts is the measure of the Day: what no decent person would do in a lifetime of ordinary life, the terror of the Cry will make everyone long to do in a moment — because on that Day, standing beside one's parents will mean sharing scrutiny with them, and no one can answer for anyone. The mother who would have died for her child in this world cannot die for him in that one; the child who owed the parents everything owes God more.

**The Parents Cannot Be Ransomed, but They Can Be Restored**

The verse's terror has often been read — especially in homiletic literature — as though the Day abolishes the family. The tradition's fuller reading, already noted at v. 34, must be kept in view: the flight is the hour's necessity, not eternity's constitution. The Qur'an's own promises of family reunion for the righteous are explicit and warm: the Gardens entered *"with the righteous among their fathers and their spouses and their offspring"* (13:23) — where, note well, the *fathers* lead the list of the restored. The Prophet ﷺ was asked by a Companion about the highest of degrees and described the people of ʿIllīyyīn, among whom the family-bonds of the believers are gathered and perfected. The doctrine balances in one sentence: on the Day of accounting, every soul stands alone; after the accounting, the righteous stand together forever. What vv. 35–36 describe is the courtroom, not the feast; the severing is procedural, not final — for those whose accounts end well.

**Ummihī Wa-Abīhi: The Order Within the Order**

Students of the Qur'an have long noticed a detail of this verse's word-order that became, centuries later, a celebrated point of Qur'anic ethics: the mother is named *before* the father. In a patriarchal seventh-century Arabia where the genealogies ran through fathers and the poetry memorialized sons, the divine order of this flight-list — and of the parallel list in the exclusion-verses of Sūrah al-Tawbah ("...nor their fathers, nor their sons, nor their brothers, nor their tribes" — 9:24 runs the male order) — places *umm* first here. The classical commentators, Ibn Kathīr among them, note the point and trace it to the hadith's ranking of the mother's claim: a man asked the Prophet ﷺ, "Who is most deserving of my good company?" He said: *"Thy mother."* The man said: "Then who?" — *"Thy mother."* — "Then who?" — *"Thy mother."* — "Then who?" — *"Thy father."* (al-Bukhārī 5971; Muslim 2548). The triple answer entered Islamic civilization as law and custom: the mother's triple claim, the father's quarter. That the Qur'an's flight-list of vv. 34–36 — brother, then mother-and-father, then spouse-and-children — mirrors something of that ordering (with the mother's bond named first among the parents) shows the same doctrine in the Book's own syntax: the nearer the debt, the nearer to the front of the list of what terror will test.

**A Psychological Reading: The Flight From One's Origins**

Beyond the theology, the verse carries a psychological truth that any reader can verify in himself: the bonds we flee under pressure are the bonds that made us — because they are the ones that know us. A man may hide his record from strangers, from colleagues, even from his brother; but before his parents stands the whole of what they saw him become — and on the Day the Cry sounds, to be seen by those who knew one best will be its own piercing. The parents' presence, in the imagination of that hour, is the mirror before which the sinner cannot pose. In this world, running from one's parents is already the paradigm of the unexamined life — the young man who will not be known by those who knew him first; the old man who cannot bear his father's look. Verse 35 universalizes the pattern at the eschatological scale: before God, all intimacy becomes transparency, and transparency becomes, for the unprepared, flight. The believer's training is therefore exactly the inverse practice now: to be known — to keep, or restore, the bonds of honest witness with one's own origins; to visit the parents while they live, as the Prophet ﷺ commanded with startling severity when a man asked about his obligations to a father or mother long dead — the instruction became the famous ruling on filial piety performed by proxy: prayer for them, kindness to their friends, honor to their graves. The one who can bear being known by them will not need to flee from them.

**Standing Beside, Not For**

One final observation completes the verse: the Qur'an never says the parents on that Day are *hostile* or *accusing* — the flight is in the fleeing, not necessarily in the faces fled from. The mother may be standing in hope; the father in mercy; the son runs not from their enmity but from their *helplessness to save him* — as the parallel of al-Maʿārij states, the sinner's kin would be *offered* as ransom and the ransom refused (70:11–13). In that light, the verse's pathos reaches its depth: the last human tragedy of the old world is a man running from the people who love him most precisely *because* their love has reached the end of its jurisdiction. What every parent felt at the door of the operating room — love with no authority to enter — is the universal condition of every bond on that Day; and only One's love will still have jurisdiction there. The sūrah's reader is left holding the verse's practical question: while the door of mercy is open — while the parents live and the Judge invites — what am I doing with the bonds I will one day be unable to hide behind, and the Account I will be unable to outsource? The next verse takes the ladder's last rung: the spouse and the children.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:36

> **and his spouse and his children.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣāḥibah and Banūn: The Two Loves That Anchor a Life**

The ladder's final rung holds the two bonds nearest the heart: *wa-ṣāḥibatihī wa-banīhi* — "and his spouse and his children." The word for spouse is telling: *ṣāḥibah*, the feminine of ṣāḥib — companion, associate, the one who accompanies — from the root ṣ-ḥ-b, the root of companionship itself (ṣuḥbah) and of the Prophet's ﷺ Companions (Ṣaḥābah). The Qur'an does not name the wife here by the legal term (zawj) but by the relational one: his *companion* — the person with whom life itself was shared, its hardships halved and its sweetnesses doubled. Elsewhere the Book gives this bond its most intimate image: *"They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them"* (2:187) — each the other's covering, protection, nearness, dignity. And the children — *banūn* — are named with the Arabic word the old society lived for: sons, heirs, the continuations of a man's name in a world where posterity was the only immortality on offer. The Meccan audience built their whole economy of meaning on māl wa-banūn — wealth and sons — the very pairing the Qur'an quotes back at them in its audit of that economy: *"Wealth and sons are the adornment of the life of this world"* (18:46). Verse 36 now takes the two anchors of that world — the companion and the heirs — and includes them in the flight: on the Day of the Cry, the man runs from the woman who was his garment and from the sons who were his future.

**Wealth and Sons as Fitnah: The Qur'an's Warning About the Dearest Bonds**

The deeper reason this rung belongs last is theological: of all earthly loves, the spouse and children are the ones most likely to be *load-bearing in the wrong place* — loved as the ends of life rather than its trusts, leaned on as the substance of identity rather than its ornaments. The Qur'an names this danger with a word that startles when first met: *"And know that your wealth and your children are a trial (fitnah), and that God — with Him is a tremendous reward"* (8:28). A *trial* — the same word used for the ordeals of persecution: the dearest gifts are a test precisely because they are dearest, and the test's question is one word: *from whom, ultimately, did they come — and to whom do they ultimately answer?* The man who answers rightly holds his spouse and children as trusts, loving them without making them his god; the man who answers wrongly builds his soul's house on what vv. 34–36 prove will be evacuated. Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn makes the diagnosis a parable of two wives — the believer's wealth and family as his enemies' channel of ruin:

> *"O you who believe! Let not your wealth and your children divert you from the remembrance of God. And whoever does that — those are the losers."* (Qur'an 63:9)

Verse 36 of ʿAbasa is that warning projected onto the Day itself: what you allowed to divert you from remembrance will, on that Day, be what you try to divert yourself with — offered, like al-Maʿārij's ransom (70:12–13), and refused.

**The Tenderness Before the Terror: What Islam Gives the Family**

The verse must be heard in the whole Qur'an's register to be heard rightly — a register in which the family is not only tested but *sanctified*. The bond of the spouse is half the religion: the Prophet ﷺ said that the one married has completed half his faith (al-Bayhaqī, from Anas). The children are, in the Sunnah's own idiom, a continuing mercy and credit: the child's discipline, teaching, and praying-for are among the few deeds whose reward outlives the parent; and the parent's death is consoled by the child's ongoing righteousness. Most tellingly for our passage: the Qur'an *itself* commands the love of family to be expressed as care for their souls, not merely their comforts — *"O you who believe! Shield yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones"* (66:6). Shield them — the verb of the verse is the verb of rescue, the fireman's verb. The believer is thus trained, in this life, to do for his family what v. 36 says no one will be able to do on that Day: to stand *between* his loved ones and the Fire — by teaching, by example, by prayer — while standing between them and the Fire is still possible. The horror of the flight is the exact inverse of the duty of the household: the verse shows the hour when rescue-delegations end, so that the believer will spend his life inside the window when they do not.

**The Children: Love That Cannot Be Legated**

A word must be spent on the children specifically, because the ancient hearer's — and the modern ear's — first protest is: *but a parent would die for a child; how can he flee?* The parallel of al-Maʿārij answers formally — the sinner *would* give the children, and everything on earth, as his ransom; the ransom is not accepted (70:13–14) — but the emotional mathematics deserves honesty. In this world, parental love routinely spends itself on the child: the mother's body in gestation, the father's decades of labor, the generational transfers of the wealthy — all of it is the daily news of love paying out. What the Day of the Cry reveals is the boundary of even that love: it can *give* everything, but it cannot *answer* for anyone. Love is real; love is mighty; love is not a currency in the court of deeds. The child's record is the child's; the parent's is the parent's; and the sudden arithmetic of that hour — every parent's account and every child's account simultaneously coming due — makes the two flee from each other, each realizing that the other's presence adds not one atom of covering, and that the scrutiny of the other's eyes is part of the exposure. It is the courtroom's logic at family scale: a mother may weep in the gallery, but she may not take the stand for her son.

**The Practical Yield: Audit the Ledger Before the Cry**

The verse's final gift to its reader is chronological. The sūrah has described a Day of unwished-for truths — but every truth of that Day is available now, at low cost, in the currency of humility. Now, while the pages of the record are still open, the believer can do what v. 36's man cannot: face the account *with* his family rather than from them — repent together, pray together, make the household a place where the Day of the Cry is spoken of over dinner, so that when its sound comes, no member of the household is found fleeing the others. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for exactly this inversion of the verse — that his family be his joy:

> *"O God, make my family my coolness (qurrata ʿayn)..."* — in the full canonical wording: *"And make my family coolness for me, and me coolness for them."* (from the comprehensive duʿāʾ in the Musnads and Sunan, reported from the Prophet's morning and evening remembrances)

A home of shared faith is the anti-type of v. 36: the one institution of this world that the Day will not dissolve but *promote* — for the families that prayed together are the families promised one another forever (13:23; 52:21). The verse's terror, rightly read, is thus an invitation with a deadline.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:37

> **For every man that Day his affair shall suffice him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Delivered: Shanun Yughnīhi**

The flight's explanation arrives in the sūrah's most quotable clause: *li-kulli imriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shanun yughnīhi* — "for every man among them, that Day, is an affair that suffices him." The noun *shan* is a rich one: affair, concern, business, state, condition — with an older overtone of dignity and splendor (the lexicons list shan as a state of beauty or gravity, a person's "standing"). The verb *yughnīhi* — suffices him, occupies him fully, renders him independent of all else — is from the root gh-n-y, the sūrah's own signature root: it gave us *istaghnā* at v. 5, the man who *deemed himself self-sufficient*. The frame is now complete, and it is one of the most elegant ironies in Qur'anic composition. The sūrah opened with a man who thought he needed nothing (istaghnā) — and it closes its reckoning with the discovery of the only real self-sufficiency: on that Day, each person's own shan will be the whole of what he has. The pseudo-sufficiency of wealth, rank, and alliance collapses; the true sufficiency — the self's own record, its own state before its Maker — stands alone. Whoever built his independence on anything else will find, on that Day, that all his props have been withdrawn at once; whoever invested in the account itself will find it *suffices* — standing for him, answering for him, radiating for him. One root, two destinies: the istaghnā of v. 5 versus the ighnāʾ of v. 37.

**Radical Individuality: The Doctrine of the Untransferrable Soul**

What the clause teaches formally is what the tradition calls the individuality of reckoning: judgment is addressed to persons, not to groups. The Qur'an legislates it across its whole length: *"Every soul is pledged for what it earned"* (74:38); *"That no laden soul bears the load of another"* (53:38); *"And fear a Day when no soul shall avail another anything, nor shall intercession be accepted for it, nor shall equivalent be taken from it, nor shall they be helped"* (2:48, addressed — pointedly — to a religious community that trusted in its ancestry). That last verse names the full set of collapsed currencies: patronage (soul for soul), intercession, ransom, aid — the four pillars of every human's lifelong risk-management portfolio, and all four are declared non-negotiable at the bar of that Day — save by God's leave, as the exception-clauses elsewhere make precise (2:255; 20:109: intercession belongs to God and is granted *to whom He wills and whose word pleases Him* — the Prophet's ﷺ own great intercession, in the authentic narratives, is for the release of reckoning itself, by permission). Verse 37 is the psychological rendering of the doctrine: the reason a man flees brother, parents, wife, and sons is that *his own shan yughnīhi* — his own case engrosses him totally. The Arabic even permits the secondary sense the older commentators relished: his own affair will be his *splendor* — for the believer, the shan is a dignity that clothes him; the very word that explains the sinner's flight describes the saint's glory. One clause, two eternities, split by the object of the sufficiency.

**The Prophet's Answer to ʿĀʾishah: The Verse on Nakedness**

The verse carries with it one of the most beloved exchanges in the hadith corpus, transmitted from ʿĀʾishah and cited for this very verse by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"You will be gathered barefoot, naked, uncircumcised."* — and ʿĀʾishah said: "O Messenger of God, men and women together, looking upon one another?" — and he said: *"O ʿĀʾishah, the matter is graver than that — each of them, that Day, will have what preoccupies him."* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the gathering-hadīth in its several wordings; the ʿĀʾishah-exchange in the form answering with the meaning of v. 37 is in the Musnad of Aḥmad and the Sunan)

The exchange is a miniature of the whole passage. Her question is the question of *this* world — modesty as we know it, the social self, the endless concern with who sees whom. His answer is the physics of *that* Day: the ego's entire economy has been foreclosed; shame-as-social-concern has been consumed by concern-as-destiny. And notice the mercy inside the severity: the total preoccupation of each soul with its own shan is, in one aspect, the Day's built-in privacy — no one has attention to spare for another's exposure, because everyone's whole attention is demanded by his own standing. The very terror that empties the world of helpers empties it also of spectators: before God, the courtroom is crowded and utterly alone.

**A World Without Networking**

For the modern reader, the verse's doctrine lands on a culture whose central technology is precisely the *avoidance of standing alone*: the network, the connection, the referral, the "who do you know." A civilization of intermediaries — résumés padded by names, causes advanced by patronage, even righteousness performed for audiences — is a civilization structured on the assumption that no one will ever truly face the audit alone. Verse 37 is the annunciation, fourteen centuries in advance, of a court with no intermediaries: no agent, no character-witness, no sponsor, no crowd-fund. The hadith's nakedness was already the image of it — stripped of the wardrobe of status — and the shan is the final stripping: not even one's own public image will attend, because the only self present will be the one that actually did the deeds. The believer's practical strategy is therefore the inverse of the networker's: invest in what will be in the room — the deeds themselves, the secret charities, the prayers of the night no one hears, the truth spoken when it cost something. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of a man who will be brought on that Day and shown his record and made to confess it deed by deed, until, having no excuse, he is pardoned by God's mercy (Muslim 2749) — the narrative of a man whose only asset in the room was the truth of his own record and the mercy of his Judge. That is the shan that suffices.

**The Two Sufficiencies and the Reader's Choice**

Close the loop with v. 5 one final time, because the sūrah frames its whole argument between the two gh-n-y words. The man of istaghnā — the self-sufficient chief being courted in Makkah — believed his wealth and standing made him independent: of the reminder, of the reminder's Sender, and (by implication) of the Day. The sūrah's verdict on that independence arrives here: it was a loan against a currency that will not exist. The only ighnāʾ that Day is one's own affair — and even that suffices only as *witness*, not as *savior*, unless God is pleased. So the verse, read from beginning to end of the sūrah, is an invitation to reorder one's portfolio while the market of mercy is still open: to transfer one's trust from the untransferable props (kin, wealth, rank, audience) to the One who will be the Day's entire context — *"Whosoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him do righteous work and associate none in the worship of his Lord"* (18:110). The next five verses will show the two possible outcomes of that reordering, written on faces: the shining, laughing faces of those whose shan sufficed them in mercy, and the dust-covered faces of those whose shan sufficed them in ruin. Between the two portraits, every reader of the sūrah stands — and decides.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:38

> **Faces that Day shall be shining, radiant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wujūhun Yawmaʾidhin Musfirah: The Light-Verse of the Book's Ending**

With verse 38 the sūrah turns its camera from the fleeing crowds to their countenances, and the final gallery opens: *wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfirah* — "faces that Day shall be shining, radiant." The adjective *musfirah* is a small lexicon in itself. The classical lexicographers derive it from the root s-f-r in its sense of *brightness*: it is the word used for the dawn's first whitening — *isfār al-ṣubḥ*, the sky's face turning light. A musfirah face is, literally, a *morning-face*: darkness passed over, light arriving, the countenance itself becoming a dawn. The metaphor chosen for the believer's resurrection-face is therefore not metal's gleam nor fire's glare but *daybreak* — the gentlest and most hopeful of lights, the light that follows a night rather than one that burns without a before. And there is a resonance across the sūrah that the attentive reciter may hear: the same root-territory (s-f-r) touched vv. 15–16, where the honored pages rest in the hands of the *safarah* — the scribes, whose name the lexicians also link to *unveiling*, the disclosing scribes. The scribes unveil; the faces are unveiled: the Book's light-heritage and the believer's face-heritage share a root, as the tradition's lovers of Qur'anic wordplay never failed to notice. What the pages are — disclosed, shining — the faces of their bearers will become.

**The Sūrah's Great Arc: From Frown to Dawn**

Read the verse against v. 1 and the whole sūrah closes its circle with a beauty that must be savored. ʿAbasa opened with a face: *He frowned* — a face darkened by a moment's misallocated attention, in this world, correctable in seconds. It ends with faces: *musfirah* — the darkness of a whole life's night passed, the light of acquittal arriving. Between the two stands everything the sūrah has taught: that faces are moral instruments; that their smallest motions are recorded; that their allocation is a worship; and that their final state is written by the sum of what they faced in this world. The Prophet ﷺ was the living bridge of the arc: the face that frowned for one moment in Makkah became, by God's training, the face of which the Companion al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib said that the Messenger of God ﷺ had the most beautiful face of all people — and Jābir said he was, when he met his Companions, like a man greeting them with a *shining, beaming* face (the reports of his beauty and his smile are among the most mass-transmitted of descriptions). The sunnah of the smiling face — his ﷺ smile was recorded as constant — is the daily practice of the sūrah's last verses: to carry a face that is already rehearsing for musfirah. The Prophet ﷺ said smiling before one's brother is charity (al-Tirmidhī 1956) — a minor resurrection of the face, several times a day.

**Whose Faces: The Believers — and the Readings of Radiance**

The verses 38–39 describe one class of faces; 40–41 their opposites; and v. 42 names the two groups. The immediate contextual reading, which the commentary literature records with delight, is personal to the sūrah's own cast: the shining faces are of the rank of Ibn Umm Maktūm — the blind striver who came fearful and was for one moment neglected — while the darkened faces belong to the rank of the self-sufficient chiefs who were courted (Āl, Ṭb, as noted in the source commentary). The sūrah thus answers its own opening scene with eschatological justice: the one whose face was turned from is given a face like daybreak; the ones whose faces were turned toward receive dust. But the verses are not private property: every generation reads its own Ibn Umm Maktūms into v. 38 — the overlooked, the disabled, the poor, the sincere — and its own chiefs into v. 40.

The commentators then name the *sources* of the radiance, and their listings are a handbook of the luminous life. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī transmit three great explanations, which may be gathered rather than opposed. First, the radiance of *ablution*: the face that washed for God in this life will be found washed in light on that Day — on the strength of the Prophet's ﷺ promise, in the two Ṣaḥīḥs, that his community will be resurrected with *ghurran muḥajjalīn* — white-blazing of face and limb from the traces of wuḍūʾ — "so whoever can extend his brightness, let him do so" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah, in their chapters on ablution). Second, the radiance of *night prayer*: the face that stood in the dark while the world slept meeting its Lord will be met by light when the world's night ends — the Qur'an itself binds night-vigil and a radiant, or rising, resurrection-face: *"Their sides forsake their beds... No soul knows what refreshment of eyes is hidden for them"* (32:16–17, a passage the early readers applied to the night-prayer people, with 32:17's "good news" often linked to the vision of God). Third — al-Rāzī's own addition — the radiance of *relief*: the faces shine simply because the burden is past, the record acquitted, the fear turned to safety; light as the physiology of joy.

**The Face as the Mirror of the Heart**

The sūrah's final physics — that on the Day, faces *tell* — was anticipated in the commentary tradition by the phrase this commentary's source transmits from al-Ṭabarsī: "the face is the mirror of the heart" (Ṭb). The face is the most public surface of the most private substance; and God's revelation has always read it so. The Qur'an's happiest people are described by their faces before their rewards: here and at 75:22 — *"Faces that Day shall be radiant (nāḍirah), looking upon their Lord"* — where the face's light is explained by the greatest of all its objects: the Beatific Vision, the looking upon God that the tradition holds to be the Paradise of Paradise. And the Qur'an's most tragic faces are described with equal precision, as the next verses will show — darkened, dust-caked, smoking. The doctrine for this life is already plain in the old reports: a man came to the Prophet ﷺ asking permission to withdraw to worship; he was told the stronger way is the way of the marketplace where the face is *tested* — for the face's light is not won in seclusion only; it is won wherever the heart kept its purity in public. The final brightness of v. 38 is thus not cosmetic but confessional: the whole biography of the heart, at last, displayed on the only canvas the world can read at a glance.

**The Believer's Career as a Long Dawn**

One last meditation, in the spirit of the verse's chosen light: dawn is a *slow* light — it begins before it brightens, and it is not fooled by a single bright hour. So, the tradition teaches, is the believer's face-career: the first streaks of musfirah appear in this life — in the prayer-lines, in the post-forgiveness countenance, in the old worshipper's face that the community recognizes as lighted — and the full brightness waits for the Day. The verse is therefore an invitation to a lifelong project: to live so that on the Day of the Cry, when every mask is stripped and every face reads its owner's record, one's own face is found among the morning-faces. The next verse will complete the portrait with two more words — laughing, joyous — the only passage where the Qur'an grants laughter to the believers' resurrection; and the pair of verses after will show the other gallery. The two exhibitions end the sūrah: a museum of faces, entrance free to all, painted by the deeds of time.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:39

> **laughing, joyous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḍāḥikatun: The Rarest Word in the Gallery**

Verse 39 adds to the radiant faces a second attribute — and it is one of the most surprising words in the Qur'an: *ḍāḥikatun* — "laughing." The verb ḍ-ḥ-k is the ordinary Arabic for laughter, and the Qur'an uses it with deliberate symmetry across its teaching about the two histories — this world's laughter and that Day's. In the Meccan sūrahs, the mockers' laughter is a recurring sin: the wicked laughed at Noah, at Moses, at Muhammad ﷺ — *"The criminals laugh at those who believe, and wink at one another when they pass by them"* (83:29–30). And the Qur'an announces the great reversal — that the laughter changes sides:

> *"So this Day those who believe laugh at the disbelievers, on couches, gazing. Are the disbelievers recompensed for what they used to do?"* (Qur'an 83:34–36)

The reversal is exact: as they mocked, they are mocked; as they winked, they are gazed upon. ʿAbasa 80:39 is the emotional core of that reversal: the believers' faces, on the Day when every other human gathering is scattered in terror, are found *laughing* — the sound of this world's scorn transmuted into the sound of that world's relief. The rarity of the word makes its point: the Qur'an is a sober book about a grave road, and it reserves its open laughter for one setting — the acquitted face at the end of history. Laughter, in the Qur'an's economy, is not forbidden in this life (the Prophet ﷺ smiled constantly and laughed with his Companions; he forbade only laughter's cruel and empty forms); but *true, full, unshadowed* laughter — the laughter with no fear behind it — belongs to the Hereafter. In this world even the happiest laugh is a creature of the moment; there, laughter is the permanent state.

**Mustabshirah: Good News in the Skin**

The third adjective of the gallery is *mustabshirah* — "joyous," from the root b-sh-r, one of the Qur'an's richest roots. On its surface, bashara/bushrā means glad tidings, good news; but the same root gives *basharah* — the human skin, the outermost human. The lexical connection (the lexicographers debated its exact pathway) has always suggested to the tradition's ear a truth the verse now images: joy that *shows on the skin*, gladness that has become visible, the body itself announcing what the record said. A face that is musfirah (dawn-bright) and ḍāḥikah (laughing) and mustabshirah (good-news-bearing) is a face on which the verdict has already been read — before a word of it is spoken. The believer on that Day is, as it were, his own herald: the acquittal is broadcast on the countenance. The Qur'an uses the same root's verbs for the believers' whole resurrection-experience, from the covenant-scene's *"He gives them glad tidings of a Paradise beneath which rivers flow"* (9:111, abridged) to the day's fullest bushrā: *"Truly the friends of God — no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve."* (10:62)

**The Order of the Three Words: Dawn, Laughter, Joy**

The three participles arrive in an order worth savoring — musfirah, ḍāḥikah, mustabshirah — because it is the order of the Day's own events in miniature. First the dawn: light arrives before comprehension does (musfirah — the face brightens at what it first sees). Then the laughter: recognition and release (ḍāḥikah — the nervous system of the soul discharging the terror that has just passed, as a man laughs when the danger clears). Then the settled joy (mustabshirah — the gladness that has found its reasons and intends to stay). It is the exact emotional sequence of every rescue ever witnessed: light, then relief-laughter, then joy. The Qur'an has encoded the psychology of salvation into its grammar of participles — three words doing the work of a whole narrative.

**The Sources of the Laughter: The Record Read Aloud**

What, concretely, is the laughter about? The commentators, gathering the Qur'an's eschatology around the word, answer: the believers laugh at what they see of their own outcome set beside what they had feared — and at the reversal of those who had made them fear. The sūrah's context supplies the immediate dramatics: the same gathering where every unprepared soul is fleeing brother and mother and child (vv. 34–36) contains faces already past the crisis — laughing — because their shan (v. 37) sufficed them in mercy: the record was opened, and it matched; the accounting was read, and it was covered. The hadith of the man whose record is read to him deed by deed — until he despairs and then is pardoned (Muslim 2749) — ends with the man's terror turned to relief so total that the tradition read it under this very verse's laughter. And beyond acquittal, the Qur'an promises the believers *entrance*: *"Enter Paradise, you and your spouses, delighted"* (43:70) — the verbs of festivity crowding the last pages of revelation. The laughter of v. 39 is thus not the last of the Day's feelings but the first of eternity's: the door-opening laugh, the homecoming laugh.

**Laughter in This World: The Verse's Gift of Perspective**

The verse also quietly re-weights the believer's relationship to laughter now. Modern culture treats laughter as an end in itself — the entertainment economy's whole product — and its laughter, being severed from meaning, decays into noise or mockery (the Qur'an's own diagnosis of hollow mirth: *"Leave them to play and be diverted until they meet their Day"* — 43:83; and the mocking laughter condemned at 83:29–30). Verse 39 restores laughter to its true station: not the goal of life, but the *fruit* of it — what grows on the far side of a life spent in fear of God and hope of Him. In this world the believer smiles and laughs, but keeps his laughter honest — never at the weak, never at truth, never as anesthesia; in the Hereafter, the restraint is repaid with the laughter that has no shadow. The great teachers of the heart used to say it of tears, and it holds of laughter too: whoever weeps in the night of this world will laugh in the morning of that one. The verse's three participles are, in the end, three promissory notes issued to every believer: dawn for the face, laughter for the voice, good news for the skin — all payable on the Day of the Cry.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:40

> **And faces that Day shall be covered with dust,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Gallery Opens: Wajhun ʿalayhā Ghabarah**

The camera cuts. After three words of light (musfirah, ḍāḥikah, mustabshirah), the sūrah turns without transition to the second gallery, and the very grammar darkens: *wa wajhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabarah* — "and faces that Day shall have upon them dust." The singular *wajh* (face) with a feminine pronoun (*ʿalayhā* — upon it) is the Arabic's usual way of distributing the image across a class: each face, one by one, wearing what is coming. The noun *ghabarah* is dust — specifically the fine, settling dust that films a surface: the dust of the trampled road, of the demolished wall, of the grave freshly closed. It is not the dust of a desert wind that brushes off; it is the dust that *settles on* and stays — the verb's construction (ʿalā — upon, over) makes the face a surface that the dust has claimed. The commentators read the image with one voice, and al-Qurṭubī gives it its classical formulation: ghabarah is the mark of abasement, of humiliation total — the face that has been cast down in the dirt. In the idiom of the old world (and of many a modern one), dust on the face is defeat's signature: the routed soldier's face-planted fall, the captive's face in the ground before his captor. The Qur'an itself uses the dusted face as shame's icon elsewhere: *"And faces shall be humbled before the Living, the Eternal"* (20:111) — with our sūrah's sister-image at 75:24: *"And faces that Day shall be dark (bāsirah)."*

**The Poetics of Contrast: Three Lights Against Two Darknesses**

Set the two galleries side by side, as the sūrah places them, and read the arithmetic — for the Qur'an's word-counts preach as surely as its words. The radiant faces receive three attributes: dawn-bright, laughing, joyous — light, sound, gladness: the full register of a face at rest. The darkened faces receive two: dust and — in the next verse — darkness. The asymmetry is the mercy of the composition: the believer's description *overflows* by one attribute; the denier's is *piled* by two weights. And where the believer's sequence moved from light to joy (dawn → laughter → good news), the denier's moves from surface to atmosphere: first the dust that lies on the face, then the darkness that gathers over the dust — humiliation first, then eclipse; the face covered, then the cover covered. The one gallery is a morning that keeps brightening; the other is a dusk that keeps closing. The Qur'an composes its eschatology in these balances deliberately — its rhetorical school (the science of al-muqāṭaʿah and al-tasrīf, the arrangement of clauses) taught later generations to read the Book's contrasts as doctrine: God is not content to *state* two destinies; He *scores* them — a symphony of light answered by a percussive fall of dust.

**Dust as the Signature of the Coverer: The Sūrah's Own Irony**

There is a deeper irony in the choice of *ghabarah* that ties the sūrah's whole vocabulary together — one the classical exegetes enjoyed pointing out. The sūrah's central sin, named at v. 17, is *kufr* — the covering of truth, the burying of the Giver beneath the gifts. And what is the sūrah's chosen punishment-image for the culmination of that sin? A face *covered* — covered with dust. The punishment rhymes with the deed: they covered, so they are covered. The Qur'an announces this poetics of just recompense as a principle: *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it; and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:7–8) — deed and display matched; and again: *"Is the recompense of goodness anything but goodness?"* (55:60) — with its dark converse implied. The man who spent his life screening the light of the reminder from his own eyes discovers, on the Day when all screens fall, that the only thing his face can still wear is a screen: dust, thick upon it. In the same key, the proud man — who would not bow his face to his Lord in this life — is raised with his face in the dirt; the miser who never brightened a face to the poor is raised with no brightness of his own. Divine justice, in the Qur'an's imagery, is not arbitrary retribution but *revelation*: the Day's punishments are the deeds themselves, come due in the currency of the body.

**The Dust of the Grave Reclaimed**

A gentler meditation also lives in the word. Dust is, throughout this sūrah, the *material of the human condition*: man was created — per the Qur'an's full anthropology — from dust and from a drop (18:37; 22:5); he returns, at v. 21, to the grave's dust; and the food-passage itself was dust made fruitful — the split earth's grain and fruit all grown from soil. Dust, in other words, is not the enemy: it is the servant-stuff of the whole sūrah — the medium of creation, agriculture, and burial alike. The difference between the galleries is therefore not that one is dusty and the other clean; it is that in the believers' case, the dust *bore fruit* — was split, watered, grown into harvest — while in the deniers' case, the dust simply *settled back*: the man of dust who refused the water of revelation ends as dust that nothing grows from. The image unites the sūrah's two great sections — the field and the faces — in a single theology: every human being is a plot of ground; the reminder is the rain; and the Day will show, on every face, what each plot grew.

**From the Frown to the Dust: The Sūrah's First Face Answered**

And once more — because the sūrah never lets the reader forget — there is v. 1 behind this verse. The sūrah opened with a frown: a face's momentary darkness, in time, over one blind man at its door. That face was corrected; the darkness passed; the frown became, by God's mercy and the Prophet's ﷺ receptivity, the teacher of every face after him. Verse 40 is the frown that was never corrected — the face that set itself, over a lifetime, against every seeker, every reminder, every truth that came near it, until the setting became permanent; the frown at eternity's door, now written in dust. The lesson for the reader standing between v. 1 and v. 40 could not be clearer: faces are still soft now; darkness still wipes off; the dust has not yet settled. The whole space of the sūrah — from its first word to its last gallery — is the space in which correction is still cheap and light is still available. The next verse deepens the darkness by one more degree before the final verdict names its owners.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:41

> **overspread with darkness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tarahuhu Qatarah: The Darkness That Overlays the Dust**

The second gallery's second and final attribute arrives: *tarahuhu qatarah* — "overspread with darkness." The verb *taraha* (root r-h-q) means to come upon and cover, to overspread, to settle over something as a layer; the construction says that this second covering *attacks* the first: darkness coming down upon the dust, layer upon layer. And the noun *qatarah* is precision itself: from the root q-t-r, whose home territory is soot, smoke, and thick dark vapor — the qitr, say the lexicons, is the blackness that settles from smoke; a qatarah is the grime of burning. The reciters and commentators noted the variant forms — qatarah and qatirah, both transmitted in the readings, the same soot-darkness under either vocalization — and the exegetes' glosses converge: a darkness rising over the face like the smoke-film over a lamp's glass. The image completes the gallery's physics: the denier's face is first *dusted* (ghabarah, v. 40) — humiliated, ground-down — and then *smoked* (qatarah) — as though the face itself had been near a burning and bears its stain. The Qur'an's Book of deeds says where the burning was: *"Then, on the Day of Resurrection, We shall wholly seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to earn"* (36:65) — and more pointedly, the Book names a darkness that is the sinner's own exhalation: *"And as for the disbelievers — their deeds are as a mirage in a lowland... or as darkness on a deep sea, covered by waves, above which are waves, above which are clouds — darknesses, one above another"* (24:39–40). Darknesses, one above another: 24:40's sea-psalm and 80:41's face are the same doctrine at two scales.

**Why the Face Darkens: The Inward State Made Visible**

The tradition insists that these verses are not cosmetics applied from outside but *revelations* of the inside — the face as the mirror of the heart, now with the veil of time removed. The Qur'an teaches that the light on the believer's face has a source — *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth"* (24:35) — and that faces are oriented either toward that Light or away from it: *"Everyone upon it passes away; and there remains the Face of thy Lord, full of Majesty and Honor"* (55:26–27) — the Face that the Qur'an elsewhere bids the believer to seek: *"And keep thy soul patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His Face"* (18:28). A lifetime of seeking that Face leaves, as it were, its trace on the seeker's own; a lifetime of turning from it leaves its trace likewise. The verses 38–41 simply announce the day of trace-reading: *"the Day when there is no benefit for wrongdoers in their excuses... the Day when every soul will be fully informed of what it did"* — and, as the Qur'an's own summary of the two faces has it: *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, looking upon their Lord; and faces that Day shall be dark"* (75:22–24). Note the causality in 75's pairing: radiance is explained by *looking upon their Lord* — the face brightened by what it faced. The darkened faces of 80:40–41 are the exact inverse: faces that faced everything except their Lord — wealth, rank, appetite, the mirror — and in the end took their color from their objects. Soot is the residue of what was burned instead of burned *for*.

**The Smoked Lamp: A Meditation on a Life's Yield**

The image of the soot-darkened face invites one further meditation in the ancient commentators' manner. A lamp that burns rightly gives light and stays clear; a lamp that burns badly — choked, misfed — gives smoke, and the smoke stains the glass that was meant to shine. The human being, in the Qur'an's own imagery, is a lamp placed in a niche (24:35): created to receive a light and to give it. Sin, in this imagery, is not merely rule-breaking; it is *misfiring* — burning fuel in a way the lamp was not made for, so that the glass itself blackens. The qatarah of v. 41 is, on this reading, the total accumulation of a misfired life: every unkind act a film, every denied mercy a film, until the face — the lamp's glass — is darkened with its own history. And the terror of the image is its permanence: in this world the glass can be wiped (repentance, in the Qur'an's vocabulary of polishing: the Prophet ﷺ said the one who repents is like one without sin; and the scholars loved the image of the heart as a mirror that rusts and is polished by remembrance); on that Day, the soot has set. The believer reading the verse is reading his own maintenance manual: the wiping is available *now*, at the price of humility; it will not be available *then*, at any price.

**The Two Coverings and the One Covering Sin**

Gather the gallery whole and the sūrah's final irony stands fully displayed — for the third time the vocabulary convicts its own audience. The sin of the sūrah's second movement was *kufr* — covering (v. 17 and its root-meditation). The end-state of the coverers is to be *twice-covered*: dust upon the face, darkness upon the dust. The sin of the third movement was *istighnāʾ* — self-sufficiency (v. 5); and its end-state was to be left with nothing but one's own affair (v. 37) — a sufficiency indeed, but of terror. The Qur'an's eschatology is thus self-consistent at every joint: each sin's punishment is the sin itself, matured — the covering covered, the self-sufficiency left alone, the frowning face itself frowning dark forever. Divine justice is not external payback but the truth of the deed outliving the deed's pretenses. And the mercy of the composition is equally consistent: for each of the blessed states there is a seed the believer can plant now — dawn for the face (begin now to brighten it for God), laughter (keep the mirth of this world clean, so it may ripen), good news on the skin (let the body already do deeds it will be glad to be seen doing). Between the dust and the dawn the reader still stands — and the sūrah has one verse left: the verdict that names the two galleries' owners, and closes the book of faces.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:42

> **Those, they are the disbelievers, the profligates.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ulāʾika Hum: The Pronoun of Verdict**

The sūrah's final verse reads the label on the second gallery: *ulāʾika hum al-kafarah al-fajarah* — "Those — they are the disbelievers, the profligates." Every word of the clause is weight-bearing. The demonstrative *ulāʾika* — "those" — is the pointing-finger of the Book: gathered across fourteen centuries of recitation, at every reading of this sūrah, to single out the faces just described. The emphatic pronoun *hum* — "they, and none other" — fixes the identification with legal precision, the grammatical seal on the verdict. And then the two plural nouns, terse and rhymed: *al-kafarah*, plural of kāfir — the disbelievers, the coverers; *al-fajarah*, plural of fājir — the profligates, the burst-beyond-bounds ones. The verse is a court's closing sentence: the faces identified, the names read, the case of Sūrah ʿAbasa closed. With this, the sūrah ends — no promise of Paradise spelled out, no description of reward, no consolatory coda: just the verdict, two nouns, and silence. The early Makkan sūrahs close this way deliberately — ʿAbasa's own structure has already given the blessed their three-attribute portrait (vv. 38–39); the last word belongs to the warning, because warning is the more urgent genre. Mercy has had its say at length (seven verses of table-blessings); the terse end is the trumpet's echo in grammar.

**Kafarah and Fajarah: Creed and Conduct in One Couplet**

The two nouns are not synonyms; the pairing is a summary of the whole human failure. *Kafarah* names the inward crime: kufr, the covering of truth, the refusal of the reminder (v. 12's "whoever wills" refused) — the sin of v. 17's curse, "how ungrateful he is!" *Fajarah* names the outward one: from the root f-j-r, to burst forth, to break open — the word used of a wadi bursting its banks, of a well gushing beyond containment. A fājir is one whose wrongdoing has *burst the banks*: appetites past their channels, behavior past decency, corruption past restraint. The Qur'an names the root in its most famous self-diagnosis of the human soul: *"and inspired it with its wickedness (fujūrahā) and its reverence (taqwāhā)"* (91:8) — fujūr set eternally opposite taqwā. The pairing in our verse — as the source commentary notes from Āl and al-Ṭabarsī — is therefore a portrait of the complete failure: *belief* corrupted (kafarah) and *behavior* corrupted (fajarah); the creed and the conduct; the heart's cover and the hand's excess. The two are coupled because, in the Qur'an's moral physics, they generate each other: cover the Giver and the accounts of giving collapse — as the sūrah's sister-passage of al-Māʿūn states in its own order: the denier of religion is he who repulses the orphan and withholds from the poor (107:1–3). Disbelief upstream, profligacy downstream; and the Day's dust and darkness simply the river's mouth.

**The Punishment Rhymes With the Crime — and So Does the Reward**

Now the sūrah's final doctrine can be stated in one sentence, gathering what verses 38–41 displayed: the two galleries are the two nouns. The *kafarah* — the coverers — are the faces *covered* (ghabarah, qatarah, vv. 40–41): the deed's name written as the deed's end. And by the same rhyme, the believers — the uncoverers, the faces that turned *toward* the light rather than over it — are the *uncovered*: shining, nothing upon them but morning. Even the structure of blessing and ruin answers the moral physics stated at the Qur'an's own close-of-case elsewhere: *"Is the recompense of goodness anything but goodness?"* (55:60). The believer reading the last verse of ʿAbasa thus reads the last of the sūrah's many self-portraits: the frowning face (v. 1), the turned face (v. 6), the dawn face (v. 38), the dust face (v. 40) — and now the names of their owners. Faces, in this Book, are biographies; and biographies end in nouns.

**A Closing Word About What the Sūrah Has Done**

Stand back, then, and take the whole of ʿAbasa in one view, as the final verse invites. In forty-two verses it has traveled from a frown in a Makkah gathering to the last faces of the resurrection — and every station of the journey has taught the same lesson in a different key. It began with the *ethics of attention*: whom you turn your face toward is your faith made visible (vv. 1–10). It showed the *message*: a reminder in honored pages, offered freely, for whoever wills (vv. 11–16). It prosecuted the *creature's forgetfulness*: man from a drop, carried down an eased road, dying, buried, raised — and still covering (vv. 17–23). It spread the *evidence of mercy*: rain, split earth, grain, vine, olive, palm, garden, fruit, pasture — a table set for man and beast (vv. 24–32). And it ended with the *Day of the Cry*, where every prop falls, every proxy fails, and every face reads aloud the record of what it faced in this world (vv. 33–42). Ethics, revelation, doctrine, evidence, eschatology — a complete religion in miniature, and all of it growing out of one blind man's interruption of one afternoon's conversation. That is the sūrah's final signature lesson, quietly under everything: heaven watches how the smallest hour is spent. The God who corrected a Prophet's frown with ten eternal verses is a God from whom nothing human is too small to matter, and no failure too fixed to be corrected — *while the invitation stands* (v. 12). The reader who closes the sūrah with verse 42's warning ringing should therefore reopen it, immediately, at verse 1: the frown was corrected in time; the faces of dust once had mornings available. So, this morning, is the choice still open: *let whoever wills, remember.*
