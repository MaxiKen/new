# Sūrah ʿAbasa (Chapter 80) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After a Frown**

Sūrah ʿAbasa is the eightieth chapter of the Qur'an: forty-two verses, revealed in Makkah in the early period of the Prophet's public mission, positioned in the muṣḥaf between al-Nāziʿāt (79) and al-Takwīr (81), and belonging to the thirtieth and final juzʾ. It takes its name from its very first word, *ʿabasa* — "he frowned" — and this is unique in the whole Book. No other sūrah is named after a gesture of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, and no other sūrah opens by narrating, in the third person, an expression of displeasure on his face. Traditional scholars also call it al-Ṣākhkhah, "the Piercing Cry," after verse 33, and al-Safarah, "the Scribes," after verse 15 (al-Ālūsī). In the received chronologies of revelation order — al-Suyūṭī's arrangement in *al-Itqān*, following al-Jaʿbarī and al-Bayhaqī — ʿAbasa falls after al-Najm (53) and before al-Qadr (97), which places it in the middle of the early Makkan years, when the Muslim community was still a small, persecuted band and the Prophet was still pleading with the aristocracy of Quraysh to give the message a hearing.

**The Incident That Opened the Sūrah**

The opening ten verses refer to one of the most famous and most instructive episodes in early Islamic history. ʿĀʾishah, the wife of the Prophet, reported:

> *"ʿAbasa wa tawallā* — 'He frowned and turned away' — was revealed concerning ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm, the blind man. He came to the Messenger of God ﷺ and kept saying, 'O Messenger of God, guide me.' Now the Messenger of God ﷺ was addressing one of the great men of the idolaters, and he had been hoping earnestly that the man would accept Islam. So he turned away from Ibn Umm Maktūm and frowned in his face and inclined toward the other, and began conversing with him. Then *ʿabasa wa tawallā* was revealed." (al-Tirmidhī 3331, who graded it ḥasan gharīb; also in Musnad Aḥmad)

The commentators name the Qurayshī notables variously: ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, Shaybah ibn Rabīʿah, Abū Jahl (ʿAmr ibn Hishām), Umayyah ibn Khalaf, Ubayy ibn Khalaf, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, and al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib. The strategic logic behind the Prophet's concentration was sound and even admirable: if a chief of Makkah embraced Islam, his clan would follow, the persecution would ease, and the mission would gain protection. Ibn Kathīr states plainly that the Prophet ﷺ was not sinning but pursuing a greater expected good; the divine correction informed him of a greater good still — that the sincere seeker standing at his shoulder outranked the indifferent notables seated before him.

**ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm: The Man Behind the Verse**

The blind man at the center of this revelation was ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm, of Banū ʿĀmir ibn Luʾayy, a clan of Quraysh and a maternal relation of Khadījah. He was among the earliest Muslims, devoted to the Prophet and to the Qur'an. After this sūrah came down, it is reported that whenever the Prophet ﷺ saw him he would greet him with the words:

> *"Welcome to him on whose account my Lord rebuked me"* (marhaban bi-man ʿātabanī fīhi rabbī) — as al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr record.

Ibn Umm Maktūm was among the first two to migrate to Madinah, where he taught the Qur'an to many. He and Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ shared the adhān:

> *"Bilāl calls the adhān by night, so eat and drink until you hear the adhān of Ibn Umm Maktūm."* (al-Bukhārī 617; Muslim 1092)

Whenever the Prophet ﷺ left Madinah on campaign he repeatedly appointed Ibn Umm Maktūm to lead the prayer and govern the city — the blind man was the Prophet's deputy in his own capital, some thirteen times according to the biographers. Verse 4:95 came down in connection with him: al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib reports that when *"Not equal are those of the believers who sit at home"* was revealed, Ibn Umm Maktūm said, "O Messenger of God, I am blind — is there an excuse for me?" and the exemption *"except those of great incapacity"* was revealed (al-Bukhārī 4513, 2831). Yet he would not accept permanent exemption from service. He asked to be given a role in battle, saying, "Place me between the two ranks and give me the standard. I will carry it and protect it, for I am blind and cannot run away." After al-Qādisiyyah (14/636) he was found dead on the field still clutching the banner of the Muslims — though other reports, noted by Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr in *al-Istīʿāb*, say he returned to Madinah and died there.

**A Rebuke Preserved: The Argument from Self-Correction**

That a book recited publicly and memorized word for word should contain a rebuke of its own reciter is a fact of the first order for the question of the Qur'an's origin. No human author composing a text to establish his own authority inserts, at the head of a chapter, an account of his own discourtesy; no ruler publishes his own lapse as the opening line of a decree. Yet the Qur'an does this again and again: *"God has pardoned thee — why didst thou permit them?"* (9:43); *"Why dost thou forbid that which God has made lawful to thee?"* (66:1); *"Had We not strengthened thee, thou wouldst surely have inclined toward them a little"* (17:74); *"It is not for a prophet that he should have captives until he has made a great slaughter in the land"* (8:67); and here, *"He frowned and turned away."* The classical defenders of prophetic infallibility (ʿiṣmah) explained such passages consistently: the prophets are protected from major sin and from any error in conveying revelation, but they may, in matters where no revelation has yet specified a ruling, exercise ijtihād and choose a good that is outweighed by a better — and God corrects them at once, which is proof of their rank rather than evidence of degradation. Al-Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ treats the ʿitāb (admonition) in *al-Shifāʾ* in exactly this light, and Ibn Taymiyyah discusses prophetic ijtihād along the same lines in *Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā*. Note too the mercy embedded in the grammar: God does not say "you frowned"; He says "he frowned," narrating the deed in the third person, and only then turns to address His Messenger directly. The Beloved is reproved in absentia, as one reproves a friend gently.

**The Architecture of Forty-Two Verses**

The sūrah moves in four panels, and the movement is deliberate. Panel one (vv. 1–10) is a human scene in real time: a frown, a blind man, a room full of important people, and a divine weighing of attention. Panel two (vv. 11–16) lifts the camera away from that room entirely to the celestial archive — honored pages, exalted and purified, in the hands of noble, righteous scribes — reminding the hearer that the message at stake is not the Prophet's opinion but a document kept in heaven. Panel three (vv. 17–32) turns to the human creature in the abstract: cursed for his ingratitude, made from a drop, eased upon his way, killed and buried and raised, and then invited to look down at his food — at the poured water, the split earth, the grain, the grapes, the olives, the palms, the gardens, the fruit, the fodder. Panel four (vv. 33–42) opens the Day itself: the Piercing Cry, a man fleeing his brother and his mother and his father and his wife and his sons, every soul absorbed in its own affair, and then the faces — shining, laughing, rejoicing; and dusty, darkened, the faces of the disbelievers, the profligates.

Two features of the composition deserve notice. The first is the frame: the sūrah begins with a face (a frown) and ends with faces (radiant or dusty). The single frown of verse 1 is answered, forty-one verses later, by the universal verdict of verse 38 and verse 40 — what a face was allowed to do to one blind man in Makkah becomes, on the Day of Judgment, what every face will wear forever. The second is the sound. The first ten verses run on a sustained open *ā* rhyme (*tawallā, al-aʿmā, yazzakkā, al-dhikrā, istaghnā, taṣaddā, yasʿā, yakhshā, talahhā*), an expansive vowel that carries the narrative without strain; verses 11–16 and 17–23 close on *-ah* (*tadhkirah, dhakarah, mukarramah, muṭahharah, safarah, bararah; akfarah, khalaqah, qaddarah, yassarah, aqbarah, ansharah, amarah*), the closed feminine ending giving the register of record and reckoning; verses 24–32 return to the open *ā* for the catalogue of the harvest (*ṣabbā, shaqqā, ḥabbā, qaḍbā, nakhlā, ghulbā, abbā*), as though the earth itself were being listed aloud; and the final panel ends on *-ih* and *-ah* (*akhīh, abīh, banīh, yughnīh; musfirah, mustabshirah, ghabarah, qatarah, fajarah*), the sounds of flight and of faces. The chapter thus has a single sustained subject — attention, gratitude, and the face — stated at human scale in verse 1 and restated at cosmic scale in verse 42.

**Why This Sūrah Still Speaks**

Sūrah ʿAbasa is, in its first ten verses, the earliest recorded constitutional clause of Islamic preaching: the sincere seeker has priority over the influential audience. Everything that follows is the Qur'an's way of explaining why that principle is true — because the message is not the preacher's property (vv. 11–16), because the human being is too small and too indebted to be ranked by worldly status (vv. 17–32), and because a Day is coming on which every calculus of prestige will be dissolved and only faces will remain (vv. 33–42). Read today, the sūrah addresses the attention economies of religious and secular life alike: the temptation to serve the donor before the student, the celebrity before the seeker, the platform before the person; the exclusion practiced by a frown that never becomes a sentence; and the disability of a heart that sees everything and understands nothing.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the forty-two verses as an independent discourse, in strict order, exactly as mandated: every verse is quoted individually from the sūrah's canonical text, then expanded through the Arabic language and its roots, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿAbbās and the early generations), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the internal logic of the teaching, and its bearing upon ordinary modern life. Verses that the source commentary treats in groups — 1–10, 15–16, 18–19, 21–22, 27–32, 34–37, 38–39, 40–42 — are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing has been shortened because the chapter is short; if anything, the brevity of ʿAbasa (forty-two verses against al-Baqarah's 286) permits each of its verses to be read at the depth the seven verses of al-Fātiḥah received.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:1

> **He frowned and turned away,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Opening That Names No Name**

The eightieth chapter of the Qur'an begins with two verbs and no subject: *ʿabasa wa tawallā* — "he frowned and turned away." The reader is not told who frowned, who turned away, from whom, or why. The identity of the actor is disclosed only obliquely in verse 3, where the address shifts abruptly to the second person: *"wa-mā yudrīka"* — "and what would apprise thee?" This grammatical withholding is one of the most studied features of the passage. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Bayḍāwī all note that God could have said *ʿabasta wa tawallayta* — "you frowned and you turned away" — and that He chose instead to report the deed as an observer would report it, in the third person. The device accomplishes two things at once. It rebukes, because the deed is stated baldly, with the two most incriminating verbs available and no mitigation attached to them. And it protects, because the Beloved is spoken of rather than spoken to; the correction arrives wrapped in the covering that the Qur'an everywhere praises in God's own dealing with His servants. There is a lesson in the technique for every human being who must correct another: the most effective rebuke is often the one that describes the act without assaulting the actor.

**ʿAbasa: The Root of the Closed Brow**

*ʿAbasa* comes from the root *ʿ-b-s*, which carries the sense of contracting, darkening, and hardening. The *ʿabūs* is the scowling face; the same root gives the Qur'anic description of the Day of Judgment as *yawman ʿabūsan qaṭarā* — "a day frowning and calamitous" (76:10) — as though the Day itself will wear the expression of those who wasted it. In Sūrah al-Muddaththir the same verb is applied to al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who heard the Qur'an, was shaken by it, and then hardened himself against it:

> *"Then he frowned and scowled; then he turned his back and was proud"* (74:22–23: *thumma ʿabasa wa basara, thumma adbara wa-stakbara*).

The contrast between the two occurrences of *ʿabasa* in the Qur'an is the interpretive key to this sūrah. Al-Walīd's frown is the frown of contempt and pride, the face of a man refusing the truth; the Prophet's frown is the frown of preoccupation, the face of a man too busy with a great task to notice a small one. The first is a sin; the second is a lapse in the ordering of goods. The Qur'an uses the same word for both because the muscle is the same and the effect on the person who receives it is the same — and because the one thing that must never be allowed to grow from the second into the first is the habit of the face.

**Tawallā: The Turning of the Body**

*Tawallā* is from *w-l-y*, the root of nearness, guardianship, and loyalty (*walī*, *mawlā*, *wilāyah*), which in the fifth form means to turn away from, to give one's back to, to abandon. The Qur'an uses *tawallā* for the hypocrites who hear the message and then withdraw: *"And if thou callest them to guidance, they will never be guided"* (18:57); for the one who hears God's signs recited and turns aside (45:8–9); for the disbeliever who *"turns away in his pride"* (22:9). The verb implies movement of the whole body, not merely of the eye. So the verse reports a sequence with a physical logic of its own: first the face contracts, then the torso rotates, then attention relocates. Every human being who has ever been interrupted knows the exact sequence, and every human being who has ever interrupted another knows it too. The Qur'an narrates it with the detachment of a camera and lets the reader recognize himself in it.

**A Micro-Gesture Recorded in a Permanent Book**

The theological weight of the verse lies in its scale. A frown lasts a fraction of a second and engages a handful of small facial muscles; it leaves no document, no witness statement, no legal consequence. Yet here it is, at the head of a chapter that a billion people will recite until the end of time, in the same Book that says:

> *"Not a word does he utter but there is a watcher by him, ready"* (50:18).

and

> *"But those who disbelieve say: What is this Book that leaves out nothing small or great but records it?"* (18:49).

The verse therefore teaches, by demonstration rather than by argument, the doctrine of divine surveillance (*murāqabah*) in its most searching form. It is not only murder and theft that are written down; the involuntary contraction of a believer's brow at the approach of an inconvenient poor man is written down. Sufi moralists from al-Muḥāsibī to Ibn al-Qayyim built entire disciplines on this fact: the examination of the heart (*muḥāsabah*) is precisely the tracking of movements too small for law and too large for God to ignore.

**The Blind Man Could Not See It**

There is a detail in the reports of the incident that the classical commentators draw out with great force: ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm was blind. He could not see the frown. The expression did its damage — if damage it did — without ever reaching his eyes, and it is at least possible that he never knew he had been frowned at. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both make the point that God nevertheless recorded the gesture, which means the moral law here is not primarily about harm suffered by the other person but about the state of the heart of the one who frowns. The verse is thus also a ruling on intention and inner courtesy: the ethics of Islam govern the unseen interior as strictly as the visible exterior, because God is the witness of both. *"Indeed God does not look at your forms or your possessions, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds"* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah). A frown that no one sees is still a decision, and every decision is a verdict about what the decision-maker values.

**The Jurisprudence of Priorities**

Read against the reports, verse 1 is not a condemnation of the Prophet's project but a correction of his ranking within it. He was doing the right work at the wrong order of priority: converting a chief of Quraysh would have saved hundreds of lives and opened the door of Makkah, and the desire for it was itself an act of mercy. What God revealed was that the ranking of persons in a gathering is not the preacher's to make, and that a sincere seeker who has walked to you on his own initiative outranks an indifferent notability whom you are courting. Ibn Kathīr summarizes this as the principle that the Prophet ﷺ was commanded henceforth to treat the poor and the sincere with precedence, and he links the verse directly to the earlier commands:

> *"And drive not away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, desiring His Face"* (6:52).

> *"Make thy soul patient with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, desiring His Face; and turn not thine eyes away from them, desiring the adornment of the life of this world"* (18:28).

Islamic jurisprudence later developed a whole literature on exactly this problem under the name *fiqh al-awlawiyyāt* — the jurisprudence of priorities — asking which good comes first when two goods compete. Sūrah ʿAbasa is its founding text, and its first ruling is that the one who has come to you seeking is entitled to be received before the one you are trying to win.

**The Modern Frown**

The contemporary form of *ʿabasa wa tawallā* is rarely a facial expression. It is the meeting that runs over while the person who waited is told to reschedule; the institution that builds a donor lounge and a side entrance; the congregation that knows the names of its benefactors and not the names of its newcomers; the group chat that goes quiet when an awkward question is asked; the "unavailable" status applied selectively. None of these is a sin in any legal code, and none of them can be prosecuted. But the sūrah that opens with an unpunished, unseen, half-second frown is telling the reader that these things are exactly the material from which the moral life is made or lost. The verse's first application is not to the Prophet's contemporaries. It is to whoever is reading it while someone waits.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:2

> **because the blind man came to him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Anonymities Facing Each Other**

Verse 1 withheld the name of the one who frowned; verse 2 withholds the name of the one who was frowned at. The Qur'an does not say "because ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm came to him"; it says *an jāʾahu al-aʿmā* — "because the blind man came to him." Both parties are unnamed, and the naming is withheld for opposite reasons. The Prophet's anonymity softens a rebuke addressed to the best of creation. The blind man's anonymity, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī explain, identifies the operative worldly factor in the scene: the only thing that made this man different from the distinguished company already seated was his disability, and the verse names that difference precisely so that the reader can see what the room saw. The Qur'an thus performs its own social analysis in three words: *al-aʿmā jāʾa* — the blind man came.

**Jāʾa: The Effort Inside the Verb**

The verb *jāʾa* ("he came") deserves more weight than its English rendering suggests. For a blind man in seventh-century Makkah, "coming" was not a stroll; it was a negotiation of uneven ground, doorways, crowds, and the risk of being knocked aside, undertaken without the one sense that makes navigation cheap. The reports emphasize that he came repeatedly and kept saying, "O Messenger of God, guide me" (*yā rasūla Allāh, arshidnī*). The initiative, therefore, was entirely his: he sought, he travelled, he persisted, he interrupted. This matters enormously for the moral arithmetic of the passage, because the Qur'an will weigh two men against each other in verses 5–10 — one who "deems himself beyond need" and stays in his seat being courted, and one who "came striving earnestly while fearful." The blind man's *jāʾa* is the first half of that verdict: he moved; the notable did not.

**ʿAmā: Blindness of Eye and Blindness of Heart**

*Al-aʿmā* is the intensive form of blindness, from the root *ʿ-m-y*. The Qur'an uses the word and its derivatives to build one of its central contrasts, stated most explicitly in the verse that functions as the theological lens over this entire incident:

> *"For indeed it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts which are in the breasts"* (22:46).

The scene in the house at Makkah is therefore an enacted parable of 22:46. Two groups are present: men with perfect eyesight whose hearts are sealed, and a man with no eyesight whose heart is open. The Qur'an will spend the rest of the sūrah drawing out the consequences — and in verse 5 it will describe the sighted notables with the root *gh-n-y* (*istaghnā*, "deemed himself beyond need"), which is the very definition of spiritual blindness: the inability to perceive one's own need. Elsewhere the Qur'an pairs blindness with deafness and dumbness for those who refuse the signs: *"Deaf, dumb, blind — so they will not return"* (2:18); *"Or like a cloudburst from the sky, wherein is darkness, thunder and lightning"* (2:19). Ibn ʿAbbās's exegetical tradition repeatedly insists that the physical senses are not the seat of perception; the heart is. On that scale the blind man of verse 2 is the most sighted person in the room, and this is why the sūrah's closing panel will give him — according to the reading of al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī — the radiant, laughing face of verse 38, while the notables receive the dust and darkness of verse 40.

**The Disabled Person in the Qur'anic Moral Order**

The Qur'an never treats disability as disgrace or as punishment, and it legislates exemption from duties where capacity is absent, while legislating full inclusion in dignity. *"There is no blame upon the blind, nor upon the lame, nor upon the sick"* (48:17; cf. 24:61, 9:91), and in the law of war, *"Not equal are those of the believers who sit at home, other than those of great incapacity, and those who strive in the way of God with their wealth and their lives"* (4:95) — a verse whose exemption clause was revealed, according to al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, specifically because of Ibn Umm Maktūm's plea (al-Bukhārī 4513). What is striking about the Islamic treatment is that exemption is from *burden*, never from *rank*: the same blind man whom the law excused from marching was appointed to lead the prayer in Madinah and to govern the city in the Prophet's absence, and he gave the adhān to the whole community beside Bilāl (al-Bukhārī 617; Muslim 1092). The community's highest public offices — the voice that announces the time of prayer, the hand that leads the prayer, the authority that administers the capital — were placed in the keeping of a man who could not see. That is the practical meaning of verse 2, and the fact that it took a divine rebuke to establish it is precisely why the rebuke was preserved.

**Why the Verse Says "Came to Him" and Not "Was Brought"**

The syntax *an jāʾahu al-aʿmā* makes the blind man the grammatical subject of his own arrival. He is not an object of charity, not a case, not a burden delivered by others; he is an agent who came. This is a detail with large implications for how religious communities should think about the people they serve. The seeker is not passive material for the preacher's program; he is a person whose arrival imposes an obligation on the one who is present. In the Qur'an's ethics of knowledge, the one who comes has a right (*ḥaqq*) — and the Prophet ﷺ will be told in verse 8, *"But as for him who came to thee striving earnestly,"* with the same verb *jāʾa*, so that the two verses form a bracket around the whole incident: the coming of the blind man (v. 2) is the fact that God wills to weigh against the sitting of the notable (v. 6).

**The Modern Blind Man at the Door**

Every institution has its Ibn Umm Maktūm: the person who arrives unannounced, without credentials, without influence, needing something that cannot be scheduled, at a moment when the institution is busy with something it considers strategic. In a mosque it is the new Muslim who asks a question during the fund-raising dinner; in a clinic it is the patient who walks in while the board is visiting; in a classroom it is the student who emails at the wrong time; in a family it is the relative who needs to be heard while everyone is managing an image. The verse's discipline is to notice that the person *came* — that effort was expended, that need was articulated, that an initiative was taken — and to let that fact generate a claim on attention before any calculation about the room's more important occupants. Accessibility, in the modern idiom, is the physical expression of this: the ramp, the Braille muṣḥaf, the audio recitation, the sign-language interpreter at the khuṭbah, the large-print text, the quiet space. But verse 2 is not about ramps. It is about the recognition that the arrival of a needy person is an event with moral priority, whether or not anyone in the room can measure its value.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:3

> **And what would apprise thee? Perhaps he would purify himself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shift from Narrative to Address**

With this verse the sūrah's grammar turns. Verses 1–2 spoke of the Prophet in the third person, as a narrator recounts a scene; verse 3 addresses him directly: *wa-mā yudrīka* — "and what would apprise thee?" (*yudrīka*, from *d-r-y*, "to know," in the causative fourth form: "what makes you know?"). The commentators observe that this shift accomplishes a subtle pastoral aim. The rebuke was delivered indirectly so as not to wound; the question that follows is put directly so that it may be answered, and so that the Prophet is restored at once to the intimacy of being spoken to by his Lord. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the whole passage moves between reproof and elevation in a single breath: God corrects His Messenger and, in the same sentence, treats him as one who is being taught a secret of the Unseen.

**Mā Yudrīka: The Interrogative of Magnification**

The formula *wa-mā yudrīka* (and its variant *wa-mā adrāka*, "and what would let thee know?") appears throughout the Qur'an as a device for announcing that something beyond ordinary human reckoning is about to be disclosed:

> *"The Calamity! What is the Calamity? And what would let thee know what the Calamity is?"* (101:1–3).

> *"And what would let thee know what the Night of Power is?"* (97:2).

> *"And what would let thee know what the Day of Judgment is?"* (82:17–18).

Where the phrase is used with the Prophet in the second person singular, the commentators distinguish two kinds. In some places — as in *"And what would let thee know? Perhaps the Hour is near"* (42:17) — the question is followed by no answer, and al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr hold that the sense is: "this is not within the knowledge given to you." In others the answer comes at once, as here, where the disclosure is *"laʿallahu yazzakkā"* — "perhaps he would purify himself." So verse 3 is not a rhetorical rebuke alone; it is the opening of an instruction about the interior of other human beings — about what may be happening inside a person whose exterior gives no sign of value to the world.

**Yazzakkā: Purification as Growth**

*Yazzakkā* is the assimilated form of *yatazakkā*, from the root *z-k-w / z-k-y*, one of the richest in the Qur'an. *Zakāh* means both purity and increase, and the two meanings are inseparable in Arabic usage: a thing is *zākī* when it is clean of defect and thereby grows. This is why the obligatory alms are called *zakāh* — the payment purifies the payer's wealth and simultaneously makes it increase: *"Take alms from their wealth whereby thou mayst purify them and make them grow"* (9:103). The same verb family describes the work of the Prophet's mission: *"He recites to them His signs and purifies them and teaches them the Book and the Wisdom"* (62:2), and the plea of the believer: *"Purify my soul, for Thou art the Best of those who purify it"* — as in the oath-sequence of Sūrah al-Shams: *"And by the soul and Him who proportioned it, then inspired it with its wickedness and its reverence; successful is he who purifies it, and failed is he who covers it over"* (91:7–10).

**"Perhaps" — The Door Left Open in the Divine Speech**

The particle *laʿalla* ("perhaps, so that, it may be") is used in the Qur'an both of God's action and of human hope. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī discuss its force here: in a divine statement *laʿalla* conveys a real, weighty expectation — not divine uncertainty, since God knows the outcome perfectly, but the magnitude of the possibility as it appears from the human side. In effect: consider what might be in this man. Ibn Kathīr renders the sense as, "perhaps he would be guided and thereby attain purification." The verse thus introduces a permanent epistemic rule for the caller to God: you do not know who will be purified. The outcome of your speech to another human being is not in your hands, and therefore no calculation of the likelihood of success may be used to decide who deserves your attention. This is precisely the logic that the Qur'an states elsewhere with full explicitness:

> *"Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but God guides whomsoever He will. And He knows best those who are rightly guided"* (28:56).

> *"Remind, for thou art but a remembrancer; thou art not a watcher over them"* (88:21–22).

> *"But if they turn away, We have not sent thee as a watcher over them; upon thee rests only the conveyance"* (42:48).

**The Unseen Harvest of an Ordinary Conversation**

There is a practical corollary that Islamic preaching ethics drew from this verse with great seriousness. The preacher, the teacher, the parent, the friend who advises — none of them can see the harvest. A sentence spoken to a person who seems insignificant may become the pivot of a life, and a campaign aimed at the powerful may produce nothing. Ibn Umm Maktūm is the Qur'an's own demonstration of the principle: the man who was nearly overlooked went on to teach the Qur'an in Madinah, to call the adhān for the first Muslim polity, to govern the Prophet's city repeatedly, and to die holding the army's standard. The "perhaps" of verse 3 was realized beyond anything the room could have imagined. This is why the classical manuals of *adab al-muʿallim* (the etiquette of the teacher) insist that a scholar may not rank his students by their social standing, their speed, their eloquence, or their usefulness to his cause: the ranking of hearts is God's property, and *"He knows best those who are rightly guided"* (28:56; 53:30).

**The Modern Application: The Invisible Candidate**

In modern terms the verse describes the person whose potential is not visible on any dashboard: the applicant without the right school, the congregant who stumbles over Arabic, the child who learns slowly, the convert who asks basic questions during an advanced lecture, the elderly person who needs the same explanation for the fourth time. Institutional life is built to optimize for measurable returns, and measurable returns systematically favor the already-qualified. The Qur'an's counter-instruction in verse 3 is not to abandon discernment but to hold open a permanent reservation for the unmeasurable: *laʿallahu yazzakkā* — perhaps he will purify himself. Perhaps this is the one. You do not know; and because you do not know, you may not close the door.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:4

> **or be reminded, such that the reminder might benefit him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Possibility, and Why It Is Distinct from the First**

Verse 3 opened with one hope for the blind man: *laʿallahu yazzakkā* — "perhaps he would purify himself." Verse 4 adds a second: *aw yaddhakkar fa-tanfaʿahu al-dhikrā* — "or be reminded, such that the reminder might benefit him." The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) treat the two as complementary stages of one process, or as two kinds of benefit that a person may receive from revelation. *Tazkiyah* is the transformation of the self: the removal of corruption and the growth of goodness in the soul. *Dhikrā* is the awakening of what the soul already knows: the reminder that stirs a dormant recognition. Not every hearer receives both. Some are purified without an articulate memory of the argument; some receive an insight that changes a single decision without yet changing the whole person. The Qur'an here lists the two possible outcomes so that neither is dismissed, and so that the preacher understands that his task is not to produce a specified result but to deliver the reminder and leave its landing to God.

**Yaddhakkar: The Root of Memory in the Qur'an**

*Yaddhakkar* is the assimilated form of *yatadhakkar*, from *dh-k-r*, the root of remembrance, mention, and reminder. It is one of the Qur'an's most frequent and most theologically loaded roots: *dhikr* is used of the Qur'an itself (*"And indeed it is a Reminder for thee and for thy people"* — 43:44), of the recitation of God's signs, of the act of remembering God in prayer (*"and remember the Name of thy Lord"* — 73:8), and of the human faculty of recollection. In the Qur'anic anthropology the human being does not need to be given truth from outside as an entirely new thing; he needs to be reminded of a truth his own constitution already acknowledges. This is the doctrine of the primordial covenant:

> *"And when thy Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their progeny, and made them bear witness over themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yea, we bear witness'"* (7:172).

Remembrance, on this account, is recovery rather than acquisition. Al-Rāzī makes this the heart of his commentary on the verse: the *dhikrā* benefits the person because it restores something that belongs to him, and that is why a hearer can be moved by a single sentence he has heard a hundred times. The Qur'an repeatedly challenges its opponents to remember what they already concede: *"Did He not make you out of a drop of sperm, then a clinging clot, then fashioned and proportioned you?"* (75:37–38). The argument is not new information; it is the removal of forgetfulness.

**"Such That the Reminder Might Benefit Him" — The Condition of Receptivity**

The clause *fa-tanfaʿahu al-dhikrā* ("such that the reminder might benefit him") introduces the verb *n-f-ʿ*, benefit, which is the Qur'an's ordinary word for what is genuinely useful: *"They will not harm you save by annoyance"* (3:111); *"and remind, for the Reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55). The verse therefore asserts a real principle: a reminder is only a reminder if it lands, and its landing depends on a condition in the receiver. Not every speech benefits every hearer. The Qur'an states with striking candor that the same revelation produces opposite effects in opposite hearts:

> *"And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers, and it increases the wrongdoers in nothing but loss"* (17:82).

> *"Say: It is, for those who believe, a guidance and a healing; and as for those who do not believe, in their ears is a deafness, and it is a blindness for them"* (41:44).

The difference lies in the receiver's posture, which is why verse 4 speaks of benefit as something that may or may not follow, and why the following verses will contrast the two postures explicitly: the one who "deems himself beyond need" (v. 5) and the one who "comes striving while fearful" (vv. 8–9). Ibn Kathīr summarizes the point tersely: the benefit of the reminder is proportionate to the fear and the striving of the one who hears it.

**The Two Postures That Determine Whether Words Work**

Taken together, verses 3–4 and 8–10 produce a small psychology of receptivity that the Islamic tradition developed at length. Two conditions make a reminder useful. The first is *khashyah* — reverential fear — described in verse 9. The second is *saʿy* — effort, striving — described in verse 8: he *came* to you, working at it. The absence of these produces the state of verse 5: *istaghnā*, the sense of being beyond need. The Qur'an names this as the root of human rebellion in the very same period of revelation:

> *"Nay! Truly man transgresses, because he sees himself beyond need"* (96:6–7).

This is one of the most consequential couplets in the Book, and it is the direct parallel to 80:5. Self-sufficiency — the felt absence of need — is not a virtue in Qur'anic anthropology but the origin of transgression (*ṭughyān*), because a person who believes he lacks nothing cannot receive anything. The one who fears and strives has, by that very fear and striving, declared that he is incomplete; and incompleteness declared is the condition of being completed. This is the logic by which the blind man, who knew he needed guidance and walked to get it, receives the "perhaps" of verses 3–4, while the notables, who needed nothing they were willing to admit, receive nothing at all.

**Why the Verse Uses "Perhaps" Twice Without Promise**

Neither verse 3 nor verse 4 promises that the purification will occur or that the reminder will benefit. Both are framed as possibilities: *laʿalla*, *aw*. Al-Qurṭubī observes that this framing protects the caller to God from two errors at once — from despising the seeker (since his benefit is possible and weighty) and from claiming ownership of the outcome (since it is only possible, and only God produces it). The teacher's duty is delivery, not manufacture. The practical instruction for the believer is to speak the reminder to whoever will hear it, including those who seem unlikely to benefit, and to be at peace about the result. In modern language: you are responsible for the quality of your attention and the honesty of your words, not for the conversion of your listener. The verse thus installs a discipline that protects the religious worker from both arrogance and burnout — the two occupational diseases of every profession concerned with changing people.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:5

> **As for him who deems himself beyond need,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Istaghnā: The Word That Names the Real Problem**

The sūrah now turns from the blind man to the other party in the room. *Ammā mani-staghnā* — "as for him who deems himself beyond need." The verb is the tenth form of the root *gh-n-y*, which carries the senses of sufficiency, freedom from want, and richness: *ghanī* is the rich man, *istaghnā* is "he considered himself sufficient, he did without, he dispensed with." The tenth form in Arabic is regularly reflexive and estimative — it reports not a fact but a person's judgment about a fact. So *istaghnā* does not mean "he was rich"; it means "he regarded himself as being beyond need." Wealth may or may not be present; the operative condition is the inner claim of independence.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both draw out the theological consequence: the object of this self-sufficiency is God Himself. The man who *istaghnā* has not merely declined an invitation; he has taken a position on his own ontological status. This is why the Qur'an treats *istighnāʾ* as the engine of human rebellion, in the passage already cited and revealed in this same Makkan period:

> *"Nay! Truly man transgresses, because he sees himself beyond need"* (96:6–7).

And in Sūrah al-Layl, where the two destinies are defined by exactly this pair of verbs:

> *"But as for him who gives and is reverent, and confirms the best — We shall surely ease him toward ease. And as for him who is stingy and deems himself beyond need, and denies the best — We shall surely ease him toward hardship"* (92:5–10).

The parallelism between 80:5 and 92:8 is exact — *istaghnā* in both — and Sūrah al-Layl adds the consequence the Qur'an sees as automatic: the one who declares his independence from God is "eased" toward hardship, meaning that his own choice becomes the path of least resistance in his life.

**The Three Kinds of Self-Sufficiency**

The commentators distinguish shades within the word. There is the self-sufficiency of wealth: the man whose money makes him feel he owes no one. There is the self-sufficiency of intellect: the man whose cleverness makes him feel he has solved the question of the universe and needs no revelation. And there is the self-sufficiency of status: the man whose position makes him feel that attending to a religious summons would be beneath him. The Qurayshī notables in the room combined all three, which is why the sūrah's diagnosis is not "they are rich" but "they deem themselves beyond need." Ibn Kathīr adds the observation that the Prophet ﷺ was hoping their wealth and rank would make them assets to Islam — and God informed him that the felt absence of need makes a person the least likely of all to receive guidance, whatever his assets.

**Why This Word Governs the Whole Sūrah**

*Istaghna* is the hinge on which Sūrah ʿAbasa turns, and its reach extends far beyond verse 5. The remainder of the sūrah is, in effect, a systematic demolition of the claim to self-sufficiency. Verses 17–22 answer it with the human being's origin: you were a drop of fluid; God created you, proportioned you, eased your way, killed you, buried you, and will raise you when He wills — at no point were you self-sufficient. Verses 24–32 answer it with the human being's daily sustenance: look at your food; you did not pour the water, you did not split the earth, you did not grow the grain. Verses 33–42 answer it with the human being's future: on the Day of the Piercing Cry you will flee from your brother, your mother, your father, your wife, and your sons, and your own affair will be all you have. The word "self-sufficient" is contradicted by a man's past, his present, and his future. This is the Qur'an's standard method of argument against pride — not to denounce it rhetorically but to enumerate the facts that make it impossible.

**The Blindness of Feeling Complete**

The connection between verse 5 and verse 2 is now visible. The blind man in the doorway was physically blind and spiritually sighted; the notables were physically sighted and, by *istighnāʾ*, spiritually blind. The Qur'an's diagnosis of unbelief is rarely a failure of evidence. It is a failure of appetite: the person who feels no lack has no room for a gift. This is a psychological observation of great precision, and it explains a phenomenon familiar to every religious community — that arguments which transform one listener leave another completely unmoved, and the difference is not intelligence but felt need. Al-Rāzī formulates the principle: guidance is a medicine, and medicine benefits only the one who knows he is ill.

**Modern Forms of Istighnā**

The contemporary versions of *istaghnā* are less about camels and clan than about the modern infrastructure of self-sufficiency: the bank balance that makes the future feel insured, the career that makes identity feel self-generated, the algorithm that supplies every desire before it is named, the analytic framework that claims to explain religion without needing it. A person in this condition is not necessarily wicked; he is simply full. And fullness is the obstacle the sūrah identifies, because the Qur'anic promise of benefit is addressed to the hungry: *"O mankind, you are the poor toward God, and God is the Rich, the Praiseworthy"* (35:15). The verse is not a statement of economic status but of ontological status, and it is the exact negation of *istaghnā*. Islamic spirituality built its disciplines on this negation — *faqr* (spiritual poverty) is the Sufi name for the awareness of one's total dependence, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have prayed, *"Do not leave me to myself for the blink of an eye"* (recorded in the ḥadīth collections of Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī from Anas, in the well-known morning supplication). To be left to oneself is the definition of ruin; to be in need of God, and to know it, is the definition of wealth.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:6

> **to him dost thou attend,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taṣaddā: The Verb of Devoted Attention**

*Fa-anta lahu taṣaddā* — "to him dost thou attend." The verb *taṣaddā* is derived by the grammarians and exegetes from *ṣ-d-d* in the sense of confronting, facing, and exposing oneself to a thing: *taṣaddā li-shayʾ* means "he addressed himself to it, he devoted himself to it, he turned his full face toward it." Al-Ṭabarī explains it as *taʿarriḍ* — presenting oneself to a matter, engaging with it deliberately. Al-Qurṭubī gives the sense as "you occupy yourself with him and turn toward him." Others connect it to *ṣadā*, echo, or to *taṣaddaqa* (to give alms), but the exegetical consensus settles on devoted, purposive attention.

The force of the verse lies in the combination of that verb with the pronoun *anta* — "you." The sentence does not merely say "attention is being given to him"; it says "*you* are giving it," with the subject pronoun explicitly stated for emphasis. The Prophet's own attention is named, singled out, and placed on the record. There is a studied precision here: God does not reproach him for his intention (which was the spread of Islam), for his speech (which was the Qur'an), or for his company (which included his own uncle al-ʿAbbās); He reproaches the allocation of his attention. Attention, the verse teaches, is a moral act — perhaps the most fundamental one, since every other act depends on it.

**Attention as a Finite Resource and a Moral Question**

The Qur'an repeatedly treats attention as something that can be spent, wasted, diverted, and squandered, and it commands its direction explicitly:

> *"And turn not thine eyes away from them, desiring the adornment of the life of this world"* (18:28).

> *"Turn thy face toward the upright religion"* (30:30).

> *"Do not strain thine eyes toward that wherewith We have provided some couples of them"* (15:88; cf. 20:131).

Modern life has made the point easier to see than it was for most of history. Attention is now an explicitly traded commodity: platforms buy it, advertisers price it, feeds compete for it, and metrics measure it. The economy of attention is the largest industry on earth, and its organizing principle is precisely *istaghnā* paired with visibility — attend to whoever is impressive, whoever pays, whoever amplifies you. Sūrah ʿAbasa intervenes in that economy at its root: the criterion for allocating attention is need and sincerity, not power and return. The verse's rebuke is not that the Prophet attended to important men but that he attended to them *while a sincere seeker was waiting*, which is the specific moral failure that attention economies are designed to normalize.

**Why the Object Comes First in the Word Order**

Arabic word order here places the pronoun *lahu* ("to him") before the verb *taṣaddā*, and the Qur'an's grammarians note that fronting produces restriction (*ḥaṣr*) — "it is *to him* that you attend." The effect is comparative: it is to *him*, and not to the other. Al-Zamakhsharī's reading of the syntax sharpens the contrast with verse 10, where the same structure appears for the blind man: *fa-anta ʿanhu talahhā* — "from him thou art diverted." The two sentences are mirror images: *to him* you attend, *from him* you are diverted. The sūrah has constructed a pair of scales, and the two men are the weights.

**The Preacher's Temptation in Every Age**

Ibn Kathīr reports the reasoning behind the Prophet's conduct: he hoped that if the chiefs accepted Islam, the people would accept it after them, since the Arabs followed their leaders. The reasoning is not only intelligible but strategically sound, and it is exactly the reasoning that religious institutions have used ever since — the patron, the platform, the influential convert, the celebrity endorsement, the wealthy donor whose support makes the whole enterprise viable. The Qur'an does not declare that reasoning invalid; it declares that it may not be exercised at the expense of the sincere seeker standing at your shoulder. The distinction is crucial and is the substance of *fiqh al-awlawiyyāt*: competing goods are ranked, and the ranking given here is that the person who has come seeking takes precedence over the person being courted. Any institution that inverts this ranking, whatever its strategic gains, has inverted the ruling of Sūrah ʿAbasa.

**A Personal Reading of the Verse**

The verse also speaks to ordinary human relationships, where attention is the rarest gift anyone gives. To attend (*taṣaddā*) to a person is to face him fully, to stop doing the second thing, to give him the whole of one's cognitive presence. Its absence is what people actually experience when they are heard half-way: the glance at the phone, the body angled toward the door, the reply prepared before the sentence ends. The Prophet's frown was a half-second; the modern equivalent lasts for hours and is called multitasking. That God should have revealed a permanent chapter about a moment of divided attention is the strongest possible statement of the value the Qur'an places on being fully present with the person in front of you — especially when that person has nothing to offer you but his need.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:7

> **though thou art not answerable, should he not be purified.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence of Release in the Middle of a Rebuke**

*Mā ʿalayka allā yazzakkā* — literally, "there is not upon you that he should not purify himself." The commentators render it in two complementary ways. Al-Ṭabarī: "you are not blameworthy if he does not purify himself." Ibn Kathīr: "you are not answerable for him if he does not become pure." Al-Qurṭubī: "it is not your responsibility that he be purified; your duty is only to convey." All three readings agree on the substance, and the substance is astonishing in its position: the sentence of release is placed in the very middle of the rebuke, immediately after the criticism of the Prophet's attention and immediately before the criticism of his diversion. God corrects His Messenger and, in the same breath, removes from him the burden that caused the error.

This is the structural key to the entire passage. The Prophet's over-attention to the notables was generated by a sense of responsibility for their belief — he wanted them to be purified and felt the weight of that outcome. Verse 7 informs him that the outcome is not his. Once that burden is lifted, the misallocation of attention loses its justification: there is no strategic case for neglecting the seeker, because there was never any responsibility for converting the notable. The rebuke and the release are one argument, not two.

**The Recurring Formula: "Not Upon You"**

The Qur'an uses this formula repeatedly, and each occurrence marks a boundary of prophetic responsibility:

> *"And if they turn away, then upon thee rests only the conveyance"* (3:20).

> *"Remind, for upon thee rests only the reminder; thou art not a watcher over them"* (88:21–22).

> *"So remind; thou art not, by the grace of thy Lord, a soothsayer or a madman"* (52:29–30, in context).

> *"Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but God guides whomsoever He will"* (28:56).

> *"Upon thee rests only the conveyance, and upon Us the reckoning"* (13:40).

> *"Say: Obey God and obey the Messenger; but if they turn away, upon him rests only what is laid upon him, and upon you rests only what is laid upon you"* (24:54).

The cumulative effect is a precise division of labor in the economy of salvation. Guidance in the sense of *hidāyat al-irshād wa'l-bayān* — showing the way, clarifying, conveying — is the prophet's task and the inheriting scholar's task. Guidance in the sense of *hidāyat al-tawfīq* — making the heart accept the way — belongs to God alone. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Taymiyyah both employ this distinction, and it is standard in Sunni theology. Verse 7 is one of its clearest Qur'anic anchors.

**The Psychological Wisdom of the Boundary**

There is a deep pastoral logic in this verse that the Islamic tradition recognized and applied. A person who believes he is responsible for other people's hearts will do one of two things: he will despair when they do not change, or he will manipulate them until they appear to change. Both are destructive, and both are common in religious leadership, in parenting, in marriage, and in friendship. The Qur'an's boundary — you are responsible for the delivery, not for the landing — is the only stable foundation for sustained moral effort. It permits the teacher to keep teaching after a hundred failures, the parent to keep advising an adult child who has walked away, the friend to keep speaking honestly without demanding a response, and the caller to God to keep knocking on doors that do not open. The Prophet ﷺ was consoled by exactly this principle at its most painful moment:

> *"Let thy soul not be consumed with grief over them, if they do not believe in this discourse"* (18:6).

> *"And it may be that thou wouldst kill thyself with grief over them, because they believe not in this discourse"* (26:3).

> *"Thou guidest not whom thou lovest"* (28:56) — revealed, according to the exegetes, concerning Abū Ṭālib, the Prophet's uncle, whose death as a disbeliever grieved him beyond measure.

Verse 7 is thus not a dismissal of concern but a correction of ownership. Care is commanded; ownership of the outcome is forbidden.

**Why the Verse Does Not Say "It Is Not Your Fault"**

Note the exact phrasing: *"thou art not answerable, should he not be purified"* — the accountability is framed around *purification*, the same word used of the blind man in verse 3. The symmetry is deliberate. The Prophet was not answerable for the notable's failure to purify himself; equally, the notable's refusal does not diminish the blind man's capacity to purify himself. Neither man's inner state is in the Prophet's hands, which is why the correct rule for him — and for every caller after him — is to give attention according to need and sincerity rather than according to expected return. Once the return is no longer your responsibility, the calculation of return becomes not merely unnecessary but prohibited.

**Applying the Boundary**

The verse supplies a discipline for anyone who advises others. Speak the truth clearly and kindly; then release the outcome. Do not measure your fidelity by another person's response, and do not purchase his response by degrading your attention to those who are actually listening. This is the practical resolution of a tension every sincere person feels — the desire to help the person who most needs help and the temptation to invest where the results are visible. Sūrah ʿAbasa settles the tension by removing the results from the equation entirely: *"thou art not answerable, should he not be purified."* The answerability lies in the direction of the face, not in the content of the other's heart.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:8

> **But as for him who came to thee striving earnestly**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn of the Scales**

*Wa-ammā man jāʾaka yasʿā* — "but as for him who came to thee striving earnestly." The particle *ammā* ("as for") resumes the contrast begun in verse 5 and completes the sūrah's central comparison. Verse 5 named one man by his inner claim — *istaghnā*, he deemed himself beyond need; verse 8 names the other by his outer movement — *jāʾaka yasʿā*, he came to you striving. The two descriptions are exact opposites in the moral physics of the Qur'an: stillness with a sense of sufficiency, against movement with a sense of need.

The verb *jāʾa* recurs from verse 2, binding the two statements of the incident together: the blind man "came to him" (v. 2), and God now says "he came to thee" (v. 8). The shift from the third person ("to him") to the second person ("to thee") is significant. In verse 2 the arrival was narrated as an event in the world; in verse 8 it is addressed to the Prophet as a personal claim. God is telling him: this man came *to you*. The arrival was not accidental and not a nuisance; it was directed at you, and it created an obligation upon you.

**Yasʿā: The Root of Earnest Movement**

*Yasʿā* comes from *s-ʿ-y*, which denotes walking with effort and purpose — running, striding, laboring toward an end. It is not idle movement. The Qur'an uses the word for the most solemn kinds of human exertion: for the hastening to the Friday prayer, *"O ye who believe, when the call is proclaimed for prayer on Friday, hasten (fa-sʿaw) to the remembrance of God"* (62:9); for the effort of the mother Hājar between the two hills, which the rites of pilgrimage preserve as *saʿy* between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah; for the striving of the believer in the way of God, *"and whosoever strives, strives only for himself"* (29:6); and for the labor of earning a living, *"and that man has only what he strives for"* (53:39). Al-Ṭabarī glosses *yasʿā* here as "he comes to you walking, in eagerness, seeking guidance and knowledge."

The word therefore carries the whole moral weight of initiative. In the Qur'anic picture, guidance is not a commodity distributed to the passively eligible; it meets the one who moves toward it. *"And those who strive for Us — We surely guide them upon Our ways"* (29:69). Ibn Kathīr quotes this verse in his commentary on 80:8–9 as the general rule of which Ibn Umm Maktūm is the specific case: the one who strives is the one who is guided.

**Why Striving Is Named Before Fear**

Verse 8 mentions the effort; verse 9 adds the fear (*wa-huwa yakhshā*). The order matters. First the movement, then the state of heart that produced it. The Qur'an is describing a person from the outside inward, exactly as an observer would: you see him come, working at it, and then you learn what is inside. This sequence answers a common spiritual error — the belief that inner feeling alone constitutes religiosity. Ibn Umm Maktūm's fear of God was real, but it became visible and effective only because it made him walk. The Islamic ethical tradition consistently measures sincerity by its cost: what a person was willing to give up, to risk, or to walk through. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the well-known report on the ranks of faith, that the strongest form of jihad is *"a word of truth before an unjust ruler"* (Abū Dāwūd 4344; al-Tirmidhī 2174, who graded it ḥasan) — because the word costs something. Here the cost was the blind man's journey, his persistence, and his willingness to interrupt a gathering of important men.

**The Blind Man's Saʿy and the Pilgrim's Saʿy**

There is a resonance worth noting between the striving of verse 8 and the *saʿy* of the pilgrimage. Between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah the pilgrim walks and runs seven times in memory of a desperate mother seeking water for her infant — an act of exertion performed by someone who had no guarantee and no capacity, and who was nevertheless rewarded with the well of Zamzam. The rite institutionalizes exactly the disposition described in 80:8–9: need, effort, fear, and the refusal to sit still. When Ibn Umm Maktūm came to the Prophet ﷺ saying "guide me, guide me," he was performing the same movement in the domain of knowledge, and God honored it by making it the occasion of a permanent chapter.

**The One Who Comes and the One Who Is Courted**

Set beside verse 6, verse 8 produces the sūrah's sharpest practical rule. The notable was seated, self-sufficient, and being pursued; the blind man was moving, needy, and being deferred. Institutions — religious, academic, commercial — naturally optimize for the first category, because the first category is where visible returns lie. The Qur'an's ranking is the reverse, and it gives the reason in the structure itself: the man who came is the one who may be purified (v. 3) and the one whom the reminder may benefit (v. 4), while the man who deems himself beyond need has closed the door from the inside. A caller to God who understands this will organize his time around arrivals rather than around targets — and will regard every unexpected arrival as potentially the more important appointment of the day.

**Modern Striving**

Contemporary life makes *saʿy* cheaper in some ways and rarer in others. Information is available without walking, which removes the physical test; but attention is fragmented by design, which removes the interior one. The striving that verse 8 describes is not merely the effort of obtaining but the effort of remaining — of sitting with a difficult text, of asking the question again after the first answer, of returning to a teacher, of finishing what was begun. Ibn Umm Maktūm's report says he "kept saying" — *wa huwa yaqūlu: arshidnī* — repetition, persistence, the refusal to take the hint. That is the striving the verse names, and it is the quality most efficiently extinguished by a culture that supplies every answer instantly and rewards nobody for waiting.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:9

> **while fearful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-Huwa Yakhshā: The State Inside the Striving**

Three words in Arabic — *wa-huwa yakhshā*, "and he is fearful" — and they contain the sūrah's definition of the person worthy of attention. The verb is *khashiya*, from *kh-sh-y*, which is not the ordinary word for fear. Arabic distinguishes *khawf* (fear of a harm that may strike, the fear of the body) from *khashyah* (fear mingled with reverence, awe, and knowledge of the greatness of the one feared — the fear of the heart). The classical lexicographers define *khashyah* as fear based on knowledge of the object's majesty, and the Qur'an uses it precisely in that register:

> *"Those of His servants who have knowledge fear (yakhshā) God"* (35:28).

> *"Who fear their Lord in the unseen"* (67:12; cf. 21:49).

> *"And as for him who feared (khāfa) the station of his Lord and forbade the soul its caprice, surely Paradise shall be his refuge"* (79:40–41).

The last of these is from Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt, the chapter immediately before ʿAbasa, and it functions as the thesis that ʿAbasa's first ten verses illustrate: the warner's message reaches the one who fears (*"Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it"* — 79:45), and here is that man, walking into the room.

**The Grammatical Force of "While"**

The particle *wa-* in *wa-huwa yakhshā* is the circumstantial *wāw* (*wāw al-ḥāl*), which indicates that the second clause describes the state in which the first action occurred: "he came to you striving *while* he was fearful." Al-Zamakhsharī notes the significance: the fear is not a later addition to the striving but its condition and its companion. The man did not first strive and then acquire reverence; reverence was already in him, and reverence is what produced the striving. This is the Qur'an's consistent psychology — *khawf* and *rajaʾ* (fear and hope) as the two wings by which the believer travels. Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Madārij al-Sālikīn*, describes fear as the whip that drives the traveler forward and hope as the rein that keeps him on the road; without fear, the traveler stops.

**Why Fear Is the Sign of Spiritual Sight**

The contrast with verse 5 is now complete and devastating. The notable felt no need, therefore he felt no fear, therefore he did not move. The blind man knew his position before God, therefore he feared, therefore he walked. The Qur'an thus identifies *khashyah* as the accurate perception of reality and *istighnāʾ* as the distortion of it. The man who is afraid is not weak; he is correctly informed. He sees what is actually true — that he is mortal, accountable, dependent, and headed toward a judgment — and his fear is the proportionate response to accurate information. The man who is unafraid is not brave; he is misinformed. This is why the Prophet ﷺ is described in the Qur'an as the one with the greatest *khashyah*: *"Say: I am the first of those who submit... "* (39:12), and the report in al-Bukhārī 5063 and Muslim 1108 from ʿĀʾishah and Anas:

> *"By God, I am the most fearful of God among you and the most knowledgeable of Him of what He has commanded."* (al-Bukhārī 5063)

Fear and knowledge rise together; the greatest knower of God is the most in awe of Him. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the one who fears God is the one who obeys Him, and the exegetes cite this as the definition embedded in 80:9: the blind man's fear was not a mood but an obedience in motion.

**The Fear Named in the Verse Is Specific**

Al-Qurṭubī and the source commentary specify that the fear mentioned in verse 9 is fear of God and of the Day of Judgment — not fear of the crowd, not fear of the notable men in the room, not social anxiety. This is an important qualification, because the man walking into that gathering was almost certainly also nervous in the ordinary human sense: he was blind, he was interrupting, he was addressing a Prophet surrounded by the aristocracy of Makkah who might mock him. The Qur'an mentions only the fear that matters. Whatever social apprehension he felt is not recorded, but his awe of God is recorded forever — and in the very act of recording it, God dignifies the emotion that a hostile room would have called weakness.

**The Balance: Fear Without Despair**

Islamic theology is careful to keep *khashyah* from collapsing into despair (*yaʾs*), which the Qur'an names as a sin in itself: *"and despair not of God's mercy; surely none despairs of God's mercy save the disbelieving people"* (12:87). The fear praised in verse 9 is the fear of a person who is moving toward God, not away from Him — the fear that produces *saʿy*, not paralysis. The Sufi manuals distinguish *khawf al-ʿiqāb* (fear of punishment), *khawf al-ḥijāb* (fear of being veiled from God), and *haybah* (awe). Ibn Umm Maktūm's fear belongs to the highest practical kind: he was afraid of missing the guidance he needed, and that fear sent him walking. The test of the health of any fear is whether it produces movement toward God or flight from Him.

**The Modern Reader and the Loss of Awe**

Modern culture has largely lost the vocabulary of awe and replaced it with the vocabulary of comfort. Religion is marketed as reassurance, and reverence is treated as an obstacle to accessibility. Sūrah ʿAbasa restores the older order: the person best positioned to receive guidance is not the one most at ease but the one most aware of what is at stake. The pastoral application is not to frighten people artificially — the Qur'an never counsels that — but to stop flattering the illusion of self-sufficiency, and to recognize that the seekers who arrive with trembling questions are the ones the message was sent for. *"Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it"* (79:45). The one who fears has already begun to be guided; the one who does not fear has already begun to be lost.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:10

> **from him thou art diverted.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Talahhā: The Verb of Being Otherwise Occupied**

*Fa-anta ʿanhu talahhā* — "from him thou art diverted." The verb *talahhā* is from the root *l-h-w*, the root of diversion, amusement, and preoccupation: *lahw* is that which occupies a person and distracts him from what is important. The Qur'an uses it for the amusements of worldly life:

> *"The life of this world is but play and diversion (lahw)"* (6:32; 29:64; 47:36; 57:20).

> *"Mutual rivalry in increase has diverted you (alhākum al-takāthur), until you visit the graves"* (102:1–2).

> *"And when they see some merchandise or some diversion, they scatter toward it and leave thee standing"* (62:11).

The last of these is instructive: the same root describes the congregation that left the Prophet ﷺ mid-khuṭbah when a trade caravan arrived. *Lahw* is what pulls attention away from the thing that matters, and in 80:10 it is applied to the Prophet's own attention in a grammatically emphatic form. Al-Ṭabarī explains *ta-talahhā* as "you occupy yourself such that you are distracted from him," and notes the reading of Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr with the doubled *tāʾ* (*ta-talahhā*), which intensifies the sense of being absorbed elsewhere.

**The Pairing of Verses 6 and 10**

Verse 6 said: *fa-anta lahu taṣaddā* — "to him dost thou attend." Verse 10 says: *fa-anta ʿanhu talahhā* — "from him thou art diverted." The two sentences are built on the same frame (*fa-anta* + prepositional phrase + verb) and are exact inverses: the preposition changes from *li-* (toward) to *ʿan* (away from), and the verb changes from devoted attention to absorbed distraction. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī remark on the symmetry: the sūrah has drawn the two men in a single balance, and the Prophet's face is the pointer between them. The rhetorical effect is to make the reader feel the injustice of the allocation physically — the same finite quantity of attention, tilted one way and away from the other, in a single moment.

**"Diverted," Not "Rejected"**

The choice of word is remarkable in its fairness. God does not say "you scorned him," "you despised him," or "you turned him away." He says *you were diverted from him* — a description that acknowledges the pull of the competing interest without inventing malice. Ibn Kathīr stresses that the Prophet's conduct contained no contempt for Ibn Umm Maktūm and no sin of the heart; the fault was one of ordering. This precision is itself a lesson in how to speak about the failings of others, including the failings of the best of people: describe what happened, in the mildest accurate terms, and leave the heart to its Lord. The Qur'an's rebuke of its own Messenger is the model of correction without cruelty.

**The Hidden Injustice of Distraction**

Distraction is the most common form of injustice precisely because it never feels like one. Nobody decides to slight a person; attention simply goes elsewhere, drawn by whatever is louder, richer, or more consequential, and the person who loses it is usually not told. Verse 10 makes visible the structure of that loss: for one man to be attended to, another must be un-attended to, because attention is not expandable in the moment. The economics are zero-sum in a way that money is not, which is why the Qur'an polices the direction of attention so carefully and why it addresses the matter here at the level of a single facial expression.

**The Two Men and the Closing Panel of the Sūrah**

The source commentary notes an interpretive thread that runs from verse 10 to verse 42. The man "diverted from" in verse 10 — the striving, fearful seeker — reappears at the end of the sūrah with a shining, laughing, rejoicing face (vv. 38–39); the man "attended to" in verse 6 — the one who deemed himself beyond need — reappears with a face covered in dust and overspread with darkness (vv. 40–41). The sūrah thus supplies its own verdict on the allocation of attention in that Makkan room. The man whom the Prophet was gently reproached for neglecting receives, in the same chapter, the radiant face of Paradise; and the men for whose sake he was reproached receive the darkened faces of the fire. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both draw this connection, and it transforms the passage from a historical anecdote into a permanent scale: what is attended to in this world does not determine what is honored in the next.

**Practical Discipline Drawn from the Verse**

Three concrete disciplines follow. First, finish the sentence of the person in front of you before turning to the person who has just entered, or before the phone — and if you must turn, say so and return. Second, audit your calendar by arrivals rather than by status: how much of your week goes to those who came seeking, and how much to those you are courting? Third, treat every instance of "I'll get to that person later" as a moral datum rather than an administrative one, because verse 10 records that the "later" of a single moment was written into the Qur'an. The blind man's reward was not diminished by the delay — God took up his case Himself — but the delay was still recorded, and the recording is the instruction.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:11

> **Nay! Truly it is a reminder—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā: The Particle That Stops the Scene**

*Kallā* — "Nay! Truly!" — is one of the Qur'an's most powerful small words, appearing thirty-three times, always in the Makkan sūrahs. Grammarians and exegetes describe it as a particle of *radʿ* and *zajr*: it repels, checks, and puts a stop to what preceded. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭabarī explain that *kallā* here closes the incident of verses 1–10 and declares that the matter just discussed is not the whole matter. The scene in the room — the frown, the blind man, the notables — is being lifted away from, and the sūrah opens its second panel.

The word functions like a judge's gavel and like a turning page at once. What preceded is finished and was not as important as it seemed; what follows is the real subject. Rhetorically this is one of the most effective uses of *kallā* in the Qur'an, because the ten verses before it are so human and so specific that the reader needs to be told that the story is not the point. Sūrah al-Muddaththir uses *kallā* in the same structural position: *"Nay! Truly it is a Reminder"* (74:54), following the description of al-Walīd's proud deliberation — and the source commentary rightly cross-references it.

**Tadhkirah: What the Qur'an Calls Itself**

*Innahū tadhkirah* — "truly it is a reminder." The noun *tadhkirah* is from the same root *dh-k-r* discussed in verse 4, and it is one of the Qur'an's own names for itself. The self-designations are numerous and each carries a distinct claim: *kitāb* (book), *furqān* (criterion), *nūr* (light), *huda* (guidance), *shifāʾ* (healing), *raḥmah* (mercy), *mawʿiẓah* (admonition), *balāgh* (sufficient conveyance), and *tadhkirah* (reminder). Each of these appears in explicit self-statements:

> *"This is but a reminder and a clear Qur'an"* (36:69).

> *"And this is a blessed Reminder which We have sent down"* (21:50).

> *"Nay, but it is a glorious Qur'an, in a Preserved Tablet"* (85:21–22).

> *"And remind, for the Reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55).

The choice of *tadhkirah* here is programmatic. A reminder presupposes that what is being remembered already exists in the one who is reminded. The Qur'an's claim, stated in verse 11 of this sūrah and developed in 7:172 (the primordial covenant), is that it is not introducing humanity to a stranger but calling it home. Al-Rāzī builds much of his theology of revelation on this: the function of the prophet is *tadhkīr*, and the function of the hearer is *dhikr*.

**Three Referents of "It"**

The exegetes identify three possible referents for the pronoun in *innahū tadhkirah*, and the source commentary lists them: the Qur'an as a whole; this sūrah in particular; or the exhortation to treat people equitably that the preceding verses contain (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī). All three readings are compatible, and the classical method allows a verse to carry them together. The most consequential reading is the third: the instruction about attention is itself "a reminder" — that is, it belongs to the revealed deposit and is not a private managerial preference. What was said about the blind man in verses 1–10 is therefore not situational advice for one man in one room; it is part of the Reminder, binding on the ummah until the Hour.

**The Prophet's Job Description, Restated**

Verse 11 also carries the sūrah's answer to the anxiety that produced the frown. The Prophet ﷺ was absorbed in the notables because he felt the weight of their guidance. *Kallā innahū tadhkirah* tells him — and every caller after him — the shape of the task: it is to remind. Not to convert, not to persuade, not to secure the allegiance of the influential, not to build the movement's public standing. To remind. And a reminder's success is measured by its delivery, not by its reception, which is why verse 12 immediately hands the outcome over to the will of the hearer: *"so let whomsoever will, remember it."* The source commentary connects this to 88:21–24:

> *"So remind; thou art but a remembrancer. Thou art not a watcher over them"* (88:21–22).

**Why a Reminder Must Be Freely Taken**

The logic of *tadhkirah* explains why the Qur'an never coerces. If the truth is already present in the human constitution, then compulsion is not only wrong but useless: a coerced assent is not a remembered truth, and remembrance must be an act of the person who remembers. This is the theological root of *"There is no compulsion in religion"* (2:256) and of *"And say: The truth is from your Lord; so let whoever will believe, and let whoever will disbelieve"* (18:29). Verse 11 states the nature of the message; verse 12 states the freedom of the receiver. Together they define the entire Islamic theory of religious communication: a reminder, delivered, and left with the one who wills.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:12

> **so let whomsoever will, remember it—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings of a Single Pronoun**

*Fa-man shāʾa dhakarah* — "so let whomsoever will, remember it." The source commentary records a well-known exegetical alternative: the pronoun could equally be read as referring to God, giving "so let whomsoever He wills, remember Him" (al-Qurṭubī). The two readings are not merely possible variants; they mark the two poles of the classical debate over divine will and human choice, and the Qur'an deliberately places both within reach of the same four words.

There is also a grammatical argument that supports the second reading, which the source notes: the noun *tadhkirah* in verse 11 is feminine, while the pronoun in *dhakarah* is masculine. If the pronoun referred back to *tadhkirah* ("remember *it*"), one would expect the feminine *-hā* (*dhakarahā*). The masculine form therefore points to a masculine referent — either God ("remember Him") or the Qur'an understood as masculine in respect of one of its other names (*al-Qurʾān*, *al-kitāb*, *al-dhikr*), which Ibn Kathīr considers the simplest resolution. Al-Qurṭubī prefers the sense "remember Him," since remembrance of God is the substance of what the Reminder contains.

**Human Will Stated at Its Plainest**

On the first reading the verse is one of the most direct statements of human moral agency in the entire Qur'an. *Fa-man shāʾa* — "so whoever wills." The verb *shāʾa* ("to will") is placed in the hand of the hearer without qualification. The same formula appears at the end of Sūrah al-Muddaththir in a passage that is functionally identical:

> *"Nay! Truly it is a Reminder; so whoever wills, let him remember it — and they will not remember unless God wills"* (74:54–56).

And in Sūrah al-Insān:

> *"Nay! This is a Reminder; so whoever wills, let him take a way to his Lord. And you do not will unless God wills"* (76:29–30).

The pattern is fixed: the invitation is issued in the language of unrestricted human choice, and the dependence of that choice on God is then stated in the next breath. Neither half may be taken alone. Sunni theology resolved the tension with the doctrine of *kasb* (acquisition), associated with al-Ashʿarī, and the doctrine of the two levels of will (*mashīʾah kaawniyyah* and *irādah sharʿiyyah*), developed at length by Ibn Taymiyyah: God wills all that occurs in the sense that nothing escapes His power and knowledge, and He commands what He loves; the human being genuinely chooses within that encompassing will and is answerable for the choice. Verse 12 states the human half; the parallel verses state the divine half; the believer is required to hold both.

**Why Freedom Is Essential to the Reminder**

There is a logical necessity in the arrangement, and it is worth spelling out. If a reminder is to be a reminder, the one reminded must be free to ignore it — otherwise the "remembering" would be an event happening to him rather than an act performed by him, and it would carry no moral value. The Qur'an insists on the reality of the choice precisely because it insists on the reality of the reward and the punishment: *"There is no compulsion in religion"* (2:256); *"And say: The truth is from your Lord; so let whoever will believe, and let whoever will disbelieve"* (18:29). Sūrah al-Kahf adds the severe clause that follows such freedom — *"We have prepared for the wrongdoers a fire whose canopy shall encompass them"* — because the dignity of a real choice includes the reality of its consequence. Verse 12 is thus the hinge between the sūrah's middle panels: the Reminder has been declared (v. 11), the freedom of the receiver has been declared (v. 12), and the remainder of the sūrah will show what the receiver has to remember (vv. 17–32) and what the choice will cost or yield (vv. 33–42).

**The Prophet's Relief**

Read in its immediate context, verse 12 is the completion of the consolation begun in verse 7. There the Prophet was told he is not answerable if the notable is not purified; here he is told that the Reminder is offered to whoever wills. The burden he was carrying — the sense that the fate of Quraysh's chiefs depended on his performance in that conversation — is formally lifted. The message is public property, freely available, and its acceptance is a matter for each will. This is why the sūrah can move so calmly from a tense human scene to a description of heavenly pages: the incident is over, and the Reminder stands independent of it.

**Modern Application: The Offer and the Opt-In**

The verse has a direct bearing on how religious truth should be presented in a plural world. The Islamic model is an offer made clearly, to whoever wills, with no coercion, no manipulation, and no ownership of the outcome. This excludes three modern practices: the high-pressure conversion technique, the emotional manipulation that exploits vulnerability, and the institutional habit of measuring faithfulness by numbers. It also excludes, on the other side, the dilution of the message to make it inoffensive — for a reminder that has been softened into a platitude is not worth anyone's will. The discipline of verse 12 is to state the truth in its full strength and then to leave it standing, as the Qur'an itself does, in the hands of whoever chooses to pick it up.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:13

> **on pages honored,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Room in Makkah to the Archive of Heaven**

The camera moves. Verse 12 closed the human scene; verse 13 opens a description of where the Reminder is kept: *fī ṣuḥufin mukarramah* — "on pages honored." The transition is one of the most striking in the Qur'an: a squabble over attention in a Makkan house, and then, without any connective explanation, the celestial record. Al-Rāzī draws the intended inference explicitly — the message for whose reception the Prophet was anxious is not his own composition and not a local event; it is inscribed in honored pages that existed before the argument and will exist after it.

**Ṣuḥuf: The Word and Its Plurals**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet, a page, a written document — a single leaf bearing writing, as opposed to a bound volume. Its own plural is *maṣāḥif*, the word from which *muṣḥaf*, the codex of the Qur'an, derives. The Qur'an uses *ṣuḥuf* in a small number of highly charged places, and each usage is relevant here:

> *"The scriptures (ṣuḥuf) of Abraham and Moses"* (53:36–37; and 87:18–19: *"Indeed this is in the former scriptures, the scriptures of Abraham and Moses"*).

> *"A messenger from God, reciting purified pages (ṣuḥufan muṭahharah)"* (98:2).

> *"Or has he not been informed of what is in the scriptures of Moses"* (53:36).

The exegetes therefore offer several identifications of the "pages" of 80:13. Some say they are the pages of the Qur'an. Others say they are the books of all the prophets, of which the Qur'an is the seal and sum. And al-Ṭabarī records the view that they are the Preserved Tablet itself:

> *"Nay, but it is a glorious Qur'an, in a Preserved Tablet (lawḥin maḥfūẓ)"* (85:21–22).

All three readings may be held together, as the classical method allows: the Reminder exists in the eternal Tablet, was conveyed in the scriptures of the earlier prophets, and is now present in the Qur'an being recited in Makkah.

**Mukarramah: Honored by What It Contains**

*Mukarramah* is from *k-r-m*, the root of nobility, generosity, and honor: *karīm* is generous and noble, *karam* is nobility of substance, and *akram* is the most honoring. The Qur'an says of itself, *"Truly it is a noble Qur'an"* (56:77), and of the Messenger, *"Truly it is the word of a noble messenger"* (69:40; 81:19). The commentators explain that the pages are honored not by their material — parchment and ink are ordinary — but by what they carry. Al-Qurṭubī states the principle: the honor of a thing follows the honor of what is in it. The same logic is applied to places (the Sacred Mosque is honored by the Kaʿbah), times (the Night of Power is honored by what descends in it), and persons (the believer is honored by his faith).

**The Principle That Everything Borrows Its Rank**

This small observation carries a large theological consequence that the classical scholars were fond of drawing: rank in the sight of God is never intrinsic to matter but always derivative of function and content. The paper of a muṣḥaf is honored because of the revelation on it; a human being is honored because of the obedience in him. *"Indeed the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you"* (49:13) — not the wealthiest, not the best-born, not the most eloquent. The verse thereby supplies, twelve verses after the incident, the definitive answer to the notables of Quraysh: they believed that lineage and wealth conferred honor; the Qur'an replies that honor is a property of content, and that the honored pages in heaven contain nothing of their clan's boasting.

**The Honor Due to the Physical Book**

Islamic law and etiquette took verse 13 as the basis for the treatment of the physical muṣḥaf. The jurists derive from the "honored" pages the rulings that the muṣḥaf should not be placed on the ground, that nothing should be laid on top of it, that it should be touched only in a state of ritual purity, and that it should be carried and shelved with respect. The textual anchor for the purity ruling is 56:79:

> *"None touch it save those made pure."*

Al-Shāfiʿī, Mālik, Abū Ḥanīfah, and Aḥmad all hold that touching the muṣḥaf requires *wuḍūʾ* on the basis of this verse and the report in the letter of the Prophet ﷺ to ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm: *"None should touch the Qur'an except one who is pure"* (Mālik, *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, and al-Dāraquṭnī). Whether "those made pure" means the angels or the ritually pure believers — or both — is debated; the practice of the ummah has taken both. A person who has grown up watching Muslims handle a muṣḥaf has already learned the exegesis of 80:13 in the body before learning it in the mind: the book is lifted, not dragged; it is kissed when it falls, in many lands; it is never placed beneath another object.

**The Modern Parallel: What We Treat as Honored**

Every culture has its honored pages — the documents it will not deface, the objects it handles with ceremony, the archives it protects at cost. What a society treats as honored reveals what it actually believes. Sūrah ʿAbasa sets the two competing honors side by side within a few verses: the honor of status, which the notables possessed and which produced no benefit (vv. 5–7), and the honor of a written reminder, which is available to whoever wills (vv. 11–13). The blind man could not read the pages, and he received their content; the chiefs could read them and did not. Verse 13 quietly reverses the world's assumption that access to honored things is a matter of qualification, and asserts instead that it is a matter of willingness.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:14

> **exalted and purified,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Marfūʿah: Raised in Rank and Raised in Place**

*Marfūʿah* is the passive participle of *rafaʿa* — to raise, to lift up, to elevate. Al-Qurṭubī records the view of the early exegetes, among them al-Zajjāj, that "exalted" means elevated in the estimation of God — honored by rank, not by altitude. Others, including al-Ṭabarī, take it spatially as well: raised in place, above the seven heavens, in the Preserved Tablet, beyond the reach of the devils. Both readings are held together in the classical exegesis, and both matter. The pages are exalted in dignity because of their content, and exalted in location because their content must be protected.

The Qur'an uses *rafaʿa* of the exaltation of persons and things repeatedly: *"And We raised high thy mention"* (94:4); *"God will raise in rank those who believe among you and those who have been given knowledge"* (58:11); *"He raises the heaven and sets the balance"* (55:7). The elevation spoken of in verse 14 is of the same kind as that promised to the people of knowledge: whatever carries the speech of God is lifted, and whoever carries it faithfully is lifted with it.

**Muṭahharah: Purified from What Cannot Be in It**

*Muṭahharah* is the passive participle of the fourth form of *ṭ-h-r*, "to purify" — made pure by an agent other than itself. The exegetes specify four kinds of purification, and Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī list them in substantially the same terms:

First, purification from alteration. The pages contain no addition and no deficiency; nothing human has entered them. This is the Qur'an's own claim, stated with legal precision:

> *"And the word of thy Lord has been fulfilled in truth and in justice. None is there to alter His words"* (6:115).

> *"Falsehood comes not at it from before it or behind it; a revelation from the Wise, the Praiseworthy"* (41:42).

Second, purification from contradiction. Al-Qurṭubī cites:

> *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an? Were it from other than God, they would have found in it much contradiction"* (4:82).

Third, purification from every impurity of meaning — no falsehood, no obscenity, no injustice, no praise of what God has condemned.

Fourth, purification from access by the impure:

> *"None touch it save those made pure"* (56:79).

Al-Rāzī adds the point that the physical handling rule and the spiritual access rule are the same rule at two levels: the one who is ritually impure may not touch the muṣḥaf, and the one whose heart is impure does not receive its meaning. This second form of exclusion is the one that the notables of Quraysh experienced: the pages were open, the recitation was public, and they could not enter.

**Why Both Words Are Needed**

*Marfūʿah* and *muṭahharah* are not synonyms, and the pairing is exact. Raisedness speaks of dignity and of distance from the low; purification speaks of content and of freedom from defect. A thing can be exalted and corrupt — a throne built on injustice — and a thing can be pure and lowly — a beggar's honest prayer. The pages of the Reminder are both: exalted in station and flawless in substance. This is the Qur'an's answer to every accusation leveled against it in Makkah — that it was poetry, that it was sorcery, that it was the dictation of a foreigner, that it was inconsistent: the accusation is not answered by argument alone but by an assertion of the document's ontological status. What you are hearing comes from pages that are honored (v. 13), exalted and purified (v. 14), held by noble and righteous scribes (vv. 15–16). The description is a certificate of authenticity issued from heaven, placed here precisely because the preceding verses concerned themselves with whether human beings would receive it.

**The Human Parallel: The Purified Vessel**

The tradition applied the same logic to the carriers of the message. The reciter of the Qur'an is expected to be pure in body and upright in conduct, not because the words lose power in an impure mouth but because the honor of the pages is meant to be visible in the person who carries them. The jurists therefore required *wuḍūʾ* for touching the muṣḥaf, and the ethicists required something harder: the Prophet ﷺ said,

> *"The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān)

and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said that the one who recites the Qur'an should be known by his night when people sleep, by his fasting when people eat, by his humility when people are proud, and by his silence when people talk. The pages are exalted and purified; the people who carry them are asked to be the same, and the ask is the whole of Islamic moral formation.

**A Contrast With the Room in Makkah**

The juxtaposition of verses 11–16 with verses 1–10 is deliberately jarring. Below: a frown, a crowd of self-sufficient men, an argument about who deserves attention. Above: honored, exalted, purified pages in the hands of noble and righteous scribes. The sūrah places the two scenes side by side so that the reader can measure them. What felt urgent in the room — the conversion of a chief — is not mentioned in the upper scene at all. What felt trivial in the room — a blind man's question — is the sort of thing those pages were made to answer. The architecture of the passage is itself the rebuke and the consolation: the Reminder is far above the politics of the gathering, and it is available to whoever wills.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:15

> **in the hands of scribes,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Safarah: A Word With Three Doors**

*Bi-aydī safarah* — "in the hands of scribes." *Safarah* is the plural of *sāfir*, from the root *s-f-r*, and this root is one of the most interesting in the Qur'an because it carries three distinct meanings, all of which the exegetes deploy here.

The first is writing. The *sifr* (plural *asfār*) is a book, and *safara* can mean to write:

> *"Or do you think that We hear not their secrets and their private counsels? Yea, and Our messengers are with them, recording"* (43:80).

> *"And they will say: Woe to us! What is this Book that leaves out nothing small or great but has numbered it?"* (18:49).

> *"And We have certainly created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are nearer to him than the jugular vein, when the two receivers, seated on the right and on the left, receive"* (50:16–17).

The second is mediation. The *safīr* (plural *sufarāʾ*) is an ambassador, a go-between, one who carries messages between two parties who cannot or will not meet directly. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both give this reading: the angels are *sufarāʾ* between God and His messengers, carrying revelation down.

The third is unveiling. *Safara* means to become clear and bright — *asfara al-ṣubḥ*, "the dawn broke"; *isfār* is the brightness of early morning. Al-Qurṭubī notes this as an allusion to the function of the Qur'an itself: it makes matters clear, it unveils what was hidden. This third sense has a remarkable consequence for the structure of the sūrah, because the very same root produces *musfirah* in verse 38 — "faces that Day shall be shining, radiant." The root that describes the angels who carry the Reminder in verse 15 describes the faces of those who received it in verse 38. The unveiling that happens in the book happens finally on the face. Whether the Qur'an intends this as a deliberate lexical frame is a matter of interpretation; that the two words share a root is a fact, and the classical exegetes noticed it.

**Angels as Scribes: The Two Identifications**

On the first reading the *safarah* are the attendant angels who record the deeds of human beings — the *kirāmān kātibīn* of 82:10–12:

> *"Indeed there are keepers over you, noble, recording, who know whatever you do"* (82:10–12).

> *"The Book will be set down, and thou wilt see the guilty fearful of what is in it"* (18:49).

On the second reading they are the angels of revelation — Gabriel foremost among them — who carry the speech of God from the Preserved Tablet to the prophets. Both identifications are coherent with "in the hands of," and al-Ṭabarī mentions a third possibility: that "the hands of scribes" refers to the human reciters and copyists of the Qur'an, the *ḥafazah* and *katabah* who write it down and transmit it.

**The Ḥadīth That Reads This Verse Over Every Reciter**

The Prophet ﷺ applied verse 15 directly to the people of the Qur'an, in one of the most beloved reports in the collections:

> *"The one who is proficient in the Qur'an will be with the noble, righteous scribes (al-safarah al-kirām al-bararah); and the one who recites the Qur'an and stumbles over it, and for whom it is difficult, will have two rewards."* (al-Bukhārī 4937; Muslim 798, from ʿĀʾishah)

The report performs a double act of generosity. It assigns the skilled reciter the company of the angels named in this verse — a rank no human can otherwise attain — and it refuses to exclude the one who struggles, giving him two rewards rather than none: one for the recitation and one for the effort. It is difficult to imagine a more exact application of the lesson of Sūrah ʿAbasa. The sūrah began with a blind man who stammered his request in front of eloquent aristocrats; the ḥadīth ends by promising the stammering reciter a double reward. In the economy of the Qur'an, difficulty sincerely borne outranks fluency carelessly possessed.

**Hands: The Organ of Transmission**

The phrase *bi-aydī* ("in the hands of") deserves a moment. The Qur'an frequently uses the hand as the image of transfer, custody, and responsibility: *"Blessed is He in whose hand is the dominion"* (67:1); *"in whose hand is the kingdom of everything"* (36:83); *"and We wrote for him upon the tablets an admonition for everything"* (7:145). To say that the pages are "in the hands of" the scribes is to say that they are held, carried, guarded, and delivered — not that they are authored. The scribes do not compose; they convey. The distinction is essential to the Qur'anic doctrine of revelation: nothing of the content originates with its carriers, whether angelic or human. This is why the Prophet ﷺ is described as a messenger and a reciter rather than as an author: *"Nor does he speak from caprice; it is nothing but a revelation revealed"* (53:3–4).

**The Human Chain of Hands**

Islamic civilization built its entire intellectual culture on this image of transmission through hands. The *isnād* — the chain of transmitters running from student to teacher to teacher back to the Prophet ﷺ — is a technology of custody: each pair of hands receives from a previous pair and is answerable for what it passes on. "This knowledge is religion," said Ibn Sīrīn (d. 110/729), "so look to whom you take your religion." The preservation of the Qur'an by *tawātur* — unbroken, mass transmission such that collusion in a lie is impossible — is the practical realization of verse 15 on earth. The pages are in the hands of scribes in heaven, and, by God's arrangement, they have never left the hands of scribes on earth: not for a generation, not for a century, not in a single one of the fourteen hundred years since.

**Modern Custody**

Every believer now holds those pages, usually in a pocket, usually unopened. The verse asks a question of the modern holder that it did not have to ask of the medieval one: what does it mean to have the honored, exalted, purified pages permanently available and largely unread? The ḥadīth's answer is the only merciful one available — that the stumbling reciter receives two rewards — because it makes the difficulty of reading an argument for reading rather than against it. The pages are not in your hands so that you may own them. They are in your hands so that they may be carried.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:16

> **noble and pious.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kirām: Nobility as a Property of Character**

*Kirām* is the plural of *karīm*, from *k-r-m* — nobility, generosity, honor. The Qur'an applies *karīm* to God Himself (*"O man, what has deluded thee concerning thy Lord, the Generous (al-Karīm)?"* — 82:6), to the Qur'an (*"Truly it is a noble Qur'an"* — 56:77), to the Messenger (*"Truly it is the word of a noble messenger"* — 69:40; 81:19), to sustenance (*"and We have brought forth therein every noble pair"* — 26:7; 31:10; 50:7), to the reward of the hereafter (*"a noble provision"* — 3:161; 8:4; 22:58), and to human beings who are honored by God: *"And We have certainly honored the children of Adam"* (17:70). The angels are described as *kirām* in several places, most notably in the title of the recording angels:

> *"Indeed there are keepers over you, noble (kirāman), recording"* (82:10–11).

Al-Qurṭubī explains *kirām* here as honored in God's sight, elevated in rank near Him. Al-Rāzī adds that the honor of the scribes redounds upon the honor of the pages, and the honor of the pages testifies to the honor of the message — a chain of dignity descending from the Sender to the sent.

**Bararah: The Root of Dutiful Goodness**

*Bararah* is the plural of *barr*, from *b-r-r*, the root of righteousness, dutifulness, and expansive goodness. It gives *birr* — the comprehensive virtue that the Qur'an defines at length in the Verse of Righteousness:

> *"Righteousness (al-birr) is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is belief in God and the Last Day, and the angels and the Book and the prophets, and giving of wealth, out of love for Him, to kinsmen and orphans and the needy and the traveler and those who ask, and for the freeing of slaves, and establishing prayer and giving alms; and those who fulfill their covenant when they covenant, and the patient in adversity and hardship and in time of conflict — those are the ones who are true, and those are the reverent"* (2:177).

The same root gives *al-Barr*, a Name of God: *"Indeed He is the All-Goodness (al-Barr), the Most Merciful"* (52:28), and *barr al-wālidayn* — dutifulness to parents, which the Qur'an joins directly to the worship of God: *"And thy Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and that you be good to your parents"* (17:23).

Applied to angels, *bararah* carries a specific theological point that the commentators draw out. Angels are described by the Qur'an as incapable of disobedience: *"They do not disobey God in what He commands them, and they do as they are commanded"* (66:6); *"And they say: The All-Merciful has taken a son — Glory be to Him! Nay, but they are honored servants; they do not precede Him with speech, and they act by His command"* (21:26–27). Their *birr* is total and unbroken. The word therefore means, of the angels, "righteous to the utmost degree," and it is a description that no human being can fully claim — which makes the promise of the ḥadīth (that the proficient reciter will be *with* them) all the more astonishing.

**The Two Adjectives Together: Rank and Character**

*Kirām bararah* pairs elevation with goodness, and the pairing is a deliberate Qur'anic habit: honor without righteousness is tyranny, and righteousness without honor is invisible. The angels are both — high in rank and flawless in conduct — and the combination is what makes them fit to carry the pages. The human lesson, drawn by al-Qurṭubī and the ethical tradition generally, is that a person entrusted with the speech of God must seek both: knowledge that elevates and character that purifies. The scholar without piety is a danger; the pious without knowledge is easily led. *"God raises in rank those who believe among you and those who have been given knowledge"* (58:11) — but the raising is of believers who have knowledge, not of either alone.

**The Scribes as a Mirror of the Blind Man**

There is a quiet symmetry between verse 16 and the incident that opened the sūrah. The Qurayshī notables were also "noble" in the world's estimation — *kirām* by lineage, wealth, and standing — but they were not *bararah*. Ibn Umm Maktūm was neither noble by lineage-claim nor impressive in appearance, but he was righteous. The verse therefore restates the sūrah's judgment in the vocabulary of the very quality the notables prided themselves on: true nobility is *birr*, and the only people fit to hold the pages are the noble and the righteous. The one who was frowning was being taught, in effect, that the company he wanted to impress is not the company heaven keeps.

**The Promise Attached to the Description**

Because the Prophet ﷺ read verse 16 as a description of attainable company, the verse became in Islamic piety a target rather than a doctrine. The memorizer of the Qur'an — often a child, often poor, frequently of no social consequence — is told that his proficiency places him among angels. The social implications of that promise in a stratified world are enormous, and the history of Islamic education reflects them: the madrasah and the *kuttāb* elevated the poor boy who could recite above the rich man who could not, and did so on the authority of this verse and its ḥadīth. The consolation for the struggling reciter, meanwhile, is the double reward: effort counts twice when ability is short. Between the two promises — company for the proficient, double reward for the struggling — the sūrah has built a system of honor that no wealth can buy and no disability can bar.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:17

> **May man perish! How ungrateful is he!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qutila: The Arabic Idiom of Invoked Destruction**

*Qutila al-insān* — literally, "man was killed," or in idiom, "may man perish," "may man be cursed." The passive verb *qutila* in this construction is not a report but an imprecation: an idiomatic formula of condemnation in classical Arabic, comparable to English "confound him!" or "perish the man!" Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both note that the phrase is an Arabic idiom of denunciation (*duʿāʾ ʿalayhi* in form, *taʿjīb* in function — an expression of wonder at the enormity of the thing). The Qur'an uses the same construction elsewhere with devastating effect:

> *"Perish the conjecturers (qutila al-kharrāṣūn), who are engulfed in heedlessness"* (51:10–11).

> *"God slay them (qātalahumu Allāh)! How are they turned away?"* (9:30; cf. 63:4).

> *"Woe that day to those who deny"* (77:15, and repeatedly in al-Mursalāt).

Al-Qurṭubī also records the alternative rendering "may man be cursed" and notes that the object of the condemnation is not every individual human being without exception but the disbelieving human being — the type, not every token.

**Mā Akfarah: The Exclamation of Wonder**

*Mā akfarah* — "how ungrateful he is!" or "how great is his disbelief!" The interrogative *mā* here is exclamatory: what a thing it is! The verb *akfara* is the fourth form of *k-f-r*, which carries two interwoven meanings in Arabic: to disbelieve and to be ungrateful. The root's literal sense is to cover, to conceal, to bury — the farmer who covers a seed with earth is a *kāfir* in the original Arabic sense (*"like rain whose vegetation pleases the cultivators (kuffār)"* — 57:20). To disbelieve is therefore to cover over what is manifest; to be ungrateful is to cover over a favor received. The two are the same act in different domains, and this is why the Qur'an moves between them without translation:

> *"And God brings forth an example: a city secure and at ease, its provision coming to it in abundance from every side; then it was ungrateful for God's favors, so God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear for what they used to do"* (16:112).

> *"Indeed man is unjust to himself, ignorant"* — as in 33:72, where he accepted the trust he could not carry.

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both emphasize that *akfarah* in 80:17 fuses the two senses: man covers the truth and covers the favor, and the covering of the favor is the mechanism of the covering of the truth. This is precisely the transition the sūrah makes in its next sixteen verses — from the imprecation (v. 17) to the enumeration of favors (vv. 18–32). God answers the accusation of ingratitude by itemizing the gifts.

**An Alternative Punctuation, and a Question Instead of a Curse**

The source commentary records al-Qurṭubī's alternative reading of the syntax: *"Mankind is accursed; for what does he disbelieve?"* — taking *mā* as an interrogative of substance rather than an exclamation. The difference is small in Arabic and large in effect. On the first reading the verse is a verdict; on the second it is a question posed to the disbeliever: on what basis? with what evidence? Ibn Kathīr notes that the condemnation falls specifically on those who deny the truth *without any supporting argument* — not because they investigated and found it false, but because they never investigated and assumed it far-fetched. The second reading is therefore the more diagnostic: it identifies the disbeliever's real failing as the absence of inquiry. The Qur'an's constant challenge — *"Bring your proof, if you are truthful"* (2:111; 21:24; 27:64; 28:75) — is aimed at exactly this: a claim held without evidence is not a conviction but a mood.

**Why the Verse Follows the Description of the Heavenly Pages**

The placement of verse 17 after verses 11–16 is rhetorically powerful. The reader has just been shown the Reminder in its celestial setting — honored, exalted, purified, carried by noble and righteous scribes. And then the verse turns to the human recipient with a curse of wonder: how ungrateful is he! The contrast is the argument. Everything on God's side has been done with maximal care: the message is preserved, purified, exalted, delivered by the noblest carriers, offered freely to whoever wills (v. 12). Everything on man's side is refusal. Al-Rāzī formulates the logic: when a benefaction is complete in its giving and rejected in its receiving, the fault lies entirely in the receiver, and the degree of the receiver's ingratitude is measured by the degree of the benefit refused. The greater the gift, the greater the *kufr* in covering it.

**A Report About a Specific Man**

Some exegetes, as the source notes (al-Qurṭubī), report that verse 17 was revealed about a particular individual who had accepted Islam and then apostatized when Sūrah al-Najm (53) was revealed. Others connect it to ʿUtbah ibn Abī Lahab, who is reported to have rejected the Prophet ﷺ after first believing. Whether the verse has a specific occasion or is general — and the principle of the exegetical tradition is that *al-ʿibrah bi-ʿumūm al-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣ al-sabab*, "the lesson is in the generality of the wording, not in the specificity of the occasion" — its subject is the human being in his capacity as a rejecter of favors. *Al-insān* here is the type: the creature who was made, proportioned, eased, buried, and will be raised, and who nevertheless covers the whole of it over.

**The Modern Reader's Own Ingratitude**

The verse is uncomfortable because it is addressed to *al-insān* — man as such — and the reader is a man or a woman of that species. The ingratitude named is not primarily theological heresy but the everyday covering-over of favors: health taken as default, food taken as entitlement, the safety of a bed taken as the natural order, the existence of the senses taken as an accident. Islamic spirituality treats *shukr* (gratitude) as the accurate perception of dependency, and *kufr* as its distortion. Ibn al-Qayyim describes gratitude as having three pillars: recognition in the heart, acknowledgment on the tongue, and use of the favor in obedience to its Giver. Verse 17 declares all three absent in the ungrateful man, and verses 24–32 will proceed to supply the material for the first two — the water, the split earth, the grain, the grapes, the olives, the palms — so that the reader may do what *al-insān* failed to do: look, and recognize, and say thanks.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:18

> **From what thing did He create him?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Begins the Argument**

*Min ayyi shayʾin khalaqah* — "from what thing did He create him?" The sūrah now begins its third panel: the case against human ingratitude, built not out of moral exhortation but out of biography. And it begins with a question, which is the Qur'an's characteristic method of argument. The question is rhetorical in form and forensic in function: it demands that the accused state his own origin. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the verse is a rebuke (*taubīkh*) — the interrogative stands in place of a statement precisely because a statement can be denied and a question cannot. The disbeliever who refuses to acknowledge God's favor can still be made to answer the question of where he came from, and once he has answered it, the remainder of the argument is his own.

**The Qur'an's Standard Opening Move**

This verse belongs to a large family of Qur'anic arguments from origin. The pattern is consistent: the human being is asked to state the material of his own making, and the answer is always the same — something negligible, something that could not produce him, something he did not choose.

> *"Was he not a drop of sperm emitted, then a clinging clot, then He fashioned and proportioned him?"* (75:37–38).

> *"Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust, then from a drop, then fashioned you as a man?"* (18:37).

> *"He created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then He brings you forth as a child"* (40:67).

> *"He is the One Who created you from clay, then decreed a term"* (6:2).

> *"O mankind, if you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then indeed We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed"* (22:5).

> *"Let man consider from what he was created: he was created from a gushing fluid, issuing from between the backbone and the ribs"* (86:5–7).

The cumulative force of these passages is one of the most distinctive features of Qur'anic argumentation. It does not argue from design in the abstract; it argues from the listener's own body, and it argues diachronically — from the nothing he was to the something he is — because that sequence is indisputable even to the person performing it.

**Why Origin Arguments Work**

There is a logic here worth making explicit, since the Qur'an relies on it constantly. Every human being concedes two facts about himself: that he exists now, and that he did not always exist. Between those two facts lies a beginning, and a beginning requires a beginning-maker, since nothing brings itself into being. The Qur'an states the syllogism in its sharpest form in Sūrah al-Ṭūr:

> *"Were they created from nothing, or are they the creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Nay, but they have no certainty"* (52:35–36).

The three options exhaust the field: created from nothing (impossible, since nothing produces nothing), self-created (impossible, since a thing cannot precede itself), or created by another (which is the claim of revelation). Verse 18 of Sūrah ʿAbasa puts the same argument in the second person, addressed to the disbeliever's own history: from what thing did He create you? Answer, and you have already conceded the Creator.

**"From What Thing" — The Deliberate Vagueness**

The question asks *min ayyi shayʾ* — "from what *thing*" — without specifying. The vagueness is functional. It invites the hearer to search his own knowledge, and the search produces the humiliation that the next verse then supplies: the thing is a drop. Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur'an often poses a question in general terms and then answers it with a particular that is beneath the dignity of the questioner, so that the answer itself becomes the argument. Here the sequence is exactly that: "from what thing?" (v. 18), and then, "from a drop He created him" (v. 19). The proud man is asked to name his raw material and is told what it was.

**The Humility of the Question**

There is a pastoral dimension to the verse that should not be missed. The question is asked of *al-insān* — man in general — and every reader answers it privately. To ask oneself honestly where one came from is to perform an act of humility that most people avoid their whole lives. The Islamic tradition therefore made reflection on origin (*al-tafakkur fī al-mabdāʾ*) and on the end (*al-tafakkur fī al-maʿād*) the two poles of spiritual reflection, and the scholars of the heart — al-Ḥārith al-Muḥāsibī, al-Ghazālī in *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, Ibn al-Qayyim — structured their moral psychology around them. A person who remembers that he was a drop cannot be arrogant for long; a person who remembers that he will be a corpse cannot be greedy for long. The two memories together produce what the Qur'an calls *taqwā*, the careful awareness that keeps a traveler safe on a dangerous road.

**The Connection Back to the Opening Scene**

Verse 18 also silently completes the rebuke of verses 1–10. The notables of Quraysh were self-sufficient in their own estimation because of lineage, wealth, and standing. The verse asks the question that dissolves all three: from what thing were you created? Whatever your clan, your father, or your fortune, your origin was the same as the blind man's — and the blind man's origin was the same as the Prophet's. The sūrah has moved from a specific incident to the universal ground on which the incident's lesson rests: the equality of human beings is not a modern moral preference but a fact about their manufacture, and verse 19 will state the fact.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:19

> **From a drop He created him, then proportioned him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nuṭfah: The Smallest Beginning**

*Min nuṭfah khalaqahū* — "from a drop He created him." *Nuṭfah* in Arabic means a small quantity of fluid, a drop, literally "that which is emitted by dripping" — from the root *n-ṭ-f*, to drip, to trickle. The Qur'an uses it consistently for the first stage of human formation, and it uses it with evident rhetorical purpose: the word is diminutive, almost contemptible in its smallness, and it is applied to the origin of the most complex creature on earth.

> *"Does man think that he will be left neglected? Was he not a drop of sperm emitted (nuṭfatan min maniyyin yumnan)?"* (75:36–37).

> *"Indeed We created man from a mixed drop (nuṭfah amshāj)"* (76:2) — the phrase *amshāj* meaning mingled, i.e., the union of male and female contributions, an observation of embryology that Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both note.

> *"And that He has created the two spouses, male and female, from a drop emitted"* (53:45–46).

> *"Then We made the drop a clinging clot, then We made the clot a lump of flesh, then We made from the lump bones, then We clothed the bones with flesh, then We produced it as another creation. So blessed be God, the Best of creators"* (23:14).

The last of these is the Qur'an's fullest embryological sequence, and the Prophet ﷺ described the same stages in the well-known report:

> *"The creation of each of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop (nuṭfah), then it becomes a clinging clot (ʿalaqah) for a like period, then a lump of flesh (muḍghah) for a like period; then God sends the angel and he breathes the soul into him..."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, from Ibn Masʿūd)

**The Sequence as an Argument**

The purpose of naming the drop is not biological description but moral argument. The stages are always narrated in order and always end with a claim on the hearer: from a drop to a clot to a lump to bones to flesh to a speaking, arguing, disputing human being — and the being who now argues against resurrection is asked how he got here. The Qur'an's favorite rhetorical question is put in the mouth of God to the denier:

> *"And he strikes for Us a comparison and forgets his own creation. He says: Who will give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He will give them life Who first brought them into being; and He has knowledge of every creation"* (36:78–79).

The argument is *a fortiori* (the Arabic tradition calls it *qiyās al-awlā*): if God produced you from a drop of fluid, producing you again from dust is less difficult, not more — from the human point of view; and from the divine point of view both are nothing. *"And He it is Who originates creation, then He brings it back again, and that is easier for Him"* (30:27).

**Fa-Qaddarah: Proportioning, Determining, Decreeing**

The verb *qaddara* — from *q-d-r*, the root of measure, capacity, power, and decree — is one of the Qur'an's most loaded words. *Qadar* is the divine measure or decree; *qudrah* is power; *taqdīr* is determination and appointment. In verse 19 the verb has three layers, all recognized by the exegetes:

The first is physical formation: He proportioned him, gave him his limbs, his faculties, his shape, arranged in due measure. *"Who created you, then proportioned you, then balanced you"* (82:7); *"Indeed We created everything with a measure"* (54:49); *"And He created everything and measured it with precise measurement"* (25:2).

The second is developmental determination: He decreed his stages — the forty-day periods of the ḥadīth, the term of gestation, the moment of the soul's breathing.

The third is the assignment of destiny: al-Qurṭubī records the view that *qaddarah* here includes the determination of his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether he would be among the wretched or the blessed — the decree written by the angel, as the ḥadīth of Ibn Masʿūd continues: *"and he is commanded with four words: to write his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or blessed"* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643).

The three layers together make the verse a complete statement of the Islamic doctrine of creation: nothing about the human being is accidental. His matter is chosen, his form is proportioned, his development is timed, and his destiny is inscribed — all before he has done anything at all.

**The Answer to Self-Sufficiency**

Read against verse 5, verse 19 is the definitive refutation of *istaghnāʾ*. The man who deems himself beyond need is a man who was a drop of fluid and who is, at every moment of his existence, an effect of a cause he did not cause. The feeling of independence is not merely mistaken; it is amnesiac. The Qur'an's remedy for it is not argument but recollection, which is why the sūrah frames its central panel as a series of reminders rather than proofs: from what thing did He create him (18); from a drop (19); then He eased his way (20); then He killed him and buried him (21); then, when He wills, He raises him (22). Five verses, five facts, none of which anyone can deny, and every one of which makes self-sufficiency absurd.

**Modern Resonance**

Contemporary science has made the Qur'an's argument more vivid rather than less. A human being begins as a single cell roughly a tenth of a millimeter across, containing about three billion base pairs of instructions, and becomes — without any direction from the person himself — a body of some thirty trillion cells, a brain of eighty-six billion neurons, an immune system that learns, a heart that beats two and a half billion times. Nobody chose any of it. The modern reader who knows more about embryology than any seventh-century audience can only feel the force of *min nuṭfah khalaqahū fa-qaddarah* more sharply: from a drop, He created him, and He measured him out. The proper response, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, is *shukr* — gratitude — and its absence is what verse 17 cursed.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:20

> **then He made the way easy for him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Sabīl: Which Way?**

*Thumma al-sabīla yassarah* — "then the way He made easy for him." *Al-sabīl* is the road, the path, the means of passage; the Qur'an uses it constantly, most famously in *sabīl Allāh*, "the way of God." The exegetes identify three referents for "the way" in this verse, and al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr record all three.

The first is the physical way out of the womb: God eased for the infant its passage from the mother's body, opened for it the road, and turned it in the birth canal toward delivery — an act of facilitation without which the child would perish. Al-Qurṭubī cites this as the opinion of a group of the Salaf, and it fits the immediately preceding verses on creation with obvious appropriateness.

The second is the way of guidance: God made the religious path available and intelligible to every human being, and gave him the faculties to walk it. This reading rests on the Qur'an's own explicit statement:

> *"Truly We guided him upon the way, be he grateful or ungrateful"* (76:3).

> *"And We showed him the two highways"* (90:10).

> *"And as for those who strive for Us, We surely guide them upon Our ways"* (29:69).

The third is the way of life in general: God made easy for each creature the function for which it was created, its livelihood, its natural behavior, its means of survival. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both cite the ḥadīth that supports this reading directly:

> *"Work, for everyone will be made easy for that for which he was created. As for one who is among the people of happiness, he will be made easy for the work of the people of happiness; and as for one who is among the people of misery, he will be made easy for the work of the people of misery."* (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2649, from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib)

Then the Prophet ﷺ recited: *"So as for him who gives and is reverent, and confirms the best, We shall surely ease him toward ease"* (92:5–7).

**Taysīr: The Root of Facilitation**

*Yassara* is from *y-s-r*, the root of ease, facility, and abundance: *yusr* is ease, *yassir* is the imperative "make easy," and the Qur'an states the principle as a law of revelation itself:

> *"And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance; so is there any who will remember?"* (54:17, 22, 32, 40).

> *"God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you"* (2:185).

> *"And He has placed no hardship upon you in the religion"* (22:78).

The Prophet ﷺ made facilitation a personal policy and a directive to his governors: *"Give glad tidings and do not drive people away; make things easy and do not make them difficult"* (al-Bukhārī 69; Muslim 1734, from Anas). And: *"Religion is easy, and no one makes religion a matter of strictness with himself but that it overcomes him"* (al-Bukhārī 39, from Abū Hurayrah). Verse 20 places this same divine characteristic at the very origin of human life: before a person has done anything, God has already made his way easy.

**The Order of the Verse: Facilitation Before Command**

There is a theological sequence in verses 19–20 that Islamic theology took seriously. God creates, proportions, and then facilitates — and only afterward does the verse speak of what the human being fails to do (v. 23: *"he has not accomplished what He commanded him"*). The order is not accidental: the command comes after the enabling. This is the Qur'anic foundation of the principle that obligation presupposes capacity (*al-taklīf mashrūṭ bi'l-istīṭāʿah*), stated in the law as *"Fear God as much as you are able"* (64:16) and *"God does not charge a soul beyond its capacity"* (2:286). A person is never required to do what he has not been equipped to do, and verse 20 records the equipping. The jurists derived from this the whole apparatus of exemptions — for the sick, the traveler, the blind, the coerced, the child, the insane — and Ibn Umm Maktūm himself became the occasion of one of them (4:95).

**Ease and the Two Highways**

The ḥadīth of ʿAlī quoted above is often misunderstood as fatalism, and Ibn Taymiyyah addresses precisely that misunderstanding: the Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ whether, if everything is facilitated according to the decree, they should then abandon action. He answered, *"Work, for everyone will be made easy for that for which he was created."* The point is not that action is pointless but that action is the sign: the way is made easy in the direction a person actually walks. Facilitation is experienced from the inside as capacity and attraction — the believer finds prayer easy and sin hard, and the reverse is equally true for the one who turns away. This is why the Qur'an describes the two destinies in the language of easing: *"We shall surely ease him toward ease"* and *"We shall surely ease him toward hardship"* (92:7, 10). Even the road to ruin is, in a sense, easy — which is what makes it dangerous.

**A Daily Reading of the Verse**

The practical application is available to anyone. Each morning a person wakes with a body that repaired itself overnight, a mind that can plan, a road to work that he has travelled a thousand times, a language he did not invent, a set of habits that carry him through the day without decision. Almost nothing in that list was achieved by effort; almost all of it was "made easy." Verse 20 asks the reader to notice the facilitation, because the notice is the beginning of gratitude — and gratitude is the sūrah's demand. The ungrateful man of verse 17 is ungrateful precisely because he experiences the eased path as his own accomplishment.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:21

> **Then He caused him to die and buried him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Amātahu: Death as a Divine Act, Not an Accident**

*Thumma amātahu* — "then He caused him to die." The verb *amāta* is the fourth form of *m-w-t*, causative: "He made him die." The Qur'an insists on this grammatical fact with unrelenting consistency — death is not something that happens to a person from outside the divine order; it is something God does to him, at a term He appointed.

> *"He Who created death and life, that He may test you which of you is best in deed"* (67:2).

> *"Every soul shall taste death"* (3:185; 21:35; 29:57).

> *"And God created you, then He causes you to die; and among you is he who is returned to the most debilitated age, so that he knows nothing after knowledge"* (16:70).

> *"Say: The death from which you flee — indeed it will meet you"* (62:8).

> *"And no soul dies except by God's permission, at a term written"* (3:145).

The theological consequence is that death has an author and a schedule, and both are in the same hands that created and proportioned. Islamic spirituality treats the remembrance of death (*dhikr al-mawt*) as the single most effective discipline of the heart: the Prophet ﷺ said, *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures"* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī 1824, from Abū Hurayrah; graded ḥasan), and ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz would gather scholars to speak of death and the Hereafter as though the funeral were in front of them. The logic is not morbidity but arithmetic: a person who remembers that verse 21 applies to him personally cannot spend his attention the way the man of verse 5 spends his.

**Fa-Aqbarah: The Dignity of the Grave**

*Fa-aqbarah* — "and He gave him a grave" or "and He buried him." The verb is from *q-b-r*, the root of the grave (*qabr*, plural *qubūr*); the fourth form *aqbara* means "to cause to be buried," and the exegetes emphasize that the form indicates God *granted* him burial rather than merely permitted it. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note the point with care: the verse mentions burial as a favor. A human being is not left as carrion; he is honored with a grave. Al-Qurṭubī draws out the legal consequence: burial of the dead (*dafn al-mayyit*) is a communal obligation (*farḍ kifāyah*) upon the Muslims, and the verse is one of its textual foundations, since God's act of giving the human being a grave establishes the norm for his treatment after death.

The Islamic rites of burial follow from this honor and are marked by simplicity and equality: the body is washed, shrouded in plain cloth, prayed over, and buried facing the qiblah, without a coffin where the soil permits, without embalming, without display. Every Muslim is buried in the same three pieces of white cloth, the rich and the poor alike, and the grave is not to be built up or plastered: the Prophet ﷺ forbade plastering graves, sitting on them, and building upon them (Muslim 970, from Jābir). The material equality of the Muslim cemetery is the visible form of verse 21 and the final refutation of *istaghnāʾ*: whatever a man owned, he goes into the ground with a shroud.

**The Earth as a Receiver, and Its One Exception**

The Qur'an speaks of the earth as receiving the dead and of burial as a return:

> *"From it We created you, and into it We shall return you, and from it We shall bring you forth another time"* (20:55).

> *"Did We not make the earth a gathering place, for the living and the dead?"* (77:25–26).

The tradition records one exception to the earth's consumption of bodies, and it is directly relevant to the honor of the verse:

> *"Verily God has forbidden the earth to consume the bodies of the prophets."* (Abū Dāwūd 1047; al-Nasāʾī 1374; Ibn Mājah 1085, from Aws ibn Aws, in the ḥadīth of sending blessings upon the Prophet on Friday)

The prophets are given a further honor in the very act that verse 21 describes for all humanity, and the Muslim sends salutations upon the Prophet ﷺ believing, on the strength of this report, that they reach him.

**The Sequence in the Verse and the Sequence in Life**

The verse joins death and burial in a single breath with no pause between them — *amātahu fa-aqbarah* — and the Arabic *fāʾ* indicates immediate sequence. There is a quiet instruction in the joining: the dead are buried promptly. Islamic law requires expedited burial, and the Prophet ﷺ said of the funeral procession, *"Hasten with the bier"* (al-Bukhārī 1315; Muslim 944, from Abū Hurayrah, in the wording: "Hasten with the funeral; if it is righteous, you are forwarding it to good, and if it is otherwise, it is an evil of which you are ridding yourselves"). The delay of burial for social convenience — waiting for relatives to fly in, waiting for a hall, waiting for publicity — runs against the immediate *fa-* of verse 21, and the jurists discuss it in those terms.

**Why Death Belongs in a List of Favors**

The most striking feature of verse 21 is its placement. Verses 18–32 are an enumeration of God's dealings with man intended to shame him out of ingratitude, and death is included in the list. The classical exegetes explain that death is a favor in at least three respects: it is the end of suffering for the afflicted; it is the gateway without which no one could reach the reward of the Hereafter; and its certainty is what gives urgency to moral life. Al-Rāzī develops the point at length — a world without death would be a world in which injustice is never rectified, the tyrant never falls, and the oppressed never rest. Death is therefore not merely compatible with mercy; in a moral universe it is required by it. The believer who recites verse 21 is being asked to see his own mortality as part of the same generous design as his own creation — which is the hardest and most liberating thing the sūrah asks.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:22

> **Then, when He wills, He resurrected him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ansharahu: The Verb of Rising**

*Thumma idhā shāʾa ansharah* — "then, when He wills, He resurrected him." The verb *anshara* is from *n-sh-r*, the fourth form of *nashara* — to spread out, to scatter, to publish, to cause to live. The root gives *nashr* (the raising of the dead, and also the spreading of a garment, and also the printing of a book) and is used in the Qur'an for resurrection several times:

> *"And God has caused you to grow from the earth as a growth (anbatakum mina al-arḍi nabātā), then He will return you into it and bring you forth (wa yukhrijukum ikhrājā)"* (71:17–18).

> *"Then look at the traces of the mercy of God — how He revives the earth after its death. Indeed He is the Reviver of the dead"* (30:50).

> *"And it is He Who sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy, and We send down from the sky pure water, that We may revive thereby a dead land"* (25:48–49).

The perfect tense of *ansharah* — "He resurrected him," though the event is future — is a standard Qur'anic device: the certainty of the divine act is expressed in the past tense, as though it were already accomplished. Al-Zamakhsharī notes this repeatedly; the future in God's speech is a past tense in waiting.

**"When He Wills": The Indeterminacy of the Hour**

The clause *idhā shāʾa* — "when He wills" — is theologically dense. It affirms resurrection as certain while withholding its time as unknown, and both halves are essential to Islamic faith. The certainty answers the disbeliever's denial; the unknowability answers his demand for a date:

> *"They will say: When is this promise, if you are truthful? They await only one cry, which will seize them while they dispute"* (36:48–49).

> *"They will ask thee about the Hour: when will it arrive? Say: The knowledge of it is with my Lord alone; none will manifest it at its time but He"* (7:187).

> *"The knowledge thereof rests only with thy Lord"* (79:44) — the closing verses of the preceding sūrah.

The withholding of the date is itself a mercy and a discipline: because no one knows when, everyone must be ready. The Prophet ﷺ said, when asked about the Hour by a questioner, *"What have you prepared for it?"* (al-Bukhārī 3688; Muslim 2639, from Anas) — redirecting the question from chronology to conduct, which is the only part of the Hour that a human being can affect.

**The Qur'an's Central Argument for Resurrection**

Resurrection is the doctrine the Qur'an argues for most persistently, and its arguments are of four kinds, all of them present in this sūrah's neighborhood.

The first is the argument from first creation:

> *"And he strikes for Us a comparison and forgets his own creation. He says: Who will give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He will give them life Who first brought them into being, and He has knowledge of every creation"* (36:78–79).

The second is the argument from the lesser to the greater:

> *"Is the creation of you more grievous, or the heaven which He built?"* (79:27).

> *"Is not He Who originates creation, then brings it back again, and Who gives you provision from the heaven and the earth? Is there a god with God?"* (27:60–62).

The third is the argument from the revival of dead land, which the Qur'an uses more than any other and which verses 24–32 of this very sūrah will deploy at length:

> *"And a sign for them is the dead earth: We revive it and bring forth from it grain, and of it they eat"* (36:33).

> *"And you see the earth barren, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every noble pair"* (22:5).

The fourth is the argument from moral necessity:

> *"Did you think that We created you in vain, and that to Us you would not be returned?"* (23:115).

> *"How can you disbelieve in God, seeing that you were dead and He gave you life; then He causes you to die; then He gives you life; then unto Him shall you be returned?"* (2:28).

Verse 22 of Sūrah ʿAbasa states the fourth argument in its shortest form: creation, death, and resurrection are a single continuous divine action, and the middle term cannot be separated from the third.

**The Grammar of the Whole Sequence**

Verses 18–22 form one unbroken sentence in five movements: from what thing did He create him (18); from a drop, then He proportioned him (19); then He eased his way (20); then He killed him and buried him (21); then, when He wills, He resurrected him (22). The sequence is the entire biography of the human being — origin, formation, facilitation, death, burial, rising — in five verses, with no gap and no alternative. Al-Rāzī remarks that the Qur'an compresses into this passage what whole books of theology attempt: the doctrine of creation, the doctrine of providence, the doctrine of death, and the doctrine of the Hereafter, each stated as an accomplished fact rather than argued as a proposition. Then verse 23 draws the moral conclusion, and it is a rebuke.

**The Effect Upon the Reader**

The practical effect of verse 22 is to reorient a life. If resurrection is certain, then every present calculation that ignores it is arithmetically wrong, and the Qur'an says so in terms of investment: the Hereafter is the better and more lasting (87:17), the life of this world is play and diversion (6:32), and the intelligent person is *"the one who calls himself to account and works for what comes after death"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws; graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī). The verse does not ask the reader to be frightened. It asks him to be accurate: there is a term, there is a return, and the One who appointed the term will bring it when He wills.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:23

> **Nay! But he has not accomplished what He commanded him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lammā: The Particle of Unfinished Business**

*Kallā lammā yaqḍi mā amarah* — "Nay! But he has not accomplished what He commanded him." The particle *lammā* is crucial to the verse's meaning and is often flattened in translation. It negates a past action with the expectation that it may yet occur — "he has not yet done it," as distinct from *lam* ("he did not do it"), which negates without implication. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both stress this: *lammā* carries the sense of *ilā al-ān* — "until now." The human being has not yet fulfilled what God commanded him, and the "not yet" leaves the door open for the remainder of his life.

*Kallā* again performs its function of repudiation, as in verse 11. The source commentary records al-Qurṭubī's observation that *kallā* can be read either as a rebuke ("no! it is not as you suppose") or in an affirmative, emphatic sense ("truly, indeed"), in which case the verse would read "Truly, he has not accomplished what He commanded him" and would lose the tone of reproach. The rebuking reading is the majority and is supported by the surrounding context: the sūrah has just enumerated five divine acts of grace (vv. 18–22), and *kallā* rejects the assumption that man has discharged his side of the account.

**Yaqḍi: To Fulfill, to Complete, to Discharge**

*Yaqḍi* is from *q-ḍ-y*, the root of settling, deciding, and completing a matter — the same root as *qaḍāʾ*, decree, and *qāḍī*, judge. To *qaḍā* a duty is to discharge it fully, as one settles a debt. The Qur'an uses it of completing the rites (*"And when you have performed the rites (fa-idhā qaḍaytum manāsikakum)"* — 2:200), of fulfilling a term (*"And when Moses fulfilled the term"* — 28:29), and of the prayer's completion (*"And when the prayer is finished (fa-idhā quḍiyati al-ṣalāh)"* — 62:10). The image is legal and financial: there is an obligation, and it has not been paid.

**Who Has Not Accomplished It? Three Readings**

Al-Qurṭubī, following the source, gives two readings of the referent. On the first, the verse addresses the disbeliever who, informed of the Resurrection, responds with the arrogance of 41:50:

> *"But if We let him taste mercy from Us after adversity has touched him, he will surely say: This is for me, and I do not think the Hour will come; and if I am returned to my Lord, surely with Him I shall have that which is most beautiful"* (41:50).

On the second reading, the rebuke falls on those who believe that they *have* accomplished what was commanded — who consider themselves complete, satisfied, finished with the demands of religion — and who, because they disbelieve in God and His Messenger, have in fact accomplished nothing. This second reading is the more disturbing and the more useful, because it applies to the religious person as much as to the irreligious.

The classical exegetes then draw the general conclusion, which Ibn Kathīr states plainly: no human being has ever fully discharged what God commanded him. The statement is not a counsel of despair but a description of the human condition, and it is the ground of the whole Islamic economy of mercy — repentance, supererogatory deeds, intercession, and the forgiveness that covers shortfall.

**The Ḥadīth That Explains the Verse**

The Prophet ﷺ said, in one of the most important reports for understanding Islamic soteriology:

> *"Be moderate and strive for what is right, and know that no one of you will be saved by his deeds." They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: "Not even I, unless God covers me with mercy from Himself and grace."* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah)

This report is the commentary that the tradition reads over verse 23. If the best of creation cannot discharge the obligation by deeds alone, then the failure stated in *lammā yaqḍi mā amarah* is universal — and the remedy is not more self-sufficiency but the opposite: the acknowledgment of shortfall, which is *faqr* (spiritual poverty), and the request for mercy, which is *duʿāʾ*. The man of verse 5, who deemed himself beyond need, is defined by his refusal to make that acknowledgment; the man of verses 8–9, who came striving while fearful, is defined by making it.

**Why the Verse Follows the Enumeration of Favors**

Verses 18–22 listed what God did; verse 23 states what man did not do. The juxtaposition is the structure of an account. On one side: creation from a drop, proportioning, facilitation, death, burial, and resurrection appointed. On the other side: nothing accomplished of what was commanded. Al-Rāzī formulates the argument: when a benefactor has done everything and the beneficiary has done nothing, the beneficiary's claim to independence is not merely false but absurd — and this is precisely the claim that verse 17 cursed and verse 5 diagnosed. Verse 23 is the verdict on the ledger.

**The Open Door in "Not Yet"**

The most merciful feature of the verse is its grammar. *Lammā* does not close the account; it leaves the balance outstanding while the debtor still lives. The Qur'an's consistent position is that repentance is available until the death-rattle: *"Repentance is not for those who go on doing evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says: Now I repent"* (4:18) — meaning that up to that moment, the door is open. Islamic spirituality treats every day as an installment on an unpayable debt, and the believer's response is not despair but the daily return: *"O people, repent to God and seek His forgiveness; indeed I repent to Him a hundred times a day"* (Muslim 2702, from al-Agharr al-Muzanī). The verse's rebuke is therefore addressed to the one who has stopped trying, not to the one who is failing while trying — and the difference between those two is the difference between the man of verse 5 and the man of verse 8.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:24

> **So let man consider his food,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Look Down**

*Fa-lyanẓur al-insān ilā ṭaʿāmih* — "so let man consider his food." The verse is a command (*amr*) in the jussive form: *falyanẓur* — "let him look." The verb *naẓara* with the preposition *ilā* means to look at, to consider, to examine deliberately — not the passive reception of images but the directed act of investigation. The Qur'an uses it constantly for the reflective observation that leads to faith:

> *"Do they not look (a-fa-lā yanẓurūna) at the camels, how they are created, and at the sky, how it is raised?"* (88:17–18).

> *"Say: Travel in the land and look (fa-nẓurū) how He originated creation"* (29:20).

> *"Do they not reflect (a-wa lam yanẓurū) upon the dominion of the heavens and the earth?"* (7:185).

The object of the looking here is astonishingly small. Not the heavens, not the mountains, not the alternation of night and day — but *ṭaʿāmih*, his food. Al-Qurṭubī explains that the verse directs the human being to the sign nearest to him and most repeated in his life: three meals a day, every day, for a lifetime, each one a demonstration of the same truth. The argument from food is the argument that cannot be avoided by travel, by education, or by abstraction, because it occurs on the reader's own plate.

**Two Directions of the Looking**

The exegetes identify two objects of consideration within the command. The first is the food's origin — the chain of causation running from the plate back through the harvest, the rain, the cloud, the sky, to the One who poured the water (v. 25). This is the direction that verses 25–32 will follow explicitly, and it is the direction that produces gratitude.

The second is the food's end — what becomes of it. Al-Qurṭubī records the view that the verse alludes to the food's consumption and elimination, and thus to what becomes of the whole world: the finest dish passes through the body and exits as waste. This second direction produces humility, and the Islamic ethical tradition used it deliberately. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the one who reflects on his food's end will not be proud of his beginning. Al-Ghazālī develops the same reflection in the *Iḥyāʾ*, arguing that the anatomy of digestion is a permanent antidote to vanity: the same mouth that tastes is the mouth that ends the process, and the body is a channel rather than a monument.

Both directions belong to a single discipline — *tafakkur*, reflective consideration — which the Qur'an repeatedly commands and which it treats as an act of worship in itself:

> *"Indeed in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for those of understanding, who remember God standing and sitting and on their sides, and reflect (yatafakkarūna) upon the creation of the heavens and the earth: 'Our Lord, Thou didst not create this in vain'"* (3:190–191).

**Why Food and Not Something Grand**

There is a deliberate pedagogical logic in choosing food. The grand signs — the heavens, the mountains, the seas — are seen occasionally and admired aesthetically; admiration can stop short of gratitude. Food is seen daily and consumed personally; it cannot be admired at a distance, because it must be taken in. The relationship it creates is not spectatorship but dependence, and dependence is the precise opposite of *istaghnāʾ*. Verse 24 is therefore the sūrah's most efficient attack on the self-sufficiency of verse 5: you are not merely a creature who was once a drop; you are a creature who must eat today, and tomorrow, and who has no power to produce his own next meal. The Qur'an states the argument in question form elsewhere:

> *"Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who grow it, or are We the Grower?"* (56:63–64).

> *"Or who is it that provides you if He withholds His provision?"* (67:21).

**The Social Dimension of the Verse**

Food in the Qur'an is never a purely private matter. The same verse that commands consideration of one's own food implies consideration of those who have none, and Islamic law builds obligations directly on the fact of eating: the fast of Ramadan, whose hunger is designed to produce awareness of the hungry; *zakāh*, whose payment is measured in foodstuffs; *zakāt al-fiṭr*, given before the ʿĪd prayer so that no one goes without on the day of celebration (*"Enrich them from asking on this day,"* as the report has it — al-Dāraquṭnī and al-Bayhaqī from Ibn ʿUmar); and the general rule of the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"He is not a believer whose stomach is filled while his neighbor goes hungry."* (al-Bukhārī, *al-Adab al-Mufrad* 112; al-Ḥākim, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

To "consider one's food" is thus also to consider its distribution. The verse that begins the sūrah's catalogue of blessings is a command to notice both the blessing and the community that shares it.

**Modern Application: The Unexamined Meal**

The contemporary reader eats further from the source of his food than any human being in history. A meal arrives packaged, priced, and stripped of its biography: no field, no rain, no hand, no season. The verse's command — *look at your food* — is in this context a countercultural instruction, and it has produced a modern movement of people doing exactly that: tracing supply chains, buying locally, learning what is in season, refusing waste. Whether or not such practices are undertaken religiously, they are the practical exegesis of 80:24. And the Islamic form of the same practice is already prescribed in the Sunnah: naming God before eating, thanking Him after, eating with the right hand, not criticizing food, not wasting it, and remembering that the provision came from a chain no human being controls. *"Say: Have you considered — if God made its water to sink away, who then would bring you flowing water?"* (67:30).

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:25

> **that We pour down water in abundance,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grammar of the Verse: What Man Is Being Told to See**

*Annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabbā* — "that We pour down water in abundance." The particle *anna* introduces the content of the consideration commanded in verse 24: let man look at his food — namely, *that We poured the water*. The construction makes the rain the first item in the chain that ends on his plate, and it makes God the grammatical subject of the pouring. The verse does not say "rain falls"; it says "*We* pour." Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both emphasize the first-person plural of majesty as an assertion of direct agency: the water cycle is not a natural mechanism that operates independently of God but an act He performs continuously.

**Ṣabbā: The Cognate Accusative and Its Force**

*Ṣababnā ... ṣabbā* — "We poured ... a pouring." The cognate accusative (*al-mafʿūl al-muṭlaq*) is an Arabic construction that intensifies the verb by repeating it as a noun: not merely "we poured" but "we poured a pouring," i.e., abundantly, thoroughly, in the manner proper to pouring. *Ṣabb* is the pouring of a liquid in quantity, as from a vessel — stronger than *anzala* (to send down) and more physical. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the punishment poured upon the enemies of the prophets — *"So thy Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment"* (89:13) — and for the scalding water of the Fire: *"Then pour over his head the punishment of scalding water"* (44:48). The word carries the sense of a controlled, directed deluge from above.

The same image appears in the parallel passage of Sūrah al-Nabaʾ, the chapter immediately preceding al-Nāziʿāt:

> *"And We sent down from the rain-clouds water pouring abundantly (māʾan thajjājā), that We may bring forth thereby grain and vegetation, and gardens of dense growth"* (78:14–16).

The resemblance between 78:14–16 and 80:24–32 is close enough that the classical exegetes read the two passages as a single theme developed twice within a few pages of the muṣḥaf.

**Water as the Qur'an's Master Sign**

Water is the most frequently cited sign in the Qur'an after the alternation of night and day, and it functions in three registers at once.

As the origin of life: *"And We made from water every living thing"* (21:30); *"And God created every creature from water; of them is one that moves upon its belly, and one that walks on two legs, and one that walks on four"* (24:45); *"And it is He Who created from water a human being, then made him lineage and marriage"* (25:54).

As the proof of resurrection: *"And you see the earth barren, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every noble pair. That is because God is the Truth, and because He revives the dead, and because He has power over everything"* (22:5–6).

As the measure of mercy and of punishment: the same water that revives a dead land drowns a disobedient people, and the Qur'an notes both without reconciling them into a single mood — *"And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers"* (17:82) is spoken of water elsewhere in almost identical terms: *"And We send down from the sky water blessed, and We cause to grow thereby gardens and the grain of harvest"* (50:9).

**The Full Water Cycle in Qur'anic Language**

The Qur'an describes the hydrological cycle with a precision that its earliest audiences recognized as an account of what they could see:

> *"God is He Who sends the winds, and they stir the cloud, and He spreads it in the sky as He wills, and He makes it fragments, and you see the rain come forth from its midst"* (30:48).

> *"Have you considered the water that you drink? Is it you who sent it down from the cloud, or are We the Sender?"* (56:68–69).

> *"Seest thou not that God causes the clouds to move, then brings them together, then piles them up, and thou seest the rain issue from their midst? And He sends down from the sky mountains of hail"* (24:43).

> *"And We send down from the sky pure water"* (25:48).

> *"Didst thou not see that God causes water to descend from the sky, and We produce thereby fruits of varying colors?"* (35:27).

> *"And indeed We have set out in the Qur'an every kind of example"* (17:41) — including this one, repeatedly, because the audience cannot escape it.

**The Human Position in the Chain**

Verse 25 places the human being at the receiving end of a process in which he has no part. He did not evaporate the sea, did not lift the vapor, did not condense the cloud, did not release the rain. Every one of those steps is performed above his head and beyond his reach, and the entire agricultural economy of the species depends on their occurrence. The Qur'an states the dependency in a verse that reads as a direct commentary on 80:25:

> *"Say: Have you considered — if your water were to sink away in the morning, who then would bring you flowing water?"* (67:30).

And in the verse about the withheld rain:

> *"And if We willed, We could send down a sign from the sky before which their necks would remain bent"* (26:4) — but He sends water instead, and the water is the sign.

**Contemporary Resonance**

The modern reader lives in the first era in which the water supply is genuinely at risk through human action — depleted aquifers, shifting monsoons, glacial loss, drought-driven migration. Verse 25 does not need to be updated for this; it needs to be read. "*We* pour the water in pouring" is a statement of who owns the tap, and the ecological crises of the twenty-first century are, in Qur'anic terms, the visible consequence of a civilization that forgot verse 25 and began to regard the water as its own production. The sūrah's command to look at food (v. 24) leads immediately to water because water is the first link in the chain that no human being can forge: everything on the plate is downstream of a gift.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:26

> **then We split the earth in fissures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shaqqaqnā: The Splitting of the Ground**

*Thumma shaqaqnā al-arḍa shaqqā* — "then We split the earth in fissures" — literally, "then We split the earth a splitting." The verb *shaqqa* means to cleave, to split open, to rend; the cognate accusative *shaqqā* intensifies it as in the preceding verse. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the splitting is the opening of the soil so that vegetation may emerge through it, and Ibn Kathīr adds that it includes the fissures made by rain, by roots, by plough, and by the shoot itself pushing upward. The image is agricultural and precise: the seed swells, the shoot exerts pressure, the crust of soil cracks, and the green emerges through the crack.

**The Same Root in a Divine Name**

The Qur'an uses the identical root for one of God's defining acts:

> *"Indeed God is the Cleaver of the grain and the seed (fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa al-nawā). He brings forth the living from the dead and brings forth the dead from the living. That is God — so how are you turned away?"* (6:95).

> *"He is the One Who cleaves the morning (fāliqu al-iṣbāḥ) and made the night for rest and the sun and moon for reckoning"* (6:96).

The two acts — splitting the seed and splitting the dawn — are named with the same verb, and both are described as forms of emergence from darkness into light. The exegetes have always noted the parallel: the shoot breaking the soil and the light breaking the night are the same miracle at two scales. And verse 6:95 immediately joins the agricultural splitting to the resurrection: *"He brings forth the living from the dead"* — which is exactly the argument of 80:24–32 in service of 80:22.

**The Revival of Dead Land as the Proof of the Hour**

The Qur'an returns to this proof more often than to any other, and always in the same terms:

> *"And a sign for them is the dead earth: We revive it and bring forth from it grain, and of it they eat"* (36:33).

> *"And you see the earth barren, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every noble pair. That is because God is the Truth, and because He revives the dead, and because He has power over everything, and because the Hour is coming — there is no doubt in it — and because God will resurrect those in the graves"* (22:5–7).

> *"And of His signs is that thou seest the earth lowly, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and grows. Indeed He Who revives it is the Reviver of the dead"* (41:39).

> *"And God sends down water from the sky and revives the earth after its death. Indeed in that is a sign for a people who listen"* (16:65).

> *"And He sends down water from the sky and revives thereby the earth after its death"* (30:24; cf. 29:63; 39:21; 45:5).

The logic is stated explicitly in 41:39: the One who revives the dead earth is the Reviver of the dead. Resurrection is not argued from metaphysics but from agriculture, and the argument is available to a farmer as readily as to a philosopher — which is the reason the Qur'an uses it so heavily in the Makkan sūrahs addressed to an audience that lived by rain.

**The Sequence in the Verse: Water, Then Splitting, Then Growth**

Verses 25–27 form a strict causal chain: We poured the water (25), then We split the earth (26), then We caused grain to grow (27). The order is agronomic and irreversible. No water, no splitting; no splitting, no growth. The Qur'an's habit of presenting these steps in order — and of making God the subject of each verb — has a theological purpose: it denies the reader any point in the chain at which he can insert his own agency. He may plant, he may irrigate, he may harvest, but at no stage does he cause. The same point is made with a rhetorical question in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah:

> *"Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who grow it, or are We the Grower? If We willed, We could make it broken stubble, and you would remain wondering"* (56:63–65).

**A Sign Available to Everyone**

There is a democratizing quality to verse 26 that the classical commentators appreciated. The heavenly bodies require clear skies and some astronomy; the mountains require travel; the alternation of night and day requires only wakefulness. But the splitting of the earth requires nothing — it happens in every garden, every crack in a pavement, every pot on a windowsill, in every spring, anywhere on earth where there is soil and water. Ibn Kathīr notes that this is why the Qur'an so often directs reflection to plants: the sign is universal and unmissable. A person who has watched a seed germinate in a jar of cotton wool has seen the argument for the Resurrection in its complete form, and the Qur'an treats him as having seen it whether or not he drew the conclusion.

**Modern Application: The Cracked Pavement**

The contemporary version of verse 26 is visible in every city: the tree root lifting the sidewalk, the weed emerging from a crack in concrete, the plant growing from a wall with no soil at all. The phenomenon is the same one the verse describes, and it carries the same meaning — life exerts pressure, and hard surfaces yield to it. In the Qur'anic reading this is not a botanical curiosity but a demonstration of the priority of life over structure, and of the One who sends it over the one who paved against it. The reader who notices a plant growing through asphalt has, in the language of the sūrah, "looked at his food" and been shown the splitting of the earth; the only remaining question is whether he will say what the verse implies, which is that Someone did this.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:27

> **and cause grains to grow therein,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥabb: The Grain That Feeds the Species**

*Fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabbā* — "and We caused grain to grow therein." *Ḥabb* is the collective noun for grain and seed — wheat, barley, and the cereals generally — and by extension the seeds and kernels of plants. The verb *anbatnā* is from *n-b-t*, "to cause to grow," the fourth form indicating causation: We caused it to grow, We made it sprout. The Qur'an uses the same formula repeatedly:

> *"And We send down from the sky water blessed, and We cause to grow thereby gardens and the grain of the harvest (ḥabba al-ḥaṣīd)"* (50:9).

> *"And a sign for them is the dead earth: We revive it and bring forth from it grain (ḥabb), and of it they eat"* (36:33).

> *"And He caused grain and the palm to grow"* — as in the description of Paradise's provisions and of the earth's.

> *"He causes to grow thereby the crops, the olives, the palms, the grapes, and of all the fruits"* (16:11).

> *"It is He Who causes gardens to grow, trellised and untrellised, and the palm, and crops of varying flavor, and the olive and the pomegranate, resembling and not resembling"* (6:141).

**Why Grain Comes First**

The order of the list in verses 27–31 is significant, and the exegetes note that the Qur'an consistently places grain before fruit. Grain is the staple — the food that sustains a population through the year, that can be stored, that provides the bulk of human calories. Fruit is the delight that comes after. The Qur'an's ordering mirrors the actual hierarchy of human need: first the sustenance, then the sweetness. In 50:9–11 the same sequence appears with the same conclusion — *"the grain of the harvest, and the palm with its spathes, and gardens dense, and fruits and pasture — a provision for the servants"* — and in 78:15–16 the parallel passage names grain and vegetation and gardens of dense growth in the same order.

There is also a theological reason for the primacy of grain. Grain is the food that must be *worked*: sown, waited for, harvested, threshed, winnowed, ground, baked. Nothing else in the list demands so much human labor in cooperation with the divine act. The Qur'an makes the cooperation explicit in 56:63–64 — *"Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who grow it, or are We the Grower?"* — and the answer defines the whole of agriculture: the human being sows; God grows. Ibn Kathīr draws the conclusion that gratitude for bread is gratitude for a joint work in which the human share is small and the divine share is total.

**The Grain as the Qur'an's Image of Multiplication**

The Qur'an chooses the grain as its central image of spiritual economics precisely because of what a grain does when it is buried:

> *"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is as the likeness of a grain that grows seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whom He wills, and God is All-Embracing, All-Knowing"* (2:261).

The buried seed is also the Qur'an's image of resurrection in the Christian and Islamic traditions alike, and the Prophet ﷺ used the image of the plant for the believer:

> *"The likeness of the believer is that of a fresh tender plant: from whatever direction the wind comes, it bends it, but when the wind becomes quiet it becomes straight. And the likeness of the wicked is that of a cedar tree, firm and straight, until God breaks it when He wills."* (al-Bukhārī 5644; Muslim 2810, from Abū Hurayrah)

Verse 27 therefore carries more than botany. The grain is the seed of charity, the model of resurrection, the image of the believer's flexibility under trial, and the daily bread of every human being — all in one word.

**The Legal and Social Centrality of Grain**

Because grain is the staple, Islamic law measures its essential obligations in grain. *Zakāt al-fiṭr* is given as a *ṣāʿ* (approximately 2.5–3 kilograms) of the local staple food — dates, barley, wheat, raisins — before the ʿĪd prayer (al-Bukhārī 1503; Muslim 984, from Ibn ʿUmar). *Zakāh* on agricultural produce is a tenth of the harvest where the crop is watered by rain and half a tenth where it is irrigated by labor (al-Bukhārī 1483, from Ibn ʿUmar). Expiations (*kaffārāt*) are paid in food: feeding ten poor persons, or sixty, according to the offense (5:89; 58:4). The whole juridical system of Islam takes for granted that grain is the measure of subsistence, and verse 27 places the production of that measure in God's hands.

**Modern Application: The Loaf and Its Chain**

A single loaf of bread today involves the labor and infrastructure of continents — seed companies, fertilizer plants, diesel, harvesters, elevators, railways, mills, bakeries — and almost none of the people who eat it can name the fields it came from. The verse's instruction is to reverse that amnesia. The Islamic practice of *taṣmiyah* (saying *bismillāh*) before a meal is precisely this reversal in miniature: an acknowledgment that the food on the table arrived by a chain the eater did not build. The Qur'an's phrase in verse 32 — *"a provision for you and for your flocks"* — will name the destination of the whole list; verse 27 names its foundation, and the foundation is a seed that God causes to grow in an earth He splits, with water He pours, for a creature He made from a drop.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:28

> **and vines and herbs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿInab: The Grape**

*Wa-ʿinaban* — "and grapes." *ʿInab* is the grape, both the fruit and, by extension, the vine that bears it. The Qur'an mentions the grape by name more often than almost any other fruit, and usually in a pairing with the date palm, exactly as here:

> *"And it is He Who causes gardens to grow, trellised and untrellised, and the palm, and crops of varying flavor, and the olive and the pomegranate"* (6:141).

> *"He causes to grow for you thereby the crops, the olives, the palms, the grapes, and of all the fruits. Indeed in that is a sign for a people who reflect"* (16:11).

> *"And We made therein gardens of palms and grapes, and We caused springs to gush forth therein"* (36:34).

> *"And a sign for them is the dead earth... and We made therein gardens of palms and grapes"* (36:33–34).

> *"And We brought forth for you therein gardens of palms and grapes; you have therein much fruit"* (23:19).

> *"Or a garden of palms and vines, and We caused rivers to flow through it"* (6:99; 18:32–34).

The grape appears in the Qur'an as a mark of settled abundance, and its cultivation is the sign of a civilization that expects to remain: vines take years to bear, and a man who plants one is making a statement about the future. The Qur'an uses the vineyard, accordingly, as the setting of its parables of ingratitude and arrogance — the owner of the two gardens in Sūrah al-Kahf (18:32–43), whose vineyard was destroyed overnight precisely because he said, *"I do not think this will ever perish"* (18:35).

**The Grape and the Wine It Was Not Given For**

The Qur'an mentions the grape's products with unusual care. Of the date and the grape it says:

> *"And from the fruit of the palms and the vines you take intoxicant and good provision. Indeed in that is a sign for a people who reason"* (16:67).

The phrase distinguishes the intoxicant (*sakar*) from the good provision (*rizq ḥasan*), and the classical exegetes — Ibn ʿAbbās foremost among them — read this verse as an early Makkan intimation of the later prohibition of wine, revealed in stages and completed in 5:90–91. The grape thus occupies a distinctive place in Qur'anic ethics: it is among the finest of God's provisions in its natural form (fresh fruit, raisins, juice, vinegar, molasses) and the source of the one consumable Islam forbids absolutely in its fermented form. The Prophet ﷺ said of vinegar, *"What a good condiment vinegar is"* (Muslim 2052), and of wine, *"Every intoxicant is unlawful"* (Muslim 2003, from Ibn ʿUmar; al-Bukhārī 5581, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī). The contrast is deliberate and instructive: the same fruit, treated two ways, produces sustenance and ruin, and the choice between them is the human being's.

**Qaḍb: The Green Fodder**

*Wa-qaḍbā* — "and green fodder" (rendered in the source as "herbs"). *Qaḍb* in classical Arabic denotes fresh green herbage cut for animal feed — alfalfa-like fodder, the cut crop — and it is connected by the lexicographers to the root *q-ḍ-b*, meaning to cut, since the plant is cut for fodder and grows again. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both give this sense: *al-qaḍb* is *al-qatt*, the green feed for livestock. Some early exegetes glossed it as "the vegetables that people eat," and the modern rendering "herbs" or "green vegetation" covers both.

The inclusion of animal fodder in a list of divine favors is theologically significant. The Qur'an does not restrict its catalogue of blessings to what human beings consume directly; it counts what their animals eat as a favor to them, because the animal's food is the human being's milk, meat, leather, transport, and labor. Verse 32 will make the point explicit: *"a provision for you and for your flocks."* The chain of provision is thus longer than it looks, and gratitude for a cup of milk is gratitude for a pasture.

**The Agricultural Realism of the Qur'an**

Readers of the Makkan sūrahs regularly notice how botanically specific they are. The Qur'an names crops, not abstractions: grain, grapes, olives, palms, gardens dense with trees, fruit, pasture, pomegranates, figs (95:1), basil (*rayḥān*, 55:12), the lotus and the acacia of Paradise (56:28–29), the gourd that shaded Jonah (37:146), the two gardens of Sheba that were turned into bitter fruit and tamarisk (34:16). This specificity is part of the Qur'an's argumentative method: it will not allow the blessing to remain general, because a general blessing can be acknowledged abstractly without gratitude. By naming the crops it forces the hearer to count them. Al-Rāzī formulates the principle: enumeration is the enemy of ingratitude, since what is listed must be answered item by item.

**Modern Application: The Plate as a List**

The contemporary equivalent of verses 27–32 is the act of reading a food's composition — something millions now do for allergens, calories, and additives, but almost never for provenance. The verse invites a different reading: to look at a meal and enumerate its debts. The bread owes the grain (v. 27); the wine vinegar and the raisins owe the vine (v. 28); the oil owes the olive and the sweetness owes the palm (v. 29); the salad owes the gardens (v. 30); the fruit and the cheese owe the fruit and the pasture (v. 31); and the whole list exists "for you and for your flocks" (v. 32). The Islamic practice of gratitude after eating — *"Praise be to God, Who fed me this and provided it for me without any might or power from myself"* (Abū Dāwūd 4043; al-Tirmidhī 3458, from Muʿādh ibn Anas; graded ḥasan) — is precisely the recitation of this list in one sentence, and its final clause is the sūrah's whole argument compressed: without any might or power from myself.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:29

> **and olives and date palms,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Trees of Arabia and the Levant**

*Wa-zaytūnan wa-nakhlā* — "and olives and date palms." These two trees are the twin pillars of the traditional economy of the Qur'an's audience and, together with grain, of the entire Mediterranean and Near Eastern diet. The olive supplies oil for cooking, lighting, medicine, and anointing; the palm supplies the date — the most concentrated, most storable, most immediately energizing food available to a desert people — along with shade, fibre, timber, and syrup. The Qur'an pairs them repeatedly (6:99, 141; 16:11; 23:19; 36:34; 50:10; 80:29), and in doing so it invokes, in two words, the whole settled civilization of its hearers.

**Zaytūn: The Blessed Tree**

The olive is given a rank in the Qur'an that no other plant shares. God swears by it:

> *"By the fig and the olive, and Mount Sinai, and this secure land"* (95:1–3).

It supplies the central image of Sūrah al-Nūr's Verse of Light:

> *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth... lit from a blessed tree, an olive, neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would almost give light even if fire did not touch it — light upon light"* (24:35).

And the Prophet ﷺ recommended it in a well-known report:

> *"Eat olive oil and anoint yourselves with it, for it comes from a blessed tree."* (al-Tirmidhī 1851; Ibn Mājah 3319, from Abū Usayd; al-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan gharīb, and scholars differ on its chain)

The commentators explain the blessing of the tree from many angles: every part of it is usable, it bears for centuries, it grows in poor soil with little water, and its oil has no waste. Al-Qurṭubī records that the olive is the only fruit described in the Qur'an as coming from a *blessed* tree, and Ibn Kathīr notes the significance of 24:35's phrase "neither of the east nor of the west" — the tree on an open hilltop, receiving sun from both directions, producing the finest oil.

**Nakhl: The Palm and Its Symbolic Weight**

The date palm is the most frequently named tree in the Qur'an, and its associations are dense. It is the tree Maryam is told to shake for fresh dates in the birth of Jesus:

> *"And shake toward thee the trunk of the palm; it will let fall upon thee fresh ripe dates"* (19:25).

It is the tree by which she conceives shelter and provision in her labor; it is among the fruits of Paradise (*"and We provide them therein with fruits and palms and pomegranates"* — 55:68; *"their sustenance therein is morning and evening"* — 19:62); and it is the image of the good word in one of the Qur'an's most famous comparisons:

> *"Hast thou not seen how God strikes a comparison: a good word is like a good tree, its root firm, its branches in the sky, yielding its fruit every season by the leave of its Lord"* (14:24–25).

Ibn ʿUmar reports that the Prophet ﷺ asked the Companions to identify this tree, and that ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar knew it was the palm but was too young to speak until his father prompted him (al-Bukhārī 61; Muslim 2811). The palm was chosen for the comparison because of its properties: the good word is firm in its root (faith), high in its branches (works), fruitful in every season (benefit to others), and its every part is useful.

The Prophet ﷺ also compared the believer to the palm: *"Among the trees there is one whose leaves do not fall, and it is the likeness of the Muslim"* (al-Bukhārī 61). And the date occupies a central place in the Sunnah: breaking the fast with fresh or dry dates or water (Abū Dāwūd 2356; al-Tirmidhī 695), the recommendation to eat seven ʿajwah dates in the morning for protection (al-Bukhārī 5445; Muslim 2047, from Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ), and the report that a house without dates is a house whose people are hungry (Muslim 2046).

**Why These Two Trees in This List**

The list of verses 27–31 moves from the staple (grain) to the fodder (qaḍb) to the two great trees to the gardens to the fruit and pasture. The olive and the palm occupy the middle because they represent the long-term investment of a settled people: trees take years to bear and decades to mature, and a society that plants them has confidence in its future. Their presence in the list is therefore an argument about the character of the blessing: God provides not only for the day's hunger but for the generation's stability. Ibn Kathīr notes that the two trees also differ in the manner of their usefulness — the olive's benefit is chiefly in its oil, extracted by pressing, and the palm's in its fruit, eaten directly — so the pair covers the two forms in which provision reaches human beings: processed and immediate.

**The Deeper Pairing: Light and Sustenance**

There is a resonance worth noting between the olive and the palm in Qur'anic symbolism. The olive's oil becomes light in the lamp of 24:35; the palm's fruit becomes sustenance in the labor of Maryam in 19:25. The two trees thus supply the two necessities the Qur'an most often names: light by which to see the way, and food by which to walk it. In the economy of verse 29, the reader is being shown that both come from the same soil, the same water, and the same Hand.

**Modern Application: The Two Trees Today**

The olive and the palm remain, in the twenty-first century, two of the most consequential cultivated plants on earth — the olive for an oil whose global production runs to millions of tonnes and whose groves define the landscape of the Mediterranean, the palm (both the date palm and its tropical relative the oil palm) for a fat found in roughly half of all packaged supermarket products. The verse's instruction is unchanged and has acquired an edge: to look at the olive and the palm is now also to ask how they are grown, by whom, at what cost to water and to forest and to labor. The Qur'an does not legislate supply chains in this verse; it commands attention, and attention asked of a modern consumer will find the questions itself. What the verse guarantees is that the oil in the bottle and the date in the hand are not products of an economy but gifts of a Creator — *"and We caused to grow therein grain, and vines and herbs, and olives and date palms"* — and that this fact survives every change in the mode of their production.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:30

> **and gardens densely planted,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥadāʾiq: The Enclosed Garden**

*Wa-ḥadāʾiqa ghulbā* — "and gardens densely planted." *Ḥadāʾiq* is the plural of *ḥadīqah*, which in Arabic is not simply a garden but a *walled or enclosed* garden — from the root *ḥ-d-q*, connected with *ḥadaqah*, the pupil of the eye, and with the notion of something encircled and protected. The *ḥadīqah* is therefore a garden with a boundary: cultivated land marked off from the wild, irrigated, tended, and defended. The Qur'an uses *ḥadīqah* for the gardens of this world and, by extension, for the gardens of Paradise:

> *"Indeed We have tested them as We tested the owners of the garden, when they swore that they would harvest it in the morning"* (68:17).

> *"And We gave them two gardens of grapevines, and We surrounded them with palms, and We placed between them a sown field"* (18:32).

> *"Indeed for the reverent there is success: gardens (ḥadāʾiq) and vines"* (78:31–32).

> *"Gardens of vineyards, and the two breasts of equal ripeness, and a full cup"* (78:32–34).

The last of these is the closest parallel in the Qur'an to verse 30, and the resemblance is exact enough that the exegetes read the two passages as a deliberate echo: the dense gardens of this world are a preview, in perishable form, of the gardens that will not perish.

**Ghulb: The Word for Thickness**

*Ghulb* is the plural of *aghlab*, from *gh-l-b*, the root of overcoming and prevailing — the same root as *ghalaba*, "he conquered," and *ghālib*, "prevailing." Applied to a garden it means thick, dense, luxuriant: trees so numerous and so closely grown that they overcome one another with their shade and their branches interlock. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *ghulbā* as "whose trees are thick and interlacing," and al-Qurṭubī adds that the word implies trees so dense that the ground beneath them cannot be seen. Ibn Kathīr records the sense as "dense with trees and fruitful."

The root's meaning of "prevailing" is not lost in this usage. A *ḥadīqah ghulbā* is a garden in which growth has prevailed — in which the plants have overcome the space. The same image appears in the Qur'an's description of the two gardens of the people of Sheba before their ingratitude destroyed them: *"And We made them two gardens of eating, on the right and on the left"* (34:15) — and after: *"We replaced their two gardens with two gardens of bitter fruit, and tamarisk, and a few lote-trees"* (34:16). Density of growth is, in the Qur'anic imagination, a sign of blessing that can be withdrawn; the garden of Saba is the sūrah's implicit warning about the fragility of *ghulb*.

**The Garden as the Qur'an's Central Image**

The garden is the organizing image of the Qur'an's eschatology. *Jannah* — Paradise — is literally "the garden," from the root *j-n-n*, to cover and conceal, the same root as *jinn* (the concealed beings) and *janīn* (the unborn child in the womb): a garden is called a *jannah* because its foliage conceals the ground and shades the one who enters it. The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise are overwhelmingly horticultural — rivers flowing beneath, fruits in clusters, shade extended, couches raised, no scorching sun and no bitter cold (76:13–14) — and they are addressed to an audience for whom a walled garden with running water was the summit of earthly luxury. The logic of the image is stated in the ḥadīth:

> *"God has prepared for His righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and nothing has entered the heart of man."* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah)

The gardens of this world are therefore not the thing itself but the language in which the thing is described — a mercy to beings who cannot conceive of a good they have never tasted.

**Verse 30 in the Sequence of the List**

Within verses 27–32 the position of the "dense gardens" is important. Grain (27) is field agriculture, exposed and open; vines and fodder (28) begin the cultivated plots; olives and palms (29) are the long-lived trees; and the *ḥadīqah ghulbā* (30) is the completed form of horticulture — enclosed, irrigated, mixed-planting, and productive in every layer. The list thus ascends from the simplest provision to the most complex, and complexity here is the measure of generosity: God does not merely feed the human being, He feeds him well, in variety, in shade, in beauty. Al-Rāzī makes this the argument of the passage: the surplus in creation — the beauty beyond the useful, the variety beyond the necessary — is evidence that the Provider intends more than survival.

**Modern Application: The Garden as Restored Attention**

The dense garden is also, in modern terms, the image of restored attention. A *ḥadīqah* requires sustained care over years: irrigation, pruning, protection from animals, patience through seasons of no yield. It is the opposite of extraction and the opposite of haste, and it produces a place whose primary function is not merely food but shelter, shade, and quiet. Islamic civilization built its cities around exactly this — the courtyard garden, the fountain, the walled orchard inside the house — and the surviving gardens of al-Andalus, Isfahan, Lahore, and Istanbul are physical commentaries on verse 30. A reader who has no garden can still perform the verse's work by noticing what he passes: the mature trees on a street, the allotment behind a wall, the dense planting in a public park. Each is a *ḥadīqah ghulbā* in miniature, and each is an argument that Someone intends human beings to live among green things rather than merely to survive.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:31

> **and fruit and pastures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fākiha: The Food of Delight**

*Wa-fākihatan wa-abbā* — "and fruit and pastures." *Fākiha* is fruit in the specific sense of the sweet produce eaten for pleasure rather than for staple sustenance — the Qur'anic distinction between *ṭaʿām* (food that nourishes) and *fākiha* (fruit that delights). The word comes from *f-k-h*, whose root sense is enjoyment and good humor: *fukāhah* is jesting, *fakaha* is to be in good spirits, and *fākiha* is the food of enjoyment. The Qur'an uses the word of the provision of Paradise repeatedly:

> *"Indeed the companions of Paradise, that Day, will be in joyful occupation — they and their spouses, in shade, reclining upon couches"* (36:55–56), *"and fruit, whatever they desire"* (36:57).

> *"And the likeness of Paradise promised to the reverent: therein are rivers of water unspoiled... and therein is fruit of every kind"* (47:15).

> *"For them is fruit, and they are honored, in gardens of delight"* (37:42–43).

The presence of *fākiha* in a list of divine favors is a small theological event: the Qur'an does not present God's provision as bare subsistence. Delight is part of the design. Al-Rāzī argues from this that the enjoyment of permissible pleasure is itself an act of gratitude when it is received as a gift, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, *"Of your world, three things have been made beloved to me: women, perfume, and the coolness of my eyes in prayer"* (al-Nasāʾī 3940; Aḥmad, from Anas) — a statement in which the delights of the world and the delight of worship are named in one breath.

**Abb: The Pasture for the Animals**

*Abb* is the pasture and fodder — the herbage that animals graze. Al-Ṭabarī records the glosses of the Salaf: *al-abb* is *kalāʾ al-anʿām*, the pasture of the livestock; Ibn ʿAbbās said it is "the grass that the animals eat"; Mujāhid said the same; al-Ḥasan said it is what grows for the animals rather than for people. The word is a *hapax legomenon* in the Qur'an — it occurs nowhere else — which is why it became famous in early Islamic history as the subject of one of the most important statements about the etiquette of exegesis.

**The Report About ʿUmar and Abū Bakr**

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both record the report concerning ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who recited this sūrah from the pulpit and came to *wa-fākihatan wa-abbā*, and said:

> *"We know what fākihah is — but what is abb?"* Then he said: *"What has been commanded of you is enough for you — obey what God has made clear and act upon it."* And in another wording: *"In what sky will shade me, and on what earth will I bear me, if I say about the Book of God what I do not know?"*

The parallel report is transmitted from Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, who was asked about *abb* and replied:

> *"Which heaven will shade me, and which earth will carry me, if I say in the Book of God what I do not know?"* (reported by Abū ʿUbayd in *Faḍāʾil al-Qurʾān* and by Ibn Saʿd, and cited by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr)

Two things should be noticed. First, neither Caliph considered the meaning of a single obscure word to be an obstacle to the sūrah's usefulness: the command is to reflect on one's food, and the list is clear enough to be reflected upon. Second, both refused to speculate about the Qur'an without knowledge, and the Islamic tradition made their refusal into a rule. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever speaks about the Qur'an by his own opinion, let him take his seat in the Fire."* (al-Tirmidhī 2950, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī)

and Ibn ʿAbbās himself distinguished between the Qur'an that every Arab understands, the Qur'an that no one is excused from ignorance of, and the Qur'an whose meaning is known only to the scholars — the third being the category into which words like *abb* fall. The incident is one of the foundations of the discipline of tafsīr and of its governing ethic: say what you know, and be silent about what you do not.

**The Pairing of Human Fruit and Animal Pasture**

The coupling of *fākiha* and *abb* in a single verse is not arbitrary. It extends the circle of provision beyond the human being to the animals he depends upon — and verse 32 will state the purpose of both: *"a provision for you and for your flocks."* The Qur'an's consistent teaching is that the animals are communities like ours:

> *"There is no creature moving on the earth, nor bird flying with its wings, but they are communities like you"* (6:38).

> *"And the cattle He created — for you in them is warmth and benefits, and of them you eat"* (16:5).

> *"And God has made for you of your homes a place of rest, and He has made for you of the hides of cattle houses..."* (16:80).

The inclusion of animal pasture in the divine catalogue means that God's provision is not addressed to human beings alone but to the whole web of dependency in which human beings live. Gratitude for the fruit on one's table therefore includes gratitude for the grass under someone else's animals.

**Modern Application: The Delight and the Fodder**

Verse 31 contains a balance that modern life has lost in both directions. Some eat only for function — calories tracked, meals replaced, nutrition optimized — and lose the *fākiha*, the delight that the Qur'an counts as a divine gift and a legitimate end. Others eat only for delight and lose all sense of the *abb*, the long chain of animal feed, land, and water behind the pleasure. The verse holds the two together in five words, and its implied instruction is to receive both as provision: the fruit as joy, the pasture as the hidden labor of the ecosystem, and neither as an entitlement. The Muslim's practical exegesis of the verse is the pair of customary phrases around every meal — *bismillāh* before, *al-ḥamdulillāh* after — which place delight inside gratitude and thereby keep it from becoming greed.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:32

> **as sustenance for you and for your flocks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Matāʿan: The Word for Provision Enjoyed**

*Matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum* — "a provision for you and for your flocks." *Matāʿ* is from *m-t-ʿ*, the root of enjoyment, benefit, and provision used for a time: *mātaʿa* means to give enjoyment, *mutʿah* is a temporary provision, and *matāʿ* is that which is enjoyed and used. The Qur'an uses the word for the goods of this life precisely because it marks them as finite enjoyments:

> *"The life of this world is but play and diversion and adornment and boasting among you, and rivalry in increase of wealth and children — like rain whose vegetation pleases the cultivators"* (57:20).

> *"The provision of this world is little, and the Hereafter is better for him who is reverent"* (4:77).

> *"And We made it a provision for the travelers (matāʿan li'l-muqwīn)"* (56:73) — said of the fire, or of the provision it represents.

> *"A provision for the servants"* (50:11) — the exact parallel to verse 32, in the passage that lists rain, gardens, grain of the harvest, palms, and dense gardens.

The parallel with 50:9–11 is close enough that the classical exegetes treat the two as the same argument made twice. And the identical phrase *matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum* occurs in Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt (79:33), the chapter three sūrahs earlier — a repeated formula that marks the end of this particular Qur'anic argument in both places.

**Why "For You and For Your Flocks"**

The dual addressee — human beings and their animals — completes the picture begun in verse 31. The provision is not only for the eater but for the whole economy of eating: the pasture for the flock, the flock for the milk and meat and hide and labor, the labor for the field, the field for the grain. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the Qur'an's inclusion of the animals' sustenance among the favors to human beings teaches that God's provision to a person extends to everything on which his life depends, and that gratitude is therefore owed for things one has never personally consumed. Ibn Kathīr makes the point in terms of lordship: the same God who feeds you feeds your cattle, and you cannot feed them without Him.

The Qur'an states the dependency of the animals on God in terms identical to the dependency of human beings:

> *"And there is no creature on the earth but its provision is upon God; He knows its place of lodging and its place of deposit. All is in a clear Book"* (11:6).

> *"And how many a creature carries not its own provision; God provides for it and for you. He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing"* (29:60).

The second of these verses is the theological core of the whole passage: the majority of creatures do not store their own food; they are fed daily. So, in truth, is the human being — his stored wealth is a fiction sustained by a chain he does not control.

**The End of the List and the Purpose of the Argument**

Verses 24–32 form a single sentence in outline: let man consider his food — that We poured the water in pouring (25), then split the earth in splitting (26), then caused to grow therein grain (27), and vines and herbs (28), and olives and date palms (29), and gardens densely planted (30), and fruit and pastures (31), as a provision for you and for your flocks (32). Nine verses, nine items, one addressee. The list is not a botanical catalogue but an indictment with a merciful purpose: it answers verse 17's curse (*"How ungrateful is he!"*) by itemizing what the ungrateful man has been given, and it answers verse 5's *istaghnāʾ* by demonstrating, item by item, that the self-sufficient man has produced none of it.

Al-Rāzī summarizes the argumentative structure of the whole central panel: verses 18–22 remind man of himself (his origin, his death, his resurrection); verses 24–32 remind him of his world (his food and its chain). The two reminders together leave no ground for ingratitude, because a person is composed of nothing but these two things — a body he did not make and a world he did not grow.

**Gratitude as the Required Response**

The Qur'an's demand after every enumeration of favors is gratitude, and it states the economy of gratitude with a promise and a warning:

> *"And when thy Lord proclaimed: If you are grateful, I will surely increase you; and if you are ungrateful, indeed My punishment is severe"* (14:7).

> *"And if you should count the favor of God, you could not number it. Indeed man is surely unjust, ungrateful"* (14:34; cf. 16:18).

> *"Then remember Me, I will remember you; and be grateful to Me and do not be ungrateful"* (2:152).

> *"O you who believe, eat of the good things We have provided for you and be grateful to God, if it is Him you worship"* (2:172).

The last of these is the practical conclusion of verse 32: eating the good is not opposed to worship but is part of it, provided it is eaten with gratitude. Ibn al-Qayyim describes gratitude as built on three pillars — recognition in the heart, acknowledgment on the tongue, and the use of the favor in obedience to its Giver — and verse 32 supplies the material for the first pillar so completely that the other two follow of themselves.

**Modern Application: The Provision Nobody Owns**

The contemporary reader eats food produced by systems of extraordinary complexity — global supply chains, industrial fertilizer derived from natural gas, hybrid seed, mechanized harvest, refrigerated transport — and this complexity makes verse 32 harder to see rather than easier, because it conceals the water and the split earth behind a series of human transactions. The illusion produced is precisely *istaghnāʾ*: the feeling that one's food is bought, and therefore owed to one's own money and one's own work. Verse 32 dismantles the illusion by naming the items rather than the transactions: water, earth, grain, vines, fodder, olives, palms, gardens, fruit, pasture. Every one of them is prior to money and independent of it. The believer's discipline is to recover the list — to say *bismillāh* over the plate and *al-ḥamdulillāh* after it, and to know that the phrase *"a provision for you and for your flocks"* is addressed to him personally, today, at this meal, by the One who poured.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:33

> **So when the Piercing Cry does come,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Ṣākhkhah: The Word and Its Sound**

*Fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhah* — "so when the Piercing Cry does come." *Al-ṣākhkhah* is an intensive active participle from the root *ṣ-kh-kh*, which denotes a sound so loud that it deafens the hearer: *ṣakhkha* means to strike the ear with a noise, and *al-aṣakhkh* in Arabic is the deaf person — one whose ear has been struck. The word therefore carries its own effect inside its morphology: the Cry is called "piercing" because it pierces the ear, and al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr both record the gloss that it will almost deafen those who hear it. Al-Qurṭubī adds the view that *al-ṣākhkhah* functions as a proper name for the Day of Judgment itself, on the pattern of the Qur'an's other names for that Day.

The Qur'an's vocabulary for the Day is famously rich, and each name carries a distinct aspect: *al-qāriʿah*, "the Calamity that strikes" (101:1–3); *al-ṭāmmah al-kubrā*, "the Great Overwhelming" (79:34); *al-ḥāqqah*, "the Inevitable" (69:1–3); *al-ghāshiyah*, "the Overwhelming" (88:1); *al-wāqiʿah*, "the Event" (56:1); *al-ṣākhkhah*, "the Piercing Cry"; *yawm al-faṣl*, "the Day of Decision" (78:17); *yawm al-dīn*, "the Day of Judgment" (1:4); *al-āzifah*, "the Approaching" (40:18). The multiplicity is itself a rhetorical device: a thing with fifteen names is a thing that will not be denied.

**The Cry and the Trumpet**

The exegetes identify *al-ṣākhkhah* as the second blowing of the Trumpet (*al-ṣūr*), at which the dead are raised, the first having caused the destruction of all that lives:

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is in the earth will swoon, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown a second time, and behold they are standing, looking on"* (39:68).

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown — that is the Day of the Threat"* (50:20).

> *"And on the Day when We will say: The Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather the sinners that Day, blue-eyed"* (20:102).

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and lo! from the graves they will come forth hastening to their Lord"* (36:51).

> *"Indeed the Day of Decision is an appointed time — the Day when the Trumpet is blown and you come in multitudes"* (78:17–18).

The angel charged with the Trumpet is Isrāfīl in the Islamic tradition, and the Prophet ﷺ spoke of him in a report that conveys the imminence the sūrah is aiming at:

> *"How can I be at ease when the angel of the Trumpet has already put the Trumpet to his mouth and inclined his head, waiting for the command to blow?"* The Companions asked what they should say, and he said: *"Say: 'God is sufficient for us, and He is the best disposer of affairs; upon God we rely.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 3551, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī; graded ḥasan)

**Jāʾat: The Coming of a Thing That Cannot Be Avoided**

The verb is *jāʾat* — "it comes," feminine to agree with *al-ṣākhkhah*. The Qur'an regularly uses *jāʾa* for the arrival of the Hour, and the choice of the verb is significant: the Hour is not described as being created or as occurring but as *coming*, like a visitor or an army. *"Has there not come upon man a period of time when he was not a thing mentioned?"* (76:1) uses the same verb of the past, and the Qur'an's eschatology consistently presents the future in the perfect tense, as though it had already arrived (compare *ansharah* in verse 22). *"The command of God is coming, so do not hasten it"* (16:1). *"The Hour has drawn near and the moon has split"* (54:1).

There is also a resonance with the sūrah's own opening. Verse 2 said *an jāʾahu al-aʿmā* — "because the blind man came to him"; verse 33 says *fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhah* — "when the Piercing Cry comes." A man came to the Prophet in Makkah and was deferred; a Cry will come to all of creation and cannot be deferred. The sūrah has placed the two arrivals at its beginning and its climax, and the comparison is the argument: if you will not attend to the one who comes to you, how will you endure the one that comes for you?

**The Structure of the Final Panel**

Verse 33 opens the sūrah's fourth and last panel, which runs to verse 42. Its structure is tight: the arrival of the Cry (33); the flight from kin (34–36); the absorption of every soul in its own affair (37); and then the two categories of faces (38–39 and 40–42). Ten verses, and in them the Qur'an disassembles every human attachment — brother, mother, father, spouse, children — and leaves the individual alone with his own case, and then clothes him in a face that reveals the verdict. Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur'an's eschatological passages almost always proceed in this order: the sound, the separation, the accounting, and the countenance; and that the countenance comes last because it is the permanent one.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Immediately After the List of Blessings**

The juxtaposition of verse 32 and verse 33 is one of the most abrupt transitions in the Qur'an, and it is deliberate. The previous ten verses ended in abundance — gardens, fruit, pasture, provision for you and your flocks — and the very next word is the Piercing Cry. The transition performs the argument that the Qur'an makes everywhere: this world's provision is real, and it is passing; the enjoyment named in *matāʿ* (v. 32) is by definition temporary, and what follows it is not another provision but a verdict. The reader who has just been made grateful for his food is now told what will become of him after the last meal. Islamic piety formalized this transition in the practice of remembering death at the table and in the grave-side meal, and in the supplication the Prophet ﷺ taught after eating: *"Praise be to God, Who fed us and gave us drink and made us Muslims"* (al-Tirmidhī 3457, from Anas) — gratitude and identity, in the presence of an end.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:34

> **that Day when a man will flee from his brother,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yawma Yafirr: The Grammar of the Day**

*Yawma yafirru al-marʾu min akhīh* — "the day when a man will flee from his brother." The accusative *yawma* is dependent on what preceded: when the Piercing Cry comes, on the day when a man flees. The verb *yafirru* is from *f-r-r*, to flee, to run away from danger — the same root as *firār*, flight, and *mafarr*, place of refuge or escape. The Qur'an uses the word of the flight of a person from God in the most striking possible construction:

> *"So flee (fa-firrū) to God. Indeed I am a clear warner from Him to you"* (51:50).

Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr both comment on the extraordinary command in 51:50 — flight *toward* God rather than away from Him — and read it together with the flight of verse 34: on that Day the flight is *from* everything that is not God, because everything that is not God has become a danger. The brother who was a shelter in this world is, in the next, a claimant.

**Al-Marʾu: The Individual Human Being**

The word *al-marʾu* is a term for the human being as an individual person, without specification of rank or tribe; its feminine is *al-marʾah*. The Qur'an's choice of *marʾ* over *insān* or *rajul* is significant here: *marʾ* emphasizes the individual in his singularity, and the verse that follows (37) will make the point explicit — *"for every man (imriʾ) of them that Day his affair shall suffice him."* The Day dissolves the collective into individuals, and the Qur'an marks the dissolution by switching to the word for the single person.

**Why the Brother Comes First**

The order of the list in verses 34–36 is one of the most discussed features of the passage: brother (34), then mother and father (35), then spouse and children (36). Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the Qur'an does not begin with the nearest in affection but with the nearest in *obligation* and in daily association. The brother is the person to whom a man is bound by blood and by the expectations of the clan — in Arabian society the brother was the first resort in dispute, the first defender in war, the first sharer in inheritance. To flee from the brother is therefore to flee from the entire structure of tribal solidarity, which is why the Qur'an begins there: the collapse of the social order is announced by the collapse of its strongest bond.

Then come the parents — the source of one's existence and the objects of the Qur'an's second great command after worship of God (*"and be good to your parents"* — 17:23; 31:14; 46:15). Then the spouse and children — the attachments a person chooses and raises, the ones whose claims are loudest in ordinary life. The list ascends in emotional intensity while descending in the possibility of help, and the climax of the passage in verse 37 states the reason for the flight.

**The Parallel Passage in Sūrah al-Maʿārij**

The Qur'an develops the same scene at greater length in Sūrah al-Maʿārij, and the comparison is essential to understanding 80:34–36:

> *"He will be shown them. The criminal will wish that he might ransom himself from the punishment of that Day by his children, and his spouse and his brother, and his kinfolk who sheltered him, and whoever is on the earth altogether — that it might then save him"* (70:11–14).

Two differences between the two passages are instructive. In al-Maʿārij the criminal wishes to *give* his family as a ransom — the family is property to be spent; in ʿAbasa he *flees from* them — the family is a threat to be escaped. Both describe the same collapse: the relationships that defined a person's identity in this world become, in the next, either a currency he is willing to sacrifice or a creditor he is trying to avoid. The Qur'an states the underlying rule elsewhere:

> *"And on the Day when the Hour is established, that Day the criminals will despair; and they will have no intercessors among their partners, and they will disbelieve in their partners"* (30:12–13).

> *"When those who were followed disown those who followed them, and they see the punishment and the ties are cut"* (2:166).

**The Flight as the Fulfilment of the Sūrah's Argument**

Verse 34 completes the reversal begun in verse 5. The notable who "deemed himself beyond need" did so because of his clan — his brothers, his fathers, his kin, the network that made him *ghanī*. The verse announces that on the Day of the Piercing Cry the very network that produced his sense of independence will be the first thing he runs from. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both draw the moral conclusion: whoever relies on kin in this world will be abandoned by them in the next, and whoever relies on God will find Him present at the moment when every kinship fails. The Qur'an states the same principle in the verse that Muslim funerals have recited for fourteen centuries:

> *"O mankind, be reverent toward your Lord and fear a Day when no father will avail his son at all, nor a son avail his father at all"* (31:33).

**Modern Application: The Network That Dissolves**

Every human being builds a network of people who will, in the ordinary course of life, defend him, provide for him, and vouch for him: family, colleagues, professional associations, online followers, institutions. Verse 34 is not a counsel to distrust them — the Qur'an elsewhere commands maintaining the ties of kinship (*ṣilat al-raḥim*) and calls the severing of them a corruption of the earth (47:22–23). It is a statement about the *limit* of every network. There is a moment — in a courtroom, in a diagnosis, in a bankruptcy, in a death — when the network cannot follow a person, and the Qur'an places that moment at the center of its eschatology so that the reader will not mistake his relationships for his security. The practical discipline is not withdrawal but ordering: love your brother, honor your parents, cherish your spouse and children, and know that none of them can carry your case.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:35

> **and his mother and his father,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two People He Cannot Choose**

*Wa-ummihī wa-abīh* — "and his mother and his father." After the brother, whom a man may have chosen as an ally, the verse names the two people whose relationship to him is entirely beyond his choice: his mother and his father. The order — mother first — is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of the mother elsewhere, where she is given the greater share of the child's obligation because she bore the greater share of the cost:

> *"And We have enjoined upon man goodness toward his parents. His mother carried him with hardship and bore him with hardship, and his weaning is thirty months"* (46:15).

> *"And We have enjoined upon man concerning his parents: his mother bore him in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is in two years — be grateful to Me and to your parents"* (31:14).

The famous ḥadīth states the ranking explicitly. A man came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked, "O Messenger of God, who is most deserving of my good company?" He said, *"Your mother."* The man asked, "Then who?" He said, *"Your mother."* The man asked, "Then who?" He said, *"Your mother."* The man asked, "Then who?" He said, *"Then your father."* (al-Bukhārī 5971; Muslim 2548, from Abū Hurayrah)

Verse 35 therefore names, immediately after the brother, the two relationships that Islam ranks highest among all human obligations — and it names them as the ones a man will flee from. The rhetorical effect is maximal: if the flight extends to the mother who bore him with hardship, then nothing remains.

**The Mother Named Before the Father: A Deliberate Emphasis**

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the Qur'an, in its references to parents, generally joins them (*al-wālidayn*) but when it separates them, it frequently names the mother first, as in 46:15, 31:14, and here. The exegetical reason given is the intensity of the mother's bond and the greater claim her suffering establishes. In the context of verse 35 this has a particular poignancy: the relationship a person would least expect to dissolve is the one with the woman who fed him from her body, and the verse announces its dissolution in the same breath as the brother's and the father's.

**Why a Man Flees From His Parents on That Day**

The commentators give several reasons, and they are complementary rather than competing. First, the flight is from the *claim*: on the Day of Judgment every person who was wronged will demand restitution, and the family is the place where most wrongs are committed and least redressed — a father's harshness, a mother's favoritism, an inheritance withheld, a child neglected. Second, the flight is from the *shame*: to be seen in one's degradation by the people whose opinion mattered most is a specific anguish, and the verse elsewhere describes the disbeliever's wish that he had never been known: *"Would that I had taken a path with the Messenger... woe to me, would that I had not taken so-and-so as an intimate friend"* (25:27–28). Third, the flight is from *uselessness*: the parents cannot help, and to remain near them is to be reminded of the help that is not coming. Al-Rāzī adds a fourth and subtlest reason: on that Day each person will be afraid that his own case will require the sacrifice of another's, and the family is the group from which such a sacrifice would naturally be demanded — exactly the scenario described in 70:11–14.

**The Contrast With the World's Order**

In this world the family is the unit of survival: the mother and father are the first protection of the child, and the child is the expected support of the aged parent. Islamic law institutionalizes both directions — the obligation of maintenance (*nafaqah*) from the parents to the child and from the adult child to the needy parent, the inheritance shares, the rules of guardianship, the prohibition of severing kinship. Verse 35 does not abolish any of this; it locates its limit. The law governs the world in which parents and children can help each other. The Day named in verse 35 is the Day in which they cannot, and the Qur'an insists that the reader know both facts simultaneously: maintain the ties now, and do not build your eternity on them.

**The Believer's Consolation in the Verse**

Not every reading of the passage is bleak, and the exegetes note an important qualification. The flight described in verses 34–36 is universal in form, but the Qur'an elsewhere promises reunion for the believing family:

> *"And those who believed and whose offspring followed them in faith — We will join their offspring to them, and We will not deprive them of anything of their deeds"* (52:21).

> *"Gardens of Eden, which they enter along with the righteous among their fathers, their spouses, and their offspring"* (13:23).

The reconciliation of the two pictures is the classical one: the flight of verses 34–36 describes the moment of reckoning, when each soul's case is its own and no intercession is possible without permission; the reunion of 52:21 and 13:23 describes the settlement, after the judgment, for those whom God has mercy upon. Ibn Kathīr states the sequence in exactly these terms. The verse is therefore a warning about the Day's procedure rather than a prophecy that every family is lost — and it makes the promise of reunion in Paradise more valuable, not less, because it shows what the reunion has survived.

**Modern Application: The Limits of Family**

Contemporary culture oscillates between two errors about family: idolatry (the family as the source of identity and the final authority) and abandonment (the family as an optional arrangement). Verse 35 corrects both. It honors the family by naming it first among the attachments whose loss defines the Day — you will flee from your mother and father, which is only a tragedy because the bond was real. And it limits the family by showing that it cannot accompany a person to the place where his case is decided. The practical instruction for a believer is to invest in family while knowing that the investment's return is not salvation, and to invest in God while knowing that the family may be restored to him there — as the Qur'an promises, and as the flight of verse 35 makes it precious that they should be.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:36

> **and his spouse and his children.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṣāḥibah: The Companion, Not Only the Wife**

*Wa-ṣāḥibatihī wa-banīh* — "and his spouse and his children." *Ṣāḥibah* is the feminine of *ṣāḥib*, from the root *ṣ-ḥ-b*, to accompany, to keep company with: the *ṣāḥib* is a companion, a fellow traveller, an associate, and *ṣāḥibah* used of a man's relation to a woman means his wife in the sense of his life-companion. The Qur'an uses the word in this sense repeatedly:

> *"The Originator of the heavens and the earth — how should He have a son when He has no spouse (ṣāḥibah)?"* (6:101).

> *"Exalted is the majesty of our Lord — He has taken neither spouse (ṣāḥibah) nor son"* (72:3).

> *"And We will drive the guilty to Hell as a thirsty herd"* — and the Qur'an speaks of the spouse on that Day as one from whom there is no rescue: *"There is no refuge"* (75:11–12, in context of *"whither is the escape?"*).

> *"And as for him who is given his book in his left hand, he will say: Would that I had not been given my book, and had not known what my account was... My wealth has not availed me, my authority has perished from me"* (69:25–29).

The choice of *ṣāḥibah* over *zawjah* (wife) is meaningful. *Zawjah* denotes the legal and complementary pair; *ṣāḥibah* denotes the companionship — the person who accompanied him through the days of his life, who slept beside him, who knew his habits and his secrets. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the marriage bond in terms of intimacy and mutual indwelling:

> *"They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them"* (2:187).

> *"And of His signs is that He created for you, from yourselves, spouses that you may find rest in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy"* (30:21).

Verse 36 announces the dissolution of precisely that intimacy — not the ending of a contract but the ending of a companionship.

**Banīh: The Sons, and What They Represent**

*Banīh* is the plural of *ibn*, "his sons." The Qur'an often uses "sons" in a form that includes descendants generally, and the parallel in Sūrah al-Maʿārij names the children without gender specification (*"his children"* — 70:11). The children are the last item in the list and, in Arabian society as in most traditional societies, the most emotionally powerful: the continuation of one's name, the support of one's old age, the object of a parent's most irrational love. The Qur'an elsewhere names children and wealth together as the two things that absorb the human heart:

> *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world, and the enduring good deeds are better in the sight of thy Lord for reward, and better for hope"* (18:46).

> *"O you who believe, let not your wealth and your children divert you from the remembrance of God; whoever does that — those are the losers"* (63:9).

> *"Know that your wealth and your children are but a trial, and that God — with Him is a mighty reward"* (8:28).

Verse 36 supplies the eschatological counterpart of these warnings: the children who were a trial in this world are among the people one flees from in the next, and the wealth that was an adornment does not appear in the list at all — it has already been discounted.

**The Completion of the List**

Verses 34–36 together name the whole of a person's human world: the brother (the ally), the mother and father (the origin), the spouse and children (the future). Nothing is left outside. Al-Rāzī notes the exhaustive character of the enumeration: the Qur'an does not say "a man will flee from his family"; it lists them one by one, so that no reader can think his own particular attachment is exempt. The method is the same as that of verses 27–31, where the provisions were listed item by item: enumeration defeats evasion. A general statement can be assented to abstractly; a list must be answered individually.

**The Reason for the Flight, and Its Severity**

The commentators explain that the flight is not from the persons but from their claims and from the impossibility of helping them. Ibn Kathīr records the view that a man flees from those he loved most precisely because their presence would increase his grief and because he will be unable to bear their appeals. Al-Qurṭubī adds the report of the ḥadīth on the gathering — the people assembled barefoot, naked, uncircumcised — and ʿĀʾishah's question about whether they will look at one another, which the Prophet ﷺ answered by pointing to the gravity of the situation (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2859). The flight of verse 36 is that gravity expressed in motion.

There is a further dimension that the Qur'an makes explicit elsewhere: on that Day some relationships will be relations of enmity rather than love, where one led the other astray:

> *"Friends will be enemies to one another that Day, except the reverent"* (43:67).

> *"Indeed you have taken idols besides God as a bond of love between you in the life of this world; then on the Day of Resurrection you will disown one another and curse one another"* (29:25).

The exception in 43:67 — *except the reverent* — is the same exception that allows the reunion promised in 52:21: friendships and marriages built on reverence survive the Day, and only those built on something else dissolve.

**Modern Application: The Attachments and the Audit**

The verse is not an instruction to love one's spouse and children less. The Qur'an commands kindness to spouses, provision for children, mercy toward both, and it describes the family as one of God's signs (30:21). The instruction is about the audit of attachments: to ask, of each relationship, whether it is built on *taqwā* — on reverence that will survive the Day — or on convenience, image, mutual indulgence, or shared neglect of God. Relationships of the first kind are promised continuation (52:21); relationships of the second are promised enmity (43:67). The modern reader can perform the audit in a single question: if the account of my life were opened today, would this person's presence beside me help my case or complicate it? The verse's answer for the unredeemed attachment is that he will not be beside you at all — you will be running.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:37

> **For every man that Day his affair shall suffice him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shaʾnun Yughnīh: The Verse That Explains the Flight**

*Li-kulli imriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shaʾnun yughnīh* — "for every man of them that Day his affair shall suffice him," or more literally, "every one of them that Day will have a concern that makes him independent of others" — that occupies him, absorbs him, and renders him unable to attend to anyone else. *Shaʾn* is a matter, a state, a concern of weight; the Qur'an says of God, *"Every day He is upon a matter (fī shaʾn)"* (55:29) — a word denoting an affair that commands a person's whole capacity. *Yughnīh* is from the root *gh-n-y* — the root of verse 5's *istaghnāʾ* — and it means to suffice, to make independent, to render free of need of another.

The verse is the explanation of verses 34–36. The flight from brother, parents, spouse, and children is not cruelty and not panic; it is the mechanical consequence of a fact: every person that Day has a concern so total that it consumes his entire capacity for attention. Al-Ṭabarī states the sense plainly: each will be busy with himself and unable to concern himself with another.

**The Return of the Root gh-n-y: A Structural Irony**

The recurrence of the root is one of the sūrah's most beautiful structural effects, and the classical exegetes did not miss it. In verse 5 the notable *istaghnā* — he deemed himself beyond need — and that false self-sufficiency was the reason he received nothing. In verse 37 every human being *yughnīhi shaʾnuh* — his own affair suffices him — and this real, enforced self-sufficiency is the condition of everyone, believer and disbeliever alike, on that Day.

The irony is exact. The man who claimed independence in this world will be given independence in the next in the only form it can exist: total absorption in his own case, with no one to help him and no capacity to help anyone. The man who knew his need in this world (vv. 8–9) will arrive at the same absorption — and then, if God has mercy on him, will be given what he asked for. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both draw the lesson: *istighnāʾ* in this life is a lie about the human condition, and verse 37 is the truth of it revealed at the moment when the lie can no longer be maintained.

**The Ḥadīth of the Gathering**

The verse is the text upon which one of the most famous eschatological reports in the collections is read:

> *"People will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised."* ʿĀʾishah said: "O Messenger of God, men and women, all looking at one another?" He said: *"The affair is too grave for that"* — and in some transmissions, he recited: *"For every man that Day his affair shall suffice him."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2859, from ʿĀʾishah)

The wording in al-Bukhārī and Muslim is *"yā ʿĀʾishah, al-amru ashaddu min an yanzura baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍ"* — "O ʿĀʾishah, the matter is more serious than that they should look at one another" — and the version in which the Prophet ﷺ answers with the verse itself is transmitted in the tafsīr literature and cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī. Either way the meaning is identical: the modesty that governs this world is not suspended by indecency in the next but by the total absorption of every person in his own case.

The same report appears with a further detail: ʿĀʾishah asked whether they would be ashamed, and the Prophet ﷺ answered that the situation is beyond shame. The commentators connect this with the description of the resurrection as a scene in which every social convention of this world is dissolved — clothes, rank, wealth, beauty, lineage — leaving only the deed. *"And you have come to Us alone, as We created you the first time, leaving behind you what We provided for you"* (6:94).

**The Theological Content: Individual Accountability**

Verse 37 states the Qur'an's doctrine of individual responsibility in its most compressed form, and the doctrine is repeated across the revelation:

> *"And no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another"* (6:164; 17:15; 35:18; 39:7; 53:38).

> *"Every soul is a pledge for what it has earned, except the companions of the right"* (74:38–39).

> *"That Day you will be brought forward, not a secret of you remaining hidden"* (69:18).

> *"And fear a Day when no soul will avail anything for another soul, nor will intercession be accepted from it, nor ransom taken from it, nor will they be helped"* (2:48; cf. 2:123).

The principle has two consequences that Islam took with equal seriousness. First, no one is punished for another's sin — not for a father's crime, not for a tribe's history, not for an ancestor's disbelief, which is the explicit rejection of the pre-Islamic Arabian practice of inherited blood-guilt. Second, no one is saved by another's merit — not by lineage from the Prophet ﷺ himself, as the report makes plain: *"O Banū Hāshim, ransom yourselves from the Fire — I can avail you nothing against God"* (al-Bukhārī 2753; Muslim 206, from Abū Hurayrah; and cf. Muslim 204, from ʿĀʾishah and others). The verse is thus the foundation of Islam's radical individualism before God, and of its equally radical rejection of inherited spiritual privilege.

**Not Isolation, but Absorption**

One nuance deserves emphasis. The verse does not say that people will be alone in the sense of solitary; the gathering is the largest assembly in the history of the cosmos, and the Qur'an describes it as a scene of crowds: *"and you come in multitudes"* (78:18), *"and We shall leave them that Day surging one upon another"* (18:99). What the verse says is that each person will be, in effect, alone *within* the crowd — absorbed in his own case to the exclusion of every other. This is the precise inversion of the worldly condition, in which a person is physically alone and mentally surrounded by everyone: thinking about other people's opinions, other people's needs, other people's judgments. On that Day the crowd is total and the privacy is total, and the two coexist.

**Modern Application: The Rehearsal**

The verse has an obvious and unsettling contemporary application. The absorption it describes — a person so consumed by his own case that he cannot attend to anyone else — has a rehearsal in this world: the emergency, the diagnosis, the courtroom, the bankruptcy, the moment of panic, when people discover what they are actually like. Islamic spirituality treated verse 37 as an invitation to rehearse deliberately, and the practice of *dhikr al-mawt* (the remembrance of death) has exactly this function: to bring forward, into a calm hour, the absorption that will otherwise arrive without warning. The Companions were known to visit the graves and to speak of the Day, not out of morbidity but because a person who has thought about his own case in advance handles it differently when it arrives. The practical question the verse leaves with the reader is the one Ibn ʿUmar drew from the Prophet's teaching: *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler"* (al-Bukhārī 6416) — because the traveler's affair is his own, and he carries it with him.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:38

> **Faces that Day shall be shining, radiant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wujūh: The Face as the Revelation of the Person**

*Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfirah* — "faces that Day shall be shining, radiant." The sūrah closes with faces, and the choice is not merely poetic. The face (*wajh*) is the part of a human being by which he is known, addressed, and judged in ordinary life; it is the seat of expression and the instrument of encounter. The Qur'an's use of the word is extensive and layered: the *wajh* is what one turns toward God (*"Wheresoever you turn, there is the Face of God"* — 2:115); it is what remains when everything else perishes (*"Everything upon it perishes, and the Face of thy Lord remains, full of majesty and honor"* — 55:26–27); it is what is submitted in worship (*"And who is better in religion than one who submits his face to God while being a doer of good?"* — 4:125); and it is what will be transformed on the Day of Judgment (*"On the Day when faces will be whitened and faces will be blackened"* — 3:106).

Al-Ṭabarī records the saying cited in the source commentary: *"the face is the mirror of the heart"* — and the Qur'an's eschatology operates on exactly that principle. On that Day the interior becomes exterior; the state of the heart, hidden for a lifetime, appears on the countenance. What a person spent his life managing — his expression — becomes the thing he can no longer manage.

**Musfirah: The Root Shared With the Scribes**

*Musfirah* is from *s-f-r*, the root discussed at verse 15: to be bright, to shine, to be revealed — *asfara al-ṣubḥ*, "the dawn broke"; *isfār* is the brightness of the early morning, and the jurists speak of *waqt al-isfār* for the time when the light is clear. The same root gives *safarah*, the scribes of verse 15, in one of its three senses (those who unveil and clarify). The lexical link is worth holding: the pages are carried by unveiling scribes (v. 15), and the faces of those who received what the scribes carried will be unveiled, bright, revealed (v. 38). The message that clarified itself in the book clarifies the face in the end.

**Why Brightness? Four Explanations From the Commentators**

The source commentary records four explanations for the radiance of the believers' faces, and each is supported by the tradition.

First, joy made visible: Ibn Kathīr explains that the faces are radiant from the joy in their hearts and the glad tidings manifest upon them — the arrival of good news produces light in the countenance, as the Qur'an describes elsewhere: *"And when one of them is given the glad tidings of a daughter, his face darkens"* (16:58) — the same mechanism working in reverse.

Second, the traces of ablution. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī connect the radiance to *wuḍūʾ*, on the strength of the Prophet's report:

> *"My community will be called on the Day of Resurrection as 'the bright-faced, bright-limbed' (ghurran muḥajjalīn) from the traces of ablution; so whoever among you can extend his brightness, let him do so."* (al-Bukhārī 136; Muslim 246, from Abū Hurayrah)

The washing of the face five times a day leaves, in the Islamic understanding, a mark that is invisible in this world and luminous in the next.

Third, the traces of night vigil. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī record the view that the night prayer (*qiyām al-layl*) produces light in the face, and the tradition cites the saying attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"Indeed the good deed gives brightness to the face, light to the heart, provision, strength in the body, and love in the hearts of creation; and indeed the evil deed gives darkness to the face, darkness to the heart, weakness in the body, deficiency in provision, and hatred in the hearts of creation."* (an athar from Ibn ʿAbbās, cited widely by Ibn al-Qayyim in *al-Jawāb al-Kāfī* and in *al-Dāʾ wa'l-Dawāʾ*)

The Qur'an's own parallel is the description of the Companions: *"Their mark is on their faces, from the trace of prostration"* (48:29).

Fourth, relief from the world's burdens: al-Rāzī adds that the believer's face will be bright because he has been released from the attachments and anxieties that darkened it in this life. The face that spent decades tight with worry will be released into openness.

**The Parallel Passages**

The Qur'an describes the radiant faces of the believers in a series of passages that read as variations on the same theme, and the source commentary rightly cross-references the nearest of them:

> *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, looking upon their Lord"* (75:22–23).

> *"You will recognize in their faces the radiance of bliss"* (83:24).

> *"Faces that Day shall be at ease, content with their striving, in a lofty garden"* (88:8–10).

> *"On the Day when God will not disgrace the Prophet and those who believed with him; their light will run before them and on their right hands"* (66:8).

> *"On the Day when you will see the believers, men and women, their light running before them and on their right hands"* (57:12).

The description in 75:22–23 adds the element that 80:38–39 implies: the radiance is not only joy but vision — the faces are bright because they are looking at God, which is the Islamic understanding of the beatific vision and the highest reward of Paradise.

**The Blind Man's Face**

The source commentary, following al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī, connects verses 38–39 back to the incident that opened the sūrah: these are the faces of the sincere seekers — the state of Ibn Umm Maktūm — as verses 40–42 are the state of the one who deemed himself beyond need. The connection completes the sūrah's argument in a way that is almost unbearably pointed. In the room at Makkah, the blind man's face was a face of need: unseeing, anxious, insistent, a face that made the important conversation awkward. On the Day of the Piercing Cry, that same face is radiant. The face that was frowned at is the face that shines; the faces that were attended to are the faces that darken. The sūrah has reversed the room, and the reversal is its permanent lesson.

**Modern Application: The Face We Manage**

Contemporary life is governed by facial management: the professional expression, the camera-ready smile, the composed video-call face, the curated photograph. Verse 38 announces that a Day is coming on which the face will be beyond management — on which it will show, with total accuracy, what a person actually was. The verse is not a threat to those who are struggling sincerely, since the radiance it describes comes from ablution, night prayer, joy, and relief, all of which are available to anyone. It is a warning to those whose faces are currently well-managed and whose hearts are not, and a consolation to those whose faces are tired, marked by grief, ill, or disfigured, and whose hearts are with God. In the Islamic scale, the beauty that matters is the one that will be visible then — and verse 38 tells the reader exactly how to acquire it: wash the face for God, keep some of the night for Him, and let the good deed do its slow, invisible work on the countenance.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:39

> **laughing, joyous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḍāḥikah: Laughter in the Qur'an**

*Ḍāḥikatun mustabshirah* — "laughing, joyous." *Ḍāḥikah* is the active participle of *ḍaḥika*, to laugh. The Qur'an mentions laughter in a small number of highly significant places, and the distribution is instructive: laughter in this world is usually portrayed as misplacement, while laughter in the next is portrayed as the natural expression of the saved.

Of this world's laughter, the Qur'an says:

> *"So let them laugh little and weep much, as a recompense for what they used to earn"* (9:82).

> *"Indeed the criminals used to laugh at those who believed, and when they passed by them they would exchange derisive glances"* (83:29–30).

> *"Will you then wonder at this discourse, and laugh and not weep?"* (53:59–60).

Of the laughter of the Hereafter:

> *"So that Day those who believed will laugh at the disbelievers, upon couches, gazing"* (83:34–35).

> *"Indeed there were of My servants a party who said: 'Our Lord, we believe, so forgive us and have mercy on us'... and you took them in mockery, until they made you forget the remembrance of Me, and you used to laugh at them. Indeed I have rewarded them this Day for what they were patient — they are the successful"* (23:109–111).

Verse 39 belongs to this second category, and its juxtaposition with verse 38 produces a single state described twice: the shining face is the same face that is laughing. The Qur'an is careful to distinguish the joy of the believers from mere amusement; the laughter here is the laughter of deliverance, and the word that follows specifies its cause.

**Mustabshirah: Joy With a Reason**

*Mustabshirah* is the tenth form of the root *b-sh-r*, the root of good news: *bashar* is a human being (from the skin's surface), *bashīr* is a bringer of glad tidings, *bashshara* is to give glad tidings, and *istabshara* is to rejoice *in response to* glad tidings received. The tenth form is crucial: it is not undifferentiated happiness but the joy of one who has been told something good and has accepted it. The Qur'an uses the root constantly for the announcement made to the believers:

> *"And give glad tidings (wa-bashshir) to those who believe and do righteous deeds, that for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow"* (2:25).

> *"And give glad tidings to the believers that they have from God a great bounty"* (33:47).

> *"O soul at rest, return to thy Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing; enter among My servants, enter My Paradise"* (89:27–30).

> *"Indeed those who say 'Our Lord is God' and then remain upright — the angels descend upon them: 'Do not fear and do not grieve, and receive glad tidings of the Garden which you were promised'"* (41:30).

The glad tidings arrive at death, in the grave, at the resurrection, at the receiving of the book in the right hand, and at the sight of the Lord. Verse 39 describes the state produced by that arrival: *mustabshirah* — rejoicing because the news has come and has come true.

**The Sequence: Radiance, Then Laughter, Then Joy**

Verse 38 gives the visible effect (the shining face); verse 39 gives the expression (laughter) and then the interior state (rejoicing in glad tidings). The order moves from surface to source, and it matches the Qur'an's usual method of describing Paradise: first what is seen, then what is felt, then why. Al-Rāzī observes that the Qur'an rarely describes reward as mere pleasure; it describes it as the confirmation of a promise, which is why the joy is *istibshār* — joy with a reason — rather than euphoria. The believer's joy on that Day is in large part the joy of having been right, of having trusted a promise that the world spent his whole life telling him was imaginary.

**Laughter and the Islamic Temperament**

The verse has an important bearing on Islamic spiritual psychology, which is sometimes misrepresented as exclusively austere. The Prophet ﷺ smiled often and laughed on occasion; the Companions described his smile as the most frequent expression on his face, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Ḥārith said, *"I never saw anyone smile more than the Messenger of God"* (al-Tirmidhī 3641). He said, *"Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face"* (Muslim 2626, from Abū Dharr). And the tradition records his laughter with his Companions, his joking without falsehood (*"I joke, but I say only the truth"* — al-Tirmidhī 1990), and his delight at a good outcome.

Verse 39 places laughter at the summit of the human destiny, which means that the Islamic understanding of the good life is not joylessness postponed until death. It is joy deferred, tested, and finally made permanent. The believer who weeps in this world from the fear of God and laughs in the next from the mercy of God is living out the Qur'an's own sequence: *"And they fall upon their faces weeping, and it increases them in humility"* (17:109); *"That you may not grieve over what has escaped you, nor rejoice over what He has given you"* (57:23) — with the rejoicing that remains reserved for the Day described here.

**The Blind Man Laughing**

Read with verse 38 and the incident of verses 1–10, verse 39 completes one of the most moving arcs in the Qur'an. The man who came to the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah was anxious, insistent, and deferential; the reports emphasize his persistence and his fear. He was frowned at in a room of important men, and he could not even see the frown. On the Day described in verse 39, that same man is laughing — and the laughter is not his alone but is the laughter of everyone who ever sought God sincerely while being made to feel inconvenient. The sūrah that begins with his exclusion ends with his joy, and in doing so it installs a permanent consolation for every person who has been deferred, interrupted, or overlooked in the business of religion.

**Modern Application: Joy With a Reason**

Modern culture produces a great deal of laughter and very little *istibshār*. The distinction is worth naming: amusement is a reaction to stimulation, and it ends when the stimulation stops; rejoicing is a response to news, and it lasts as long as the news is true. The believer's task, on the reading of verse 39, is to cultivate the second — to live as a person who has received a promise and is waiting for its fulfillment — rather than to accumulate the first. The practical instruments are already prescribed in the tradition: the recitation of the Qur'an, which is itself a book of glad tidings (*"a guidance and glad tidings for the believers"* — 27:2); the remembrance of God, by which hearts find rest (13:28); gratitude, which increases the blessing (14:7); and patience in adversity, which the Qur'an promises will be repaid with the salutation of the angels: *"Peace be upon you for what you were patient — how excellent is the final abode"* (13:24). Verse 39 is the end of that process, and the sūrah places it at the summit so that the reader will not mistake the road for the destination.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:40

> **And faces that Day shall be covered with dust,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Category, and the Conjunction That Introduces It**

*Wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabarah* — "and faces that Day shall be covered with dust." The verse opens with *wa-* and then repeats the structure of verse 38 exactly: *wujūhun yawmaʾidhin* — "faces that Day" — followed by a description. The repetition is the Qur'an's standard device for the eschatological diptych, and it appears in the neighboring sūrahs in nearly identical form:

> *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, looking upon their Lord; and faces that Day shall be gloomy, thinking that a calamity will be done to them"* (75:22–25).

> *"On the Day when faces will be whitened and faces will be blackened"* (3:106).

> *"Faces that Day shall be humbled, laboring, weary, entering a blazing fire"* (88:2–4).

> *"And faces that Day shall be downcast, upon them is dejection; those are the disbelievers, the profligates"* — cf. 75:24–25.

The symmetry is not decorative. It expresses the Qur'an's most fundamental claim about the human future: that the species divides into exactly two outcomes, and that the division will be visible on the face. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the Qur'an consistently describes the Day through faces rather than through bodies, because the face is where a person's identity is displayed to others — the same reason that verse 38's radiance is a public fact rather than a private feeling.

**Ghabarah: The Dust of Humiliation**

*Ghabarah* is from the root *gh-b-r*, which carries two related meanings in classical Arabic: dust, and the past that has gone by (*ghabara* — it passed; *al-ghābir* — what has gone). The primary sense here is dust, and the commentators are unanimous that the dust signifies humiliation and degradation. Al-Ṭabarī explains it as the dust that covers the face — the mark of a person who has been brought low, as a defeated man's face goes into the ground. Ibn Kathīr states that it indicates "complete and utter humiliation." Al-Qurṭubī adds the observation that the dust is not accidental (as dust from a journey might be) but is a covering laid upon the face, indicated by the preposition *ʿalayhā* — "upon it."

The Qur'an uses the image of dust on faces elsewhere for the same purpose:

> *"And faces that Day shall be covered with dust, overspread with darkness"* (80:40–41) — the pair read together as one description of the same faces.

> *"And when one of them is given the glad tidings of a female, his face becomes darkened (muswaddan) and he is filled with inward grief"* (16:58) — the same darkening of the face, produced in this world by the refusal of a gift.

The physical logic is recognizable to anyone who has watched a human face in shame: it falls, it darkens, it cannot be held up. Verse 40 makes that falling permanent.

**The Contrast With the Frown of Verse 1**

There is a structural echo in this verse that deserves attention. The sūrah opened with a face contracting in displeasure — *ʿabasa*, the brow furrowing — and it closes with faces covered in dust. The first face belonged to the best of creation and its contraction lasted a moment, and it was recorded forever in a book of mercy. The second faces belong to those who rejected that mercy, and their condition will last as long as the state lasts. Al-Rāzī draws the comparison in his commentary on the sūrah's structure: the Qur'an takes a single human expression seriously enough to make it the subject of a chapter, and then shows the reader what an expression looks like when it can never be changed.

The lesson is a moral one about faces. Every face in this world has the power to welcome or to dismiss, and the sūrah's whole argument is that this power is accountable. The face that dismisses here becomes a face covered with dust there; the face that welcomes — even awkwardly, even late, even after a frown has been corrected by revelation — becomes radiant and laughing. The Qur'an has made the treatment of faces its subject from the first word to the last.

**The Social Meaning of Dust**

In the Arabian world of the revelation, dust on the face had a specific social meaning: it was the visible sign of defeat, of mourning, and of supplication. The defeated went into captivity with dust upon them; the mourner cast dust on his head; the supplicant put his face in the ground. Islamic worship preserved the last of these in the most reverent act of the prayer — the prostration, in which the believer places his face, the highest part of his body, on the ground before God. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"The nearest a servant is to his Lord is when he is in prostration"* (Muslim 482, from Abū Hurayrah). The distinction between the two dusts is therefore decisive: the face that puts itself in the dust before God voluntarily will not be given the dust of verse 40 involuntarily. The Islamic tradition states this in the well-known formulation: whoever humbles himself before God, God raises him; and whoever is too proud to put his face on the ground here will be put into the ground there.

**Modern Application: The Faces We Cannot Hide**

Verse 40 speaks to a condition that modern technology has made universal: the visibility of the inner state. Faces are now recorded continuously — on cameras, in photographs, in video calls, in the archives of every platform — and the record of a face is permanent in a way that no generation before has experienced. Verse 40 announces a permanence of a different order, in which the face will display not what was recorded but what was true. The practical instruction for the believer is the one the tradition has always given: work on the interior, since the exterior will eventually be compelled to match it. The good deed brightens the face; the evil deed darkens it — slowly, invisibly, and then, on that Day, publicly.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:41

> **overspread with darkness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tarhaquhā: The Darkness That Comes Upon**

*Tarhaquhā qatarah* — "overspread with darkness." The verb *tarhaqu* is from *r-h-q*, meaning to overtake, to come upon, to cover by force — the same root as *rahīq*, the sealed wine of Paradise in 83:25, and as the description of the Day as *al-rāhiq* in contexts of overwhelming arrival. The form indicates that the darkness is not merely present but *arrives upon* the face and settles over it. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *qatarah* as a darkness combined with dust — the blackness that gathers on a face — and Ibn Kathīr states that it is a darkness and gloom that overcomes the face from the intensity of the fear and the grief in the heart. Al-Qurṭubī records the view that *qatarah* is the blackness of soot or of smoke, and the exegetes connect it to the Arabic *qatara*, which denotes both the dust-dark color and, in a related sense, narrowness and stinginess (*taqṭīr*, *qatr*).

**Why Dust and Darkness Together**

Verses 40 and 41 describe the same faces twice, and the two descriptions are not redundant. The dust is humiliation — the social dimension of disgrace, what others see. The darkness is grief and terror — the interior dimension, what the person feels. Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabarī (as the source commentary notes) connect the two descriptions to the two charges of verse 42: the punishment for disbelief is the dust-covered face, and the punishment for profligacy is the face overspread with darkness. The pairing thus covers both the outer and the inner consequence of a rejected life.

The Qur'an uses the same double description in the parallel passage of Sūrah al-Qiyāmah:

> *"And faces that Day shall be gloomy (bāsirah), thinking that a calamity will be done to them (fāqirah)"* (75:24–25).

*Bāsirah* here means darkened and scowling — the commentators explain it as the opposite of *nāḍirah* (radiant) in verse 22 — and *fāqirah* is the calamity that breaks the back. The structure is identical to 80:40–41: the darkened face and the disaster it anticipates.

**Darkness as the Qur'an's Master Image for Unbelief**

The pairing of light and darkness is the Qur'an's fundamental symbolic structure, and it is applied with absolute consistency: faith, guidance, revelation, and Paradise are light; disbelief, misguidance, and the Fire are darkness. The names of God include *al-Nūr*, and the Qur'an calls itself light:

> *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth"* (24:35).

> *"A Book which We have sent down to thee, that thou mayst bring forth mankind from the darknesses into the light, by the leave of their Lord"* (14:1).

> *"And We made it a light whereby We guide whom We will of Our servants"* (42:52).

> *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light. And those who disbelieve — their patrons are the idols; they bring them out of the light into the darknesses"* (2:257).

Note the plural in the original: *ẓulumāt*, darknesses. Light in the Qur'an is one and darkness is many, because there is only one truth and there are countless ways of missing it. The darkness that overspreads the faces of verse 41 is the accumulation of a life spent in those plural darknesses — and the Qur'an's diagnosis of unbelief is not that it is a different light but that it is the absence of light.

**The Face That Refused to Shine**

The connection between verse 41 and the sūrah's opening panel is now complete. The man of verse 5 deemed himself beyond need; the man of verse 6 was attended to; and neither asked for light. Verse 41 states the outcome: the face that would not receive the Reminder becomes the face from which light is withheld. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of the spiritual consequence of refusal, stated elsewhere with legal precision:

> *"And when they deviated, God made their hearts deviate"* (61:5).

> *"Nay! But that which they used to earn has covered their hearts (rāna ʿalā qulūbihim)"* (83:14).

> *"And We have placed upon their hearts coverings, lest they understand it, and in their ears a deafness"* (6:25; 17:46; 18:57).

The covering is not arbitrary; it is the confirmation of a choice already made. Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim both develop the point extensively: God's "sealing" of a heart is the fulfillment of that heart's own repeated refusal, and the punishment of darkness is the natural end of a life lived without light. Verse 41 is therefore the visible form of an invisible process that began long before the Day.

**Modern Application: The Darkened Countenance**

The athar attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and cited at verse 38 has its second half here: the evil deed gives darkness to the face, darkness to the heart, weakness in the body, deficiency in provision, and hatred in the hearts of creation. Everyone has met such a face — not literally blackened, but closed, hardened, unresponsive, unable to receive joy or to give warmth. The Islamic tradition holds that this is not merely a metaphor but a real effect of persistent wrongdoing on the countenance, and that it is reversible by repentance, since *"God replaces the evil deeds with good"* (25:70) for those who return. Verse 41 describes the state at the point of no return. The reader who recognizes the beginning of it in his own face — in the hardness that comes from habituated lying, envy, or cruelty — has, by that recognition, been given the opportunity the verse is warning him not to waste.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:42

> **Those, they are the disbelievers, the profligates.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Naming at the End**

*Ulāʾika humu al-kafaratu al-fajarah* — "those, they are the disbelievers, the profligates." The sūrah ends by naming the people whose faces were described in verses 40–41, and the naming follows the Qur'an's habitual pattern of identification through the demonstrative *ulāʾika* ("those are") followed by the emphatic pronoun *hum* ("they"): a construction of specification and confirmation. The same formula closes the parallel passage in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah and appears throughout the Qur'an in verdicts both of condemnation and of praise: *"Those are they whom God has guided"* (6:90); *"Those are the truthful, and those are the reverent"* (2:177); *"Those are upon guidance from their Lord, and those are the successful"* (2:5).

**Al-Kafarah: The Plural of Covering**

*Al-kafarah* is the plural of *kāfir*, from the root *k-f-r* discussed at verse 17: to cover, to conceal, to bury — and hence to disbelieve (covering the truth) and to be ungrateful (covering the favor). The plural form *kafarah* is a broken plural of the type that indicates a category or class rather than a mere count. The source commentary records the reading of al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabarī that the two descriptions — *al-kafarah* and *al-fajarah* — refer to the same group from two angles: disbelief defines their thoughts (*iʿtiqād*), and profligacy defines their actions (*aʿmāl*). The pairing is thus not two kinds of people but one kind of person described in both dimensions: what he believed and what he did.

This dual description answers a question the Qur'an raises repeatedly: is unbelief only a matter of ideas? The answer given here is no. The *kāfir* of verse 42 is also a *fājir* — his error of belief issued in error of conduct, and the two are named together as a single indictment. The Qur'an's consistent position is that belief and action are inseparable: *"those who believe and do righteous deeds"* occurs as a formula more than fifty times, and its opposite — disbelief and corruption — is treated with the same consistency.

**Al-Fajarah: The Root of Breaking Through**

*Al-fajarah* is the plural of *fājir*, from *f-j-r*, whose root sense is to break open, to split, to burst forth. The same root gives *fajr*, the dawn — the breaking of light through the darkness, as in Sūrah al-Fajr (89:1) and in the phrase *"and the Qur'an of the dawn"* (17:78). A *fājir* is therefore one who breaks through the bounds — who bursts the limits set by God, as water bursts a dam. Al-Qurṭubī explains *fujur* as the going beyond the limit in sin, and Ibn Kathīr glosses *al-fajarah* as those who were disobedient in their deeds, transgressors against God's commands.

The root's two products — dawn and profligacy — are the same image in opposite directions: a breaking through that brings light, and a breaking through that destroys the container. The Qur'an uses both:

> *"Nay! Man desires to break through what is before him (li-yafjura amāmah), asking: When is the Day of Resurrection?"* (75:5–6).

> *"Indeed the wicked (al-fujjār) are in Hellfire, and indeed the righteous (al-abrār) are in bliss"* (82:13–14).

> *"And indeed the Book of the wicked is in Sijjīn... and indeed the Book of the righteous is in ʿIlliyyīn"* (83:7–18).

Note the echo with verse 16 of this sūrah: the angels who carry the pages are *bararah*, the plural of *barr* — the righteous, the dutiful — from the same root as *abrār*. The sūrah's final verse names the profligates (*fajarah*), and its sixteenth verse named the dutiful (*bararah*); the two plurals are the two human destinations, and both are described in terms of the pages they either carry or refuse.

**The Punishment Matched to the Charge**

Al-Ālūsī, as the source commentary records, draws a precise correspondence: the punishment for disbelief is the face covered with dust (v. 40), and the punishment for profligacy is the face overspread with darkness (v. 41). The logic is one of proportion: disbelief is a covering of the truth, and its recompense is the covering of the face with humiliation; profligacy is a breaking of the bounds in pursuit of pleasure, and its recompense is the darkness that comes when the pleasure is gone and the account is opened. The Qur'an states the principle of proportionality in a verse that functions as the rule of the entire Hereafter:

> *"And whoever comes with a good deed will have ten times its like, and whoever comes with an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like, and they will not be wronged"* (6:160).

**The Sūrah's Closing Circle**

Verse 42 closes the circle that verse 5 opened. The man who "deemed himself beyond need" is now named: he is of *al-kafarah al-fajarah*, and his face is dusty and dark. The man who "came striving while fearful" (vv. 8–9) is named in the other panel: his face is radiant, laughing, rejoicing (vv. 38–39). The sūrah's two characters have been carried from a room in Makkah to the Day of the Piercing Cry, and the verdict is given in ten verses without commentary, because the faces do the commenting.

The whole chapter thus resolves into a single argument, and it is worth stating in one line: the allocation of attention in this world determines nothing, and reveals everything. The blind man received no attention and received Paradise; the notables received all the attention and received dust. What mattered was not who was attended to but who came seeking, who feared, who covered, and who broke through the bounds. Sūrah ʿAbasa was revealed to correct a frown, and it ends by describing the two kinds of faces that will exist forever — one of them shining because it sought God in a room where it did not belong, the other darkened because it belonged to the room and sought nothing else.

**The Last Word of the Sūrah**

It is fitting that the final word is *al-fajarah* — the ones who broke through — because the sūrah's whole purpose was to call human beings back inside a boundary: the boundary between attention and dismissal, between gratitude and ingratitude, between the reminder offered and the reminder refused. The word *fājir* describes a person who will not stay inside a limit, and the sūrah ends by naming him, so that the reader may ask the only question that matters: which of the two plurals is mine? *Kirāman bararah*, the noble and the dutiful, whose faces will be radiant — or *al-kafarah al-fajarah*, the disbelievers and the profligates, whose faces will be dusty and dark. The forty-two verses exist to make that question answerable, and the answer, in the Qur'an's own terms, is available to whoever wills: *"so let whomsoever will, remember it"* (80:12).
