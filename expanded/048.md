# Sūrah al-Fatḥ (Chapter 48) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A victory whose shape was not immediately visible**

Sūrah al-Fatḥ, “The Opening” or “The Decisive Victory,” is a Madinan sūrah of twenty-nine verses. It was revealed as the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his Companions returned from al-Ḥudaybiyah in the sixth year after the Hijrah. They had left Madinah intending the lesser pilgrimage, wearing the pilgrim garb and bringing sacrificial animals. They did not leave as a raiding force. Yet Quraysh prevented them from entering Makkah and, after difficult negotiations, a treaty was made. Its terms looked painful to a number of Companions: the pilgrimage would be postponed; the Prophet ﷺ would return that year; and a clause concerning persons who left Makkah appeared, on its face, to favor Quraysh.

The sūrah asks the reader to re-evaluate that moment by God’s measure rather than by the first emotional impression. A victory can be a battlefield conquest, but it can also be the opening of a road that conflict had closed: recognition of a community, security for travel, space for the message to be heard, restraint of bloodshed, and the exposure of which hearts are sincere. Al-Ḥudaybiyah brought all of these. The truce allowed people from different tribes to meet Muslims without the pressure of war, and many entered Islam in the following period. It also established the Muslims as a party with whom Quraysh had to negotiate. What seemed like a concession was an opening.

The Prophet ﷺ himself taught the community how to receive this revelation. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reported that the Prophet ﷺ said after its descent:

> *“Tonight a sūrah has been revealed to me which is dearer to me than all that the sun rises upon.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

The statement is a corrective to narrow calculations. Land, wealth, and immediate public approval are not the only measures of gain. Revelation that clarifies God’s pleasure and gives a community its moral direction is more precious than all visible possessions.

**The architecture of a sūrah about trust**

The sūrah moves in a deliberate sequence. It opens by calling the treaty a clear victory and connects that victory with forgiveness, completion of blessing, guidance, and divine aid (48:1–3). It explains that tranquillity descends into believing hearts so that their faith grows, then contrasts their promised Garden with the punishment prepared for hypocrisy and idolatry (48:4–7). The Messenger’s role is defined as witness, bearer of glad tidings, and warner; the pledge given to him at al-Ḥudaybiyah is then given its immense theological weight (48:8–10).

The middle of the sūrah examines the excuses of those Bedouin tribes who stayed behind, distinguishes real incapacity from opportunistic reluctance, and honors the Companions who pledged beneath the tree (48:11–19). The final movement explains why fighting was restrained near Makkah, exposes the destructive zealotry of jāhiliyyah, assures the believers that the Prophet’s vision of pilgrimage will be fulfilled, and concludes with a portrait of the Prophet ﷺ and the community formed around him (48:20–29). Thus politics, worship, character, divine decree, and the Hereafter are never treated as separate subjects. The treaty is read as an event in history and as a school for the soul.

**Fatḥ: opening before conquest**

The title *al-Fatḥ* comes from the root *f-t-ḥ*, “to open.” The same root can describe opening a locked door, opening a difficult case by judgment, opening provision, and granting military victory. Arabic therefore allows the sūrah to hold several meanings together without confusion. The treaty opened a blocked route to the Sacred Mosque; it opened diplomatic recognition; it opened the way to Khaybar and, later, Makkah; and it opened hearts to Islam. The Qur’an uses the root in a related prayer of Moses:

> *“Our Lord, decide between us and our people in truth; and You are the best of those who decide.”* (Qur’an 7:89)

The word rendered “decide” in that context is from the same family. A divine *fatḥ* is not merely a human triumph; it is God’s opening of what is just, true, and beneficial after an apparent closure.

This matters in ordinary life. A person may mistake a delayed job, a failed proposal, a postponed journey, or a refused request for simple defeat. Sūrah al-Fatḥ does not teach that every disappointment is secretly pleasant, nor does it excuse passivity. It teaches something more disciplined: the believer does not possess enough knowledge to pronounce final judgment on an unfolding event. One should plan carefully, act lawfully, consult wisely, and then leave the unseen consequences to the One who sees the entire road.

**The moral contrast at the end**

The last verses place two interior conditions side by side. The opponents are described as carrying *ḥamiyyat al-jāhiliyyah*: the heated, proud zealotry of the Age of Ignorance. The believers receive *sakīnah*: settled tranquillity from God. One condition turns humiliation or disagreement into rage; the other enables a person to remain principled without becoming reckless. This contrast makes the sūrah a lasting guide for families, communities, leadership, and public conflict. Strength is not measured by the loudness of reaction. At al-Ḥudaybiyah, strength was the ability to accept a difficult settlement without abandoning truth or losing dignity.

What follows treats every verse as its own complete unit while reading it within this larger design. The commentary draws on the source text, Qur’anic cross-references, established Prophetic reports, and the major lines of the classical tafsīr tradition. Its recurring question is the sūrah’s own question: when the way seems closed, what would it mean to trust the God who opens?

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:1

> **Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A verdict that overturned first impressions**

The first word, *innā* — “indeed We” — comes with emphasis. It is not a tentative forecast but a divine verdict pronounced over a situation many believers experienced as a setback. *Fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā* literally conveys, “We have opened for you an opening made clear.” The perfect tense presents the victory as granted and settled even while its wider consequences were still unfolding. God names the event before history has made the name obvious to everyone.

Most reports from Companions and the dominant explanations of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī connect this verse first with the Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyah. Some exegetes also include the later conquest of Makkah, and others include every opening that followed it. These views need not compete. Al-Ḥudaybiyah was the near opening that made later openings possible. A seed may not look like a harvest, but it is truly part of the harvest’s beginning.

Al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib made the point in a report preserved by al-Bukhārī:

> *“You count the conquest of Makkah as the victory, and it was a victory. But we used to count the pledge of al-Riḍwān on the day of al-Ḥudaybiyah as the victory.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

The Companions who had lived through the tension understood that “victory” was larger than entering a city by force. They had watched a vulnerable community meet the leading power of Arabia as an equal negotiating party. More importantly, they had learned to follow the Prophet ﷺ when the wisdom of his decision was not yet apparent.

**The linguistic breadth of fatḥ**

The root *f-t-ḥ* gives the verse unusual depth. A door is *maftūḥ* when it is opened. A judge “opens” a case by issuing a decisive ruling. Provision can be “opened” to a servant. And a victory in war is a *fatḥ* because it opens what hostile power had blocked. Each shade is relevant here. Quraysh had blocked the pilgrims’ entrance to the Sacred Mosque; the agreement opened the possibility of return the following year. Hostility had kept tribes apart; the truce opened contact. Misunderstanding had pressed on the Companions’ hearts; revelation opened the event’s real meaning.

The Qur’an identifies God as the ultimate opener and judge:

> *“Say, ‘Our Lord will gather us together, then He will judge between us in truth. And He is the Opener, the Knowing.’”* (Qur’an 34:26)

Calling God *al-Fattāḥ* means that no human obstruction is final before Him. It does not mean every door a person desires will open. It means that the doors which truly lead to faith, justice, provision, repentance, and the fulfillment of His promise remain under His command, not under the command of rivals.

**Victory without the intoxication of triumph**

This verse also corrects a destructive habit: defining success only by immediate domination of an opponent. The Muslims did not abandon their religion at al-Ḥudaybiyah; they did not deny the sanctity of the Kaʿbah; they accepted a temporary arrangement that protected the larger mission. Their restraint was not surrender of principle. It was principled patience under prophetic leadership.

The Qur’an later connects the treaty to the growth of faith and the peaceful entry into Makkah. This makes the verse a model for judging difficult decisions: ask not only, “What did we gain today?” but also, “What truth did we preserve? What harm did we prevent? What possibility for lasting good did we open?” A surgeon’s incision may look like injury if seen without knowledge of the illness and the cure. In the same way, an unpleasant concession may be part of healing when it preserves life and makes recovery possible.

**A personal reading of the clear opening**

The verse should not be turned into a promise that every individual plan must succeed in the form imagined. Rather, it trains a believer to distinguish between an immediate preference and a clear good known to God. A lost opportunity can protect one from a worse attachment. A period of restraint can preserve a relationship from words that cannot be recalled. A delayed accomplishment can deepen competence and sincerity. The lesson is not to call pain painless; it is to refuse to call God absent merely because the wisdom of His opening has not yet become visible.

The Qur’an gives the broader principle elsewhere:

> *“Perhaps you dislike a thing while it is good for you, and perhaps you love a thing while it is bad for you. God knows, and you do not know.”* (Qur’an 2:216)

At al-Ḥudaybiyah, the community was asked to live inside that sentence. Verse 1 announces that they had done so not in vain: the door they thought had closed was, by God’s knowledge, the beginning of a manifest opening.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:2

> **So that God may forgive you what came before of your sin and what will come after, complete His favor upon you, and guide you to a straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Victory joined to spiritual gifts**

This verse explains the purposes bound to the opening of verse 1. The first purpose named is forgiveness, then completion of blessing, then guidance. The order is revealing. A political event is not treated as an independent achievement of human strategy; it is placed inside God’s care for His Messenger ﷺ. The greatest gifts are not territory or reputation, but pardon, divine favor, and steadfast direction.

The expression *li-yaghfira laka* does not mean that God needed victory in order to learn whether to forgive. The Arabic *lām* can express a consequence, result, or wisdom attached to an event. Classical commentators explored how the verse relates to the Prophet’s protected rank. Sunni scholarship affirms the Prophet’s *ʿiṣmah*: God protected him from betrayal of revelation and from persistence in sin. When the Qur’an mentions his seeking forgiveness or receives a gentle correction, it teaches his community the perfection of humble servanthood; it does not reduce his trustworthiness as God’s Messenger.

Ibn Kathīr and others cite the Prophet’s own practice after this promise. ʿĀʾishah asked why he prayed at night until his feet became swollen when God had forgiven him. He replied:

> *“Should I not be a grateful servant?”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This response gives the verse its lived interpretation. Forgiveness does not create complacency. The one most assured of God’s favor was also among the most humble, worshipful, and grateful of people.

**Past and future: completeness rather than negligence**

The phrase *mā taqaddama min dhanbika wa mā taʾakhkhara* — “what came before of your sin and what will come after” — is an Arabic expression of comprehensiveness. Some early exegetes linked “before” and “after” to the period before and after al-Ḥudaybiyah; others understood it as a total declaration of forgiveness. The essential meaning is that the Prophet ﷺ stands under extraordinary divine care, not that he is licensed to disregard obedience.

The verse instructs every believer indirectly. Forgiveness in Islam is neither denial of wrongdoing nor despair over it. It is a return to God that acknowledges the fault, repairs what can be repaired, and trusts the mercy of the One who accepts repentance. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“By God, I seek God’s forgiveness and repent to Him more than seventy times a day.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

If the Messenger ﷺ made repentance a regular practice, it cannot be a sign of spiritual failure for ordinary believers. It is spiritual realism. A person who never asks forgiveness may be protecting an image of self-sufficiency rather than displaying innocence.

**The completion of divine favor**

*Wa yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalayka* — “and complete His favor upon you” — joins the verse to the Qur’an’s broader language of divine completion. The blessing includes prophethood, the preservation of the message, the strengthening of the Muslim community, the eventual peaceful entry into Makkah, and the fulfillment of the mission. Some commentators also include the ultimate favor of Paradise and the highest honor granted to the Messenger ﷺ in the Hereafter.

The Qur’an uses related language when announcing the perfection of the religion:

> *“Today I have perfected for you your religion, completed My favor upon you, and chosen for you Islam as your religion.”* (Qur’an 5:3)

Completion does not mean that a believer’s daily life becomes free from trial. It means that God has completed the guidance necessary to travel through trial. A map can be complete even when the journey contains steep roads. The favor of God is above all the giving of a path that does not deceive its traveler.

**Guidance after victory**

The verse ends by naming *ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā*, a straight path. This is significant because success can become a test more dangerous than hardship. Defeat often exposes dependence; victory can tempt people into pride, cruelty, rivalry, or the illusion that human skill alone secured the result. The guidance requested and granted here protects the Prophet ﷺ and his community from misusing what God opens.

A straight road is one that leads reliably to its destination without moral detours. In modern terms, a person may gain professional influence, public attention, wealth, or a disputed victory, yet lose the straight path if that gain produces arrogance or injustice. The Qur’an’s sequence insists that real victory culminates in guidance, not merely in being seen as victorious. The following verse completes the promise by naming the source of all assistance: God’s mighty help.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:3

> **And that God may help you with a mighty help.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Help that belongs to the Mighty**

The verse is short, but its brevity gives it force: *wa yanṣuraka Allāhu naṣran ʿazīzā*. The help is explicitly God’s, and it is called *ʿazīz* — mighty, invincible, precious, and difficult to overcome. The root *ʿ-z-z* carries strength and dignity. When God grants *naṣr*, it is not a fragile advantage dependent on the moods of allies or the calculations of enemies.

The verse does not deny human causes. The Prophet ﷺ planned, consulted, negotiated, travelled, endured risk, and led. The Companions pledged their lives and bore disappointment without abandoning their allegiance. Yet the Qur’an teaches that causes do not rule independently of their Creator. The same people, numbers, weapons, treaties, and opportunities can produce different results according to God’s wisdom. The Qur’an states the principle plainly:

> *“Victory is only from God, the Mighty, the Wise.”* (Qur’an 3:126)

Calling God “Mighty” rules out helplessness; calling Him “Wise” rules out the idea that power is arbitrary. His aid is strong and purposeful.

**Aid may arrive as protection from a battle**

Readers sometimes imagine divine help only in dramatic rescue or military success. Al-Ḥudaybiyah itself corrects that imagination. God’s help appeared partly in the prevention of a battle. Makkah contained believers whose identities were not known to the Muslims. A violent entry could have killed them. It could have violated the sacred precinct and given Quraysh a grievance with which to inflame Arabia. The treaty prevented a wider harm while opening the road to a more enduring victory.

This is one of the sūrah’s most important moral lessons: not every avoided conflict is cowardice, and not every escalation is courage. A powerful person may show greater strength by refusing the momentary satisfaction of retaliation when restraint serves justice and preserves innocent life. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“The strong person is not the one who overcomes people by his strength, but the strong person is the one who controls himself while angry.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The hadith is not a call to accept oppression passively. It identifies self-command as a higher form of strength. Sūrah al-Fatḥ shows that such command can shape history.

**Aiding the Messenger means aiding the message**

God’s help to His Messenger ﷺ also took the form of revelation, the loyalty of sincere hearts, the spread of the message, and the eventual completion of his mission. The Prophet is not helped merely as a private individual. He is helped as the bearer of a trust for humanity. The verse therefore turns the believers away from personality cult and toward the message he carried. To love and support the Messenger ﷺ is to believe him, obey what God revealed through him, preserve his Sunnah, and embody the mercy and justice with which he was sent.

The Qur’an gives his mission its universal frame:

> *“And We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds.”* (Qur’an 21:107)

Any claim to defend the Prophet ﷺ that abandons justice, truthfulness, or mercy contradicts the purpose for which he was aided. The “mighty help” of this verse is not permission for believers to make religion an instrument of their own resentment; it is God’s support for the truthful mission of His Messenger.

**Confidence without self-reliance**

For a believer facing pressure, the verse provides confidence without ego. There is a difference between saying, “I will succeed because I am enough,” and saying, “I will use every lawful means available and depend on God for what I cannot control.” The second posture does not weaken effort. It frees effort from panic, because the servant’s duty is faithfulness, while the final result belongs to God.

The first three verses thus form a unified opening: a clear victory, forgiveness and completed favor, and mighty help. The following verse explains one of the most intimate forms that help takes — not necessarily an external change, but *sakīnah* placed inside believing hearts.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:4

> **He is the One who sent down tranquillity into the hearts of the believers so that they might increase in faith along with their faith. To God belong the forces of the heavens and the earth, and God is All-Knowing, All-Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sakīnah in a moment of pressure**

The word *sakīnah* comes from the root *s-k-n*, which conveys stillness, dwelling, and settling after movement. It is not mere relaxation or the absence of emotion. In the Qur’an, *sakīnah* is a divinely given composure that lets a believer remain clear-minded, faithful, and morally steady when fear, grief, anger, or uncertainty could otherwise rule the heart.

At al-Ḥudaybiyah, the believers had reasons for intense feeling. They had come longing to worship at the Kaʿbah. They were stopped outside Makkah. Rumors spread that ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān had been killed. The treaty’s terms were hard to hear. In such a setting, tranquillity was not natural indifference. It was divine help that prevented disappointment from becoming rebellion against the Prophet ﷺ or distrust of God.

The Qur’an describes a similar gift elsewhere:

> *“Then God sent down His tranquillity upon His Messenger and upon the believers, and sent down forces you did not see.”* (Qur’an 9:26)

This parallel shows that inward steadiness is itself part of God’s unseen forces. A calm heart at the right moment can prevent an injustice, preserve a covenant, and protect a whole community from choices made in anger.

**Faith can increase**

The clause *li-yazdādū īmānan maʿa īmānihim* — “so that they might increase in faith along with their faith” — is a major Qur’anic proof that faith increases. The early Sunni scholars, including those represented by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, explained that faith includes conviction in the heart, speech of the tongue, and deeds of the limbs; it grows through knowledge, obedience, reflection, and tested patience, and it weakens through heedlessness and sin.

The Qur’an repeatedly speaks this way:

> *“When His verses are recited to them, they increase them in faith, and upon their Lord they rely.”* (Qur’an 8:2)

> *“As for those who believe, it increases them in faith, and they rejoice.”* (Qur’an 9:124)

The point is psychologically exact. Faith is not a static label acquired once and then left untouched. Trust becomes deeper when a believer sees a divine promise prove true, obeys during difficulty, learns more of revelation, or chooses patience over a harmful impulse. Al-Ḥudaybiyah became a classroom in which obedience during uncertainty increased the believers’ certainty after the wisdom became clear.

**The hosts of heaven and earth**

The verse then shifts from the heart to the cosmos: “To God belong the forces of the heavens and the earth.” *Junūd* means armies, hosts, or forces at God’s disposal. The phrase does not require that every divine aid be visibly military. Angels are among God’s hosts, but so are weather, circumstance, time, conscience, the loyalty of truthful people, the failure of an unjust plan, and the peace He causes to descend into a heart. God did not need the believers to win by the means they expected. He owns means they do not see.

The end of the verse gives two Names that interpret the entire event: *ʿAlīman Ḥakīman*, “All-Knowing, All-Wise.” God knew the hidden believers in Makkah, the fragile state of the new Muslim community, the future consequences of the truce, and the real intentions of every party. His wisdom placed the opening in a treaty rather than in the confrontation some people desired.

**Practicing tranquillity rather than performing it**

Modern people are often told to “stay calm,” sometimes in ways that dismiss real pain or injustice. Qur’anic *sakīnah* is different. It does not ask a wronged person to deny the wrong, nor does it require silence in the face of evil. It gives enough inner steadiness to tell the truth accurately, choose the right means, and refuse to let rage take possession of the conscience.

Practical paths toward this tranquillity include prayer, Qur’an recitation, truthful counsel, avoiding impulsive speech, and remembering the limits of one’s knowledge. When an alarming message appears, a believer can pause before forwarding it. When a family argument begins, one can delay the reply that would turn hurt into lasting injury. This is not weakness; it is a small form of the *sakīnah* that preserved the believers at al-Ḥudaybiyah. Verse 5 now names the final fruit of faith strengthened in this way: entrance into the Gardens and the removal of evil deeds.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:5

> **So that He may admit believing men and believing women into gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding there forever, and remove from them their evil deeds. That, in the sight of God, is a great triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Men and women named together before God**

The verse explicitly names *al-muʾminīna wa al-muʾmināt*: believing men and believing women. This wording is a deliberate Qur’anic pattern. The ultimate promise is not confined to male participants in public events, nor are women treated as a background detail in the community of faith. Both are addressed as moral agents, bearers of faith, recipients of tranquillity, and heirs of Paradise.

The Qur’an states the same principle with complete clarity:

> *“Whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, while being a believer, those will enter Paradise and will not be wronged even as much as the speck on a date stone.”* (Qur’an 4:124)

At al-Ḥudaybiyah, women were part of the believing community whose homes, families, security, and spiritual future were bound to the treaty. In the later verses of this sūrah, the presence of unknown believing men and women in Makkah becomes one reason fighting was withheld. Their inclusion in verse 5 prepares the reader to see them as fully significant before God.

**Gardens with rivers: permanence after instability**

The Qur’anic image *jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* evokes gardens beneath which rivers flow. For the first hearers of the Qur’an, a garden was not an abstract luxury. In a dry land it signified shade, water, food, safety, and a settled home. “Rivers flowing beneath” intensifies the image: the garden’s life is continuously supplied, not dependent on a temporary rain or a precarious human system.

The verse adds *khālidīna fīhā* — “abiding there forever.” The promise responds to a deep human insecurity. Every worldly good is exposed to loss: health changes, homes are left, loved ones die, governments fall, and even a peaceful season eventually ends. Paradise is described as lasting precisely because no good news can fully settle the human heart if it is always threatened by disappearance. The believer’s hope is not simply for a better temporary condition; it is for a final home where loss has no authority.

**Removal before entry**

The verse links entry to the Gardens with *yukaffira ʿanhum sayyiʾātihim*: God removes or covers their evil deeds. The verb belongs to the root *k-f-r*, which here carries the sense of covering over, erasing, or wiping away. The same root used negatively for covering truth is used positively for God covering a servant’s faults through mercy and repentance. This linguistic contrast is meaningful: the believer turns away from concealing truth and turns toward the God who conceals forgiven sin from disgrace.

The promise does not make good deeds a purchase price by which creatures compel God. It joins faith, repentance, and righteous effort to His mercy. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone.” They said, “Not even you, Messenger of God?” He said, “Not even me, unless God envelops me in mercy from Him.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Deeds are essential because they manifest sincere faith and train the soul; yet Paradise remains, at its deepest level, a gift of God’s mercy.

**The great triumph redefined**

The final phrase, *fawzan ʿaẓīmā* — “a great triumph” — deliberately redefines success. The believers were returning from a journey in which they had not entered Makkah or completed the rites they intended. God tells them that the truly great triumph is not exhausted by a completed pilgrimage, a treaty, or material spoils. It is forgiveness and permanent nearness to Him in Paradise.

This does not belittle worldly concerns. Islam commands believers to seek justice, protect the vulnerable, honor agreements, and work responsibly. It does, however, place every worldly result under a final horizon. A person may lose a visible contest while winning integrity, forgiveness, and eternal reward. Conversely, a person may gain public status while losing the soul. The following verse presents the reverse outcome for those who meet divine events with suspicion, hypocrisy, and idolatry.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:6

> **And so that He may punish the hypocritical men and hypocritical women, and the idolatrous men and idolatrous women, who harbor an evil thought about God. Upon them is the turn of evil. God has become angry with them, has cursed them, and prepared Hell for them. What an evil destination!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The thought about God that corrupts everything else**

The verse sets hypocrites and idolaters together because both carry a false estimate of God. They *yaẓunnūna bi-llāhi ẓanna al-sawʾ* — “harbor concerning God an evil thought.” In the immediate setting, some assumed that the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions would be destroyed at al-Ḥudaybiyah and never return to Madinah. Their prediction was not a neutral military analysis. It implied that God would abandon His Messenger, fail to fulfill His promise, or leave truth defenseless before falsehood.

The Qur’an exposes the same mentality in another account of fear and confusion:

> *“They were thinking of God other than the truth — the thought of ignorance — saying, ‘Do we have any part in the matter?’ Say, ‘Indeed, all matters belong to God.’”* (Qur’an 3:154)

An “evil thought” about God can take several forms: imagining that He is unjust, that He does not know, that His promise is empty, that repentance is useless, or that material power proves His approval. The verse is not condemning a believer who suffers passing questions, fear, or sadness. Human hearts are tested. It condemns the settled suspicion that makes a person judge revelation by short-term advantage and then turn away when obedience becomes costly.

**Hypocrisy and idolatry: two failures of allegiance**

The hypocrite outwardly joins the believers while inwardly placing safety and loyalty elsewhere. The idolater publicly directs worship or ultimate allegiance to partners besides God. Their forms differ, but both fracture the unity of trust owed to God. The hypocrite wants the social benefits of faith without surrendering the heart; the idolater distributes to created things the reliance and devotion that belong to the Creator alone.

The Qur’an’s inclusion of men and women again establishes individual accountability. A person is not excused by the sins of a group, tribe, spouse, or political camp. The same individual moral seriousness that supports the promise in verse 5 supports the warning in verse 6. God’s judgment is not based on gender or ancestry but on faith, intention, and action.

**The evil turn comes back upon its authors**

*ʿAlayhim dāʾirat al-sawʾ* may be rendered, “upon them is the evil turn of events.” They waited for misfortune to circle around the believers, but it circled back upon them. The phrase captures a recurring Qur’anic moral law: the person who makes another’s ruin his hope often becomes trapped in the very disposition that ruins him. Envy poisons peace before any external punishment arrives. Treachery destroys trust, including the treacherous person’s ability to live without fear of betrayal. Cynicism turns every sign of good into evidence for despair.

This is why the Qur’an says:

> *“The evil plot does not engulf except its own people.”* (Qur’an 35:43)

The verse should not be simplified into the claim that every wrongdoer is immediately defeated in this world. Some harms persist, and their victims need protection and justice. Rather, it teaches that evil contains the seed of its own moral reversal and that no appearance of temporary success removes a person from God’s final accounting.

**Anger, curse, and Hell in the language of justice**

The verse speaks of God’s anger, curse, and preparation of Hell. Classical Sunni scholars affirm what God affirmed of Himself without likening Him to creation. Divine anger is not a creaturely loss of control or an irrational temper. It is a real attribute fitting His majesty, inseparable from knowledge and justice. The Qur’an simultaneously negates all likeness between Creator and creature:

> *“There is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”* (Qur’an 42:11)

God’s curse means removal from His special mercy because of persistent chosen rebellion. Hell is called an evil destination because it is the end of a road built on false allegiance. The severity of this language protects a truth that sentimentality can obscure: betrayal, idolatry, and deliberate corruption of faith are not harmless private preferences. They damage souls, relationships, and societies, and they matter before the Lord of all worlds.

**Hope must not become suspicion, and fear must not become despair**

The warning has a practical balance. A believer should not become presumptuous during ease, assuming worldly comfort is proof that God approves of every choice. Nor should one become suspicious of God during hardship, assuming delay means abandonment. The right posture is to act, repent, seek counsel, and hold tightly to the trust that God is wise even when the full wisdom is hidden. Verse 7 immediately repeats that all the hosts of heaven and earth belong to Him, grounding both the promise to believers and the warning to those who distrust Him.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:7

> **To God belong the forces of the heavens and the earth, and God is Almighty, All-Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The repeated declaration gives the warning its ground**

This sentence repeats the central statement of verse 4: “To God belong the forces of the heavens and the earth.” There it followed the descent of tranquillity into believers’ hearts; here it follows the threat of punishment. Repetition connects mercy and judgment to the same source. God can support whom He wills and bring to account whom He wills because every power, seen and unseen, is already His possession.

The repetition also restrains human reactions. The believers are not told to believe that victory comes from their own numbers, weapons, or skill. Nor are they told to become anxious because an enemy seems powerful. An enemy’s resources remain created and contingent. The Qur’an asks:

> *“If God helps you, none can overcome you; but if He forsakes you, who is there that can help you after Him?”* (Qur’an 3:160)

This does not dismiss careful planning. It puts planning in its correct place. Human beings use means because God commanded responsible action; they do not worship means as though they independently create outcomes.

**What are God’s hosts?**

The Arabic *junūd* includes armies and organized forces. Angels are explicitly among God’s hosts, but the Qur’anic expression is wider. Wind, rain, disease, time, social change, the resolve of hearts, the failure of a conspiracy, and the emergence of unexpected allies may all become means by which God’s will is carried out. A person does not need to identify every hidden mechanism to recognize the principle: the field of causation is much larger than the field human beings can see.

The Qur’an describes the limits of human knowledge after the Battle of Badr:

> *“And no one knows the forces of your Lord except Him.”* (Qur’an 74:31)

The verse prevents two errors. One is superstition, the attempt to claim knowledge of every unseen cause. The other is reductionism, the assumption that only visible material causes are real. Islam affirms ordinary causes and also affirms the Creator who owns them all.

**Mighty and Wise: power is not arbitrary**

The closing Names, *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*, recur throughout the sūrah. *Al-ʿAzīz* is the One no force can defeat, frustrate, or compel. *Al-Ḥakīm* is the One whose action is perfectly placed, purposeful, and wise. Together they correct two distorted pictures of power: raw power without moral purpose, and good intention without capacity. God is neither helpless goodness nor blind force. His might carries out His wisdom, and His wisdom directs His might.

This pair offers particular consolation in periods when injustice appears organized and resourced. The believer may not know when or how a wrong will end, but does not conclude that the universe has become ownerless. The forces of heaven and earth have not changed masters. The divine response may be patience, exposure, protection, transformation, or a judgment deferred to the Hereafter; none of these possibilities are beyond the Mighty, the Wise.

**The discipline of humble agency**

In practical life, the verse produces humility in success and steadiness in weakness. A leader who receives influence should recognize that it is a trust, not proof of personal greatness. A community facing opposition should avoid despair and avoid the mirror-image temptation to copy the enemy’s wrongdoing. Since God owns the hosts, believers are accountable for their means as well as their aims.

The next verses turn from God’s universal power to the mission of the Messenger ﷺ. His authority is not self-created. He is sent by the One to whom all forces belong, as a witness, bearer of glad tidings, and warner.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:8

> **Indeed, We have sent you as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, and a warner.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A threefold description of prophetic mission**

The verse addresses the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ: *innā arsalnāka shāhidan wa mubashshiran wa nadhīrā*. Each participle describes an enduring task. He is a *shāhid*, a witness; a *mubashshir*, one who brings joyful good news; and a *nadhīr*, one who warns of danger before it arrives. The sequence is not merely honorific. It explains why the Messenger must be believed, loved, obeyed, and taken seriously.

The Qur’an uses the same description in Sūrah al-Aḥzāb:

> *“O Prophet, indeed We have sent you as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, a warner, a caller to God by His permission, and a luminous lamp.”* (Qur’an 33:45–46)

The repeated wording establishes that the Prophet’s mission joins truth-telling with mercy. He does not merely condemn. He gives glad news of forgiveness, Paradise, divine nearness, and a meaningful life to those who believe and act rightly. Nor does he merely console. He warns that choices have consequences and that injustice, idolatry, and hypocrisy are not morally neutral.

**Witness to what?**

Classical commentators mention several connected meanings of “witness.” The Prophet ﷺ bears witness to the oneness of God and delivers His revelation faithfully. On the Day of Resurrection, he will also bear witness that the message reached his community. The Qur’an describes this responsibility:

> *“And thus We have made you a middle community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and the Messenger may be a witness over you.”* (Qur’an 2:143)

Witnessing requires knowledge and integrity. A witness who alters testimony betrays both the truth and those affected by it. The Prophet’s character before revelation — known among his people as trustworthy — and his transparent delivery of a Qur’an that sometimes corrected him are signs that he did not use prophethood for self-protection. The message remained above the messenger’s personal preference because it came from God.

**Glad tidings and warning work together**

Human beings are not moved by fear alone or by hope alone. Fear without hope can create despair, hiding, and paralysis. Hope without accountability can become self-deception. The Messenger ﷺ was sent with both, so the heart remains balanced: it fears the justice of God yet hopes intensely for His mercy.

The Prophet ﷺ communicated this balance in a hadith qudsī:

> *“God says: ‘I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me.’”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

A believer should think well of God’s mercy, generosity, and acceptance of repentance, while never using that mercy as an excuse to persist in wrongdoing. The good news encourages return; the warning makes return urgent.

**The Prophet is not reduced to a political leader**

In the setting of al-Ḥudaybiyah, it would be easy to read the Prophet ﷺ only as a negotiator or statesman. The verse lifts the event to its real level. His political decisions served a prophetic mission: making God’s truth known, protecting the community, and opening a path for people to hear the message freely. His authority was moral and revealed before it was political.

This has an ongoing consequence for Muslims. Loyalty to the Prophet ﷺ cannot be confined to admiration for one episode of his life or to public slogans. It requires learning his guidance in worship, family life, trade, speech, mercy, courage, conflict, and repentance. The verse that follows explains the response expected from those who receive his message: faith in God and His Messenger, support for the mission, honor, and continual glorification of God.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:9

> **So that you may believe in God and His Messenger, support him, honor him, and glorify Him morning and evening.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From prophetic sending to human response**

The purpose clause, *li-tuʾminū bi-llāhi wa rasūlihi*, makes faith in God and faith in His Messenger inseparable in the Qur’anic sense. Faith in the Messenger ﷺ is not worship of a human being. It is acceptance that God sent him, that he conveyed revelation truthfully, and that following him is the appointed way to follow God. To claim belief in God while rejecting the messenger God chose is to set one’s own judgment above divine wisdom.

The Qur’an states the relationship in another concise verse:

> *“Whoever obeys the Messenger has indeed obeyed God.”* (Qur’an 4:80)

Obedience remains directed to God, while the Messenger ﷺ is the authoritative teacher and living model through whom the Book was conveyed and explained.

**Who is supported and honored?**

The pronouns in *tuʿazzirūhu wa tuwaqqirūhu* have been discussed by grammarians and commentators. A leading interpretation, reported by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, takes them as referring to the Messenger ﷺ: support him and honor him. Another sees one or both as referring to God in the sense of supporting His religion and magnifying His command. The next phrase, “and glorify Him morning and evening,” is generally understood to refer to God.

The meanings cohere rather than conflict. Believers support God’s cause by supporting His Messenger and the truth he brought. They honor the Messenger ﷺ without raising him to divinity. The Qur’an protects both sides of this balance: love and reverence are due, but worship is due only to God.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against religious exaggeration:

> *“Do not exaggerate in praising me as the Christians exaggerated in praising the son of Mary. I am only His servant, so say: the servant of God and His Messenger.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

This hadith is not a reduction of the Prophet’s rank. It preserves his rank from distortion. He is the most honored servant and Messenger of God, and precisely because he is God’s servant, he is not an object of worship.

**Supporting the mission in truthful ways**

The root of *tuʿazzirūhu* conveys strengthening, aiding, and defending. At al-Ḥudaybiyah, support included accompanying the Prophet ﷺ, holding firm beneath the tree, accepting his decision, and refusing to let disappointment become disobedience. In later circumstances, support includes learning and transmitting his Sunnah faithfully, defending him from false claims with knowledge and good character, and bringing his mercy into one’s conduct.

Support never permits lying, injustice, or cruelty in the Prophet’s name. The Qur’an has already defined him as a mercy to the worlds. A defense that violates his truthfulness does not support him; it betrays the very character it claims to defend. In a time of instant reaction and online outrage, the verse calls for something more demanding than volume: informed loyalty, dignified speech, and ethical consistency.

**Morning and evening remembrance**

The verse ends with *tusabbiḥūhu bukratan wa aṣīlā*: glorify Him morning and evening. *Tasbīḥ* means declaring God free from every imperfection, limitation, partner, injustice, and false comparison. It is both praise and purification of one’s idea of God. Morning and evening are named not to limit remembrance to two moments but to mark the day’s boundaries. A believer begins and closes the day by re-centering the heart upon God.

The Qur’an repeatedly recommends this rhythm:

> *“O you who believe, remember God with abundant remembrance, and glorify Him morning and evening.”* (Qur’an 33:41–42)

Regular remembrance is a protection against the conditions exposed earlier in the sūrah: bad assumptions about God, restless fear, and allegiance driven by changing advantage. It makes faith a lived orientation rather than an occasional statement. Verse 10 then shows that the pledge of allegiance at al-Ḥudaybiyah was not merely a political gesture; it was a covenant made, in its deepest reality, with God.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:10

> **Indeed, those who pledge allegiance to you are actually pledging allegiance to God. The Hand of God is over their hands. Whoever breaks his pledge breaks it only to his own harm; and whoever fulfills the covenant he has made with God, He will grant him a tremendous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The pledge beneath the tree**

This verse refers to the pledge at al-Ḥudaybiyah, later known as *Bayʿat al-Riḍwān*, the Pledge of Divine Good Pleasure. When a report circulated that the Prophet’s envoy, ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, had been killed in Makkah, the situation became dangerous. The Prophet ﷺ called those with him to pledge that they would stand firm and not flee. The Companions came forward beneath a tree and placed their hands in his hand. The report about ʿUthmān proved false, but the pledge revealed the sincerity of those who took it.

The verse begins with emphasis: “Indeed, those who pledge allegiance to you are actually pledging allegiance to God.” The Prophet ﷺ was the recipient of their physical pledge, but the covenant’s true object was God because the Messenger acted by His command. This is a powerful honor for the Prophet ﷺ and a powerful warning to anyone who makes a religious commitment casually.

**“The Hand of God is over their hands”**

The phrase *yadu Allāhi fawqa aydīhim* has been discussed extensively in Islamic theology. It must be read alongside the Qur’an’s uncompromising declaration:

> *“There is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”* (Qur’an 42:11)

The early Sunni approach affirms what God affirmed of Himself while refusing to imagine, compare, or ask “how.” Other Sunni scholars explain the expression in the Arabic language of power, support, favor, and confirmation: God’s power and covenant stand above their hands and their pledge. Both approaches reject two errors — stripping the verse of all meaning, and likening God to created bodies. The shared foundation is divine transcendence: the Creator is not contained by the forms of His creation.

In its immediate rhetorical setting, the expression magnifies the seriousness and security of the pledge. Their hands were joined to the Messenger’s hand, but God was the witness, guardian, and recipient of their covenant. No human observer could reduce that act to tribal politics.

**A broken oath first harms the oath-breaker**

*Fa-man nakatha fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsih* — whoever breaks his pledge breaks it only against himself. The verb *nakatha* evokes untying or undoing something firmly bound. An oath-breaker may imagine that he has outmaneuvered a leader or avoided a cost. The verse says that he has only damaged his own soul: he loses the reward of loyalty, trains himself in treachery, and meets God with a violated covenant.

The Qur’an gives a broader ethical instruction:

> *“Fulfill the covenant of God when you have made it, and do not break oaths after confirming them while you have made God your guarantor over you.”* (Qur’an 16:91)

This applies beyond the historical pledge. Commitments in marriage, employment, friendship, public office, testimony, and religious duty all form part of a believer’s moral life. Not every agreement is absolute; Islam recognizes justice, changed circumstances, and lawful release. But deliberate betrayal for selfish gain remains a wound that first enters the betrayer’s own character.

**The tremendous reward of fidelity**

The counterpart is *wa-man awfā bimā ʿāhada ʿalayhu Allāha fa-sa-yuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīmā*: whoever fulfills what he pledged to God will receive a tremendous reward. *Awfā* means to fulfill fully, without reducing the due. The reward is “tremendous” because the covenant concerns the whole direction of life: faithfulness to God, His Messenger ﷺ, and the moral commitments flowing from that faith.

A believer does not need to make dramatic public pledges in order to live this verse. Every prayer honestly performed, every trust returned, every temptation resisted when no one sees, and every word kept at personal cost participates in the larger covenant. The verse dignifies such unseen fidelity. It assures the servant that keeping faith with God is never a wasted loss, even when it looks costly in the immediate moment.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:11

> **Those of the Bedouins who stayed behind will say to you, “Our possessions and our families kept us occupied, so ask forgiveness for us.” They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts. Say, “Who can prevent anything from God for you, if He intends harm for you or intends benefit for you?” Rather, God is fully aware of what you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An excuse that exposes a deeper preference**

The verse turns to *al-mukhallafūna mina al-aʿrāb*: Bedouin Arabs from tribes around Madinah who stayed behind when the Prophet ﷺ set out toward Makkah. The word *mukhallafūn* suggests people left behind, but the context makes clear that this was not simply an unavoidable absence. They feared Quraysh and judged the journey too dangerous. Once the Prophet ﷺ returned safely, they offered an excuse framed in ordinary responsibilities: property and family had occupied them.

Islam does not belittle property or family. Both are gifts and trusts. The Qur’an commands provision for dependents and honors lawful work. The problem is not that they had families to care for; it is that they used good responsibilities to conceal a choice made from fear and weak trust. A worthy duty can become an excuse when it is selectively invoked to avoid a clearer duty.

The verse says, “They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts.” This is a concise definition of a hypocritical habit: the tongue performs repentance while the heart has not yet made the turn that repentance requires. Asking the Prophet ﷺ to seek forgiveness is good when it arises from real remorse. It becomes manipulation when it is merely a way to preserve standing after the danger has passed.

**No possession can shelter a person from God’s decree**

The Prophet ﷺ is told to ask a question that dissolves the premise of their excuse: who can control God’s decision if He intends harm or benefit? Their concern for wealth and family had been treated as though it could secure them against the future. Yet no possession, social connection, or strategy can independently guarantee safety or provision.

The Qur’an states the same reality elsewhere:

> *“If God touches you with harm, none can remove it except Him; and if He intends good for you, none can repel His bounty.”* (Qur’an 10:107)

This does not mean believers neglect worldly means. A parent should work, save responsibly, protect a family, and prepare thoughtfully. It means that means must not become objects of ultimate reliance. Property is a tool; it is not a refuge from God. Family is a mercy; it is not a substitute for obedience to the One who entrusted them to us.

**Seeking forgiveness requires truthful return**

The request “ask forgiveness for us” teaches an important distinction. It is permissible and good to ask a righteous person to make duʿāʾ for one’s forgiveness. The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ for supplication during his life, and believers may ask one another for prayer. But no request for another person’s duʿāʾ removes the need for personal repentance. One must acknowledge the sin, leave it, regret it, repair rights where possible, and resolve not to return.

The Qur’an describes authentic repentance as more than speech:

> *“O you who believe, turn to God in sincere repentance.”* (Qur’an 66:8)

*Sincere repentance* — *tawbatan naṣūḥā* — is repentance that repairs the inner split between word and heart. The verse therefore calls readers to examine their own apologies. Do we seek forgiveness because we hate the wrong we did, or because we dislike the consequences of being exposed? The question is uncomfortable precisely because it protects the soul from rehearsed piety without transformation.

**God’s awareness reaches behind the excuse**

The verse closes with *bal kāna Allāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīrā*: God is fully aware of what you do. *Khabīr* means not only one who knows facts, but One intimately aware of hidden realities, motives, and consequences. Human beings may accept an explanation because they lack access to the heart. God does not confuse outward vocabulary with inward truth.

This awareness should produce both fear and relief. It warns the person who manages appearances that the real record cannot be edited. It comforts the person whose sincere intentions are misunderstood by people, because God knows what they cannot see. In a culture that rewards public display, the verse returns moral seriousness to its proper center: the integrity of what one says, intends, and actually does.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:12

> **Rather, you thought that the Messenger and the believers would never return to their families, and that was made attractive in your hearts. You thought an evil thought, and you became a ruined people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The hidden calculation is named**

Verse 12 removes the respectable cover of the previous excuse. The real thought was that the Messenger ﷺ and the believers would not return to their families. The tribes did not simply stay behind because tasks were pressing; they calculated that the Muslim party was likely to be annihilated and chose distance from what they perceived as a losing cause.

This gives the verse a timeless relevance. Faith is often tested when obedience looks unsafe, unpopular, expensive, or unlikely to succeed. The temptation is to remain near truth while it is socially advantageous, but to withdraw when it demands courage. The sūrah identifies that calculation as a spiritual danger because it judges God’s promise solely by the visible balance of power at one moment.

**A thought made beautiful to the heart**

*Zuyyina fī qulūbikum* — “it was made attractive in your hearts” — describes how a false judgment can become emotionally persuasive. The wrongdoing is not always experienced as ugly by the person committing it. Fear can dress itself as prudence; greed as responsibility; cowardice as neutrality; opportunism as realism. When the heart desires an excuse, it can make a weak argument feel wise.

The Qur’an warns against this process repeatedly:

> *“Have you seen the one whose evil deed was made attractive to him, so he sees it as good?”* (Qur’an 35:8)

This is why moral self-examination requires more than asking, “Does this feel right to me?” Feelings are real, but they can be educated, distorted, or flattered. Revelation, trusted counsel, and honest attention to consequences help a person test whether an attractive thought is actually true.

**The evil assumption behind opportunism**

The verse calls their estimate *ẓanna al-sawʾ*, an evil thought. It was evil not merely because it predicted danger but because it detached God from the event. They assumed the Messenger ﷺ could be defeated and God’s cause extinguished by the force of Quraysh. The Qur’an’s answer to that imagination is not a denial that believers face danger. It is the assurance that falsehood has no final power over truth.

> *“They want to extinguish the light of God with their mouths, but God will complete His light, even though the disbelievers dislike it.”* (Qur’an 61:8)

The verse does not authorize believers to make careless predictions of worldly success. It commands a deeper confidence: no hostile campaign can erase the truth God intends to preserve, and no faithful act is lost merely because it is performed in a weak moment.

**Būr: people emptied of good**

The final description is *qawman būrā*. The word *būr* conveys ruin, barrenness, or something devoid of good. Classical lexicographers compared it to land that no longer produces. It is a severe description because their problem was not one incorrect prediction. It was a heart-condition that rendered them unable to recognize goodness when it stood before them.

There is a social dimension as well. A community built on opportunism cannot generate trust. If people join only when profit is expected and disappear when sacrifice is required, no family, institution, or society can remain sound. The verse exposes this kind of calculation as self-destructive before it is politically unhelpful.

**Turning fear into faithful caution**

The opposite of their error is not reckless denial of danger. Islam distinguishes courage from foolishness. The Prophet ﷺ took precautions, used scouts, negotiated, and listened to advice. The opposite is to let fear be governed by faith and reason rather than by panic and self-interest. One can recognize risk while still remaining truthful, loyal, and obedient.

For modern readers, this verse reaches into decisions where ethics cost something: refusing dishonest profit, defending an absent colleague from slander, preserving a prayer in a pressured schedule, or standing with a vulnerable person when doing so carries social risk. The question is not whether fear appears; fear is human. The question is whether fear is permitted to make an evil thought seem beautiful.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:13

> **And whoever does not believe in God and His Messenger — then indeed We have prepared a blazing Fire for the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disbelief as a rejection of a disclosed trust**

This verse gives the ultimate seriousness of the issue. The previous verses were not simply about military participation or tribal loyalty. They were about belief in God and His Messenger ﷺ. To reject God’s Messenger after the truth has been made clear is to reject the One who sent him and the guidance brought through him.

The Qur’an’s use of *kufr* includes covering, concealing, or refusing a truth. It is not a license for human beings to pronounce final judgment on every individual’s hidden state. God alone knows precisely what message reached a person, how it was understood, what obstacles existed, and how much culpable rejection occurred. The verse speaks of the divine judgment of those who knowingly reject faith, not of a believer’s right to be harsh or unjust toward other people.

The Qur’an preserves the distinction between conviction and conduct:

> *“Let there be no compulsion in religion. Truth has become clear from error.”* (Qur’an 2:256)

Faith cannot be produced by coercion. The role of believers is clear invitation, honest witness, justice, and mercy; the final judgment belongs to God.

**Why the Fire is called prepared**

*Fa-innā aʿtadnā lil-kāfirīna saʿīrā* — “We have prepared for the disbelievers a blazing Fire.” The verb conveys preparation and readiness. It teaches that moral reality has consequences, even when they are delayed from earthly sight. Hell is not a theatrical threat added to frighten people into conformity. In Qur’anic theology, it is the final exposure of a life that persistently chose separation from truth, mercy, and the Lord who gave it existence.

The word *saʿīr* emphasizes a kindled, blazing fire. Its severity should be read beside the Qur’an’s countless invitations to repentance. God warns because He does not want servants to walk blindly toward destruction. The warning is mercy in the form appropriate to a danger that cannot be safely minimized.

The Prophet ﷺ described God’s readiness to accept return:

> *“God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant when he repents to Him than one of you who finds his camel after losing it in a barren land.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The contrast is essential. The verse is not an invitation to despair. It tells the person who has rejected truth that the road has a real end, while the hadith tells the person who turns back that God’s mercy is not reluctant.

**A warning against treating revelation as optional advice**

The verse also challenges the modern habit of treating every moral claim as a private preference. If God is Creator and Messenger-sender, revelation is not one lifestyle suggestion among others. It is an address that calls for response. This does not mean every detail is easy or every question is answered instantly. It means the core posture is one of humility before God rather than self-enthronement.

The Bedouin tribes in the context had made safety and advantage the standard by which they judged the Prophet’s call. The verse restores the real standard: belief in God and His Messenger. The following verse immediately places forgiveness and punishment under God’s complete sovereignty, while ending with His Names, the Forgiving and the Merciful.

---
## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:14

> **To God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. And God is Ever-Forgiving, Ever-Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sovereignty before forgiveness and punishment**

The verse begins by grounding forgiveness and punishment in universal sovereignty: *li-llāhi mulku al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ*. *Mulk* means dominion, ownership, authority, and the right to dispose. God does not judge as one limited official inside a larger system. He is the Creator and owner of the heavens and earth. Every life, act, opportunity, and final return exists within His dominion.

The Qur’an repeats this declaration to dissolve every false claim of independent power:

> *“To God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is within them, and He is over all things powerful.”* (Qur’an 5:120)

A person may possess something legally for a time, but this is entrusted possession, not absolute ownership. The verse teaches humility to the wealthy, courage to the vulnerable, and responsibility to everyone: all will return to the true Owner.

**Divine will is never injustice**

“He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills” must be understood with the Qur’an’s other declarations about God’s justice, wisdom, and mercy. It does not mean God acts capriciously or punishes innocent people without cause. He says:

> *“Indeed, God does not wrong people at all, but people wrong themselves.”* (Qur’an 10:44)

Classical scholars therefore explained that His will is wise, not arbitrary; He forgives by grace and punishes with justice. No one can impose an obligation upon God or demand Paradise as a right earned independently of mercy. At the same time, no one is treated unfairly. The servant’s responsibility is real, divine knowledge is complete, and the final judgment is perfectly just.

This balance protects against two opposite errors. One error is despair: “My sins are too great, so there is no point returning.” The other is false security: “God is forgiving, so my choices do not matter.” The verse corrects both: He is sovereign and may punish; He is forgiving and merciful, so no repentant servant should despair.

**The verse ends with hope**

The last Names are *Ghafūran Raḥīman*: Ever-Forgiving, Ever-Merciful. *Ghafūr* conveys repeated and abundant forgiveness. *Raḥīm* conveys enduring mercy directed to those who receive it. Their placement after the mention of punishment teaches that the final note is not despair. Even the Bedouins criticized in the previous verses are not declared beyond the possibility of return. Verse 16 will give them another summons and an opportunity to show obedience.

The Qur’an addresses those who have wronged themselves with an invitation of immense breadth:

> *“Say, ‘O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. He is the Ever-Forgiving, the Ever-Merciful.’”* (Qur’an 39:53)

This is not a promise of forgiveness without repentance, but it is a promise that repentance is never blocked by the size of a sin. The door closes only when life’s opportunity to return has ended.

**Living as a trustee, not an owner**

A practical fruit of believing in God’s dominion is that it changes the way a person handles power. Parents do not own children; they are entrusted with them. Employers do not own workers; they hold a responsibility. Officials do not own public power; they are accountable trustees. Even one’s body and time are trusts. This awareness turns authority from self-expression into service.

The verse’s sequence — dominion, forgiveness, punishment, mercy — is therefore not abstract theology. It gives the ethical foundation for the next passage, in which those who stayed behind want to join only when material spoils seem likely. They will be taught that God, not human opportunism, decides how provision and reward are distributed.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:15

> **Those who stayed behind will say, when you set out toward spoils to take them, “Let us follow you.” They wish to alter the word of God. Say, “You will not follow us. God has said this before.” Then they will say, “Rather, you are jealous of us.” But they understand only a little.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Eagerness after the danger has passed**

The verse anticipates what happened after al-Ḥudaybiyah, when the Muslims set out toward Khaybar and its spoils. Those who had declined the journey toward Makkah now wanted to accompany the believers. Their request sounds reasonable — “Let us follow you” — but the Qur’an uncovers its timing and motive. They wanted to join when material gain looked likely, after they had withdrawn when the earlier journey seemed dangerous and spiritually demanding.

This is not a criticism of someone who sincerely repents and then seeks to participate in good. Islam welcomes repentance. The problem is the attempt to obtain a specific reward promised to those who had shown loyalty at al-Ḥudaybiyah, while treating the earlier sacrifice as irrelevant. Actions have moral meaning; one cannot rewrite their history merely by appearing when the profit becomes visible.

**The word of God cannot be adjusted to convenience**

“They wish to alter the word of God” refers, according to the majority explanation, to God’s promise concerning the spoils of Khaybar for those who were present at al-Ḥudaybiyah. The issue is not that people can alter God’s revealed words in themselves — they cannot — but that they seek to alter how His judgment is applied to them. They want the reward while avoiding the faithfulness for which it was given.

This temptation is not limited to war spoils. People may want the social honor of religious identity without its ethical cost, or want trust after breaking a trust without accepting the work of repair. The verse establishes that divine justice is not fooled by late-arriving claims. God knows what was chosen when the test was real.

The Qur’an gives a general warning against selective religion:

> *“Do you believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part?”* (Qur’an 2:85)

The question is not only about formal belief. It addresses the impulse to accept commands that suit one’s advantage and evade those that require sacrifice.

**The psychology of accusing others of envy**

When corrected, the tribes are predicted to say, “Rather, you are jealous of us.” This is a familiar reversal. Instead of admitting that their earlier choice had consequences, they accuse the faithful of selfish exclusion. Envy becomes an easy explanation because it protects self-image: if others withhold a reward, it must be because they resent us, not because we failed a test.

The verse ends, “They understand only a little.” Their knowledge is confined to surface gain. They understand the immediate value of spoils but not the moral logic of loyalty, divine promise, repentance, or delayed wisdom. Limited understanding is dangerous when it is combined with confidence. A person sees one fragment of the situation and treats it as the whole.

**Reward is more than material allocation**

The verse challenges a commercial approach to faith. The people who went to al-Ḥudaybiyah received a promise of spoils, but their real distinction was not wealth. They had trusted the Prophet ﷺ at a difficult hour and pledged their allegiance beneath the tree. Material recompense was a mercy, yet it was not the highest gift. God’s pleasure, the strengthening of faith, and lasting reward are greater.

In ordinary life, the same principle appears when people seek the visible benefits of commitment without sharing its unseen burdens. A student wants the credential but not the study; a colleague wants credit but not responsibility; a friend wants trust but not truthfulness. The verse asks for moral coherence. If one missed a chance to do right, the remedy is not entitlement; it is truthful repentance and a new pattern of obedience.

Verse 16 offers precisely such a new opportunity. The people who stayed behind are not simply dismissed. They will be called to another test in which sincere obedience can be shown.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:16

> **Say to those of the Bedouins who stayed behind, “You will be called against a people of great military might. You will fight them, or they will submit. If you obey, God will give you a beautiful reward; but if you turn away as you turned away before, He will punish you with a painful punishment.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A future summons leaves the door open**

This verse is notable for its balance of warning and opportunity. Those who stayed behind are not told that their earlier failure has made all future obedience meaningless. They will be called again, this time against “a people of great military might,” and their response will reveal whether their apology was true. Their past is serious, but it need not become their permanent identity.

Classical commentators differed over the historical identification of the powerful people: some related it to Banū Ḥanīfah during the wars against apostasy after the Prophet’s death; some to the Byzantines or Persians; others to Hawāzin and Thaqīf after the conquest of Makkah. The Qur’an does not name them, and its moral purpose does not depend on choosing one identification with certainty. What is clear is that the Bedouins would face another real call in which courage, loyalty, and obedience could be demonstrated.

**“Fight them or they submit”: a contextual command**

The wording belongs to a seventh-century setting of armed conflict, treaty relationships, and tribes that threatened or fought the Muslim community. It must be read with the Qur’an’s larger rules governing war: fighting is not a free license for religious coercion, vengeance, or aggression. The Qur’an says:

> *“Fight in the way of God those who fight you, but do not transgress. Indeed, God does not love transgressors.”* (Qur’an 2:190)

And it says:

> *“If they incline to peace, then incline to it also, and rely upon God.”* (Qur’an 8:61)

The central point of this verse is not a permanent instruction to seek conflict. It is that a community facing actual attack or grave threat cannot preserve justice through empty declarations alone. Some responsibilities require personal cost. The people who stayed behind had to learn that sincere allegiance is tested by readiness to bear lawful burdens, not by words offered after safety is secured.

**Obedience carries a beautiful reward**

*Ajran ḥasanā* — “a beautiful reward” — includes the immediate and final consequences of sincere obedience. In the immediate world, those who share a community’s burdens may receive protection, legitimate provision, trust, and a restored place in the community. In the Hereafter, the reward is greater and lasting. The Qur’anic word *ḥasan* is more than “adequate”; it conveys goodness, beauty, and fitting generosity.

The contrast with the “painful punishment” is not arbitrary. A person who turns away twice — after receiving warning, clarification, and another chance — has made a clearer moral choice. The verse holds people responsible without making repentance impossible. God’s justice includes both the seriousness of repeated evasion and the availability of a new beginning.

**The difference between remorse and regret**

The verse gives a useful test for the difference between remorse and mere regret. Regret says, “I dislike what I lost.” Remorse says, “I dislike the wrong I did and will act differently when the same principle costs me again.” The Bedouins’ future response would distinguish the two. This distinction is relevant wherever someone apologizes after breaking a promise. Words matter, but changed conduct is what gives words their weight.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that actions are evaluated by their inner direction:

> *“Actions are only by intentions, and each person will have only what he intended.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Intention is not an invisible excuse that replaces action. It is the inward reality that must become visible in consistent action when the opportunity comes.

**A beautiful reward for costly faithfulness**

Many ordinary duties resemble this verse in a quiet form. Caring for an elderly parent, returning money no one would know was kept, refusing a lucrative dishonest practice, or admitting an error that risks reputation can feel like answering a difficult summons. The verse assures the believer that obedience is never merely a loss. Its reward may not be immediate or public, but God calls it beautiful because it restores the soul to truth and places the servant under His promise.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:17

> **There is no blame upon the blind, nor any blame upon the lame, nor any blame upon the sick. Whoever obeys God and His Messenger, He will admit him into gardens beneath which rivers flow; and whoever turns away, He will punish him with a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy recognizes real incapacity**

After speaking of a call that may require fighting, the Qur’an immediately exempts those with genuine inability: the blind, the lame, and the sick. The order of the verses is itself legal and ethical teaching. Responsibility is real, but responsibility is measured by capacity. Islam does not praise a system that treats physical impairment or illness as moral failure.

The Qur’an gives the general principle elsewhere:

> *“God does not burden any soul beyond its capacity.”* (Qur’an 2:286)

The exemption is not a statement that blind, disabled, or ill people have less spiritual worth. It concerns a particular duty whose physical demands they may not be able to meet. The Qur’an and Sunnah repeatedly honor people with disabilities as full members of the community. ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, who was blind, was entrusted by the Prophet ﷺ to call the adhān and to lead the people in Madinah on some occasions of the Prophet’s absence.

**Law is built on ability, not a performance of strength**

The juristic principle drawn from such verses is that obligations are adapted to real ability. A sick person may pray sitting or lying down when standing is impossible; a traveler has concessions; someone unable to fast may have another ruling suited to the condition. Such rulings are not loopholes for negligence but expressions of divine mercy and realism.

The Prophet ﷺ gave a simple instruction to ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn when illness prevented normal standing in prayer:

> *“Pray standing; if you are unable, then sitting; and if you are unable, then on your side.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

The hadith preserves both elements of the verse: worship remains important, but its form is made mercifully possible. God does not demand a theatrical imitation of capacity that a person does not possess.

**Exemption from blame is not exclusion from reward**

The verse moves directly from exemption to the universal promise: whoever obeys God and His Messenger will enter the Gardens. The physically exempt person is not left at the edge of the community’s spiritual promise. Obedience takes the form available to each person. A person unable to join a journey or battle may have patience, prayer, knowledge, care for dependents, financial support, truthful counsel, and inward sincerity. God knows the capacity and intention of every servant.

The Prophet ﷺ comforted those who had remained in Madinah for legitimate reasons during an expedition:

> *“Indeed, in Madinah there are men whom you did not travel any distance or cross any valley except that they shared with you in the reward; illness held them back.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

The report should not be used to glorify avoiding duty. It honors those whose sincere intention was blocked by a real excuse. The distinction between them and the opportunists of verses 11–16 is precisely the distinction the Qur’an has been drawing.

**Turning away remains a moral choice**

The final warning concerns the person who *yatawalla* — turns away. This is different from inability. A person may be physically capable yet refuse a clear obligation through arrogance, fear, or selfish advantage. The Qur’an’s mercy toward genuine limits does not erase moral responsibility where choice remains.

This balance has social importance. Communities should not shame the sick or disabled for what they cannot do, and they should not allow privileged people to hide deliberate neglect behind the language of “personal circumstances.” Compassion and accountability are not opposites. Each protects the other from distortion.

**A community made accessible by revelation**

In daily practice, this verse asks Muslims to remove unnecessary barriers from communal life: make places of worship accessible, avoid treating illness as spiritual inferiority, listen to those whose needs differ, and value contributions not measured by physical visibility. It also asks each person to worship and serve within the actual capacity God has given. The measure is not comparison with another body or another life, but faithful response to one’s own entrusted ability.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:18

> **God was certainly pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you beneath the tree. He knew what was in their hearts, so He sent down tranquillity upon them and rewarded them with a near victory.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The pledge receives God’s explicit pleasure**

This verse returns to the pledge beneath the tree and gives it a distinction unmatched in the Qur’an: *laqad raḍiya Allāhu ʿani al-muʾminīna* — “God was certainly pleased with the believers.” The word *raḍiya* denotes divine good pleasure, acceptance, and approval. The pledge was not valuable because it was emotionally dramatic; it was valuable because God knew the truth in the hearts of those who made it.

The historical moment was tense. The believers had heard that ʿUthmān might have been killed while acting as the Prophet’s envoy. They pledged to stand firm with the Messenger ﷺ, even if the situation became dangerous. The Qur’an’s praise does not rest on tribal excitement or blind group loyalty. It rests on faith, sincerity, courage, and obedience to the Messenger of God.

Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ saying concerning the people of the pledge:

> *“No one who gave the pledge beneath the tree will enter the Fire.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Scholars treat this as a special prophetic testimony to those believers present, alongside the Qur’an’s own witness to God’s pleasure with them. It calls later Muslims to honor the Companions without turning their history into partisan abuse or treating them as objects of worship. They are honored servants of God, not rivals to Him.

**God knew what was in their hearts**

*Fa-ʿalima mā fī qulūbihim* — “He knew what was in their hearts.” Their outward pledge was visible to people, but its sincerity was visible only to God. Some may have felt fear; courage in Islam does not require the absence of fear. Their merit was that fear did not dissolve their faithfulness. They chose loyalty to truth even while uncertainty was real.

This clause gives a profound comfort to sincere believers whose inner struggle is hidden. God does not evaluate a person by outward calm alone. He knows the anxiety a person resists, the temptation a person refuses, the grief carried without complaint against God, and the intention prevented by lack of capacity. He also knows when a public display conceals a private betrayal. No earthly audience can make the final assessment.

**Tranquillity as the fruit of commitment**

Because God knew their hearts, He sent down *sakīnah* upon them. In verse 4, tranquillity was described as a means for faith to increase; here it is connected directly to the pledge. Sincere commitment does not always remove difficulty, but it gives the heart an inner coherence that brings steadiness. A person who has decided whom he serves no longer spends every fearful moment renegotiating his principles.

This is why the verse does not praise mere enthusiasm. Enthusiasm rises and fades with circumstance. *Sakīnah* is settledness. It allowed the Companions to receive a treaty they did not immediately understand, return without completing their pilgrimage, and remain obedient until the opening became apparent.

**The near victory**

*Fathan qarīban* — “a near victory” — is widely understood to include the conquest of Khaybar, which followed soon after al-Ḥudaybiyah. Some exegetes include the later conquest of Makkah. The phrase teaches that God’s timing is not vague. What looks distant to human impatience may be “near” in the scale of divine planning.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Know that victory comes with patience, relief with distress, and ease with hardship.”* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded sound by hadith scholars)

The hadith does not guarantee a preferred outcome on a preferred timetable. It teaches a pattern: faithful endurance is not empty waiting. God can bring relief through the very trial that seemed to close the way.

**What this verse asks of later believers**

A believer cannot recreate the unique pledge beneath the tree, but can inherit its moral qualities: loyalty without fanaticism, courage without recklessness, and trust in prophetic guidance when desire presses in another direction. In a smaller setting, it may mean keeping an ethical promise when coworkers reward compromise, remaining truthful when a lie would be easier, or accepting a wise decision after consultation even when it disappoints personal preference. God’s knowledge of hearts makes such hidden faithfulness weighty.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:19

> **And abundant spoils that they would take. And God is Almighty, All-Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Material recompense in its proper place**

This verse completes the sentence begun in verse 18. God rewarded the believers with a near victory and with abundant spoils. The immediate historical referent is commonly understood as Khaybar, whose resources became available soon after al-Ḥudaybiyah. The believers had returned from the pilgrimage disappointed at being prevented from entering Makkah; God gave them provision, security, and a visible sign that the treaty had not been a defeat.

The Qur’an does not pretend that material needs are unreal. A community needs food, safety, resources, and the ability to care for families. Lawful material provision can be a mercy from God and a strengthening of people who have sacrificed for what is right. But the order of the passage remains important: divine pleasure, knowledge of hearts, tranquillity, and victory are mentioned before spoils. Wealth is a result and a trust; it is not the defining purpose of faith.

**Spoils are not a license for greed**

In the Qur’anic legal framework, spoils from a lawful conflict were regulated rather than left to the strongest. They were not personal plunder taken by individual fighters. Their distribution was subject to divine instruction and communal justice, including provision for those with rights and needs. The Qur’an says:

> *“And know that whatever you take as spoils, then one fifth belongs to God and the Messenger, and to relatives, orphans, the needy, and the traveler.”* (Qur’an 8:41)

The rule shows that even in the harsh setting of war, wealth is not morally ownerless. The vulnerable are remembered. The community’s material gain is placed under a higher accountability.

**Mighty and Wise governs the distribution**

The verse ends, as verse 7 did, with *ʿAzīzan Ḥakīman*: Almighty, All-Wise. God had the power to give the believers abundance, and His wisdom determined its time and form. This pairing prevents the reader from treating Khaybar as a simple reward mechanism in which wealth automatically proves righteousness. In the Qur’an, both abundance and scarcity can be tests. The right response to abundance is gratitude, justice, and generosity; the right response to scarcity is patience, lawful effort, and trust.

The Qur’an warns against the assumption that wealth is proof of divine honor:

> *“As for man, when his Lord tests him and is generous to him and favors him, he says, ‘My Lord has honored me.’ But when He tests him and restricts his provision, he says, ‘My Lord has humiliated me.’ No indeed!”* (Qur’an 89:15–17)

The corrective is direct. Provision is not a simple measure of moral status. It is part of the field in which status is tested.

**The near victory teaches patient stewardship**

The Companions’ experience offers a practical lesson: the person who remains faithful in a period that appears unproductive may later be entrusted with more than expected. The correct response is not, “Now our earlier sacrifice was worthwhile only because we gained.” It is, “God has shown His generosity; how should we use this trust rightly?” That is the difference between gratitude and greed.

Verse 20 expands the promise beyond this particular provision. It shows that what believers have already received is a sign and a beginning, not the whole of what God is capable of opening.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:20

> **God has promised you abundant spoils that you will take; then He hastened this for you and restrained people’s hands from you, so that it may be a sign for the believers and so that He may guide you to a straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A promise larger than the first gift**

The verse distinguishes between “abundant spoils” promised more broadly and something God “hastened” for the believers. Classical exegetes commonly understood the hastened gift as Khaybar and the wider promise as other openings to come. The wording preserves a useful spiritual order: God gives an early sign of His promise without limiting His generosity to that first sign.

The purpose of a material gift is made explicit: it is an *āyah*, a sign for believers, and a means of guidance to the straight path. It is not simply a reward to enjoy and forget. When provision arrives after hardship, it can become evidence that God’s wisdom was present during the hardship too. The sign should enlarge trust, not inflate the ego.

**Restrained hands can be a greater gift than victory by force**

“He restrained people’s hands from you” refers to God’s protection from hostile hands and, in the wider context, the restraint of Muslim hands as well. At al-Ḥudaybiyah, violent confrontation was prevented. The verse names restraint as a blessing, not as an embarrassing lack of action.

This is a major correction to cultures that equate honor with immediate retaliation. Every prevented injury has moral weight. A conflict avoided may preserve lives, future relationships, the credibility of a cause, and the possibility of a just settlement. The Qur’an later says of the same setting that God restrained both sides in the valley of Makkah (48:24). The double restraint prevented an escalation whose harms the participants could not fully see.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that gentleness is not ornamental weakness:

> *“Gentleness is not in anything except that it beautifies it, and it is not removed from anything except that it disgraces it.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Gentleness does not mean surrendering truth or abandoning protection of the vulnerable. It means choosing the right degree and manner of response rather than letting emotion dictate action.

**Signs must lead to guidance**

The phrase “so that it may be a sign for the believers and so that He may guide you to a straight path” places interpretation at the center of history. The same event can be experienced by two people and produce opposite results. One sees a treaty, provision, or prevented battle and learns gratitude and trust. Another sees only a short-term gain and learns entitlement. The outward event is shared; guidance determines what the heart learns from it.

The straight path is not merely a set of ideas. It is a way of reading God’s gifts and trials correctly. The believer neither worships the gift nor despairs at its delay. He uses it as a sign pointing beyond itself to the Giver.

**Provision, security, and the ethics of success**

In modern life, “spoils” may not be the immediate category, but the verse’s ethic applies to earnings, promotions, institutional growth, and opportunities gained after a difficult period. When a person obtains something desired, the question is not only “How can I keep it?” but “What sign and responsibility has God placed in it?” A lawful gain should strengthen prayer, family care, charity, integrity, and service, not make a person more indifferent to God.

God’s restraint of hostile hands also invites gratitude for ordinary protections that people often overlook: a conflict that did not escalate, an accident avoided, a harsh message unsent, a legal dispute settled, or a rumor that failed to spread. These may be among the quiet signs that keep a life from breaking. Verse 21 then speaks of openings beyond the believers’ present capacity, all of which remain encompassed by God’s power.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:21

> **And other gains that you were not able to attain, but God has encompassed them. And God is powerful over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Beyond the horizon of present ability**

The verse speaks of other gains and openings that the believers were not yet able to attain. *Lam taqdirū ʿalayhā* acknowledges a plain fact: there were things beyond their present reach. The Muslims at Madinah had limited numbers, vulnerable routes, and enemies on several sides. Before the treaty with Quraysh, a northern expedition could expose them to danger from the south. Human limitation was real; the Qur’an does not ask believers to pretend otherwise.

Yet the verse adds, *qad aḥāṭa Allāhu bihā* — God has encompassed them. His encompassing knowledge and power already include what the believers cannot reach, see, or organize. The classical commentators offered several historical applications: future victories, Khaybar, Makkah, and the later openings toward Byzantine and Persian lands. The breadth of the wording allows the principle to exceed any single event. God’s promise is not restricted by the current capacity of His servants.

**Encompassment is not human possession**

There is an important difference between “God has encompassed them” and “you already possess them.” The verse creates hope without feeding entitlement. A believer may be assured that no good is beyond God’s power while still being required to wait, prepare, and act within lawful limits. What God encompasses may be granted at a different time, in a different form, or withheld because something else is wiser for the servant.

The Qur’an says:

> *“Perhaps you dislike a thing while it is good for you, and perhaps you love a thing while it is bad for you. God knows, and you do not know.”* (Qur’an 2:216)

This is not a slogan for avoiding responsibility. It is a cure for the despair that arises when present capacity is treated as the measure of all possibility.

**Power over all things and the use of means**

The verse ends with *wa kāna Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā*: God is powerful over all things. Divine power is comprehensive; nothing that is possible lies outside it. But the Qur’an’s teaching on power never licenses magical thinking. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions used effort, strategy, preparation, and consultation. Trust in God does not replace means; it corrects the illusion that means rule independently.

The Prophet ﷺ instructed a man who asked whether to leave his camel untied and simply rely on God:

> *“Tie it and rely upon God.”* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī)

The maxim captures the verse’s balance. The believer acknowledges limits, takes the means that are available, and refuses to convert those limits into a prison for hope.

**A cure for both despair and fantasy**

This verse corrects two opposite habits. One says, “Because I cannot do this now, it can never happen.” The other says, “Because God is powerful, I need not build capacity or accept reality.” The first turns a present limitation into despair; the second turns faith into fantasy. The Qur’anic way is more mature: God encompasses what you do not, so work faithfully within what is entrusted to you and leave the unseen field to Him.

For an individual, this can mean continuing a sincere effort toward education, recovery, family repair, or service when the desired result seems far away. For a community, it means building sound institutions and moral character instead of demanding immediate grand outcomes. God’s power is not a substitute for patience; it is the reason patience is rational.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:22

> **If those who disbelieved had fought you, they would certainly have turned their backs; then they would have found neither protector nor helper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A counterfactual mercy**

The verse explains one dimension of what was avoided at al-Ḥudaybiyah. Had Quraysh chosen battle rather than treaty, they would have been defeated and would have found no protector or helper. The statement is not a celebration of violence. Its place in the sūrah is to show that the treaty did not arise because God was unable to help the believers. Restraint was chosen by divine wisdom, not forced by weakness.

This distinction matters. People sometimes mistake patience for incapacity and compromise for surrender. The Qur’an refuses that interpretation. God could have granted a decisive military outcome, but chose a path that protected other goods: the sanctity of Makkah, the hidden believers living there, the possibility of peaceful contact, and the longer opening of hearts.

**Protector and helper: all false supports fail**

The two terms, *waliyy* and *naṣīr*, convey a protecting patron and an assisting helper. The disbelievers of Makkah relied on tribal status, the prestige of guardianship over the Kaʿbah, alliances, and numbers. The verse teaches that none of these can protect a person against God’s judgment. The same principle occurs throughout the Qur’an:

> *“They have taken protectors besides Him; but God — He is the Protector, and He gives life to the dead, and He is over all things powerful.”* (Qur’an 42:9)

Worldly support is not condemned in itself. Families, neighbors, laws, institutions, and alliances can be legitimate means of protection. The error is to treat them as ultimate refuges or to use them to sustain wrongdoing. Every created support has limits; God alone is the Protector without limit.

**The morality of deterrence**

The verse also carries a principle of deterrence. A community that is known to have no capacity to defend itself can invite aggression. The Qur’an recognizes the need for strength sufficient to prevent harm, while binding that strength to justice and restraint. The aim is not domination for its own sake. It is the protection of worship, life, covenant, and the possibility of peace.

The Qur’an directs believers:

> *“Prepare against them whatever force you are able, and steeds of war, by which you may deter the enemy of God and your enemy.”* (Qur’an 8:60)

The word “deter” is important. The most humane use of strength is sometimes the strength that prevents a battle from occurring. Al-Ḥudaybiyah shows the complementary truth: when peace is offered and a greater good is served, accepting a treaty can also be a form of strength.

**Do not turn a historical promise into personal aggression**

The verse belongs to a specific prophetic context. It cannot be isolated to justify private violence, hostility toward neighbors of other faiths, or contempt for human beings. Islamic ethics requires justice even with opponents and prohibits transgression. The Prophet ﷺ dealt with treaties, neighbors, and individuals with an extraordinary commitment to keeping covenants and preventing needless harm.

For readers today, the verse teaches that truth does not need to borrow its confidence from aggression. A person can be firm in faith, able to protect rights, and still committed to peace wherever peace is possible. Verse 23 gives the larger principle: God’s established way does not grant enduring victory to those who fight His messengers and truth.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:23

> **This is the established way of God which has passed on before; and you will never find any change in the established way of God.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sunnat Allāh: a dependable moral pattern**

The phrase *sunnata Allāhi allatī qad khalat min qablu* means God’s established way or recurring pattern in history. *Sunnah* here means a normative, consistent mode of divine dealing, not merely a custom. The verse does not say that every event repeats in the same outward form. It says that God’s moral order is reliable: persistent falsehood, tyranny, and hostile resistance to truth do not possess a permanent future, even when they appear dominant for a time.

The Qur’an uses the same expression when speaking of earlier communities:

> *“This is the way of God with those who passed on before, and you will never find any change in the way of God.”* (Qur’an 33:62)

The purpose is not to turn history into a simplistic chart in which every suffering proves guilt or every success proves divine approval. Prophets themselves were persecuted, and believers may endure long hardship. Rather, it establishes that God’s promises, moral judgment, and ultimate justice are not defeated by temporary appearances.

**No change does not mean no human responsibility**

A distorted reading might make people passive: “If God’s way will prevail, our choices do not matter.” The Qur’an rejects this. God’s established way includes moral agency, cause and consequence, repentance, striving, patience, and accountability. People are not spectators to fate. Their choices are among the means through which history unfolds, and they are judged for those choices.

The Qur’an links communal change directly to human moral change:

> *“Indeed, God does not change the condition of a people until they change what is within themselves.”* (Qur’an 13:11)

This verse does not contradict 48:23; it explains part of it. God’s unchanging way includes the reality that corruption has consequences and sincere reform requires inward change as well as slogans.

**The historical lesson of al-Ḥudaybiyah**

At al-Ḥudaybiyah, the believers were being trained to see beyond a single day’s advantage. The apparent strength of Quraysh did not make its opposition to the Messenger ﷺ permanent. The treaty, which some initially found hard to accept, became part of the process by which Quraysh’s power to block the message was undone. God’s way unfolded through patience, treaty, social contact, and eventually the opening of Makkah with comparatively little bloodshed.

The lesson is especially important when a community feels pressured to compromise its principles because wrongdoing appears entrenched. It should neither despair nor adopt unjust methods in response. Divine patterns cannot be manipulated by impatience. A cause that claims to serve God while abandoning truthfulness, mercy, and justice has already stepped outside the pattern it invokes.

**Stable principles in a changing world**

Modern life changes rapidly in technology, institutions, and language. The verse says that fundamental moral realities do not change with fashion. Lies still corrode trust. Exploitation still harms the vulnerable. Arrogance still blinds. Courage, repentance, justice, and mercy still bear fruit. A person can become confused if novelty is mistaken for moral exemption. God’s established way is a compass that keeps the believer from believing that new circumstances have abolished old truths.

The next verse provides an immediate example. God restrained the hands of both sides near Makkah after giving the believers an advantage. His unchanging way includes not only defeat of wrong but merciful restraint when restraint better serves His wisdom.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:24

> **He is the One who restrained their hands from you and your hands from them in the valley of Makkah, after He had given you victory over them. And God is Seeing of what you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Restraint on both sides**

The verse is remarkable because it credits God with restraining *their hands from you and your hands from them*. The blessing was reciprocal restraint. A confrontation near Makkah could have produced bloodshed in the sacred precinct and destroyed the larger good the treaty would bring. God prevented the hands of opponents from harming the Muslims and prevented Muslim hands from responding in a way that would cause wider injury.

Reports in the tafsīr literature describe groups from Makkah attempting surprise attacks near al-Ḥudaybiyah. They were apprehended, and the Prophet ﷺ did not permit their killing after they were under control. The precise number differs across reports, but the moral point is clear: the Messenger ﷺ did not make vulnerability an excuse for vengeance. Security and justice were restored without turning the moment into needless slaughter.

**Victory can include the power not to retaliate**

The verse says this restraint came “after He had given you victory over them.” The Muslims had the upper hand in the incident. This detail is decisive. Mercy toward an opponent is most morally weighty when one has the capacity to retaliate. It shows that restraint was not merely enforced by weakness; it was chosen under God’s guidance.

The Prophet ﷺ’s practice gives this a human face. When he entered Makkah after years of persecution, he did not turn the conquest into mass revenge. He announced safety and gave broad amnesty. The details of individual legal cases remained subject to justice, but the overall pattern was mercy, not humiliation.

The Qur’an commands this ethical possibility:

> *“Repel evil with that which is better, and then the one between whom and you there was enmity may become as though he were a devoted friend.”* (Qur’an 41:34)

This is not a command to ignore danger. It is a command to distinguish between necessary protection and the appetite to punish after danger has passed.

**The valley of Makkah and the sanctity of place**

The mention of the valley of Makkah heightens the gravity of the event. Makkah’s Sacred Mosque and surrounding sanctuary have a special inviolability in Islam. Fighting there carries a distinct seriousness. Quraysh had barred peaceful pilgrims from the sanctuary, a wrong the sūrah will name in verse 25. Yet the Muslim response was not permitted to disregard every limit in correcting that wrong.

This teaches a general legal and moral principle: a just cause does not make every method lawful. Sacred ends require sacred means. The believer cannot defend truth by lying, protect honor by injustice, or seek peace through treachery. A moral boundary has value precisely when it remains a boundary under pressure.

**God sees what every party does**

The verse ends with *wa kāna Allāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīrā*: God is Seeing of what you do. The Name *al-Baṣīr*, the All-Seeing, includes both visible acts and the ethical quality of how they are done. He sees an aggressor’s attempt, a defender’s restraint, a leader’s decision, and the internal motive of each person involved.

This is a crucial corrective in conflict. People often judge only outcomes: who appeared strong, who won the argument, who forced a concession. The Qur’an makes conduct visible to the ultimate Judge. The believer therefore asks not only “Did we prevail?” but “Were our hands restrained by justice? Did we honor the limits God set?”

**The everyday valley of Makkah**

Most readers will not face a military confrontation, but many face moments when they have a clear advantage over someone who has harmed them: a spouse’s weakness in an argument, an employee’s mistake, a rival’s public embarrassment, or possession of information that could humiliate another. Verse 24 asks what victory will mean in that moment. Can a person protect truth and a real right while restraining the hand from needless injury? That is not sentimental weakness. It is the strength the Prophet ﷺ modeled, made possible by the knowledge that God sees.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:25

> **They are the ones who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque and kept the offerings from reaching their place of sacrifice. Had it not been for believing men and believing women whom you did not know — lest you trample them and thereby incur blame without knowing — We would have punished those who disbelieved among them with a painful punishment. This was so that God might admit whom He wills into His mercy. Had they been clearly separated, We would have punished the disbelievers among them with a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The wrong that is clearly named**

This verse does not romanticize the treaty. Quraysh’s conduct is explicitly condemned. They disbelieved, barred the Muslims from the Sacred Mosque, and prevented the sacrificial animals from reaching their lawful place. The pilgrims had come with sacrificial offerings and the visible signs of pilgrimage. To obstruct them from worship in the sanctuary was a serious violation, especially from those who claimed guardianship of the Kaʿbah.

The Qur’an has condemned barring people from God’s places of worship before:

> *“And who is more unjust than one who prevents the name of God from being mentioned in His mosques and strives toward their ruin?”* (Qur’an 2:114)

Naming this injustice matters because patience must never be confused with denial. The believers did not say that being barred was right. They did not change their belief about the Sacred Mosque. They accepted temporary restraint while maintaining a truthful account of what had happened.

**Unknown believers changed the moral calculation**

The verse then gives a reason for restraint that the Muslims did not know at the time: there were believing men and believing women in Makkah whose identities were concealed. An attack could have “trampled” them — caused them death or harm without recognizing them. The Arabic image of trampling conveys the terrible possibility of innocent people being caught beneath a force that cannot distinguish them.

This is among the Qur’an’s clearest assertions of the duty to protect innocent life in the chaos of conflict. God withheld permission for a battle not because He was unable to defeat the wrongdoers, but because the human cost included believers unknown to the Muslim force. The lives of those unknown people were not expendable collateral to a political objective.

The Qur’an states the universal gravity of unlawful killing:

> *“Whoever kills a soul — unless for a soul or for corruption in the land — it is as though he has killed all humanity; and whoever saves one, it is as though he has saved all humanity.”* (Qur’an 5:32)

Although this verse has its own context, it expresses the same moral seriousness: human life cannot be treated as a disposable instrument.

**Mercy works through knowledge we do not possess**

The clause “whom you did not know” is central to the sūrah’s spiritual education. The believers could see that they had been barred from worship. They could not see the hidden believers inside Makkah or the chains of consequence that a battle would set in motion. God could see both. The treaty therefore contained a mercy concealed from the people most affected by it.

This principle should be handled carefully. It does not tell victims of injustice to dismiss their pain by guessing at hidden wisdom. Nor does it make every political decision automatically wise. It gives believers humility about the limits of their knowledge. Before demanding a drastic act, one should remember that there may be vulnerable people, histories, harms, and future consequences outside the frame one currently sees.

**The Prophet’s response to the interrupted rites**

When the treaty was concluded, the Prophet ﷺ instructed the Companions to slaughter their sacrificial animals and shave their heads, completing the rites available to them outside Makkah. They were deeply grieved and slow to move, not out of conscious rebellion but from shock and sorrow. Umm Salamah, may God be pleased with her, advised the Prophet ﷺ to begin the action himself. The report records her counsel:

> *“Go out and do not speak to any of them until you have slaughtered your sacrificial animal and called your barber to shave your head.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

He did so, and when the Companions saw him, they followed. The incident displays prophetic leadership, the wisdom of Umm Salamah, and the power of an embodied example when words have reached an emotionally overwhelmed audience.

**“That God may admit whom He wills into His mercy”**

The verse gives a further purpose of restraint: God may admit whom He wills into His mercy. This includes the hidden believers protected in Makkah and may include people who would later enter Islam during the period of peace. The treaty opened social contact and reduced the atmosphere in which tribal propaganda had made honest hearing difficult. Mercy here is not passive indulgence; it is the creation of space in which guidance and repentance can become possible.

The final conditional statement — had the believers and disbelievers been clearly separated — emphasizes that God’s restraint was informed, precise, and just. He distinguished the actual wrongdoers, but the human forces on the ground could not make that distinction without harming the innocent. The verse should make every reader wary of collective punishment, indiscriminate anger, and rhetoric that treats unnamed civilians as morally invisible.

**A rule for power under uncertainty**

The moral logic reaches far beyond warfare. In family, law, workplace discipline, and public policy, people often demand a sweeping response to real wrongdoing. Verse 25 asks whether the response will crush those who are not guilty, who are not visible, or who cannot protect themselves. The presence of one unknown vulnerable person may change what justice requires.

The verse does not weaken justice. It purifies justice of haste. The believer must oppose wrong, but with knowledge, proportion, and mercy. At al-Ḥudaybiyah, God’s restraint protected hidden believers and prepared an opening that a rush toward confrontation could have destroyed.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:26

> **When those who disbelieved placed zealotry in their hearts — the zealotry of the Age of Ignorance — God sent down His tranquillity upon His Messenger and upon the believers, and made them adhere to the word of reverence; they were more worthy of it and entitled to it. And God is Knowing of all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ḥamiyyah: heat that overrules truth**

The verse identifies the interior engine of Quraysh’s resistance: *al-ḥamiyyata ḥamiyyata al-jāhiliyyah*. *Ḥamiyyah* comes from a root connected with heat. It denotes heated partisanship, wounded pride, and an inflamed refusal to yield even when justice requires yielding. It is not healthy courage or principled concern. It is passion that has made the heart too hot to recognize truth.

The addition “of the Age of Ignorance” is particularly important. *Jāhiliyyah* does not simply mean a lack of information or literacy. In Qur’anic usage it describes a moral condition in which inherited pride, tribal loyalty, appetite, and unexamined custom rule over revelation and justice. A person can have much information and still act with the zealotry of jāhiliyyah if ego prevents him from admitting what is right.

At al-Ḥudaybiyah, the Makkans resisted even the wording “In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful” at the beginning of the treaty and would not permit “Muhammad, the Messenger of God” in its written form. The Prophet ﷺ accepted the treaty’s wording rather than let a title become the cause of bloodshed, while never relinquishing the truth that he was God’s Messenger. Their refusal was not an intellectual answer to revelation; it was wounded communal pride.

**Tranquillity is the opposite of zealotry**

The response is striking: God sent down *sakīnah* on the Messenger ﷺ and the believers. The sūrah has returned to this word because *sakīnah* is the direct cure for heated pride. Where *ḥamiyyah* makes a person react to preserve face, *sakīnah* gives the heart freedom to choose what is true even when it feels humiliating in the moment.

The contrast is intensely practical. In an argument, the zealotry of ignorance hears a correction as an insult and answers with escalation. Tranquillity asks, “Is this true? What response will please God? What harm will follow if I speak or act now?” The second person is not less committed; he is more governed by principle than by the need to win a moment.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“The strong person is not the one who overcomes people by his strength, but the strong person is the one who controls himself while angry.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This is the human scale of the verse. The moral victory may be to keep the tongue from words that make reconciliation impossible.

**The word of reverence**

*Kalimat al-taqwā* — “the word of reverence” — was interpreted by most early authorities as the testimony *lā ilāha illā Allāh*, “there is no god but God.” Some commentators include related formulas of faith, such as the basmalah, or the whole word of sincere obedience. The broadest meaning includes the creed that produces *taqwā*: a conscious guarding of oneself through awareness of God.

The testimony is called the word of *taqwā* because it reorders every loyalty. If there is no deity worthy of worship but God, then tribe, party, wealth, anger, and public approval cannot become ultimate masters. The believer may love a family and community deeply, but cannot make them a reason to reject what God has made true.

The Qur’an describes the root of this reverence:

> *“O you who believe, be mindful of God as He deserves to be minded, and do not die except as Muslims, submitting to Him.”* (Qur’an 3:102)

Taqwā is not constant anxiety. It is careful awareness, like a person walking a dangerous path with attention rather than rushing blindly or freezing in fear. The word of faith gives that awareness a center.

**More worthy and entitled to it**

God says the believers were “more worthy of it and entitled to it.” This is not a claim of ethnic or tribal superiority. It means that their faith, humility, and obedience made them fitting recipients of the word of reverence and the tranquillity it carries. Spiritual privilege is not inherited by bloodline or claimed by slogan. It is received through truthful response to God.

The Qur’an gives the universal criterion:

> *“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you.”* (Qur’an 49:13)

The verse therefore turns the historical contrast into self-examination. Does a person’s religious identity make him more humble before truth, more merciful in disagreement, and more able to restrain pride? If not, he may possess the vocabulary of faith while reenacting the *ḥamiyyah* the verse condemns.

**God knows the heat and the restraint**

The ending, “God is Knowing of all things,” assures the reader that no inner condition is hidden. God knew the zealotry in the hearts of the opponents and the sincerity, grief, and restraint in the hearts of the believers. He knows who remains silent from wisdom and who remains silent from cowardice; who speaks boldly from truth and who speaks loudly from ego. This knowledge makes the verse a continual prayer: may God exchange the heat of ignorance in our hearts for the tranquillity and word of reverence He gave to His Messenger ﷺ and the believers.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:27

> **God has certainly fulfilled His Messenger’s vision in truth: you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, if God wills, in security, with your heads shaved or your hair shortened, without fear. He knew what you did not know, and He granted, besides that, a near victory.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A true vision, but not the timetable people assumed**

Before leaving Madinah, the Prophet ﷺ had seen in a dream that he and his Companions would enter Makkah and perform the rites at the Sacred Mosque. Prophets’ true dreams are a form of revelation. The believers naturally expected the vision to be fulfilled on that very journey. When they returned from al-Ḥudaybiyah without entering Makkah, some felt deep disappointment and asked how the vision could be true.

This verse gives the answer: God had fulfilled the vision “in truth,” but the believers had added a timetable that was not contained in the vision. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have answered ʿUmar’s question by asking whether he had said they would enter that year. The distinction is decisive. A promise can be certain in substance while its time and route remain known only to God.

The vision was fulfilled the following year, when the Muslims entered Makkah to perform the lesser pilgrimage in security under the treaty’s terms. The sūrah thus turns disappointment into a lesson in how revelation is understood: do not deny the truth of God’s promise merely because your own first interpretation of its timing was incomplete.

**“If God wills” and the humility of certainty**

The phrase *in shāʾa Allāh* — “if God wills” — appears within a statement that God has certainly fulfilled. It does not introduce doubt into God’s promise. It teaches servants the etiquette of relating even to certain truth: every future event occurs by God’s will, and human beings should not speak as though they possess the future independently.

The Qur’an instructs the Prophet ﷺ:

> *“Do not say of anything, ‘Indeed, I will do that tomorrow,’ except, ‘if God wills.’”* (Qur’an 18:23–24)

Saying “if God wills” should not become an empty verbal habit or a way to avoid responsibility. It is a truthful acknowledgment that one plans and intends, but does not own the future. It teaches confidence without arrogance.

**Shaved or shortened, secure and without fear**

The details of shaved heads and shortened hair are not incidental. They are signs that the pilgrims would complete the rites of *ʿumrah*. Some would shave their heads and some would trim their hair, both recognized acts of release from pilgrimage consecration. The verse gives the believers a concrete picture of the fulfillment they had longed for: not merely entering the city, but entering the Sacred Mosque safely and completing their worship.

The Prophet ﷺ made a special supplication for those who shaved their heads, and then for those who shortened them:

> *“O God, have mercy on those who shave their heads.” They said, “And those who shorten, Messenger of God?” He repeated, “O God, have mercy on those who shave their heads,” and then said, “And those who shorten.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The report illustrates that the rites are not bare symbols. They train humility, equality, and release from ordinary markers of status. People who enter the sanctuary do so as servants before God.

**He knew what you did not know**

This clause is the interpretive center of the verse. God knew the hidden believers in Makkah, the dangers of immediate conflict, the benefits of the truce, the future openings of Khaybar and Makkah, and the way the treaty would allow the message to spread. The Companions knew only the immediate pain of turning back from the sanctuary.

The verse provides a disciplined approach to unexplained delay. It does not tell a person that every desired outcome will occur, nor does it permit invented claims about precisely why a hardship happened. It teaches the certainty of God’s knowledge where ours ends. This is a source of patience, not speculation.

> *“It may be that you dislike a thing while God places much good in it.”* (Qur’an 4:19)

The good may become visible later, as it did at al-Ḥudaybiyah, or may remain part of the unseen wisdom that believers entrust to God.

**Near victory beside delayed fulfillment**

God granted “besides that, a near victory.” The phrasing shows that a delayed desire can be accompanied by another gift already near. The Companions did not enter Makkah that year, but God gave them the treaty, Khaybar, increased security, and the beginning of an opening that would lead to Makkah’s peaceful conquest. The wise believer learns to recognize the gifts that arrive in an unexpected form rather than staring only at the one delayed gift.

In ordinary life, this may mean that a closed path brings a different ability, friendship, safety, or spiritual maturity that would otherwise not have been gained. One should grieve losses honestly, but also remain attentive to the nearer victories God may already be giving.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:28

> **He is the One who sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, to make it prevail over every religion. And sufficient is God as Witness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance before prevalence**

The verse defines the Prophet’s mission by two gifts: *al-hudā*, guidance, and *dīn al-ḥaqq*, the religion of truth. Guidance is knowledge of what is true and the path to live by it. The religion of truth is Islam as the final revelation sent to Muhammad ﷺ, confirming the original call of the earlier prophets: worship God alone, obey His messengers, act justly, and prepare for the Hereafter.

The Qur’an presents this continuity clearly:

> *“He has prescribed for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah, and what We revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: establish the religion and do not be divided concerning it.”* (Qur’an 42:13)

Muslims are required to believe in the revelations and messengers God sent before Muhammad ﷺ. At the same time, they believe that the Qur’an is the final, protected criterion and that obedience to the final Messenger ﷺ is now part of obedience to God. This is neither contempt for earlier prophets nor a denial of their true revelation. It is belief in the completion of the prophetic chain.

**What does “make it prevail” mean?**

The Arabic *li-yuẓhirahu ʿalā al-dīni kullihi* can convey making the religion of truth manifest, superior in proof, victorious, or predominant over every other way of life. Classical commentators mention these connected dimensions. Islam prevailed in its proofs: its message of pure monotheism, moral accountability, and prophetic continuity answered the falsehoods of idolatry. It prevailed historically in the Arabian Peninsula during the Prophet’s life and then spread beyond it. Many scholars also connect complete universal manifestation with events still to come by God’s decree.

The verse must not be read as a command for forced conversion. The Qur’an explicitly says:

> *“There is no compulsion in religion. Truth has become clear from error.”* (Qur’an 2:256)

Truth prevails most fundamentally by being made clear, embodied with justice, protected from suppression, and granted success by God. A coerced statement cannot create sincere faith in the heart. The Prophet’s mission was to convey, invite, establish justice, and remove barriers to truthful hearing; final guidance remains God’s gift.

**Religion of truth as a lived moral order**

*Dīn* is wider than a private set of beliefs. It includes worship, moral responsibility, law, community, and accountability before God. Calling it the religion “of truth” means its claims about God, the human being, revelation, and the Hereafter are not made to flatter temporary desire. It tells the wealthy that wealth is a trust, the powerful that power will be judged, the weak that they have dignity before God, and all people that death does not erase moral responsibility.

The verse therefore challenges Muslims first. Islam cannot be said to “prevail” in a meaningful moral sense when Muslims ignore its justice, trustworthiness, mercy, prayer, and care for the vulnerable. The most persuasive witness to the religion is not triumphal speech but a community whose conduct makes the guidance recognizable.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“The best of you are those who are best in character.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Good character is not a peripheral decoration to creed. It is part of the truthful manifestation of the religion.

**God is sufficient as Witness**

The verse ends, *wa kafā bi-llāhi shahīdā*: God is sufficient as Witness. He witnesses that the Messenger ﷺ was sent with guidance; He witnesses the truth of revelation; and He witnesses how people respond to it. The phrase frees the believer from desperate dependence on human validation. A truth may be mocked, ignored, or opposed for a time without becoming less true.

It also restrains religious boasting. God is the sufficient Witness, so no Muslim needs to fabricate achievements, demean others, or force appearances of success in order to prove the religion. The task is faithful witness. God knows the final manifestation of His guidance better than any generation does.

## Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:29

> **Muhammad is the Messenger of God. Those with him are firm against the disbelievers and merciful among themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, seeking bounty from God and His good pleasure. Their mark is on their faces from the effect of prostration. That is their description in the Torah. And their description in the Gospel is like a plant that puts forth its shoot, then strengthens it, so it grows thick and stands firm upon its stalk, delighting the sowers, so that through them He may enrage the disbelievers. God has promised those of them who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and a tremendous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The final identity: Messenger of God**

The sūrah ends with the sentence whose deletion from the written treaty had pained the believers: *Muḥammadun rasūlu Allāh* — Muhammad is the Messenger of God. What was removed from a temporary document is restored by revelation in permanent form. Quraysh’s refusal to write the title could not alter its truth. The verse therefore closes the sūrah with quiet certainty, not with revenge. God Himself bears witness to the rank that human opponents had denied.

To call Muhammad ﷺ the Messenger of God means that he is a human servant chosen to receive, convey, and explain revelation. Muslims love him, obey his authentic Sunnah, send blessings upon him, and honor his household and Companions. They do not worship him. His highest rank is inseparable from his servanthood to God.

The Qur’an instructs the believers:

> *“There has certainly come to you a Messenger from among yourselves, grievous to him is what you suffer, deeply concerned for you, and to the believers compassionate and merciful.”* (Qur’an 9:128)

The final portrait of the sūrah will show the community being shaped by the same prophetic qualities: strength governed by principle, mercy among believers, worship, hope, and steady growth.

**Firmness is not cruelty; mercy is not weakness**

“Those with him are firm against the disbelievers and merciful among themselves.” The phrase *ashiddāʾu ʿalā al-kuffār* must be read within the sūrah’s setting of active hostility from forces seeking to block worship and harm the community. It denotes firmness, resolve, and refusal to surrender faith to hostile pressure. It does not authorize cruelty toward every non-Muslim person, individual hostility, or injustice toward peaceful neighbors.

The Qur’an itself sets the boundary:

> *“God does not forbid you from being kind and just toward those who do not fight you because of religion and do not drive you from your homes. Indeed, God loves those who are just.”* (Qur’an 60:8)

The believers’ firmness means they do not dilute truth, betray their community, or cooperate in oppression. Their mercy among themselves means they do not reproduce the harshness of the outside conflict within their own ranks. A community unable to show compassion to its own members has not understood this verse, however loudly it speaks of strength.

The Prophet ﷺ made mercy a condition of religious maturity:

> *“The merciful are shown mercy by the Most Merciful. Be merciful to those on earth, and the One above the heavens will be merciful to you.”* (Sunan al-Tirmidhī; graded sound)

The verse thus gives a two-sided ethic: principled resilience where truth is attacked, and tenderness, forgiveness, service, and mutual protection within the community of faith.

**Bodies that testify through worship**

“You see them bowing and prostrating.” Their loyalty is not merely political or tribal; its deepest source is worship. *Rukūʿ* and *sujūd* — bowing and prostration — are physical acts in which the body expresses the truth that the heart acknowledges: God alone is greatest, and the servant is not self-sufficient.

Prostration is especially significant. The forehead, a visible symbol of human dignity, is placed upon the earth before the Creator. This is not degradation. It is liberation from every false master — ego, public opinion, wealth, fear, and power. A person who willingly bows before God is less likely to bow inwardly before what is beneath God.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“The nearest a servant is to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so increase supplication.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The community at the end of Sūrah al-Fatḥ is therefore not defined first by victory or spoils. Its visible distinguishing act is prayer. Its members seek *faḍlan mina Allāh wa riḍwānā* — bounty from God and His good pleasure. They ask for provision, but they do not stop at provision; they seek the Giver’s approval.

**The mark of prostration is humility’s trace**

“Their mark is on their faces from the effect of prostration.” Classical commentators recorded several explanations. Some understood it as light, humility, or a gentle dignity evident in the believer’s face and conduct. Others related it to the physical trace that may appear from frequent prostration. Others connected it to the radiance believers will have on the Day of Resurrection. These meanings need not all be forced into one narrow physical sign.

The safest and richest reading is that sincere worship leaves an effect. It may be visible in calmness, modesty, truthfulness, patience, and a face not hardened by arrogance. A mark on the forehead alone proves nothing; someone may acquire it through habit while carrying pride or injustice. The Qur’an’s concern is the effect of *sujūd*, not a bodily badge detached from the heart.

The Qur’an describes the inner purpose of worship:

> *“Successful indeed are the believers: those who are humble in their prayer.”* (Qur’an 23:1–2)

Humility is the mark that joins prayer to character. The believer should ask not only, “How often do I prostrate?” but also, “What is my prostration doing to my treatment of people?”

**Remembered in the Torah and Gospel**

The verse says that this is their likeness in the Torah, and then gives their likeness in the Gospel through the image of a growing plant. Muslims affirm that God sent the Torah to Moses and the Gospel to Jesus, peace be upon them both, and that the original revelations contained truth and guidance. The Qur’an does not require a Muslim to identify a specific surviving sentence in every present manuscript in order to affirm its own statement. Its claim is that the righteous community gathered around the final Messenger fits a pattern already known in the history of revelation.

This connection dignifies the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions by placing them within the single line of prophetic guidance, not outside it. The final message is not an isolated invention; it confirms the enduring call of the prophets to worship God and live righteously.

**A plant that grows by stages**

The Gospel likeness is one of the Qur’an’s most beautiful communal images. The plant puts forth a shoot, strengthens it, grows thick, and stands firmly on its stalk, delighting the sowers. Every stage matters. At first the Muslim community was small and fragile. The Prophet ﷺ was the first root and planter by God’s permission; the Companions were the shoots around him. Through revelation, sacrifice, teaching, prayer, mutual care, and divine aid, they gained strength without severing themselves from the root.

The image corrects fantasies of instant transformation. Healthy plants grow gradually. They require roots, water, light, cultivation, and time. A community that seeks strength while neglecting worship, knowledge, character, and mutual mercy is like a person demanding fruit from a plant whose roots were never tended. It may look impressive briefly, but it cannot stand.

The phrase “delighting the sowers” recognizes the joy of those who see patient labor bear fruit. Teachers, parents, scholars, community servants, and all sincere workers for good know this joy: a child learns integrity, a student becomes useful, a fractured relationship heals, a community becomes more faithful. The Qur’an honors steady cultivation over spectacle.

**Growth that unsettles opponents of truth**

The verse says that through them God may enrage the disbelievers. In context, this refers to those who actively hated the growth of Islam and sought to extinguish its light. Their rage does not define the believers’ conduct; it is a consequence of seeing a truthful, rooted community survive the campaign against it. The believers are not commanded to seek others’ anger or to make provocation a religious goal. They are commanded to become what the verse describes: worshipful, merciful, firm, and fruitful.

A person who hates justice may indeed be troubled when justice grows. A person invested in prejudice may resent a community that lives with dignity and solidarity. The Qur’anic response is not reciprocal hatred; it is steadfastness in good character and trust in God’s judgment.

**The promise is tied to faith and righteous deeds**

The sūrah ends with a careful qualification: God has promised forgiveness and a tremendous reward *to those among them who believe and do righteous deeds*. The phrase does not turn the Companions’ honor into a blank cheque for anyone who merely claims affiliation. It establishes the Qur’anic rule for every generation: forgiveness and great reward belong to faith that bears fruit in righteous action.

The ending gathers the whole sūrah. Its opening promised the Prophet ﷺ forgiveness, completed favor, guidance, and mighty help. Its conclusion promises believers forgiveness and tremendous reward. Between these promises lies the road by which a community is formed: trust during delay, tranquillity instead of zealotry, fidelity to covenant, mercy under power, worship, and patient growth.

Sūrah al-Fatḥ therefore leaves the reader with a mature picture of victory. Victory is not merely overcoming an opponent. It is becoming, by God’s grace, a community whose roots are faith, whose posture is worship, whose internal life is mercy, whose public stance is principled, and whose growth stands firm enough to delight those who cultivate good. This is the opening that no temporary obstruction can finally close.
