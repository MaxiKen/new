# Sūrah al-Zumar (Chapter 39) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Named for Two Processions**

Sūrah al-Zumar, "The Throngs" or "The Groups," takes its name from the two great processions with which it closes: the disbelievers driven to Hell in throngs (v. 71) and the believers driven to the Garden in throngs (v. 73). The word *zumar* (sing. *zumrah*) names a company moving together — not a scattered crowd, but ordered ranks. The sūrah therefore ends as a sorting of mankind into two files, after having spent seventy verses teaching why those files form. It is also known as *al-Ghuraf*, "The Abodes," after the mention in verse 20 of the lofty chambers prepared for those who reverence their Lord. Both titles point to destination: one names the companies that arrive, the other names the dwellings that wait.

Most of the sūrah is Makkan, revealed in the middle of the Makkan period, immediately after Sūrah 34, Sabaʾ. A number of commentators — among them al-Ālūsī and Ibn al-Jawzī — hold that verses 53–55 are Madinan, revealed concerning Waḥshī, the Abyssinian slave who gained his freedom by killing the Prophet's uncle Ḥamzah at Uḥud in 3/625 and later came fearing that no repentance remained for him. Some restrict that occasion to verse 53 alone (Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭūsī); others extend it through verses 53–59. A further minority opinion places verses 10 and 23 in the Madinan period. The opening verse can be read as a continuation of the last two verses of Sūrah Ṣād, so that the Book's own sequence already binds this chapter to the one before it.

**Pure Religion as the Spine of the Chapter**

From one angle the central theme is *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity, purification, the devotion of religion entirely to God. The sūrah presses this demand more densely than any other chapter: verses 2, 11, and 14 each command worship of God with religion devoted entirely to Him, and verse 3 declares that unto God belongs the *pure* religion. Around that spine the chapter constructs a long contrast between two human types: those whose hearts are single, and those whose hearts are divided among protectors, idols, aspirations, and excuses. Rhetorical questions punctuate the contrast: *Are those who know and those who do not know equal?* (v. 9). Declarations of guidance and misguidance frame it: *Whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he; and whomsoever God guides, none shall lead him astray* (vv. 36–37). A passage on death (vv. 42–52) shows the desperate end of those who disbelieve. Then comes verse 53, which many scholars have called among the most hopeful verses of the Qur'an, opening the door of mercy even to those who have been prodigal against their own souls. The remaining verses exhort repentance and then display, at length, the two ends of the two companies in the Hereafter (vv. 68–75).

**A Night Recitation of the Prophet**

According to a report from ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited al-Zumar and Banū Isrāʾīl (Sūrah 17, al-Isrāʾ). Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both transmit the report. The pairing is not accidental: al-Isrāʾ opens with the Night Journey and the signs of God in the heavens; al-Zumar closes with the Throne, the angels encircling it, and the praise that ends all history. Together they bookend a night: the servant recites the journey of the Messenger and then the journey of every soul to its Lord, and only then sleeps.

What follows treats each of the seventy-five verses as an independent discourse. Verses that the source commentary groups (30–31, 36–37, 39–40, 43–44, 69–70, 71–72) are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is long.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:1

> **The revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Without a Verb, Because the Fact Needs None**

The sūrah opens with a noun-sentence that contains no verb: *tanzīlu al-kitābi mina Allāhi al-ʿAzīzi al-Ḥakīm*. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī note that such openings can be read with an implied demonstrative: "This is the revelation of the Book from God." The silence of the verb is itself a claim. A verb would narrate an event that happened; a noun-sentence presents a standing fact. The Book is not being introduced as a recent occurrence in Makkah; it is being identified as what it is — a sending-down whose origin is God. The same formula, with only the last Name changed, opens Sūrahs 40, 45, and 46:

> *"The revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Knowing."* (Qur'an 40:2)

> *"The revelation of the Book from God, the Mighty, the Wise."* (Qur'an 45:2; 46:2)

The recurrence teaches that several doors of the Qur'an open on the same lintel: before any argument, warning, or story, the origin is named. Whoever disputes what follows must first dispute *from whom* it came.

**Tanzīl: Speech That Travels Downward**

*Tanzīl* is a verbal noun of the intensive pattern: not merely "a sending" but a sending-down in successive, measured descents. The Qur'an uses *nazzala* and *anzala* throughout to describe revelation as cargo lowered from a height. The image is not of a man reaching up to seize insight, but of the High reaching down with an address. That verticality governs the believer's posture toward the text: one does not audit a letter from the King by the standards of the village post office. It also binds this verse to verse 2, which will add *bi'l-ḥaqq* — "in truth" — and to verse 5, which will say that God created the heavens and the earth *in truth*. Revelation and creation share one adverb because they share one Author.

**Two Names That Frame Every Command That Follows**

The verse does not end with "from God." It ends with two Names: *al-ʿAzīz*, the Mighty, and *al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise. Al-Rāzī observes that pairing might with wisdom is a standing Qur'anic habit at the head of legislative and doctrinal sūrahs, because might without wisdom is tyranny, and wisdom without might is advice that cannot be enforced. *Al-ʿAzīz* names the One who cannot be overpowered, whose decree is not vetoed, whose Book cannot be cancelled by the councils of Makkah. *Al-Ḥakīm* names the One whose every word is placed with purpose — nothing random, nothing excessive, nothing missing. Together they tell the first hearers: what you are about to be commanded (sincere worship, exclusive devotion, patience, repentance) issues from Power that can carry it out and from Wisdom that has measured it to the soul.

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on the parallel openings, notes that these Names also answer two pagan objections at once. Against those who said the Prophet ﷺ invented the recitation, *from God* is the chain of custody. Against those who said a human book could not bind a tribe, *the Mighty* is the Enforcer. Against those who found the commands strange, *the Wise* is the Legislator's credential.

**Hearing the Book as If from God Himself**

The Sufi commentators, recorded in the source tradition, describe three levels of Qur'anic hearing. First, one hears as if the Prophet ﷺ were reciting. Then, as if Gabriel were reciting. Then, as if God Himself were reciting. They sometimes cite this verse and 40:2 in support of the third level, because the sentence attributes the sending-down to God without intermediary named in the clause. The pedagogical point does not require the full Sufi itinerary to be accepted: even the sober jurists agree that the reciter is not performing literature. He is receiving, in the present tense of recitation, a speech whose origin is the Mighty, the Wise. That is why the Prophet ﷺ taught refuge before recitation:

> *"When you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan."* (Qur'an 16:98)

The refuge is not a superstition. It is the acknowledgment that what follows is not ordinary speech, and that the heart which receives it must be guarded.

**A Threshold for a Sūrah About Sincerity**

Set at the head of a chapter whose burden is *ikhlāṣ* — the purification of worship from every partner — this verse performs a necessary first service. Before the servant is told to devote religion entirely to God, he is told whose Book is speaking. Sincerity toward a text of uncertain origin is impossible; one cannot give the whole heart to a rumor. By naming origin, might, and wisdom in a single breath, verse 1 makes the coming command of verse 2 rational: worship God, because this is His speech, and He is able and wise enough to deserve the whole of the religion.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:2

> **Indeed, We have sent down unto thee the Book in truth; so worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Identification to Address**

Verse 1 named the Book in the third person. Verse 2 turns to the second person and names the recipient: *innā anzalnā ilayka al-kitāba bi'l-ḥaqq* — "Indeed We have sent down unto thee the Book in truth." The shift from *tanzīl* (the fact of sending-down) to *anzalnā ilayka* (We sent it down *to you*) is the shift from theology to commission. The Prophet ﷺ is not a finder of a book; he is the addressed trustee of a sending. The emphatic *innā* ("indeed We") and the royal plural of majesty close the door on any theory of human authorship in the same motion that opens the door of duty.

**"In Truth": Why the Adverb Is Not Ornament**

*Bi'l-ḥaqq* — in truth, with truth, by truth — is one of the Qur'an's most repeated adverbs of revelation. It appears of this Book in 2:176, 2:213, 3:3, 4:105, 5:48, 6:114, 39:41, and 42:17. In 4:105 the same sending-down in truth is immediately tied to judgment:

> *"Indeed, We have sent down to you the Book in truth, that you may judge between people by that which God has shown you."* (Qur'an 4:105)

Here the same sending-down is tied to worship. The two sequels are not rivals. Judgment without worship becomes politics; worship without the truth of the Book becomes private emotion. Al-Zamakhsharī reads *bi'l-ḥaqq* as both the content (what is sent is true) and the purpose (it is sent *for* the establishing of truth). Al-Rāzī adds a third sense: the sending itself is a true act, not a metaphor or a dream. All three senses serve the coming command. One does not devote an entire religion to a maybe.

**The Command That Is the Sūrah's Spine**

*Fa-uʿbudi Allāha mukhliṣan lahu al-dīn* — "so worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him." The *fāʾ* ("so") is causal: because the Book is from God in truth, therefore worship. *Uʿbud* is the imperative of *ʿibādah*, worship and servitude together — not a ceremony extracted from life, but the orientation of the whole self. Ibn Taymiyyah's definition, which became standard, is the right measure of the word:

> *"Worship is a comprehensive name for everything that God loves and is pleased with, of sayings and deeds, inward and outward."* (Ibn Taymiyyah, *Kitāb al-ʿUbūdiyyah*)

Under that definition the command of this verse is not "perform more rites." It is "give God the whole range of what He loves, and give it unmixed."

**Ikhlāṣ: The Word That Means Unmixed**

*Mukhliṣan* is the active participle of *ikhlāṣ*, from the root *kh-l-ṣ*, which in Arabic means to purify, to strain, to make a thing free of admixture — as one strains butter from milk or gold from ore. *Dīn* here is the entire way of life offered to God: prayer, vow, fear, hope, trust, law, love. The verse does not say "be sincere in some of your religion." It says: make the religion itself *khāliṣ* — unalloyed — *for Him*. The same construction returns in this sūrah at verses 11 and 14, and elsewhere at 7:29, 10:22, 29:65, 31:32, 40:14, 40:65, and 98:5:

> *"And they were not commanded except to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him, as ḥunafāʾ, and to establish the prayer and give the alms — and that is the religion of standing truth."* (Qur'an 98:5)

Al-Zamakhsharī glosses the pair of verses 2 and 3 together: God requires that obedience to Him be total and purified of any defect. Al-Rāzī calls *ikhlāṣ* "the leader of all devotional acts" — the quality without which the rest of the caravan does not move.

The Prophet ﷺ placed this quality at the root of every deed in the first ḥadīth of nearly every collection:

> *"Actions are only by intentions, and every person shall have only what he intended. So whoever's emigration was to God and His Messenger, his emigration was to God and His Messenger; and whoever's emigration was to a worldly gain he could obtain or a woman he could marry, his emigration was to that to which he emigrated."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The ḥadīth is the practical tafsīr of this verse. Two men may perform the same bowing; the verse asks to whom the bowing was strained and purified.

**Why Sincerity Must Be Commanded, Not Assumed**

The human heart does not default to singleness. It defaults to mixture: a little God, a little reputation, a little fear of people, a little love of comfort. The Makkan environment made the mixture almost official — God acknowledged as Creator, then surrounded with intercessors "to bring us nigh," as verse 3 will quote them saying. The verse therefore does not congratulate the Prophet ﷺ on sincerity already achieved; it commands it as the first fruit of receiving the Book. The logic is the logic of a trust: a trustee who mixes the principal with his own pocket has already betrayed the trust, even if the amount mixed is small.

A useful analogy is food offered to a guest. If a generous table is laid and then a single drop of what the guest cannot eat is stirred into every dish, the whole table is spoiled — not because the drop outweighed the feast, but because mixture itself was the offense. *Ikhlāṣ* is the refusal to stir any other diners into God's portion.

**What the Command Asks of a Modern Life**

The verse's modern pressure is exact. Contemporary life multiplies audiences: employers, followers, families, algorithms, one's own self-image. Each audience asks to be a partner in the religion — a reason to pray, a reason to give, a reason to speak. The verse does not forbid living among audiences; it forbids *sharing the religion* with them. Work may be seen; worship's object may not be shared. The daily test is simple and severe: if the watchers vanished, would the deed remain? If it would not, it was never *mukhliṣ*. The sūrah will spend the next seventy-three verses showing what happens to mixed religion and what is prepared for the unmixed.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:3

> **Behold! Unto God belongs the pure religion, and those who take protectors apart from Him [say], "We do not worship them, save to bring us nigh in nearness unto God." Truly God will judge between them regarding that wherein they differ. Truly God does not guide one who is a disbelieving liar.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Religion as God's Property**

*Alā lillāhi al-dīnu al-khāliṣ* — "Behold! Unto God belongs the pure religion." The particle *alā* is a call to attention, the verbal equivalent of a hand raised. What it announces is a claim of ownership: the *dīn* that is *khāliṣ* — pure, strained, unmixed — belongs to God, not to the worshipper who offers it and not to the intermediaries who claim a share in it. The root *kh-l-ṣ* is the same root as *mukhliṣ* in verse 2. Together the two verses teach a double truth: God requires purified religion from the servant (v. 2), and purified religion is already His by right (v. 3). The servant does not invent sincerity as a gift to God; he returns to God what was always God's.

Al-Zamakhsharī reads the pair as a demand that obedience be total and free of defect. Al-Rāzī, as noted, calls *ikhlāṣ* the leader of all acts of devotion. Both readings converge on a practical point: mixed worship is not a lesser form of the pure religion. It is another religion.

**The Excuse That Has Never Died**

The verse then quotes the theology of the partners: those who take *awliyāʾ* — protectors, patrons, friends-in-power — apart from God, and say, *mā naʿbuduhum illā li-yuqarribūnā ilā Allāhi zulfā* — "We do not worship them except to bring us nigh in nearness unto God." The confession is historically Makkan. The Quraysh did not deny Allāh as Creator; they surrounded Him with named beings — al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt, and others — whom they described as daughters or intercessors. Their theory was a theory of access: the great King is too high; one approaches Him through courtiers.

The Qur'an treats this theory not as a near-miss of monotheism but as its contradiction. Nearness to God is not brokered. The Book elsewhere records the end of the brokers:

> *"Why, then, did they — whom they had taken as gods apart from God and as a means of drawing nigh [unto God] — not help them? Nay, they forsook them."* (Qur'an 46:28; cf. 34:40–41)

The excuse fails twice: the intermediaries cannot help, and the worship offered to them was never a ladder to God. It was a substitution.

**Why Intercession-Religion Feels Reasonable — and Why It Is Not**

The psychology of the excuse is easy to recognize, which is why the verse bothers to quote it rather than merely condemn it. Human beings approach powerful people through secretaries, and they naturally project the same architecture onto heaven. The error is a category error about God. A human king is limited in attention; God is not. A human king is approached through protocol because he can be ignorant of petitioners; God *knows what lies within breasts* (v. 7). A human king may be flattered by a court; God is *beyond need* of the worshippers themselves (v. 7). To install intermediaries "for nearness" is therefore to treat God as if He were a man. That is why verse 4 will immediately deny that God takes a child, and why verse 67 will say they did not measure God with His true measure.

The same reflex survives wherever people treat saints, graves, amulets, parties, or "the universe" as the real addressee of need, while God is retained as a distant chairman. The verse's quote is a mirror, not only an archive.

**Judgment Reserved, Guidance Withheld**

Two closings complete the verse. First: *inna Allāha yaḥkumu baynahum fī mā hum fīhi yakhtalifūn* — "Truly God will judge between them regarding that wherein they differ." The same assurance appears at 2:113, 10:93, 16:124, 32:25, and 45:17. Differences about God that cannot be settled in history will be settled by God. The clause is both a consolation to the Prophet ﷺ — you are not required to win every argument — and a warning to the arguers: delay of verdict is not absence of verdict.

Second: *inna Allāha lā yahdī man huwa kādhibun kaffār* — "Truly God does not guide one who is a disbelieving liar." Two nouns, not one. *Kādhib* is a liar — here, one who lies about God, describing Him as needing partners. *Kaffār* is an intensive of *kufr*: a persistent coverer of truth, an ingrate. Guidance is not denied arbitrarily; it is denied to the one who has made lying about God and covering the truth into a character. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the clause as the moral consequence of the excuse itself: a theology built on a false report about God cannot be a road to God.

**The Difference Between Asking the Righteous to Pray and Making Them Lords**

A necessary distinction, which later scholarship labored to keep clear, belongs here. The Qur'an itself affirms intercession *by God's leave* (2:255; 20:109; 34:23; 53:26), and this sūrah will say in verse 44 that intercession belongs entirely to God — meaning He owns it and grants it. Asking a living righteous person to supplicate for one is not the worship quoted in this verse. Taking protectors *apart from Him*, worshipping them, and justifying the worship as a ladder — that is the quoted religion. The difference is the difference between asking a fellow servant to ask the King, and kneeling to the fellow servant as if he were the door. Verse 3 is aimed at the second.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:4

> **Had God wanted to take a child, He would have chosen whatsoever He willed from that which He created. Glory be to Him; He is the One, the Paramount.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Hypothetical That Destroys the Claim**

The verse proceeds by concession-and-collapse, a method the Qur'an uses when an opponent's premise is being dismantled from inside. *Law arāda Allāhu an yattakhidha waladan* — "Had God wanted to take a child" — grants, for a moment, the opponent's verb. Then the rest of the clause takes it away: *la-ṣṭafā mimmā yakhluqu mā yashāʾ* — "He would have chosen whatsoever He willed from that which He created." Even in the impossible hypothetical, the "child" would be a creature selected from creation, not a partner in divinity. The argument is surgical. It does not merely say "God has no son." It says: sonship, as you mean it, cannot attach to the Creator, because anything He might "take" would already be *from that which He created* — and a created thing is not a child of the Uncreated in any sense that shares His essence.

Al-Zamakhsharī, given the Makkan setting and the previous verse's criticism of the idolaters, reads the primary target as the Quraysh's attribution of daughters to God — a notion the Qur'an attacks in many places (2:116; 6:100; 17:40, 111; 18:4; 19:88–93; 21:26; 37:149, 153; 43:16, 81–82; 52:39; 72:3). Ibn Kathīr notes that some commentators also hear in it a rejection of the Christian claim that Jesus is the son of God, and of the claim attributed in 9:30 to some Jews regarding Ezra. The wording is wide enough to cover every ascription of offspring. The historical first address, in a Makkan sūrah, is the pagan ascription.

**Subḥānahu: The Word That Clears the Name**

*Subḥānahu* — "Glory be to Him," or more literally, "He is far above." *Tasbīḥ* is the act of declaring God free of every unbecoming attribute. The word is placed exactly where the unbecoming attribute (taking a child) has been named, so that the tongue that has just heard the falsehood is immediately given the cleaning formula. The Qur'an uses this same exclamation wherever sonship is denied:

> *"They say: 'God has taken a child.' Glory be to Him! He is the Self-Sufficient."* (Qur'an 10:68)

> *"And they say: 'The Compassionate has taken a child.' You have certainly come forth with a monstrous thing."* (Qur'an 19:88–89)

The pattern is doctrine as liturgy: the denial is not left as a proposition; it is made into praise.

**Al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār: Oneness That Cannot Be Partnered**

The verse closes with two Names: *huwa Allāhu al-Wāḥidu al-Qahhār* — "He is the One, the Paramount." *Al-Wāḥid* is oneness of essence, of right to worship, of action: not a one among others, but the One to whom no second can be added. *Al-Qahhār* is the intensive of *qahr* — overpowering, subduing, being so dominant that resistance is not a real option. The pairing is precise. Oneness without overpowering might be a lonely unit in a crowded field; overpowering without oneness might be a tyrant among rivals. Together they say: there is one God, and nothing stands up to Him. Sūrah Ṣād, which immediately precedes this chapter, had already used the Name:

> *"What, has he made the gods into one God? This is indeed an astounding thing."* ... *"The Lord of the heavens and the earth and what is between them, the Mighty, the Forgiving."* (Qur'an 38:5, 66)

And 38:65: *"There is no god but God, the One, the Paramount."* The present verse continues that proclamation into a sūrah whose entire argument is that religion must be as unpartnered as God is.

**Why "Taking a Child" Is Not an Honor to God**

People sometimes imagine that ascribing a child to God is a form of praise — as if fertility, dynasty, and succession were perfections. The verse teaches the opposite. Childbearing belongs to creatures who perpetuate themselves because they die. God does not die, does not need an heir, and does not produce from His essence a second self. To honor Him with a child is to honor Him with a need. The Qur'an's repeated *subḥānahu* is therefore not a nervous refusal of intimacy with God; it is the protection of God's actual perfection from a compliment that would diminish Him.

The same logic judges every later attempt to "humanize" God by giving Him a family, a biography of need, or a partner who "completes" Him. Completeness that requires a second is not completeness. The One, the Paramount, is already complete; the pure religion of verses 2–3 is the only worship that matches that completeness.

**Living Under a God Who Has No Heir**

Practically, the verse frees the servant from two anxieties. First, the anxiety of the court: if God had a family or a cabinet of divine relatives, religion would become the art of knowing whom to please beside Him. Second, the anxiety of succession: pagan and imperial religions often bind piety to dynasties because their gods, like their kings, must be succeeded. A God who does not take a child cannot be inherited by a tribe, a church's bloodline, or a state's mythology. The servant deals with God directly, now, as verse 3 already required — and the next verses will show that this God is the same One who created the heavens, the night, the day, and the child in the womb.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:5

> **He created the heavens and the earth in truth. He rolls the night up into the day and rolls the day up into the night, and He made the sun and the moon subservient, each running for a term appointed. Is He not the Mighty, the Forgiving?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation "in Truth" as the Twin of Revelation "in Truth"**

*Khalaqa al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa bi'l-ḥaqq* — "He created the heavens and the earth in truth." The adverb is the same *bi'l-ḥaqq* that described the sending-down of the Book in verse 2. The sūrah is quietly teaching that revelation and creation are bound by one underlying reality. Creation is, in a sense, God's first revelation: a world that is not a joke, not a waste, not a random heap. The same claim is repeated across the Book (6:73; 16:3; 29:44; 45:22; 64:3), and elsewhere as the denial of play:

> *"We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them in play."* (Qur'an 21:16; cf. 44:38–39)

If the world is created in truth, then worship mixed with falsehood (vv. 2–4) is not only a religious error. It is a mismatch with the structure of existence.

**The Rolling of Night and Day**

*Yukawwiru al-layla ʿalā al-nahāri wa yukawwiru al-nahāra ʿalā al-layl* — "He rolls the night up into the day and rolls the day up into the night." The verb *kawwara* is the verb of wrapping a turban, coiling a thing upon a thing so that one covers the other by degrees. The commentators note the precision: night is not switched off; it is wound over the day, and the day wound over the night, in a continuous wrapping. Elsewhere the same phenomenon is described as making the night pass into the day and the day into the night (22:61; 31:29; 35:13; 57:6; cf. 3:27). The image is daily, public, and undeniable. Anyone who has watched dusk knows the wrapping. The verse takes that common sight and names its Agent, immediately after denying that this Agent has a child. The argument is: the One who wraps the entire sky around the earth every evening is not in need of offspring.

**Sun and Moon Made Subservient**

*Wa sakhkhara al-shamsa wa'l-qamar* — "and He made the sun and the moon subservient." *Taskhīr* is the placing of a thing into useful service, the way a river is made to turn a mill. The sun and moon are not gods, not independent powers, not objects of worship. They are employees of a term: *kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā* — "each running for a term appointed." The same subservience is mentioned at 7:54, 13:2, 29:61, 31:29, and 35:13, and is made explicitly *for human beings* at 14:33:

> *"And He has made the sun and the moon subservient unto you, constant, and He made the night and the day subservient unto you."* (Qur'an 14:33; cf. 16:12; 45:12–13)

Two doctrines sit in the one clause. Against paganism: the celestial bodies are servants, not lords. Against human arrogance: their service to us is a vicegerency we did not invent (6:165; 10:14; 35:39), a trust that will be audited. The "term appointed" further binds astronomy to eschatology. The race of sun and moon is not endless; it runs toward a named end, as this sūrah will show when the trumpet is blown (v. 68) and the heavens are enfolded (v. 67).

**"Is He Not the Mighty, the Forgiving?"**

The verse ends with a question that is really a verdict: *alā huwa al-ʿAzīzu al-Ghafūr* — "Is He not the Mighty, the Forgiving?" Verse 1 opened with *the Mighty, the Wise*. Verse 5 closes the creation-panel with *the Mighty, the Forgiving*. Might has been displayed in the wrapping of day and night and the subjugation of sun and moon. Forgiveness is named because the same Power that could crush the deniers of verses 3–4 is the Power that still addresses them. Al-Rāzī and others note that pairing *ʿAzīz* with *Ghafūr* after a creation-sign is a moral invitation: the One who owns the sky is able to punish, and able to pardon. The question-form invites the hearer's own tongue to answer yes — and once the tongue has said yes, the religion of partners in verse 3 has already lost.

**The Daily Reminder Written on the Sky**

The practical yield of the verse is a way of seeing. Dawn and dusk are not empty weather. They are the wrapping of a King who has no child, whose Book is true, and whose forgiveness is still on offer. A person who watches the sun set as *taskhīr* — a servant running its term — is less likely to worship careers, celestial omens, or his own stamina as if they were lords. The term appointed for the sun is also a parable of the term appointed for the watcher. Verse 6 will bring the same Author from the sky down into the womb.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:6

> **He created you from a single soul, then made from it its mate, and sent down for you of cattle eight pairs. He creates you in your mothers' wombs, creation after creation, in threefold darkness. That is God, your Lord; to Him belongs sovereignty. There is no god but He. How, then, are you turned away?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From One Soul: The Origin That Makes All Kin**

*Khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin thumma jaʿala minhā zawjahā* — "He created you from a single soul, then made from it its mate." The *nafs wāḥidah* is Adam, as Ibn Kathīr and the majority of commentators read it, following 4:1, 6:98, 7:189, and 31:28. The point is not a biology lesson for its own sake. It is a moral origin-story: every later division of tribe, color, and class is secondary to a single created soul. The mate made *from it* is Eve, and the Qur'an elsewhere presents this pairing as a gift meant to produce rest and mercy:

> *"And among His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates, that you might find rest in them, and He placed between you love and mercy."* (Qur'an 30:21; cf. 16:72; 42:11)

The God who is too majestic to take a child (v. 4) is not too majestic to design companionship for His creatures. Transcendence in this Book does not mean indifference to the human need for a mate.

**Cattle Sent Down: Provision as Revelation's Twin**

*Wa anzala lakum mina al-anʿāmi thamāniyata azwāj* — "and sent down for you of cattle eight pairs." The verb *anzala* — sent down — is the same verb used of the Book. Water, iron, livestock, and scripture all "come down" in Qur'anic speech because all provision originates above the recipient. The eight pairs are the male and female of sheep, goats, camels, and oxen, as 6:143–144 enumerates. Some are for burden, some for slaughter, some for milk and wool (6:142). The human being who worships idols is here reminded that the animals he eats were not supplied by those idols.

**Creation after Creation in Threefold Darkness**

*Yakhluqukum fī buṭūni ummahātikum khalqan min baʿdi khalqin fī ẓulumātin thalāth* — "He creates you in your mothers' wombs, creation after creation, in threefold darkness." The phrase *khalqan min baʿdi khalqin* is read by the commentators as the successive stages of 23:13–14 and 22:5: drop, clot, lump, bones, clothing of flesh, then a new creation. The "threefold darkness" is classically the darkness of the belly, the darkness of the womb, and the darkness of the membrane (placenta) — so Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn. A child is assembled where no human eye can supervise and no human hand can assist. The verse thus takes the most intimate human fact — that each of us was once an unseen work in a sealed place — and makes it a proof of lordship.

The Prophet ﷺ described the same hidden work in a ḥadīth that the commentators regularly attach to the embryology verses:

> *"The creation of one of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop, then he is a clot for a like period, then a lump for a like period, then the angel is sent to him and breathes the spirit into him, and is commanded to write four words: his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or blessed."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

Even the writing of destiny occurs in that darkness. The One who writes there is the One to whom the sūrah is calling for unmixed worship.

**"That Is God, Your Lord" — Identification after Evidence**

*Dhālikumu Allāhu rabbukum lahu al-mulk* — "That is God, your Lord; to Him belongs sovereignty." The demonstrative *dhālikum* points back to everything just listed: the single soul, the mate, the cattle, the threefold darkness. Lordship is not an empty title. It is the name of the One who actually did these things. *Lahu al-mulk* — to Him belongs sovereignty — means there is no rival estate. Then the testimony of tawḥīd: *lā ilāha illā huwa* — "there is no god but He." The verse has argued its way to the shahādah: from embryology and livestock to the negation of every other god.

**"How, Then, Are You Turned Away?"**

*Fa-annā tuṣrafūn* — "How, then, are you turned away?" The same astonishment appears at 10:32. *Ṣarf* is a turning aside, a being diverted from a road. The question is not "why do you not know?" It is "how, given what you know in your own birth, do you still veer?" Al-Ṭabarī and others hear in it amazement that anyone would prefer the sons and daughters ascribed to God — the false nearness of verse 3 — to the One who assembled them in threefold darkness. The modern form of the veering is not always idol-stone. It is the habit of tracing one's origin to chance, one's provision to the market, and one's children to medical technique, as if the darkness of the womb had no Author. The verse does not deny secondary causes. It denies that secondary causes are lords.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:7

> **If you do not believe, surely God is beyond need of you. He is not pleased with disbelief for His servants. And if you are grateful, He is pleased therewith for you; and none shall bear the burden of another. Then unto your Lord is your return, and He shall inform you of that which you used to do. Truly He knows what lies within breasts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Needlessness: The Attribute That Frees Worship from Flattery**

*In takfurū fa-inna Allāha ghaniyyun ʿankum* — "If you do not believe, surely God is beyond need of you." The same needlessness is stated at 3:97 and 29:6. It is the necessary counterpart of verses 2–3. If God needed human religion, then *ikhlāṣ* would be a favor we do Him, and unbelief would be a loss He suffers. The verse cuts that vanity at the root. God is *ghanī* — rich, independent, free of need. Unbelief does not impoverish Him. The pure religion belongs to Him (v. 3) whether or not a given generation offers it.

**Displeasure at Kufr, Pleasure at Gratitude — for the Servants' Sake**

*Wa lā yarḍā li-ʿibādihi al-kufr* — "He is not pleased with disbelief for His servants." *Wa in tashkurū yarḍahu lakum* — "And if you are grateful, He is pleased therewith for you." Al-Ṭabarī records two readings of "His servants": some restrict it to the believers whom God protects from ruin, citing:

> *"As for My servants, truly you have no authority over them."* (Qur'an 15:42; 17:65)

Others apply it to all human beings. In either reading the theological point, which al-Zamakhsharī stresses, is that God derives no benefit from faith and suffers no harm from ingratitude. He is displeased with *kufr* because *kufr* ruins the servant. He is pleased with *shukr* because gratitude is the servant's own road to salvation. Pleasure and displeasure here are moral, not needy.

A ḥadīth qudsī, transmitted in Muslim and cited by Ibn Kathīr in this connection, states the independence with unmatched scope:

> *"O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as pious as the most pious heart among you, that would add nothing to My kingdom. O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were as wicked as the most wicked heart among you, that would subtract nothing from My kingdom."* (Muslim 2577, from Abū Dharr)

Worship is for us. That is why it can be sincere. A God who needed praise could be flattered; a God beyond need can only be loved and obeyed.

**No Soul Carries Another's Load**

*Wa lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā* — "and none shall bear the burden of another." The sentence is one of the Qur'an's pillars of justice (6:164; 17:15; 35:18; 53:38). *Wizr* is a load, a crime's weight. No parent, tribe, church, or nation can be made to stand in the dock for another person's chosen deeds. The doctrine simultaneously comforts the wrongly accused and strips the sinner of the hope that someone else's piety will be seized to pay his account. Intercession, when it occurs, occurs by God's leave as mercy, not as a transfer of guilt (see 39:43–44).

**The Return, the Information, the Inner Breast**

*Thumma ilā rabbikum marjiʿukum fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* The informing is a repeated Qur'anic scene (5:105; 6:60, 108, 159; 9:94, 105; 24:64; 62:8). It is not that God needs to learn; it is that the servant will be made to know his own record without remainder. And the last clause explains why the record is complete: *innahu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr* — "Truly He knows what lies within breasts." The *ṣadr* is the breast as the seat of secrets, motives, and concealed thoughts (3:119; 5:7; 8:43; 11:5; 31:23; 57:6; 64:4; 67:13). He will inform people not only of what they did but of why they did it. Verse 2's demand for *ikhlāṣ* is therefore not unenforceable idealism. The Auditor already reads the intention.

**Why This Verse Belongs Immediately After the Womb**

Verse 6 showed God creating in a darkness no one else could enter. Verse 7 shows Him knowing a darkness no one else can enter — the breast. The two darknesses are one argument: the One who assembled you unseen is the One who will read you unseen. Gratitude is rational; transferred blame is not; flattery of a needy deity is obsolete. The next verse will show what the ungrateful heart actually does when harm and blessing alternate.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:8

> **And when harm befalls man, he calls upon his Lord, turning unto Him. Then when He bestows a blessing from Himself upon him, he forgets the One upon whom he called before and sets up equals unto God to lead astray from His path. Say, "Enjoy your disbelief a little; truly you shall be among the inhabitants of the Fire."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prayer That Only Pain Can Teach**

*Wa idhā massa al-insāna ḍurrun daʿā rabbahu munīban ilayh* — "And when harm befalls man, he calls upon his Lord, turning unto Him." *Mass* is a touching, a contact — harm need not crush; it only has to touch. *Munīb* is one who turns back, from *inābah*, the turning of the heart toward God after having faced elsewhere. The Qur'an treats this reflex as a recurring human fact (6:40–41; 10:12; 17:67; 30:33). Storm at sea, illness in the night, the doctor's waiting room, the failing business — the same mouth that was busy with rivals becomes, for a spell, monotheist. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the calling is in peril or adversity; Ibn Kathīr adds times of need. The verse is not praising this prayer as complete faith. It is recording it as evidence: the soul knows, when pressed, Whom to call.

**The Forgetfulness That Comfort Teaches**

*Thumma idhā khawwalahu niʿmatan minhu nasiya mā kāna yadʿū ilayhi min qabl* — "Then when He bestows a blessing from Himself upon him, he forgets the One upon whom he called before." Relief arrives, and the file is closed as if the cry had never been uttered. The Qur'an names the same sequence at 10:12 and 30:33: tasting mercy after harm, then turning away. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabarsī describe the reversion as heedlessness and the pursuit of caprice. The blessing is *minhu* — "from Him" — so the forgetting is not a failure of information. It is a failure of loyalty.

Then the forgetting becomes theology: *wa jaʿala lillāhi andādan li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīlih* — "and sets up equals unto God to lead astray from His path." *Andād* are counterparts, rivals, likes. The man who in pain knew he had no rival now installs rivals in ease — patrons, ideologies, self, the very means that seemed to deliver him. The purpose clause *li-yuḍilla* may describe the effect of his act (he leads others astray) or the inner aim of the rivals. Either way, comfort has been allowed to become a false god.

**Kufr as Ingratitude, and the Little Enjoyment**

*Qul tamattaʿ bi-kufrika qalīlan* — "Say: Enjoy your disbelief a little." *Kufr* here carries its root sense of covering: covering a blessing, ingratitude. Al-Jalālayn gloss *qalīlan* as the duration of this life, which is little beside the Hereafter. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the delights themselves are "little" compared to what is lost. The command to "enjoy" is the Qur'an's severe irony — the same tone as "eat and enjoy a little" addressed to deniers elsewhere. It is not permission. It is a measurement: this is all you have purchased.

*Innaka min aṣḥābi al-nār* — "truly you shall be among the inhabitants of the Fire." The end is named without metaphor. The man who used God as an emergency number and then returned to rivals has not committed a small social lapse. He has chosen a company.

**The Psychology of Fair-Weather Monotheism**

The verse is one of the Qur'an's most exact portraits of religious inconsistency, and it needs no seventh-century costume to be recognized. People pray in hospitals and forget in health. Nations call on God in war and credit themselves in peace. Individuals make vows in anxiety and renegotiate them in safety. The pattern is not mysterious. Pain reduces the field of attention to what is real; comfort multiplies distractions until the real is one option among many. Taqwā, in the image this project has used before, is driving a dangerous road with attention; this verse describes the driver who only grips the wheel in the skid and then, when the road straightens, takes both hands off to wave at rivals.

The cure implied by the sūrah is not to seek a life without blessing. It is to let the emergency's knowledge survive into the blessing — to remember in ease the Lord one found in harm. Verse 9 will now present the opposite human type: the one who is devout in the night, before any skid.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:9

> **What of one who is devoutly obedient during the watches of the night, prostrating and standing [in prayer], wary of the Hereafter and hoping for the Mercy of his Lord? Say, "Are those who know and those who do not know equal? Only possessors of intellect reflect."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Unfinished Question That Completes Itself**

*Am man huwa qānitun ānāʾa al-layli sājidan wa qāʾiman* — "What of one who is devoutly obedient during the watches of the night, prostrating and standing?" The verse is a rhetorical question whose second half is elided. Ibn al-Jawzī and others supply the obvious completion: is he like the one in verse 8? Another reading treats *am man* as *a man* — "O one who is devoutly obedient" — an address rather than a comparison (Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). Both readings yield the same contrast: the fair-weather caller of verse 8 against the night-long servant of verse 9.

*Qānit* is one who is constantly obedient, still, devoted — the word used of Mary (33:35; 66:12) and of the devoted generally (2:238; 3:17). *Ānāʾ al-layl* are the watches, the stretches of the night. The posture is named in two states: *sājidan wa qāʾiman* — prostrating and standing — so that the night is not a single gesture but a repeated rising and falling. This is the opposite of verse 8's emergency prayer. It is prayer when nothing is wrong.

**Fear and Hope in One Heart**

*Yaḥdharu al-ākhirata wa yarjū raḥmata rabbih* — "wary of the Hereafter and hoping for the Mercy of his Lord." The verse refuses the two cheap simplifications of religion: terror without hope, and hope without wariness. The night worshipper carries both. Ibn Kathīr records that the Prophet ﷺ asked a man on his deathbed, "How do you feel?" The man said, "I am fearful and hopeful." The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"These two are not joined together in a man's heart at a moment like this, but that God grants him that for which he hopes and protects him from that which he fears."* (transmitted in the tafsīr literature; Ibn Kathīr cites it here)

The pairing is the Fātiḥah's own pairing: mercy named before judgment, then judgment named so that mercy will not become presumption. Al-Ghazālī later called hope the wing and fear the bridle. This verse puts both in a man standing and prostrating while other people sleep.

**"Are Those Who Know and Those Who Do Not Know Equal?"**

*Qul hal yastawī alladhīna yaʿlamūna wa alladhīna lā yaʿlamūn* — "Say: Are those who know and those who do not know equal?" The question is among the Qur'an's most quoted, and it is not a celebration of worldly information. In this context knowledge is the knowledge that produces night-obedience, wariness, and hope. The Book elsewhere compares believer and disbeliever to seeing and blind (6:50; 13:16; 35:19), hearing and deaf (11:24), light and darkness (13:16). A ḥadīth extends the comparison even among believers:

> *"The virtue of the pious scholar over the devoted worshipper is as the virtue of the full moon over all the other heavenly bodies."* (Abū Dāwūd 3641; al-Tirmidhī 2682; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

Knowledge here is not a diploma. It is the inner seeing that makes the night useful.

**Ulū al-Albāb: The People of the Kernel**

*Innamā yatadhakkaru ulū al-albāb* — "Only possessors of intellect reflect." *Lubb* (pl. *albāb*) is the kernel, the pith, the core of a thing — as opposed to the shell. *Ulū al-albāb* are those who possess the human core: insight that reaches the inside of things (2:179, 197, 269; 3:7, 190; 5:100; 13:19; 38:29; 39:18, 21; 40:54; 65:10). Ibn ʿArabī, commenting on this verse, reads the "kernel" as the knowledge sent down from God, and reason as its shell — useful only if it is used to reach the pith, not if it is treated as food in itself (*Futūḥāt* III 120.32–35). The practical sense, shared by the sober commentators, is that reflection (*tadhakkur*) is not a hobby of clever people. It is the work of those who still have a core unconsumed by distraction.

**Why the Night Is the Test of Knowledge**

The verse places knowledge next to night-worship for a reason. Anyone can appear knowing in the marketplace of talk. The night removes the audience. What remains is whether the knowledge has reached the kernel: does it make a person stand and prostrate, fear the Meeting, and hope in mercy when no one is grading him? Verse 8's man knows God well enough to call Him in harm. Verse 9's man knows God well enough to call Him in the dark without harm. The sūrah's question — are they equal? — is meant to be answered with the body, at night.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:10

> **Say, "O My servants who believe, reverence your Lord. For those who do good in this world there is good; and God's earth is vast." Surely those who are patient shall be paid their reward in full without reckoning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address That Confers Dignity**

*Qul yā ʿibādi alladhīna āmanū ittaqū rabbakum* — "Say: O My servants who believe, reverence your Lord." God names them *My servants* before He commands them. The possessive is an honor: they are not merely people who believe; they are claimed. *Taqwā* of the Lord is then asked of them — the protective mindfulness this commentary has compared to driving a dangerous road with both hands on the wheel. Belief is the identity; taqwā is the conduct that protects the identity from being stolen by ease, fear, or custom.

Some commentators (al-Ālūsī, Ibn al-Jawzī) considered this verse among those possibly Madinan, because its counsel of patience and of the earth's vastness fits the pressure to emigrate. Whether Makkan or Madinan in dating, its meaning is not locked to one year.

**Good in This World for Those Who Do Good**

*Li-alladhīna aḥsanū fī hādhihi al-dunyā ḥasanah* — "For those who do good in this world there is good." Al-Jalālayn reads the good as Paradise. Al-Ṭabarsī allows both: good here (health, peace, a life that is not all bitterness) and good there. *Iḥsān* is excellence — doing what is beautiful, doing what is due as if seeing God. The sentence is a law of moral agriculture: what is planted as *ḥasan* grows as *ḥasanah*. It is not a promise that every doer of good will be rich. It is a promise that good is not wasted into a void.

**"God's Earth Is Vast"**

*Wa arḍu Allāhi wāsiʿah* — "and God's earth is vast." Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, and al-Ṭabarī read this as encouragement to emigrate from the company of idolaters — the counsel given to the early Muslims of Makkah, and given generally in 4:97 and 29:56:

> *"O My servants who believe, My earth is vast, so worship Me."* (Qur'an 29:56)

Al-Ṭabarsī also hears a reference to the land of God in the Garden, sought by righteous deeds. Both senses teach the same refusal of claustrophobia. When a place makes taqwā impossible — through persecution, through a household's corruption, through a market that allows no honest living — the believer is not required to call that prison "home." The earth is God's, not the tyrant's and not the tribe's. Modern application includes physical hijrah where necessary, and also the inner refusal to treat a corrupt workplace, feed, or circle as the only habitable world.

**Patience Paid Without Reckoning**

*Innamā yuwaffā al-ṣābirūna ajrahum bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — "Surely those who are patient shall be paid their reward in full without reckoning." *Ṣabr* here stands opposite the wavering of verse 8. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī gloss *without reckoning* as not having to endure the ordinary weighing in the way others do — a reward poured, not counted coin by coin. The Book elsewhere says God pays the patient their wage without measure in the same spirit (cf. 16:96). Patience in this sūrah is not passivity. It is remaining on the pure religion when harm comes, when blessing comes, and when the earth must be crossed to keep the religion.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Patience is illumination."* (Muslim 223)

And:

> *"Whoever remains patient, God will grant him patience; and no one is given a gift better and more encompassing than patience."* (al-Bukhārī 1469; Muslim 1053)

Verse 10 thus gathers three provisions for the believing servant: taqwā as the stance, iḥsān as the labor, ṣabr as the wage-scale, and a vast earth as the geography of obedience. Verses 11–14 will now put the same religion into the Prophet's own mouth as a personal commission.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:11

> **Say, "Truly I have been commanded to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Made to Recite His Own Job Description**

*Qul innī umirtu an aʿbuda Allāha mukhliṣan lahu al-dīn* — "Say: Truly I have been commanded to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him." The verse is almost the twin of verse 2, but the grammar has shifted. Verse 2 commanded the Prophet ﷺ directly: *so worship*. Verse 11 commands him to *say* that he has been commanded. The public is now the audience. The Messenger does not present *ikhlāṣ* as his personal taste or as a tribal reform. He presents it as an order he himself is under.

*Umirtu* — "I have been commanded" — is the passive of commission. The hidden commander is God. In a culture of self-made religious entrepreneurs, this passive is a credential: the speaker is not the source. Al-Rāzī notes that having the Prophet ﷺ announce his own commanded sincerity closes the accusation that he sought lordship over the people. A man who says "I too am under orders to worship without partners" is not recruiting worshippers of himself.

**Why the Same Sentence Must Be Said Twice**

The sūrah's repetition of *mukhliṣan lahu al-dīn* (vv. 2, 11, 14) is not filler. Verse 2 is legislation for the Messenger. Verse 3 is diagnosis of the partners' religion. Verses 4–10 are evidence and contrast. Verse 11 is proclamation: now that the case has been made, say it aloud. The same phrase in 7:29, 40:14, 40:65, and 98:5 shows it is a Qur'anic slogan of tawḥīd, as central as the shahādah, because the shahādah's "no god but God" must be lived as unmixed *dīn*.

**Commanded Sincerity as a Protection of the Community**

When the founder of a community announces that he is commanded to sincerity, he sets a standard his followers cannot later relax into personality-cult. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"Do not exaggerate in praising me as the Christians exaggerated in praising the son of Mary. I am only a servant, so say: the servant of God and His Messenger."* (al-Bukhārī 3445)

Verse 11 is that humility in revealed form. The next verse will push it further: he is commanded not only to worship sincerely, but to be the first of those who submit.

**The Spoken Commission as a Fence**

When sincerity is only felt and never said, it can be renegotiated in private. When it is commanded to be *said*, it becomes a covenant the community can remember. The Prophet ﷺ was made to announce his orders so that later generations could not invent a different Muhammad — a politician of blended worship or a chieftain of tribal gods. *Umirtu* is both humility and freedom: humility, because the self is not the legislator; freedom, because the self is no longer enslaved to every new religious fashion.

Everything this sūrah still commands — patience, night prayer, repentance without despair — is already qualified by this verse. A patient man who is patient for reputation is not yet the patient of verse 10. Verse 11 is the key signature of the remaining score: whatever notes follow, they are in the key of *mukhliṣan lahu al-dīn*.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:12

> **And I have been commanded to be the first of those who submit."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**First Among a People, Not First in History**

*Wa umirtu an akūna awwala al-muslimīn* — "And I have been commanded to be the first of those who submit." The same self-description appears at 6:14 and 6:163. Ibn al-Jawzī and others explain that "first" means first of his community or his generation — not first of all human submitters, since earlier prophets and their followers are called *muslimīn* throughout the Qur'an (e.g., 2:128–133; 3:67; 12:101). Moses says, "I am the first of the believers" (7:143; cf. 26:51) in the same relative sense: first of those addressed by the revelation just given. *Awwal* can also mean foremost, as at 3:96 and 6:14.

The theological yield is a doctrine of continuity and of leadership together. Continuity: Muhammad ﷺ enters a river of submission that Abraham, Moses, and Jesus already entered. Leadership: in the new community formed by this Book, he must be the first into the water. He does not stand on the bank pointing.

**Islām as the Name of the Act, Not a Brand Invented in Makkah**

*Muslimīn* here are those who *aslama* — who handed themselves over to God. The verse therefore does not command the Prophet ﷺ to be the first member of a later-named world religion in the sociological sense. It commands him to be the first to enact the surrender that the religion names. The distinction matters for how Muslims read other prophets: they are not outsiders to "our" religion; we are latecomers to theirs, completed and guarded in this final Book.

**Why a Messenger Must Go First**

Leadership that consists of "do as I say, not as I do" cannot carry *ikhlāṣ*, because *ikhlāṣ* is visible only in a life. The Prophet ﷺ's night prayer, his fear of a tremendous day (v. 13), his refusal of partners, his being the first to submit — these are the credentials of verse 2's command. He ﷺ said:

> *"Pray as you have seen me pray."* (al-Bukhārī 631)

And more generally, the Qur'an itself makes his life a criterion:

> *"There has certainly been for you in the Messenger of God an excellent example."* (Qur'an 33:21)

Verse 12 is the inner form of that example: before he is an example in details of law, he is an example in being the first to surrender. A parent, teacher, or imam who reads this verse is being told the same order of operations: enter the obedience you command, and enter it first.

**The Honor of Going First**

A community cannot be asked to enter a river whose leader remains dry. The command to be *awwala al-muslimīn* is not a prize of rank; it is a burden of sequence. The Prophet ﷺ fasted the days he legislated, stood the nights he recommended, and faced the harm he asked others to endure. Later exaggeration that treats him as a being who did not share our need to submit actually violates this verse. He is first *of those who submit*, not first *instead of* those who submit.

Parents who wish children to pray, teachers who wish students to be honest, and imams who wish congregations to be sincere are all under the same sequence: *be first*. Because earlier prophets are named *muslim* in the Book, the verse also forbids the smallness of thinking that surrender to God began in the seventh century. Continuity itself is a form of *ikhlāṣ*: the religion is not a brand to be patented.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:13

> **Say, "Truly I fear, should I disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous day."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger Who Does Not Exempt Himself**

*Qul innī akhāfu in ʿaṣaytu rabbī ʿadhāba yawmin ʿaẓīm* — "Say: Truly I fear, should I disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous day." The sentence is repeated verbatim at 6:15 and 10:15. Its placement here, immediately after "I have been commanded to be the first of those who submit," completes a triad: commanded to worship with sincerity (v. 11), commanded to be first in submission (v. 12), and confessing fear of disobedience (v. 13). No verse in the triad says "I am safe because I am the Messenger."

The conditional *in ʿaṣaytu* — "if I disobeyed" — is not a prediction that he will. It is the grammar of moral seriousness. Fear is attached to the possibility of disobedience as such, not to a career of it. The *yawm ʿaẓīm* is the Day of Resurrection, tremendous in duration, in disclosure, and in consequence.

**Fear as a Form of Knowledge**

This fear is not panic. It is the knowledge named in verse 9: wariness of the Hereafter joined to hope. The Prophet ﷺ, already promised forgiveness, still stood in night prayer until his feet swelled, and when asked why, said:

> *"Shall I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4836; Muslim 2820)

And he ﷺ taught:

> *"I am the most knowing of God among you, and the most fearful of Him."* (al-Bukhārī 6101, in meaning; cf. his statement in al-Bukhārī 5063)

If the most guided heart still feared a tremendous day, then safety-by-affiliation is a fiction. Affiliation with the Prophet ﷺ is walking behind a man who himself fears God — not hiding in his shadow as if the Day were cancelled.

**A Rebuke to Two Audiences**

To the pagans of Makkah, the verse says: the man you accuse of seeking kingship is a man who fears punishment if he disobeys. To the later Muslim who treats the shahādah as an insurance policy requiring no obedience, the verse says: the Messenger of that shahādah did not treat his office that way. Disobedience remains disobedience even on the tongue that recites revelation. The hope of verse 53 — that God forgives all sins — will not cancel this verse; it will invite the fearful to repent, not the reckless to presume.

**Wakefulness Is Not Gloom**

The fear of a tremendous day is often mistaken for unhealthy anxiety. Neurosis fears shadows and opinions. This fear fears a real Day whose Master is the Compassionate of the Fātiḥah. It is therefore rational, like a driver's attention on a mountain road. The Prophet ﷺ joked, loved, raced ʿĀʾishah, and also said he feared disobedience if it should occur. Wakefulness is not gloom.

Some treated affiliation with the Prophet ﷺ as a cancellation of fear. This verse stands in the way. The man through whom safety comes still says *innī akhāfu*. The ḥadīth of intercession and the promise of verse 53 sit beside this sentence, as hope sits beside wariness in verse 9. A religion of fear alone produces despair; a religion of safety alone produces the scoffers of verse 56.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:14

> **Say, "God do I worship, devoting my religion entirely to Him."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Object Fronted, the Mixture Refused Again**

*Quli Allāha aʿbudu mukhliṣan lahu dīnī* — "Say: God do I worship, devoting my religion entirely to Him." The proper Name is placed before the verb, the same restriction-grammar as *iyyāka naʿbudu* in the Fātiḥah: God — and no other — do I worship. For the third time in fourteen verses the sūrah names *ikhlāṣ*. Verse 2 was command; verse 11 was commission reported; verse 14 is present-tense enactment. *Aʿbudu* is "I worship" now. *Dīnī* — "my religion" — makes the devotion personal and total: not a department of life called worship, but the speaker's whole *dīn*.

**Why the Prophet Must Say It in the First Person**

A community can hide inside "we." The first person singular here is a signature. The Messenger ﷺ stakes his own worship, not a committee's. In the confrontation with Quraysh, this is also a declaration of independence: my religion is not the inherited mixture, and I am not negotiating a blend. Verse 15 will complete the separation by telling them to worship what they will apart from Him — a dismissal, not a permission.

**Ikhlāṣ as Daily Recitation, Not a Once-Said Creed**

Because the verse is prefaced with *qul* ("say"), it is meant to be spoken, and spoken often. The believer who recites it is not quoting a historical telegram. He is being trained to put the Name first in the sentence of his life. The test remains the test of verses 2 and 11: if reputation, fear of people, or appetite shares the object of the verb *aʿbudu*, the sentence has been altered even if the mouth has not.

**The Possessive Surrendered**

*Dīnī* — "my religion" — is a dangerous phrase, because it can mean "the religion I own and edit." Here the possessive is immediately surrendered: my religion, devoted entirely *to Him*. The speaker owns the offering, not the legislation. The fronted Name (*Allāha aʿbudu*) trains the tongue against the habit of leading with the self. God is named first, then the worship, then the unmixed quality of the *dīn*.

By the third occurrence of *ikhlāṣ* in fourteen verses, the reader is meant to feel the Qur'an's pedagogy: important things are repeated because hearts leak. A single command at verse 2 could be filed as introduction. A third in the first person present tense files it as the standing state of whoever recites.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:15

> **So worship whatsoever you will apart from Him." Say, "Truly the losers are those who lose their souls and their families on the Day of Resurrection. Yea! That is the manifest loss."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Permission That Is a Threat**

*Fa-uʿbudū mā shiʾtum min dūnih* — "So worship whatsoever you will apart from Him." Ibn Kathīr reads the sentence as both a threat and a repudiation. It resembles 6:19, where the Prophet ﷺ is told to say he is quit of what they ascribe as partners, and it resembles the final separation of Sūrah 109:

> *"Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion."* (Qur'an 109:6)

The imperative is not legal permission. It is the weary clarity of a completed argument: after verses 1–14, if they still want the rivals, they are not being physically stopped — they are being told that the choice is now theirs, and that its cost will be named in the next breath. The tone is the tone of a physician who has explained the poison and then says, "Drink if you insist."

**Loss Defined as the Loss of Self and Family**

*Qul inna al-khāsirīna alladhīna khasirū anfusahum wa ahlīhim yawma al-qiyāmah* — "Say: Truly the losers are those who lose their souls and their families on the Day of Resurrection." *Khusrān* is commercial ruin — the word of a trade that ends in bankruptcy (cf. 2:16; 22:11). The bankrupt of that Day have lost *anfusahum* — their own souls — because they wasted the capital of life: time, capacity, the chance to repent. They have lost *ahlīhim* — their families — because, as al-Ṭabarī explains, they will have no family in the Hereafter to whom they can turn for comfort. The righteous, by contrast, enter with their righteous fathers, spouses, and progeny (13:23; 40:8; 43:70). Hell is not only fire. It is the final unavailability of every human bond that was used as an excuse not to worship God alone.

**"The Manifest Loss"**

*Alā dhālika huwa al-khusrānu al-mubīn* — "Yea! That is the manifest loss." *Alā* again calls attention. *Mubīn* means clear, unconcealable. Many losses in this world can be disguised as strategy, deferred, refinanced. This one cannot. The verse teaches a redefinition that every age needs: the real loser is not the one who lost money, status, or argument in Makkah or in a modern market. The real loser is the one who arrives at the Resurrection having forfeited himself and his people. All other uses of the word "loss" are metaphors beside this one.

**Clear Difference Without Cruelty**

The dismissal "worship what you will" is often misread as contempt for people. The later address "O my people" (v. 39) and the Prophet's ﷺ long grief over his folk forbid that reading. What is dismissed is the *religion* of partners, not the humanity of those who hold it. Sūrah 109's "to you your religion" is the same surgical cut.

Many sins are committed *for* family: ḥarām earnings to feed a house, refusing hijrah because a clan would be angry. The verse says that the very ones for whom the religion was mixed will not be there as comfort. By contrast, 52:21 promises that those who believed and whose descendants followed them in faith will be joined. The way to keep one's people is not to prefer them to God.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:16

> **Above them they shall have canopies of fire and below them canopies; with that does God strike fear into His servants. O My servants! Reverence Me!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fire as Roof and Floor**

*Lahum min fawqihim ẓulalun mina al-nāri wa min taḥtihim ẓulal* — "Above them they shall have canopies of fire and below them canopies." *Ẓulal* are shades, canopies, covering layers. Al-Jalālayn glosses them as layers of fire. The image is the opposite of the Garden's shade and of the rivers running below (v. 20). Here the "shade" burns, and the ground burns. Compare 29:55:

> *"On the Day when the punishment shall cover them from above them and from beneath their feet."* (Qur'an 29:55)

There is no direction left that is not punishment. The losers of verse 15 are now located in space: enclosed.

**Fear as Mercy's Severe Form**

*Dhālika yukhawwifu Allāhu bihi ʿibādah* — "with that does God strike fear into His servants." Al-Jalālayn takes "servants" as the believing servants, as the next vocative suggests. Al-Ṭabarī allows all human beings. The verb *yukhawwif* is causative: He makes them fear. Fear in this verse is not a mood God enjoys producing. It is a warning-shot meant to move people off a road. A religion that never names fire is not kinder than this verse; it is less honest, and therefore less kind, because it leaves the servant unwarned.

**"O My servants! Reverence Me!"**

*Yā ʿibādi fa-ttaqūn* — "O My servants! Reverence Me!" The vocative is possessive again, as in verse 10: *My* servants. Even the warning is addressed to those He claims. *Fa-ttaqūn* — so fear Me, so be wary of Me, so take shelter from My punishment by obedience to Me. Taqwā here is exactly the careful walking: the canopy of fire is the cliff beside the path; awareness of it is what keeps the feet on the path. The next verse will show that those who shun false deities are not merely fleeing fire; they are being given glad tidings.

**When Shade Itself Burns**

Arabic *ẓulal* is ordinarily a mercy-word: shade in a desert. The verse fills the mercy-word with fire. Things people use as metaphors of safety — roof, floor, canopy — can be made of the opposite material when the Lord of materials so decrees. A person who trusted buildings and "cover" as ultimate is shown cover that burns.

The vocative after the fire is not "O enemies" but "O My servants." The warning is an act of claim. A physician who still says "my patient" while describing gangrene is refusing to give the patient up. Taqwā in the last clause is the practical response: take Me as the One to be wary of. The fear God strikes is meant to move the feet inland, as a cliff-edge sign is meant to do — not to push the walker over.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:17

> **And as for those who shun false deities and worshipping them, and turn unto God, unto them glad tidings. So give glad tidings to My servants,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shunning Ṭāghūt: The Negative Half of Tawḥīd**

*Wa alladhīna ijtanabū al-ṭāghūta an yaʿbudūhā wa anābū ilā Allāh lahumu al-bushrā* — "And as for those who shun false deities and worshipping them, and turn unto God, unto them glad tidings." *Ijtināb* is a strong avoidance — steering well clear, not brushing past. *Al-ṭāghūt* denotes false authorities, false objects of worship, false causes (2:256–257; 4:51–52, 60, 76; 5:60; 16:36). Al-Ṭabarī reads the shunning as shunning the worship of all that is other than God; some take *al-ṭāghūt* as singular for Satan. The word's root is the same *ṭughyān* of overflowing bounds (as Pharaoh "transgressed"). Ṭāghūt is whatever exceeds its creaturely limit and demands a worship it is not owed.

The verse thus states the negative half of the shahādah first: *lā ilāha* — there is no ṭāghūt to be worshipped — then the positive: *wa anābū ilā Allāh* — they turn unto God. *Inābah* is the turning already seen in verse 8, but here it is not the turning of panic. It is the turning of people who have left false lords and gone home.

**Glad Tidings as the Other Half of Fear**

Verse 16 struck fear. Verse 17 announces *bushrā* — glad tidings. The sūrah will not let fear stand alone, just as verse 9 would not let night-worship be only wariness without hope. The content of the tidings is unpacked in verses 18–20: guidance, intellect, lofty abodes, rivers, a promise God will not fail. The Prophet ﷺ is then told: *fa-bashshir ʿibād* — "so give glad tidings to My servants." His office is not only *nadhīr* (warner) but *bashīr* (bearer of good news). The servants who receive the news are defined in the next verse by how they listen.

**Modern Ṭāghūt Without Statues**

A statue is only one costume of ṭāghūt. Any system, appetite, leader, or ideology that demands the obedience owed to God — that overflows its limit — is in the word's range. Shunning it does not always mean leaving a city; it means refusing worship, even when the false lord has a modern name: the market as final judge, the nation as final object of sacrifice, the self as final law. The glad tidings are not for those who merely dislike other people's idols. They are for those who turn *unto God*.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:18

> **who listen to the Word, then follow what is most beautiful of it. It is they whom God has guided; it is they who are the possessors of intellect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hearing That Continues into Following**

*Alladhīna yastamiʿūna al-qawla fa-yattabiʿūna aḥsanah* — "who listen to the Word, then follow what is most beautiful of it." *Yastamiʿūn* is the form of listening that leans in, not the passive overhearing of *yasmaʿūn*. The *qawl* is the divine Word — the Qur'an — as Ibn Kathīr and the majority take it. The *fāʾ* ("then") is sequential: listening is not the end. Following is. Ibn Kathīr compares it to what was said to Moses:

> *"Take hold of them with strength, and command thy people to hold to the best of them."* (Qur'an 7:145)

Understanding without action is incomplete hearing. The servants of verse 17 are identified as those whose ears are connected to their limbs.

**What "the Most Beautiful of It" Does Not Mean**

Al-Zamakhsharī and others warn against reading *aḥsanahu* as if some of the Qur'an were ugly or disposable. The Book is all beautiful (v. 23 will call it the most beautiful discourse). "Most beautiful" here means: they distinguish between the good and the best, the permitted and the preferred, the merely lawful and the most virtuous, and they choose what is closer to God and richer in reward. It can also mean they follow it in the best manner — taking its concessions without exploiting them and its commands without dragging the feet. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī in related discussions of verse 55 emphasize acting in accord with revelation, not ranking verses by private taste.

A dangerous modern misuse is "I follow the verses I find most beautiful" as a filter that deletes law, warning, or anything that costs. That is the opposite of this verse. The people praised here follow the Word *because* it is from God; their "most beautiful" is the most complete obedience, not the most comfortable excerpt.

**Guidance Named as Already Given**

*Ulāʾika alladhīna hadāhumu Allāh* — "It is they whom God has guided." Guidance is God's act, as verses 36–37 will insist. Yet the verse has just described their listening and following. The two are not rivals. God guides; the sign of having been guided is this kind of hearing. *Wa ulāʾika hum ulū al-albāb* — "it is they who are the possessors of intellect." The kernel-people of verse 9 reappear. Intellect in this sūrah is not raw IQ. It is the core that, when the Word is heard, follows the most beautiful of it. A brilliant mind that hears and does not follow is, in this lexicon, still in the shell.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:19

> **What of one for whom the Word of punishment comes due? Wilt thou save one who is in the Fire?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Limit of the Messenger's Office**

*Afa-man ḥaqqa ʿalayhi kalimatu al-ʿadhāb* — "What of one for whom the Word of punishment comes due?" The question is again elided: is he to be delivered? Then the address to the Prophet ﷺ: *afa-anta tunqidhu man fī al-nār* — "Wilt thou save one who is in the Fire?" The function of the Messenger is to deliver the message and to guide those whom God has chosen to guide. He does not extract from the Fire those upon whom the Word has come due. The verse resembles:

> *"And it is for God to show the way, for some of them lead astray. Had He willed, He would have guided you all together."* (Qur'an 16:9; cf. 13:31; 14:4; 35:8)

Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī note an alternate reading that folds the two clauses into one sentence: as for the one upon whom the Word has come due, will you save him from the fire? The meaning is the same.

**Compassion Without Omnipotence**

The verse is a consolation to a Messenger known to grieve over his people (18:6; 26:3; 35:8). Love of the lost does not confer the power to unlose them against their sealed refusal. It is also a boundary for every later caller, parent, and teacher: you may warn, weep, argue in the best manner, and model the night-worship of verse 9. You may not appoint yourself savior. The Fire in the question is spoken of as already occupied (*man fī al-nār* — one who is in the Fire), as if the end-state were present to God's knowledge. Human effort remains real; it does not rewrite that knowledge.

**The Word That Comes Due**

*Ḥaqqa ʿalayhi* means the Word has become binding, deserved, settled. It is not a random sentence. It is the arrival of a consequence after warning, signs, and refusal — as verses 56–59 will later make explicit when souls lament too late. The next verse will show the opposite settlement: God's promise of lofty abodes, which He will not fail.

**Love That Does Not Seize the Keys**

The Prophet ﷺ's grief over his people is attested in 18:6 and 26:3. This verse does not rebuke that grief as weakness. It locates its limit. Love of the lost is itself a prophetic trait; appointing oneself their extractor from the Fire is a trait of lordship. The keys of verse 63 have not been given to the caller.

Parents of adult children who have left the prayer, teachers of students who mock, and friends of those who persist in harm all stand inside this question. You may recite the signs. You may warn of the meeting. You may weep. You may not rewrite the Word that has come due. The discipline is what keeps daʿwah from turning into coercion, and what keeps the caller's own heart from collapsing into the despair verse 53 will forbid.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:20

> **But as for those who reverence their Lord, they will have lofty abodes above which are lofty abodes, and rivers running below. That is God's Promise; God will not fail the tryst.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghuraf: The Abodes That Name the Sūrah**

*Lākin alladhīna ittaqaw rabbahum lahum ghurafun min fawqihā ghurafun mabniyyah* — "But as for those who reverence their Lord, they will have lofty abodes above which are lofty abodes, built." This is the verse from which the sūrah's other name, *al-Ghuraf*, is taken. *Ghurfah* is an upper chamber, a room raised above the ground. The image is stacked height: rooms above rooms, the architecture of honor. Against verse 16's canopies of fire above and below, here the above is further honor and the below is rivers.

The Prophet ﷺ described such abodes:

> *"In the Garden there are abodes whose inside can be seen from the outside and whose outside from the inside."* He was asked for whom they were, and said: *"For those who speak kindly, feed the hungry, and stand in prayer at night while people are sleeping."* (Ibn Kathīr cites it here; transmitted in the musnads and in al-Tirmidhī's range of descriptions of Paradise)

The night-worship of verse 9, the kind speech implied by verse 18's following of the most beautiful, and the feeding of others — these are the building materials of the *ghuraf*.

**Ranks Visible as Stars**

In a ḥadīth that appears to allude to these stacked abodes, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The people of the Garden will look upon one another in the abodes just as you look upon the stars of the east and the stars of the west rising on the horizon, according to their excellence in rank."* They asked, "Are they the prophets?" He said: *"Yea! By Him in Whose Hand is my soul — and the people who believed in God and His Messenger and who affirmed the messengers."* (Ibn Kathīr)

Height in Paradise is not a caste of blood. It is excellence of faith and affirmation, visible as starlight is visible — without envy, because the lookers are themselves in the Garden.

**Rivers Below, Promise Kept**

*Tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "rivers running below" — the Qur'an's standing landscape of Paradise (throughout, e.g., 2:25). *Waʿda Allāh lā yukhlifu Allāhu al-mīʿād* — "That is God's Promise; God will not fail the tryst." The same assurance appears at 3:9, 3:195, and 13:31. *Mīʿād* is an appointed meeting. God does not miss appointments. In a world where human promises are routinely refinanced, this clause is meant to be leaned on with the whole weight of a life. Taqwā is costly in Makkah and in every later marketplace; the verse says the invoice of that cost is already accepted by a Paymaster who does not default.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:21

> **Hast thou not considered that God sends down water from the sky, conducts it as springs in the earth, then brings forth crops of diverse colors? Then they wither and thou seest them yellowing. Then He turns them to chaff. Truly in that is a reminder for possessors of intellect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Full Life-Cycle in One Glance**

*A-lam tara anna Allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍ* — "Hast thou not considered that God sends down water from the sky, then conducts it as springs in the earth?" The question *a-lam tara* — "have you not seen?" — treats the evidence as already public. Water descends, is guided into springs, then *thumma yukhriju bihi zarʿan mukhtalifan alwānuh* — "then He brings forth crops of diverse colors." The field is a palette. Then: *thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāmā* — "then they wither and you see them yellowing; then He turns them to chaff." Green, then gold of death, then fragments.

The parable of the world's life is drawn with the same water at 18:45 and 57:20:

> *"Set forth for them a parable of the life of this world: it is like water We send down from the sky. Then it mixes with the vegetation of the earth. Then it becomes chaff, scattered by the winds."* (Qur'an 18:45)

**Why the Intellect Needs a Field, Not a Slogan**

*Inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-ulī al-albāb* — "Truly in that is a reminder for possessors of intellect." For the third time the kernel-people are named (vv. 9, 18, 21). What they are to remember is not a botanical curiosity. It is that every human green is on the same schedule: descent of provision, a season of color, yellowing, chaff. *All things perish save His Face* (28:88). The Mighty who wraps night and day (v. 5) and creates in threefold darkness (v. 6) also writes mortality on every harvest.

The reminder is meant to break two illusions at once: the illusion of the proud, who think their green is permanent, and the illusion of the despairing, who think yellowing is the last word. After chaff comes, in God's order, either the Fire's canopies or the *ghuraf*. The field is not the destination. It is the sermon.

**Living as People Who Have Seen the Yellowing**

A culture that hides death, dyes the yellow, and markets perpetual spring is a culture trying not to be *ulū al-albāb*. The verse does not command gloom. It commands accurate sight. Plant, harvest, give thanks, and do not build your final house in a field whose own end you have already watched. Verse 22 will now move the same contrast from crops to breasts: some expanded for submission, some hardened against remembrance.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:22

> **What of one whose breast God has expanded for submission, such that he follows a light from his Lord? Woe unto those whose hearts are hardened to the remembrance of God! They are in manifest error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Breast as the Place Where Religion Either Opens or Shuts**

*Afa-man sharaḥa Allāhu ṣadrahu li'l-islām fa-huwa ʿalā nūrin min rabbih* — "What of one whose breast God has expanded for submission, such that he follows a light from his Lord?" The comparison is again elided: is he like the one whose heart is sealed? Al-Jalālayn supplies the missing clause. The *ṣadr* is the breast as the locus of the heart and the fountainhead of the spirit (al-Bayḍāwī): the seat of inspirations, thoughts, and intentions. *Sharḥ al-ṣadr* is expansion, opening, relief — the opposite of constriction. God says to Moses, "Expand my breast" (20:25), and to the Prophet ﷺ, "Did We not expand your breast?" (94:1). Expansion is associated with belief; constriction with disbelief (6:125).

Al-Bayḍāwī reads "a light from his Lord" as God opening the heart to *maʿrifah* (spiritual knowledge) and guidance. The person does not wander. He walks *upon* a light, as 2:5's guided walk *upon* guidance.

**The Prophet's Description of the Expanded Heart**

The manner of the expansion is described in a report the commentators (al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Qurṭubī) attach to this verse. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"When light enters the heart, it expands and becomes spacious."* He was asked, "What is the sign of that?" He said: *"Constant turning toward the Abode of Eternity, shunning the abode of delusion, and preparing for death before it arrives."*

The sign is not a private sensation that cannot be checked. It is a reorientation of loves: eternity drawing, delusion receding, death prepared for. Verse 21's yellowing field has entered the breast as light.

**The Hardened Heart and the Cry of Woe**

*Fa-waylun li'l-qāsiyati qulūbuhum min dhikri Allāh* — "Woe unto those whose hearts are hardened to the remembrance of God." *Qaswah* is hardness, like a stone that water will not enter (cf. 2:74). *Wayl* is a cry of ruin. Hearts may be described as hardened, sealed, or covered as a result of their own wrongdoing and the consequent loss of Divine favor, so that they no longer receive messages or remembrance (2:7, 74; 5:13; 6:25; 7:101; 9:87; 16:108; 17:46; 18:57; 30:59; 47:16; 57:16; 63:3). Moses even asked God to harden Pharaoh's heart as judgment (10:88). Hardness is not a mysterious accident. It is a destination reached by refusing softness often enough.

*Ulāʾika fī ḍalālin mubīn* — "They are in manifest error." The error is *mubīn* — clear — even if it is unclear to them, as the hypocrites of al-Baqarah were unaware of their own disease. The contrast with the expanded breast is the sūrah's contrast throughout: single, lit, remembering; or hard, dark, and wandering.

**How a Heart Stays Expansible**

The verse implies a practice. Remembrance (*dhikr*) is the water; hardness is what happens when the water is refused. The Qur'an itself is called *dhikr*. Prayer is remembrance. The night-watches of verse 9 are remembrance. A modern heart is hardened not only by crime but by continuous noise that never lets the breast be a place where light could enter. The expanded breast is spacious; the hard heart is crowded and closed. Verse 23 will now describe the Book that makes skins quiver and then soften — the opposite of hardness, given in words.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:23

> **God has sent down the most beautiful discourse, a Book consimilar, paired, whereat quivers the skin of those who fear their Lord. Then their skin and their hearts soften unto the remembrance of God. That is God's Guidance, wherewith He guides whomsoever He will; and whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Beautiful Discourse**

*Allāhu nazzala aḥsana al-ḥadīth* — "God has sent down the most beautiful discourse." Al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī identify *aḥsan al-ḥadīth* as the Qur'an itself — most beautiful in meaning and in expression. *Ḥadīth* here is speech, discourse, a recital that happens in time; *aḥsan* is the superlative already used of following in verse 18. What the servants were told to follow as "most beautiful" is now named as a sending-down from God. Some commentators considered this verse Madinan; the beauty it describes does not depend on that dating.

**Consimilar and Paired**

*Kitāban mutashābihan mathānī* — "a Book consimilar, paired." *Mutashābih* here is not the technical term of 3:7 (verses whose meaning is not decisive on the surface). Ibn Kathīr warns against confusing the two uses. Here it means that parts resemble parts: each portion as eloquent as the next (al-Rāzī); recurring phrases (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī); qualities such as beauty and wisdom, or truth and inimitability, found throughout (al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī); each part confirming the others (al-Qurṭubī). *Mathānī* — paired, oft-repeated — refers to the Qur'an's way of setting opposites in adjacent frames: Heaven and Hell, believer and disbeliever, pious and profligate, reward and punishment, fear and hope. This very sūrah is an exhibit of that pairing.

**Skin That Quivers, Then Softens**

*Taqshaʿirru minhu julūdu alladhīna yakhshawna rabbahum thumma talīnu julūduhum wa qulūbuhum ilā dhikri Allāh* — "whereat quivers the skin of those who fear their Lord. Then their skin and their hearts soften unto the remembrance of God." This is the only verse that mentions the skin's quivering at revelation. Other verses mention hearts (8:2) and eyes that overflow (5:83; 19:58). Here the body itself is included. Al-Qurṭubī reports that this described the Companions: their eyes would water and their skins quiver when the Qur'an was recited. *Dhikr Allāh* may mean the Qur'an as Reminder, or the remembrance of God that the recitation produces, as in:

> *"Those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God. Are not hearts at peace in the remembrance of God?"* (Qur'an 13:28)

The sequence is itself a pairing: fear's tremor, then hope's softening. Neither state is left to freeze. A piety of permanent shivering would be despair; a piety of unearned softness would be heedlessness. The Book moves the same skin through both.

**Guidance That Cannot Be Hijacked**

*Dhālika hudā Allāh yahdī bihi man yashāʾ* — "That is God's Guidance, wherewith He guides whomsoever He will." "That" may point to the Qur'an as just described, or to the fear-and-softening He grants (al-Qurṭubī). *Wa man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-mā lahu min hād* — "and whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he." The Qur'an says of such a one that there is no way (4:88, 143; 42:46), no guide (7:186; 13:33; 40:33), and no protector (18:17; 42:44). In this context, the one whose skin never quivers and never softens has not found another path. He has found no path.

**A Criterion for Recitation**

The verse quietly legislates how the Qur'an is to be received. Recitation that never touches the skin is not the recitation this verse describes. Recitation that produces only aesthetic shiver without later softening of conduct is incomplete. The Companions' quivering was not theater; it was the body agreeing with the meaning. A later generation that recites with virtuosity and remains hard of heart is closer to verse 22's woe than to this verse's guidance. The next verse will show the face that has nothing left to guard itself with but itself.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:24

> **What of one who guards with his face against the evil punishment on the Day of Resurrection? And it shall be said unto the wrongdoers, "Taste that which you used to earn."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face Used as a Shield**

*Afa-man yattaqī bi-wajhihi sūʾa al-ʿadhābi yawma al-qiyāmah* — "What of one who guards with his face against the evil punishment on the Day of Resurrection?" The elided comparison is: is he like one who comes secure? Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī compare it to:

> *"Is one who is cast in the Fire better, or one who comes in security on the Day of Resurrection?"* (Qur'an 41:40)

> *"Is one who walks with his face stooped down more guided, or one who walks upright upon a straight path?"* (Qur'an 67:22)

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī explain "guards with his face" as meaning that the face is the first to meet the Fire, or that he is dragged on his face into it. The face — in Arabic and in human instinct the seat of dignity, identity, and honor — is reduced to a shield because the hands, which should have been used in good, are chained. The image is of a person so poor in protective deeds that he has only his countenance to interpose.

**Taste What You Earned**

*Wa qīla li'l-ẓālimīna dhūqū mā kuntum taksibūn* — "And it shall be said unto the wrongdoers: Taste that which you used to earn." The same tasting of earned punishment appears at 7:39. *Kasb* is earning, the commercial word: they are being served their own wages. God does not feed them an arbitrary menu. The verb *dhūqū* (taste) makes the punishment sensory and intimate — not a rumor about fire but fire on the tongue that once denied.

**Dignity as Something Spent in This World**

The practical sting of the verse is that faces are already being trained. A face that will not bow in prayer, that hardens at remembrance (v. 22), that lies against God (v. 32), is a face rehearsing its poverty of shields. A face that prostrates in the night (v. 9) is a face that will not need to serve as a fire-guard. The wrongdoers' meal is what they earned; the reverent's *ghuraf* (v. 20) is what God promised. Both are consistent with the lives that preceded them.

**Hands That Should Have Been Shields**

In this world the hands are the ordinary shields: they give, they raise in prayer, they write a contract honestly, they push away ḥarām. On the Day described here, those hands are unavailable, and the face — the place of sujūd, of honor, of identity — is pressed into service as a guard. The image is not random cruelty. It is the disclosure of a life that never trained the limbs as protection.

> *"The Fire will eat all of the son of Adam except the mark of prostration. God has forbidden the Fire to eat the mark of prostration."* (al-Bukhārī 806; Muslim 182)

The ḥadīth of the people who prostrated is the mercy-twin of this verse. A face that spent its nights in verse 9's watches has already been claimed as a place the Fire may not eat. A face used only for display and denial has nothing left but itself to interpose.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:25

> **Those before them denied, and the punishment came upon them whence they were not aware.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial Has a History, and So Does Its Outcome**

*Kadhahaba alladhīna min qablihim* — "Those before them denied." The Makkan hearers are placed in a file older than themselves: peoples who said of their messengers what Quraysh said of Muhammad ﷺ. *Fa-atāhumu al-ʿadhābu min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūn* — "and the punishment came upon them whence they were not aware." The same phrase appears at 16:26 and 16:45. *Lā yashʿurūn* is the unawareness already met in other portraits of ruin: the blow does not arrive from the direction they were scanning.

Al-Ṭabarī and the narrative commentators fill the clause with the known ends: the flood upon Noah's people, the plagues upon Pharaoh's, the storm upon Lot's, the cry and the wind upon ʿĀd and Thamūd. Each people had a theory of safety — walls, numbers, gods, engineering. The punishment used a door they had not posted a guard upon.

**The Strategic Blind Spot**

Human beings naturally fortify against the last disaster they remember. The verse says that divine seizure is not obliged to repeat a familiar route. A nation may watch its borders and be undone by its morals; a person may insure his property and be undone by his heart. Unawareness here is not lack of intelligence. It is the misplacement of attention — scanning the horizon for armies while the threat is already in the well, the womb, the market, or the soul. Verse 26 will add that even the worldly disgrace, when it comes, is only a sample.

**The Unposted Door**

Noah's people watched for armies and were taken by water. Thamūd demanded a sign and were taken by a cry. Quraysh fortified against raid and were, in the end, undone by a recitation they could not answer and a history they could not stop. The pattern is not that God is capricious. It is that the moral order is not obliged to attack along the wall you built.

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr, on the parallel of 16:45, emphasize that the blow coming "whence they were not aware" includes the suddenness of divine seizure after long respite. Respite is itself a door people fail to post a guard upon: they take delay as acquittal. Verse 42's sleep is a nightly reminder that a taking can occur from inside the house, in the bed, without an army on the horizon.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:26

> **Thus God had them taste disgrace in the life of this world; yet the punishment of the Hereafter is surely greater, if they but knew.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Taste, Not the Meal**

*Fa-adhāqahumu Allāhu al-khizya fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā* — "Thus God had them taste disgrace in the life of this world." *Khizy* is disgrace, humiliation, the public undoing of pride. The drowned, the overthrown, the remnant left as a sign — these are this-worldly tastings. The verb is again *taste*: a sample placed on the tongue so that the intelligent might infer the rest.

*Wa la-ʿadhābu al-ākhirati akbar law kānū yaʿlamūn* — "yet the punishment of the Hereafter is surely greater, if they but knew." The comparative *akbar* is not rhetorical inflation. This world's worst disgrace is still inside a world that ends. The Hereafter's punishment does not end, and it is not mixed with the remaining comforts that even a ruined life still contains — sleep, a cool hour, a friend's hand. "If they but knew" locates the tragedy in knowledge refused, not in information unsent.

**Why Worldly Calamity Is Not a Full Theodicy**

The verse also disciplines the believer's reading of history. When a tyrant falls, that is *khizy* in this world; it is not yet the whole justice of God. When a tyrant dies in his bed, that is not acquittal; the greater punishment may simply have been deferred. The verse therefore neither lets the oppressed think this world is the final court, nor lets the comfortable think the absence of disgrace is a verdict of innocence. Knowledge — *law kānū yaʿlamūn* — would rearrange both panic and complacency.

**Sample Size and the Greater Remainder**

A taste is given so that the intelligent will infer the remainder. Pharaoh's drowning, ʿĀd's wind, the disgrace of a fallen tyrant on a news screen — these are samples. They are not the meal. The verse therefore corrects two misreadings of history. The first: "this world already paid them, so the ledger is closed." The second: "this world did not pay them, so there is no ledger." Both forget *akbar* — greater. The Hereafter's punishment is greater because it is unmixed with remaining comforts and uncut by death.

"If they but knew" is the sūrah's refrain of wasted epistemology. Knowledge was available: in the parables (v. 27), in the uncrooked recitation (v. 28), in the yellowing field (v. 21). The comparative *akbar* only works for a mind that still compares. A mind that has made this world the only scale cannot even hear the sentence.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:27

> **And indeed We have sent forth for mankind in this Quran every kind of parable, that haply they may remember,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Parables as a Complete Kit**

*Wa la-qad ḍarabnā li'l-nāsi fī hādhā al-Qurʾāni min kulli mathal* — "And indeed We have set forth for mankind in this Qur'an every kind of parable." *Mathal* is a likeness that makes an unseen moral visible — the grain and the seven hundred, the good tree and the bad, the water that becomes chaff (v. 21), the man with quarreling partners (v. 29). Al-Ṭabarī reads "every kind" as including the accounts of the destroyed peoples just alluded to. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Qurṭubī allow "every parable people would need for religious understanding." Compare 30:58–59.

The claim is not that the Qur'an is an encyclopedia of folk tales. It is that no necessary type of reminder has been omitted. The servant who still "does not get it" is not facing a curriculum gap. He is facing a reception gap.

**"That Haply They May Remember"**

*Laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* — "that haply they may remember." *Laʿalla* in divine speech is not uncertainty in God. It is the language of purpose offered to freedom: the parables are there so that remembrance might occur. Al-Bayḍāwī glosses "that they may take heed." And 29:43 adds the restriction:

> *"And these parables We set forth for mankind, but none understand them except those who know."* (Qur'an 29:43)

The parables are for all; the understanding is for those who still have a kernel (vv. 9, 18, 21). Verse 28 will now specify the language of the reminder and its freedom from crookedness.

**No Missing Genre of Warning**

The Qur'an's parables include agricultural (v. 21), domestic (v. 29), commercial (2:261), and historical (the destroyed towns). "Every kind" means the curriculum is not missing a teaching style the student can blame. The one who still "does not get it" is facing a reception problem, as 29:43 states: none understand them except those who know.

*Laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* treats the hearer as free. Parables do not coerce. They invite remembrance. A parable that has been heard a hundred times without *tadhakkur* has not failed as literature; the kernel (v. 9) has failed as organ. The next verse will add that the language of the reminder is clear and uncrooked, so that even the medium cannot be blamed.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:28

> **an Arabic Quran, bearing no crookedness, that haply they may be reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Arabic as Eloquence, Not Ethnic Privilege**

*Qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan* — "an Arabic Qur'an," literally an Arabic recitation (cf. 12:2; 20:113; 41:3; 42:7; 43:3). Al-Rāzī hears in it an allusion to the supreme eloquence that cannot be matched:

> *"Say: Surely if mankind and jinn banded together to bring the like of this Qur'an, they would not bring the like thereof, even if they supported one another."* (Qur'an 17:88)

The first audience was addressed in the tongue they knew most inwardly, so that no one could say the warning was obscure by language. Later nations receive it by translation of meaning and by learning of the original; the choice of Arabic is a historical mercy to the first hearers and a standing challenge of inimitability, not a racial sacrament.

**No Crookedness**

*Ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — "bearing no crookedness" (cf. 18:1). Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, and al-Rāzī take this as the absence of contradiction:

> *"Had it been from other than God, they would surely have found much discrepancy therein."* (Qur'an 4:82)

A crooked road wastes the traveler. A crooked book would waste the soul. The straight path of the Fātiḥah has a straight speech to match it.

**Remembrance First, Then Taqwā**

Al-Rāzī notes the sequence of endings: verse 27 ends with *that haply they may remember*; verse 28 with *that haply they may be reverent*. Remembrance, knowledge, and understanding precede true taqwā. One cannot carefully walk a path one has not yet noticed. The parables (v. 27) produce remembrance; the uncrooked Arabic recitation (v. 28) is meant to produce the wariness that verse 10 commanded. Verse 29 will now give a single parable that returns the whole sūrah to its theme: a heart with many masters versus a heart with one.

**Straight Speech for a Straight Path**

The Fātiḥah asked for *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*. This verse supplies a recitation *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — without crook. A crooked map cannot guide a straight walk. Al-Rāzī's observation that remembrance (v. 27) precedes taqwā (v. 28) is a psychology of education: first notice, then be wary. Commands dumped on an unremembering heart produce either rebellion or empty compliance.

Arabic here is a mercy to the first audience and a challenge of iʿjāz to every audience. Translation of meaning is necessary for those who do not know the tongue; it does not replace the original's claim. The verse's purpose clause — *that haply they may be reverent* — measures success not by literary admiration but by taqwā. A recitation praised as beautiful and left unlived has not yet reached this verse's *laʿalla*.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:29

> **God sets forth a parable: a man in whom quarreling partners share, and a man belonging to one man: are the two equal in likeness? Praise be to God! Nay, but most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Slave of Many and the Slave of One**

*Ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan rajulan fīhi shurakāʾu mutashākisūn wa rajulan salaman li-rajul* — "God sets forth a parable: a man in whom quarreling partners share, and a man belonging to one man." The first is the idolater — owned by many masters who *mutashākisūn*, quarrel, pull against one another, disagree about his time, his money, his loyalty. The second is *salam* — wholly yielded, at peace, belonging to one. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī all read the pair as idolater versus monotheist. The question *hal yastawiyāni mathalā* — "are the two equal in likeness?" — expects no.

**The Heart as the Real Slave**

The parable also maps onto the inner life, as later commentators (including al-Ājurrī's strain as cited in the source) noted: a heart divided among many interests against a heart devoted entirely to God. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said:

> *"Whosoever makes his aspirations one aspiration — that is, God — God suffices him for the aspiration of this world. Yet whosoever is dispersed by his aspirations, in whichever valley of the world he may be destroyed, God has no concern for him."* (cited in the commentary tradition on this verse)

And in another ḥadīth:

> *"Whoever makes the world his aspiration, God divides his affairs for him, places his poverty before his eyes, and nothing of the world comes to him except what has been apportioned for him. Yet whoever makes the Hereafter his aspiration, God gathers his affairs together for him, places his wealth in his heart, and the world comes to him though it be reluctant."* (Ibn Mājah 4105; Aḥmad — in this meaning)

Read this way, the verse restates the sūrah's spine: *devoting religion entirely to Him* (vv. 2, 11, 14) and *the pure religion* (v. 3). Only the heart with one master can be at peace. The quarreling partners of a modern person need not be named idols. They may be career, audience, family pressure, appetite, anxiety, and self-image — each tugging a different order, none able to pay a full wage.

**"Praise Be to God! Nay, but Most of Them Know Not"**

*Al-ḥamdu lillāh* — the praise that opened the Fātiḥah — is inserted as a verdict: God is praised because the inequality is obvious, and because He has made it obvious. *Bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn* — "Nay, but most of them know not." The refrain that most of mankind do not know, do not understand, do not give thanks, do not believe, runs through the Qur'an (7:187; 16:75; 12:103–106; 2:243). Majority is not a proof of the divided heart's wisdom. The next two verses will place even the Prophet ﷺ and his opponents under the same mortality, so that no master among the quarreling partners can claim to be deathless.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:30

> **Surely thou wilt die, and surely they will die.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence That Levels Every Pretension**

*Innaka mayyitun wa innahum mayyitūn* — "Surely thou wilt die, and surely they will die." The address is to the Prophet ﷺ first. Even he — recipient of the Book (v. 2), first of those who submit (v. 12) — is *mayyit*, one who will die. Then they, the opponents, the quarreling masters' clients, the night-worshippers, everyone: *mayyitūn*. The Arabic uses the noun-form of the dead, not only a future verb, as if death were already their description, delayed only by a term.

The verse ended a particular Makkan fantasy: that if they waited long enough, this reciter would perish and his recitation with him. It also ended a later Muslim fantasy: that love of the Prophet ﷺ means denying his death. When he ﷺ did die, Abū Bakr recited this meaning to a people in shock:

> *"Whoever worshipped Muhammad, Muhammad has died. Whoever worshipped God, God is Alive and does not die."* (al-Bukhārī 3667, 3668)

And he recited 3:144: *"Muhammad is not but a messenger; messengers have passed away before him..."*

**Death as the Shared Appointment Before the Dispute**

The verse is not a melancholy slogan. It is the necessary preface to verse 31's court date. There is no arbitration between Prophet and people in this world's last instance; both parties will be moved to another dock. For the believer, the sentence is also a mercy: it forbids the personality cult and forbids despair at the Messenger's absence, because the religion was never his mortality's hostage. For the opponent, it forbids the strategy of waiting the preacher out. Both will die; neither will escape the next verse.

**Living as People Already Described as Mortal**

To be told "you are a dead man" while still breathing is the Qur'an's way of making taqwā rational. Plans, grudges, and pride that assume permanence become foolish on the tongue of this verse. The field yellows (v. 21); the reciter dies; the deniers die. What remains is the Book sent down in truth and the Lord who does not die. Verse 31 will open the court in which the dead, now raised, dispute.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:31

> **Then on the Day of Resurrection, before thy Lord will you dispute.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Court After the Two Deaths**

*Thumma innakum yawma al-qiyāmati ʿinda rabbikum takhtaṣimūn* — "Then on the Day of Resurrection, before your Lord will you dispute." *Thumma* (then) is the then of resurrection, not of a later debate in Makkah. *Takhtaṣimūn* is mutual disputation, claims filed against claims. Ibn Kathīr records that after this verse was revealed, al-Zubayr asked the Prophet ﷺ whether the worst sins that had passed between them in this world would return. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Yes. They will indeed return to you until everyone who has a claim has presented that to which he has a claim."* Al-Zubayr said: *"By God, the affair is severe."* (Ibn Kathīr)

The dispute includes the wronged against the wrongdoer, the truthful against the liar, and, in a report from Ibn ʿAbbās, even the soul against the body — each blaming the other — until an angel says they have passed judgment against themselves (Ibn Kathīr). It resembles 41:19–21, where ears, eyes, and skins bear witness and are asked why they testified.

**Not a Sectarian Weapon First**

Some read the verse as a reference to disputes among Muslims after the Prophet's ﷺ death (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarsī). Some Shiʿi commentators refer it to the usurpation of ʿAlī's right. Al-Ṭabarī's caution about reading extra persons into verse 33 applies in spirit here: the wording is general. Every claim will be filed. Using the verse only to indict one's historical enemies, while not fearing one's own file, is to miss al-Zubayr's response: *the affair is severe*.

**Why Disputation Is Postponed to That Day**

This world cannot finish moral accounting. Evidence is hidden, power silences claimants, death interrupts testimony. The verse promises a court with no missing party and no silenced witness, *before your Lord* — not before a tribe's elders. The practical effect is double: do not despair of justice delayed, and do not imagine that a won argument here is a closed case. Verse 32 will name the worst case a person can bring to that court: lying against God and denying the truth when it came.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:32

> **So who does greater wrong than one who lies against God and denies the truth when it comes to him? Is there not within Hell an abode for the disbelievers?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Superlative of Ẓulm**

*Fa-man aẓlamu mimman kadhaba ʿalā Allāhi wa kadhdhaba bi'l-ṣidqi idh jāʾah* The question is a Qur'anic refrain (6:21, 93, 144; 7:37; 10:17; 11:18; 18:15; 29:68; 61:7). *Ẓulm* is misplacement, injustice, putting a thing where it does not belong. Nothing is more misplaced than a lie *about God* — attributing partners, offspring, or speech He did not speak — and a denial of *al-ṣidq*, the truth, when it has already arrived. Ibn Kathīr reads the lying as including the claim that God has partners or children. The second crime is timed: *idh jāʾah* — when it came to him. Refusal after arrival is not ignorance. It is rejection.

**Hell as the Fitting Abode**

*A-laysa fī jahannama mathwan li'l-kāfirīn* — "Is there not within Hell an abode for the disbelievers?" The question is not information-seeking. It is the sealing of fitness: a lie against God and a denial of arrived truth have a *mathwā* — a dwelling-place, a settled abode — and it is Hell. Compare 29:68 and 39:60. The word *mathwā* answers verse 20's *ghuraf*: everyone is housed; the question is which house matches the life.

**Arrival Makes Innocence of a Different Kind Impossible**

The verse is especially sharp for people who have heard. Before the truth comes, a person may be astray in the sense of unreached (as 93:7 used *ḍāll* of the Prophet's ﷺ own pre-revelation state). After it comes, the file changes. Modern forms of the two crimes remain: speaking about God what He did not say (in slogan, fatwa-without-knowledge, or theology of desire), and denying what has become clear because it would cost. Verse 33 will now name the opposite people: those who come with the truth and confirm it.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:33

> **And whosoever comes with the truth and confirms it, it is they who are reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Acts That Make One Taqwā**

*Wa alladhī jāʾa bi'l-ṣidqi wa ṣaddaqa bihi ulāʾika humu al-muttaqūn* — "And whosoever comes with the truth and confirms it, it is they who are reverent." *Jāʾa bi'l-ṣidq* is to arrive bearing truth — in speech, in testimony, in the message itself. *Ṣaddaqa bihi* is to confirm it, to hold it true, to back it with assent. Al-Ṭabarī prefers the general reading: all who are called to tawḥīd, affirm the Prophet ﷺ, and act on his message; or those who come with the Qur'an on the Day of Resurrection saying, "This is what You gave us and we followed it." Some named the one who comes with the truth as the Prophet ﷺ or as Gabriel, and then inserted "the one who confirms it" as Abū Bakr or the Muslims (Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). Al-Ṭabarī rejects the need for such addition. Some Shiʿi readings refer both to ʿAlī. The wording itself is a description, not a proper name: whoever bears truth and confirms it.

**Taqwā as Correspondence Between Mouth and Heart**

The *muttaqūn* here are not people of vague caution. They are people in whom *ṣidq* (truthfulness) and *taṣdīq* (confirmation) meet. The liar against God (v. 32) splits word from reality. These join them. In a court where disputes will be filed (v. 31), the reverent are those whose file already contains correspondence: they said the true thing and they believed it. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"You must be truthful, for truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise... and beware of lying, for lying leads to iniquity, and iniquity leads to the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī 6094; Muslim 2607)

Verse 33 is that ḥadīth in revealed form, with taqwā as the title of those who practice it. Verse 34 will name their wage: whatever they will, with their Lord.

**Ṣidq as a Whole-Life Trait**

Coming with the truth is not only the Prophet's ﷺ office. It is the believer's daily craft: testimony in court (2:282–283), weights in the market (83:1–3), speech in a family, a résumé, a message. Confirming the truth is the inward twin: not merely saying what is true when it profits, but holding it true when it costs. The *muttaqūn* of this verse are people whose inner and outer files already match, before verse 31's court forces the match.

Al-Ṭabarī's refusal to restrict the verse to one named hero is itself a mercy. The description remains open. Anyone who arrives bearing *ṣidq* and confirming it is enrolled. The opposite enrollment was named in verse 32: lying against God and denying the truth when it came. Two roads, two titles: *aẓlam* and *muttaqūn*.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:34

> **With their Lord they shall have whatsoever they will — that is the recompense of the virtuous —**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Will That No Longer Harms**

*Lahum mā yashāʾūna ʿinda rabbihim* — "With their Lord they shall have whatsoever they will." The same open will is promised at 16:31, 25:16, 42:22, and 50:35. In this world, human will is dangerous because it is mixed and ignorant; that is why it is bounded by law. In the Garden, the will of the reverent has been healed: what they wish is what does not harm, and what they wish is given. *ʿInda rabbihim* — with their Lord — locates the gift in nearness, not in a warehouse of objects. The *ghuraf* of verse 20, the rivers, the whatever-they-will of this verse, are all in the presence of the Lord they reverenced when it cost.

**The Title: The Virtuous**

*Dhālika jazāʾu al-muḥsinīn* — "that is the recompense of the virtuous." *Iḥsān* is the excellence of verse 10 and the rank of the ḥadīth of Gabriel: to worship God as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, He sees you (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8). The wage matches the work. Those who did what is beautiful are paid with a will that has become beautiful. Verse 35 will add a further mercy: not only gift, but the removal of their worst deeds and the payment for their best.

**A Will That Can Be Trusted**

"Whatsoever they will" is a dangerous sentence if said of this world's will, which verse 8 showed forgetting God after a blessing, and which verse 49 showed claiming blessing as a diploma. In the Garden the will has been educated by taqwā and iḥsān. What they wish no longer collides with what is true. The gift is therefore not indulgence. It is the restoration of desire to health.

*ʿInda rabbihim* locates every gift in nearness. Objects without the Lord would not be Paradise; they would be a longer dunya. The virtuous are paid with a presence in which wishing is safe. That is why the commentators pair this verse with 50:35 — *"They shall have whatsoever they will with their Lord; that is the recompense of those who were devoted"* — and with the ḥadīth that God says to the people of Paradise:

> *"I have given you what you see, and I will give you what is better than it."* They say, "What is better?" He says: *"My good pleasure, and I will never be angry with you thereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 6549; Muslim 2829)

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:35

> **that God may absolve them of the worst of that which they have done and render unto them their reward for the best of that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Double Bookkeeping of Mercy**

*Li-yukaffira Allāhu ʿanhum aswaʾa alladhī ʿamilū wa yajziyahum ajrahum bi-aḥsani alladhī kānū yaʿmalūn* Two operations: *takfīr* — covering, erasing, absolving — of the *worst* of their deeds; and *jazāʾ* — repayment — according to the *best* of their deeds. The believers are not paid at the average. They are forgiven at the bottom and rewarded at the top. The same principle appears at 9:121, 16:96–97, 24:38, 29:7, and 46:16.

**Why "the Worst" Is Named**

Even the *muḥsinūn* have a worst. Taqwā is not impeccability. The verse refuses two errors: the error that the virtuous have nothing to forgive, and the error that their worst will be placed on the scale at full weight while their best is discounted. God's dealing with those who came with truth and confirmed it is asymmetrical in their favor — which is what mercy means in a just court.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The good deeds of the righteous are the evil deeds of those brought near."*

— a saying of the later spiritual teachers that captures a truth already here: the standard is high, and the Judge still erases the worst for those who lived in iḥsān. And in a ḥadīth:

> *"God has one hundred portions of mercy... and He withheld ninety-nine for the Day of Resurrection."* (Muslim 2752; al-Bukhārī 6000)

Verse 35 is one of the forms that ninety-nine will take: worst erased, best repaid. The next verses will return from this mercy to the polemical present: God suffices His servant, though they frighten him with those apart from Him.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:36

> **Does God not suffice His servant? Yet they would frighten thee with those apart from Him; and whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Should End Anxiety**

*A-laysa Allāhu bi-kāfin ʿabdah* — "Does God not suffice His servant?" The expected answer is yes. Al-Ṭabarī reads: did God not suffice Muhammad ﷺ as He sufficed the prophets before him? Ibn Kathīr allows the servant to be any believer who worships only Him and relies only upon Him. The parable of verse 29 is still in force: the man belonging to one master has a master who is enough; the man of quarreling partners is always short of a final protector.

*Kāfī* is the One who is sufficient — enough as witness, guardian, defender, provider. The Qur'an repeats *ḥasbunā Allāh* — "God is sufficient for us" (3:173) — and *ḥasbiya Allāh* (9:129). The question-form here is meant to shame fear of rivals: if the answer is yes, why is the heart still bargaining with others?

**Frightened with What Is Less than God**

*Wa yukhawwifūnaka bi'lladhīna min dūnih* — "Yet they would frighten thee with those apart from Him." The idolaters thought that naming their gods' displeasure would move the Prophet ﷺ. The tactic is perennial: frighten the monotheist with the anger of the party, the market, the ancestors, the "forces," the crowd. The verse names the tactic so it can be recognized when it is not wearing Makkan names. Nothing apart from God has, in the last analysis, a fright worth a servant's rerouting — because those things do not suffice, and God does.

**No Guide for the One God Leaves**

*Wa man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-mā lahu min hād* — "and whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he." The clause, already heard at verse 23, is repeated because the frightened servant might look for a third party to navigate by. There is none. Guidance is not a commodity the idols can sell. The one who turns from the Sufficer toward the frighteners is not entering a different guidance system. He is exiting guidance. Verse 37 will complete the couplet: the one God guides cannot be led astray by those frighteners.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:37

> **And whomsoever God guides, none shall lead him astray. Is not God Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance That Cannot Be Undone by Intimidation**

*Wa man yahdi Allāhu fa-mā lahu min muḍill* — "And whomsoever God guides, none shall lead him astray." The frighteners of verse 36 are hereby disarmed. They can threaten; they cannot unguide the one God has guided. The Book repeats the principle: whomsoever God guides is rightly guided (7:178; 17:97; 18:17). This is not a license to spiritual arrogance. It is a promise that the servant who holds to God as sufficient is not finally at the mercy of social weather. Hearts turn only between two fingers of the Compassionate, as the Prophet ﷺ said (al-Tirmidhī 2140; Ibn Mājah 3834). That is why the guided still pray *ihdinā* every day. The verse denies that idols and crowds are the turners.

**Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance**

*A-laysa Allāhu ʿazīzan dhī intiqām* — "Is not God Mighty, Possessor of Vengeance?" (cf. 3:4; 5:95; 14:47). Unlike the idols whose wrath was advertised to scare the Prophet ﷺ, God actually possesses *intiqām* — the just reclaiming of right from the one who violated it. Vengeance here is not a mood. It is the Name of a justice that the wronged and the messengers were denied in this world. The Mighty can protect His servant; the Possessor of Vengeance can requite those who tried to frighten him with emptiness.

**Courage as Theology, Not Temperament**

Some people are brave by nerve. This verse offers a different courage: the courage of a true map of power. If God suffices, guides, and requites, then the cost of taqwā is rational. If He does not, then even a brave temperament is only a gamble. The sūrah is forming servants whose steadfastness is an inference from the Names, not a surge of adrenaline. Verse 38 will now use the pagans' own confession — that God created the heavens and the earth — against their trust in others.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:38

> **And wert thou to ask them, "Who created the heavens and the earth?" They would surely say, "God." Say, "Then have you considered those upon whom you call, apart from God? If God desires some harm for me, could they remove His Harm, or if He desires some mercy, could they withhold His Mercy?" Say, "God suffices me; in Him trust those who trust."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Confession That Should Have Ended Idolatry**

*Wa la-in saʾaltahum man khalaqa al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa la-yaqūlunna Allāh* — "And wert thou to ask them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they would surely say, 'God.'" The Qur'an records this confession often (10:31; 23:84–89; 29:61, 63; 31:25; 43:9, 87). The Quraysh still believed in a supreme Creator at the head of a pantheon. The verse does not deny their theology's first article. It prosecutes them with it. Al-Ājurrī's strain in the source notes that the agency of a Creator above all others was evident to them. Evidence they already grant is then turned on the rivals they still call upon.

**The Two Tests No Idol Passes**

*Qul a-fa-raʾaytum mā tadʿūna min dūni Allāh in arādaniya Allāhu bi-ḍurrin hal hunna kāshifātu ḍurrihi aw arādanī bi-raḥmatin hal hunna mumsikātu raḥmatih* Two hypotheticals, both unanswerable. Compare:

> *"And if God should touch thee with affliction, none can remove it save He; and if He desires some good for thee, none can hold back His Bounty."* (Qur'an 10:107; cf. 3:160; 36:23; 48:11)

The rivals cannot open what God shuts or shut what God opens. Therefore calling upon them is not caution. It is waste.

**"God Suffices Me"**

*Qul ḥasbiya Allāh ʿalayhi yatawakkalu al-mutawakkilūn* — "Say: God suffices me; in Him trust those who trust." *Ḥasbiya Allāh* is both "God is enough as a witness between me and you" (4:79, 166; 17:96; 29:52) and "God is enough as Guardian" (4:81, 132; 33:3, 48). *Tawakkul* is the trust that verse 36 asked about: not passivity, but the tying of the camel and then the resting of the heart (al-Tirmidhī 2517). Those who truly trust, trust in Him — not in the unremovable harm's supposed preventers.

The sentence is given to the Prophet ﷺ to say, and therefore given to every servant to learn. It is the verbal form of the parable's second man: belonging to one, sufficed by one. Verses 39–40 will now tell the Prophet ﷺ to let the others act on their position while he acts on his — and to wait for the knowledge that will distinguish the two punishments.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:39

> **Say, "O my people! Act according to your position; I, too, am acting. Soon you shall know"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Farewell Formula That Is a Warning**

*Qul yā qawmi iʿmalū ʿalā makānatikum innī ʿāmil* — "Say: O my people! Act according to your position; I, too, am acting." The same formula appears at 11:93 and 6:135. *Makānah* is position, standing, the place one occupies in a scheme. Ibn Kathīr reads the injunction as a threat: perform the deeds your religion prescribes, as I will perform the deeds mine prescribes, and soon the truth of the two religions will be known. It is not a modern "you do you" of moral relativism. It is the end of negotiation: I will not join your position; you have been warned; continue if you will; knowledge is coming.

**"O My People" — Still Kin While Separating**

*Yā qawmī* keeps the bond of peoplehood even in rupture, as Shuʿayb and Ṣāliḥ and Moses addressed their people. The Prophet ﷺ does not cease to be of Quraysh by refusing their gods. Love of people is not the same as sharing their *makānah*. The verse models a stance needed in every age of mixed families and cities: clear difference of worship without theatrical hatred of kin.

The sentence is unfinished: "Soon you shall know" hangs over into verse 40, where the two knowledges — who is disgraced, who is enduringly punished — are named. The pause at the verse-end is itself rhetorical: the knowledge is about to be specified, and it is not knowledge anyone should want to learn too late.

**Ending the Debate Without Ending the Kinship**

*Iʿmalū ʿalā makānatikum* is the Qur'an's way of closing a negotiation that has become a stalling tactic. Hud, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, and Noah use similar formulas. The Prophet ﷺ remains *qawmī* — my people — while refusing their *makānah*. The modern temptation is either to blend positions to keep kinship, or to dissolve kinship to keep the position. This verse does neither.

"Soon you shall know" is not a taunt of a man unsure of his ground. It is the confidence of a man who has been told that knowledge is coming from elsewhere — from the two punishments of verse 40. Acting on one's position is therefore not relativism. It is the last clarity before the exam's answers are published.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:40

> **upon whom there comes a punishment that disgraces him and upon whom there falls a punishment enduring."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Punishments, Two Horizons**

*Man yaʾtīhi ʿadhābun yukhzīh wa yaḥillu ʿalayhi ʿadhābun muqīm* — "upon whom there comes a punishment that disgraces him and upon whom there falls a punishment enduring." The first is *khizy* in this world — the disgrace of verse 26 — defeat, exposure, the collapse of a position that looked solid. The second is *ʿadhāb muqīm* — a punishment that stays (cf. 5:37; 9:68; 11:39). Ibn Kathīr's reading of 39–40 as a warning is completed here: you will know which religion's people are humiliated now and which are punished without end.

**Knowledge as the Thing They Thought They Already Had**

Verse 39 promised "soon you shall know." Verse 40 names the content. They thought they knew that their gods protected, that their position was honor, that the Prophet's ﷺ position was disgrace. The coming knowledge inverts the labels. Disgrace is not poverty in Makkah; it is the punishment that humiliates. Endurance is not the tribe's long memory; it is the punishment that does not lift.

The believer reading these two verses is not invited to gloat. He is invited to inspect his own *makānah*: am I acting on a position that can survive both horizons? The next verse will return to the Book sent down for mankind in truth, and to the fact that guidance profits only the guided.

**Honor and Duration, Reassigned**

Quraysh measured honor by the ḥaram, by lineage, by the crowding of their gods around the Kaʿbah. They measured duration by tribal memory. This verse reassigns both words. *Yukhzīh* — that disgraces him — is the true ʿār, the true shame. *Muqīm* — enduring — is the true lastingness. A humiliation that ends at the grave is still inside a little life (v. 8's *qalīlan*). A punishment that stays is the first thing that deserves the word "forever" in a human mouth.

The believer uses the verse as a compass, not as a gloat. Which of my current shames are only Makkan — loss of face, loss of market — and which would still be shame if the two horizons of this verse were visible? Acting on a *makānah* that can survive both the disgrace-test and the endurance-test is the only acting verse 39 left open.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:41

> **Truly We have sent down unto thee the Book for mankind in truth. Whosoever is rightly guided, it is for the sake of his own soul. And whosoever goes astray only goes astray to the detriment thereof. And thou art not a guardian over them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Book for Mankind, in Truth — Again**

*Innā anzalnā ʿalayka al-kitāba li'l-nāsi bi'l-ḥaqq* Verse 2 said the Book was sent to the Prophet ﷺ in truth so that he should worship with sincerity. Here *li'l-nās* is added: for mankind. The mercy of revelation is not a private gift to a prophet. It is a public sending whose truth is the same truth as creation's (v. 5). See 2:176, 213; 3:3; 4:105; 5:48; 6:114; 42:17.

**Guidance Profits the Guided; Misguidance Harms the Astray**

*Fa-mani ihtadā fa-li-nafsih wa man ḍalla fa-innamā yaḍillu ʿalayhā* Almost identical wording appears at 10:108 and 17:15. God has no need of their guidance (v. 7). The profit and the loss are the human being's. This is the moral counterpart of "none shall bear the burden of another" (v. 7). You cannot donate your guidance into God's treasury to enrich Him, and you cannot dump your misguidance onto His estate to impoverish Him. You can only save or harm the one soul you actually are.

**"Thou Art Not a Guardian over Them"**

*Wa mā anta ʿalayhim bi-wakīl* — "And thou art not a guardian over them" (cf. 6:107; 42:6; 10:108; 17:54; 25:43). Al-Rāzī reads this as forbidding the Prophet ﷺ to torment himself or expend his soul in regrets (18:6; 26:3; 35:8) over those who refuse. Elsewhere:

> *"Thou art only a warner, and God is Guardian over all things."* (Qur'an 11:12)

> *"God suffices as a Guardian."* (Qur'an 4:81, 132; 33:3, 48)

The office is *balāgh* — delivery — not custody of other people's wills. Every later caller inherits the same limit. Verse 19 asked, "Wilt thou save one who is in the Fire?" Verse 41 answers in administrative language: you are not their *wakīl*. Verse 42 will now show Who actually takes souls.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:42

> **God takes souls at the moment of their death, and those who die not, during their sleep. He withholds those for whom He has decreed death, and sends forth the others till a term appointed. Truly in that are signs for a people who reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Takings: Death and Sleep**

*Allāhu yatawaffā al-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā wa allatī lam tamut fī manāmihā* *Tawaffī* is the taking in full, the receiving of a soul as one receives a completed deposit. The Angel of Death is the sent agent (32:11), but the Taker named is God. In death the soul is fully detached from the body; in sleep, partially (al-Rāzī). Compare 6:60: *"He it is Who takes your souls by night."* A ḥadīth says:

> *"Sleep is the brother of death, and the people of the Garden do not die."* (al-Qurṭubī cites it here)

Every night is a rehearsal of dying; every waking is a rehearsal of resurrection. The signs are not rare. They are nocturnal.

**Withheld and Sent Back**

*Fa-yumsiku allatī qaḍā ʿalayhā al-mawta wa yursilu al-ukhrā ilā ajalin musammā* The sleeper whose death has been decreed does not return to the body; the other is sent back until his named term (6:2, 60; 10:49; 13:2; 46:3). Al-Rāzī reads the sending-forth in this context as the return of souls to bodies at waking — a reprieve until the final taking.

The Prophet ﷺ taught a bedtime speech that enacts this verse:

> *"In Thy Name, my Lord, I lay down my body, and in Thee I raise it. If Thou takest my soul, have mercy upon it; and if Thou sendest it back, protect it with that with which Thou protectest Thy righteous servants."* (al-Bukhārī 6320; Muslim 2714; Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī cite it here)

To sleep without this awareness is to treat the brother of death as mere rest. To sleep with it is to enter the rehearsal as a servant.

**Signs for People Who Reflect**

*Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yatafakkarūn* — "Truly in that are signs for a people who reflect." Reflection here has a concrete object: the nightly taking. A people who think will not need a rare miracle to believe in resurrection. They have died a little and been returned every morning of their lives. The same Lord who withholds and sends back can withhold finally and send forth at the trumpet (v. 68). Verse 43 will ask why, given this Taker of souls, they still take intercessors apart from Him.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:43

> **Or do they take intercessors apart from God? Say, "Even though they have not any power and do not understand?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Intercessors Who Cannot Even Understand**

*Ami ittakhadhū min dūni Allāhi shufaʿāʾ* — "Or do they take intercessors apart from God?" The *am* continues the astonishment: God takes the souls, and they take intercessors other than Him? *Qul a-wa-law kānū lā yamlikūna shayʾan wa lā yaʿqilūn* — "Say: Even though they have not any power and do not understand?" Al-Qurṭubī notes they do not understand — neither the idols nor, in the idolaters' practice, the dead objects called upon. Powerless and mindless: the two disqualifications of a court advocate. An intercessor in human law must have standing and must know the case. These have neither.

**The Qur'anic Rule of Intercession Restated in Advance**

The Book does not deny all intercession. It denies intercession *apart from God* as a right seized by creatures. Verse 44 will say intercession belongs entirely to God. Other verses allow it by His leave (2:255; 20:109; 34:23; 43:86; 53:26). The present verse is aimed at the Makkan structure of verse 3: we worship them only to draw near. Nearness is not for sale through powerless patrons. The next verse will locate all intercession, all sovereignty, and all return in one place.

**Advocates Without Standing**

Human courts require of an advocate two things this verse names as missing: *milk* (power, standing, something owned that could be used) and *ʿaql* (understanding of the case). Stones, graves treated as independent doors, and living personalities treated as unasked lords fail both tests. Even a righteous dead person has no independent *milk* of intercession; what he has, if anything, is by leave (v. 44).

The psychological pull of a visible advocate is strong. People want a face in the court. The Qur'an's answer is not that God is cold. It is that the Face they want is already His, and that He has taught them how to ask (v. 46) and has promised a permitted intercession to the Prophet ﷺ. Inventing a parallel bar of powerless patrons is not extra caution. It is a vote of no confidence in the Judge who takes the souls (v. 42).

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:44

> **Say, "Unto God belongs intercession altogether. To Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth. Then unto Him shall you be returned."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Intercession as God's Property**

*Qul lillāhi al-shafāʿatu jamīʿā* — "Say: Unto God belongs intercession altogether." *Jamīʿā* is altogether, entirely, the whole of it. Ownership of intercession means He grants it, withholds it, and sets its conditions. Prophets intercede by His leave; the Prophet ﷺ is granted the great intercession in authentic ḥadīth (al-Bukhārī 4712; Muslim 193). None of that contradicts this verse. It instantiates it. What verse 43 condemned was taking intercessors *apart from Him* — as independent doors. See 2:48 and 2:255 in their commentaries in this project’s earlier work: no soul avails another except as He permits.

**Sovereignty, Then Return**

*Lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "To Him belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth." The Owner of the estate is the Owner of the petitions filed in it. *Thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn* — "Then unto Him shall you be returned." Ibn Kathīr reads the return as the Judgment; al-Ṭabarī, as the taking to account for idolatry. The three clauses are one argument: He owns intercession, He owns the kingdom, you are going back to Him. Therefore the religion of rival intercessors is a misfiling of a petition in a court that has only one Judge.

**How a Believer Asks**

The practical shape of this verse is a way of asking. Ask God. Ask God by His Names. Ask God to accept the intercession He has promised His Messenger ﷺ. Do not build a parallel court. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> *"If you ask, ask of God; if you seek help, seek help of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516)

Verse 44 is that counsel as theology. Verse 45 will show the idolaters' emotional proof that their court is the wrong one: they recoil when God is mentioned alone.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:45

> **And when God is mentioned alone, the hearts of those who believe not in the Hereafter recoil. But when those apart from Him are mentioned, behold, they rejoice.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Litmus of the Heart**

*Wa idhā dhukira Allāhu waḥdahu ishmaʾazzat qulūbu alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi'l-ākhirah* — "And when God is mentioned alone, the hearts of those who believe not in the Hereafter recoil." *Ishmaʾazza* is to shrink, shudder away, bristle in disgust. Al-Ṭabarī: their hearts withdraw in disdain. Al-Zamakhsharī: distress and rancor. *Wa idhā dhukira alladhīna min dūnihi idhā hum yastabshirūn* — "But when those apart from Him are mentioned, behold, they rejoice." The same hearts that contract at tawḥīd expand at shirk. The emotional evidence is itself a verdict. Love and aversion disclose theology more honestly than slogans.

**Unbelief in the Hereafter as the Root of the Recoil**

The verse specifies *those who believe not in the Hereafter*. If there is no Meeting, then religion is only a social decoration, and a God who insists on being mentioned *alone* is a threat to the decorations — the clan gods, the festivals, the identities built around partners. If there is a Meeting, then mentioning God alone is relief. The recoil is therefore not a taste in liturgy. It is a symptom of denying the Day.

Some commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī) link the verse to the episode around Sūrah al-Najm and the so-called "cranes," when the idolaters rejoiced at a supposed mention of their gods and then 53:23 named those gods as mere names without authority. Whether or not one accepts the details of that report, the verse's psychology is independent of it: mixed religion loves mixed mention.

**Modern Recoils**

A heart can still bristle when God is mentioned without a human mascot, a party, a trend, or a "and also..." A heart can still rejoice when the creature is praised with the language of worship. The verse invites a self-test: what mention makes me expand, and what mention makes me shrink? Verse 46 will now put a prayer on the Prophet's ﷺ tongue that answers the recoil: a prayer to the Originator and Knower who judges the differences.

---
## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:46

> **Say, "O God! Originator of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the Unseen and the seen, Thou judgest between Thy servants regarding that wherein they differ."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer That Is Also a Creed**

*Quli Allāhumma fāṭira al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍi ʿālima al-ghaybi wa'l-shahādati anta taḥkumu bayna ʿibādika fī mā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* *Fāṭir* is from *faṭara*, to split, to cleave, to bring forth by cleaving the unseen into the seen. In the Qur'an the Name always appears as Originator of the heavens and the earth (6:14; 12:101; 14:10; 35:1; 42:11). *ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa'l-shahādah* gathers the hidden and the witnessed (6:73; 9:94; 13:9; 23:92; 32:6; 59:22; 62:8; 64:18). The Judge of differences (already promised in v. 3) is the One who made the world and who misses nothing in it.

**How the Prophet ﷺ Opened His Night Prayer**

ʿĀʾishah was asked how the Prophet ﷺ began his supererogatory prayers at night. She said he would say:

> *"O God, Lord of Gabriel, Michael, and Seraphiel, Originator of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the Unseen and the seen, Thou judgest between Thy servants regarding that wherein they differ. Guide me, by Thy Leave, regarding that of the truth wherein there is dispute. Truly Thou guidest whomsoever Thou wilt unto a straight path."* (Muslim 770; Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī cite it here)

The verse is thus not only a polemic sentence for the pagans. It is a night-prayer of the Messenger ﷺ. In a world of disputed truth, the servant does not appoint himself the final judge. He asks the Originator to guide him in what is disputed, while affirming that judgment belongs to Him.

**Leaving Verdicts with the Only Competent Court**

Verse 3 said God will judge between them. Verse 31 said they will dispute before their Lord. Verse 46 teaches the tongue what to do in the meantime: praise Him as Originator and Knower, and assign the verdict to Him. This is not quietism about falsehood. The Prophet ﷺ still recited, warned, and separated (vv. 14–15). It is the refusal to play God in the inner court of bitterness while the outer court of preaching continues. Verse 47 will now show the evildoers' last attempt to buy their way out of that court.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:47

> **Were those who work evil to possess all that is on the earth and the like of it besides, they would seek to ransom themselves with it from the terrible punishment on the Day of Resurrection. And there will appear unto them from God that which they had not reckoned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ransom That Will Not Be Accepted**

*Wa law anna lilladhīna ẓalamū mā fī al-arḍi jamīʿan wa mithlahu maʿahu la-ftadaw bihi min sūʾi al-ʿadhābi yawma al-qiyāmah* The Qur'an repeats the futility of ransom (2:48, 123; 3:91; 10:54; 13:18; 57:15). 3:91 says an earth full of gold would not be accepted. 70:11–14 says the guilty would wish to ransom himself with his children, spouse, brother, clan, and all on the earth. The present verse doubles the earth's contents — all of it and the like of it — and still the bid fails. What could not be bought with sincerity in the time of choice cannot be bought with quantity in the time of regret.

**"That Which They Had Not Reckoned"**

*Wa badā lahum mina Allāhi mā lam yakūnū yaḥtasibūn* Al-Ṭabarī: they had not reckoned they would be taken to account at all. Al-Qurṭubī: deeds they thought good that were bad (such as idol-worship); or sins they meant to repent of and never did; or the discovery that forgiveness required a repentance they never performed. Compare 50:22:

> *"You were indeed heedless of this. Now We have removed from you your cover; so today your sight is piercing."* (Qur'an 50:22)

Muḥammad ibn al-Munkadir, the reciter, used to say of this verse, "I fear that there will appear unto me from God that which I had not reckoned" (al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī). The pious took the verse as a warning to themselves, not a description of others only.

**Reckoning as a Daily Habit**

ʿUmar's counsel — take yourselves to account before you are taken to account — is the verse's preventive. What appears suddenly on that Day is what was left unexamined. A life that regularly inspects intention, debt to others, and unpaid repentance is a life reducing the unreckoned remainder. Verse 48 will specify what appears: the evils earned, and the mocked reality coming true.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:48

> **The evils of that which they have earned will appear unto them, and that which they used to mock will beset them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Record Made Visible**

*Wa badā lahum sayyiʾātu mā kasabū* — "The evils of that which they have earned will appear unto them." Al-Ṭabarī: presented in the books of deeds given in the left hand (17:13–14; 18:49; 69:25; 84:7–10). Al-Rāzī: or the effects of the deeds — the forms of retribution they earned. Either way, *kasb* (earning) is again the keyword. They are not shown a random tragedy. They are shown their wages.

**The Mocked Thing Surrounds Them**

*Wa ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn* — "and that which they used to mock will beset them" (cf. 6:10; 11:8; 16:34; 21:41; 40:83; 45:33; 46:26). *Ḥāqa* is to close in, surround, come down as an inescapable consequence. What they laughed at — resurrection, fire, the warning, the reciter — becomes the climate they cannot leave. Al-Ṭabarī: no escape. Al-Rāzī: it comes from every direction. And:

> *"God does not wrong people in the least, but people wrong themselves."* (Qur'an 10:44)

Mockery is not a harmless social tone. It is a way of refusing to prepare, and the thing refused as a joke becomes the thing that encloses. Verse 49 will return to the psychology of blessing claimed as personal desert — another form of not reckoning.

**When the Joke Becomes the Climate**

Mockery is a way of refusing to prepare without having to refute. A refutation can be answered. A joke claims to have already won. *Ḥāqa bihim* says the joke becomes the weather: not an object in front of them but a surrounding. Al-Rāzī's "from every direction" means there is no remaining angle from which the old laugh still works.

The evils that appear are *sayyiʾāt mā kasabū* — evils of what they earned. The Book of deeds (18:49) that "leaves out nothing small or great" is the mechanism of appearance. What was edited out of the public story is edited back in. The verse is therefore a doctrine of memory: God does not forget the file that the self forgot, and the self will be made to read it.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:49

> **And when harm befalls man, he calls upon Us. Then, when We confer upon him a blessing from Us, he says, "I was only given it because of knowledge." Nay, it is a trial, but most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Verse 8 Repeated, Then Diagnosed More Deeply**

The first half repeats the pattern of verse 8 and of 10:12 and 30:33: harm, then calling, then blessing. The new element is the sentence the man says in ease: *innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm* — "I was only given it because of knowledge." Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī list the senses: because God knew I deserved it; because I have a special position; because I knew I deserved it; because my knowledge earned it; because of knowledge God taught me. Korah says the same in 28:78: *"I have only been given it on account of knowledge I possess."* Al-Rāzī calls the whole sequence a great contradiction: they admit in pain that none but God can protect them, then ascribe their gains to themselves.

**Trial, Not Trophy**

*Bal hiya fitnah wa lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn* — "Nay, it is a trial, but most of them know not." The blessing is *fitnah* — a test, a furnace, a sorting. Al-Rāzī: one must show gratitude when it comes and patience when it goes. Knowledge that produces *ikhlāṣ* would read blessing as a question: will you remember the One you called in harm? Knowledge that produces Korah's sentence reads blessing as a diploma. Most do not know which knowledge they are using.

**The Modern Diploma of Ease**

Few today credit idols for their promotions. Many credit "my degrees, my hustle, my network, my intelligence." The grammar is Korah's. Skill is real; secondary causes are real; the verse does not command stupidity. It forbids the last ascription. The same mouth that begged in the hospital and then said "I got better because of science" — without denying science — has still failed the trial if God has been dropped from the sentence. Verse 50 will say that earlier peoples said the same, and their earnings did not avail.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:50

> **Those before them did say the same; yet that which they used to earn availed them not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Is Older than Quraysh**

*Qad qālahā alladhīna min qablihim* — "Those before them did say the same." Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī identify "the same" as the claim of verse 49. Korah is the named exhibit (28:76–81); unnamed exhibits fill the destroyed cities of verses 25–26. The argument "I have this because I know" is not an achievement of modern self-esteem. It is an antique.

**Earnings That Do Not Avail**

*Fa-mā aghnā ʿanhum mā kānū yaksibūn* — "yet that which they used to earn availed them not" (cf. 15:84; 40:82). Al-Qurṭubī notes that *mā* can also be interrogative: "in what way did their earning avail them?" Both readings empty the portfolio. On the Day when neither wealth nor children avail, except him who comes with a sound heart (26:88–89), the knowledge-diploma of Korah is paper.

The verse is meant to break the spell of successful unbelievers. Their earnings are visible; their availing is not. Verse 47 has already shown them trying to ransom themselves with doubled earths. Verse 51 will say the evils of those earnings befell the earlier people, and will befall these too.

**Antique Self-Attribution**

Korah's sentence is old because the temptation is structural. Skill feels like origin. The man who studied, traded, and won can see every hour of his labor and none of the hours of his creation in threefold darkness (v. 6), none of the rain that grew the field (v. 21), none of the term appointed that could have ended the labor last night (v. 42). "Those before them said the same" collapses the pride of originality. You have not discovered a new philosophy of success. You have repeated a destroyed people's motto.

*Mā aghnā ʿanhum* empties the museum of successful unbelief. Palaces, libraries of "knowledge I possess," and caravans of earning stand as exhibits of non-availing. The sound heart of 26:89 is the only remaining currency. Verse 52 will then relocate provision in God's outspreading and straitening, so that even the believer's labor cannot be misread as Korah's diploma.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:51

> **So the evils of that which they earned befell them. And as for those who do wrong among these, the evils of that which they have earned will befall them, and they cannot thwart [it].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Past as a Completed Case-File**

*Fa-aṣābahum sayyiʾātu mā kasabū* — "So the evils of that which they earned befell them." The first half of the verse closes the file of "those before them" (v. 50). What they earned did not avail (v. 50); instead, the evils of that earning *aṣābahum* — struck them, hit their appointed mark. History in the Qur'an is not entertainment. It is case law.

**"Among These" — The File Still Open**

*Wa alladhīna ẓalamū min hāʾulāʾi sa-yuṣībuhum sayyiʾātu mā kasabū* *Hāʾulāʾi* ("these") are the Quraysh and the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries. The future prefix *sa-* makes the strike a matter of time, not of doubt. The same moral physics applies across centuries. Wrongdoing is not insulated by being recent.

**They Cannot Thwart It**

*Wa mā hum bi-muʿjizīn* — "and they cannot thwart it." They cannot outrun God in this life or the next (al-Rāzī; cf. 35:44; 72:12), nor thwart the promised punishment:

> *"Indeed, that which you are promised shall come to pass, and you cannot thwart it."* (Qur'an 6:134)

Power, delay, walls, and alliances are not *iʿjāz* — not a rendering of God incapable. The word is the same root as the Qur'an's own inimitability: they cannot produce a disabling miracle against the consequence. Verse 52 will now remove the last intellectual alibi: provision is not a function of their cleverness.

**Same Physics, Open File**

The Qur'an's use of *hāʾulāʾi* — "these" — is a refusal to let the present tense hide inside antiquity. People imagine that lessons apply to "them," the excavated. This verse names the living as the next case. *Sa-yuṣībuhum* is future, not subjunctive. The evils of earning will strike as they struck.

*Mā hum bi-muʿjizīn* strips the last fantasy of the powerful: that they can render God incapable. Armies, walls, wealth, and delay are not iʿjāz. The word is chosen with irony, because the Qur'an itself is the *muʿjizah* they could not match (17:88). They cannot disable the consequence any more than they could produce the like of the Book. The moral order is not a rival poet to be out-versed.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:52

> **Do they not know that God outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will? Truly in that are signs for a people who believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision as Divine Act, Not IQ**

*A-wa-lam yaʿlamū anna Allāha yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu wa yaqdir* — "Do they not know that God outspreads and straitens provision for whomsoever He will?" The question answers verse 49's "I was given it because of knowledge." Al-Jalālayn: He outspreads as a test and straitens as a trial. The same pairing appears at 13:26; 17:30; 28:82; 29:62; 30:37; 34:39; 42:12. Al-Rāzī: one does not receive blessing or misfortune based on intelligence or ignorance, nor on natural disposition or outside forces, but because God provides and withholds as He wills.

Secondary causes remain: work, skill, weather, trade. The verse relocates the last why. Two equally skilled people, two equally foolish people, do not always eat the same. The believer is meant to see a Sign in that remainder, not a puzzle that abolishes effort.

**Signs for People Who Believe**

*Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūn* — "Truly in that are signs for a people who believe" (cf. 6:99; 16:79; 27:86; 29:24; 30:37). Al-Ṭabarī: people who affirm the truth when it is made clear know that the One who does this could only be God. Belief is the organ that reads provision's expansion and contraction as speech. Unbelief reads them as Korah read them: as a mirror of the self.

This verse closes the long section on death, sleep, false intercession, ransom, mockery, and the arrogance of earned blessing (vv. 42–52). Verse 53 will open the most hopeful door in the Qur'an: even the prodigal are told not to despair of mercy.

**Labor Without the Last Ascription**

The verse does not cancel work. The Prophet ﷺ tied the camel (al-Tirmidhī 2517) and worked commerce. What it cancels is the last "because." Two equally skilled people eat differently; two equally idle people eat differently. The remainder is *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdir* — He outspreads and He measures. Al-Jalālayn's pairing of expansion as test and contraction as trial matches verse 49's *fitnah*. Wealth is not a diploma; poverty is not always a verdict of stupidity.

*Li-qawmin yuʾminūn*: belief is the organ. Without it, expansion looks like Korah and contraction looks like cosmic spite. With it, both look like speech from the Keyholder (v. 63). The signs are public; the reading requires īmān. This verse closes the arrogance-block (vv. 42–52) so that verse 53 can open to prodigals who have nothing left to ascribe to their knowledge except the damage to their own souls.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:53

> **Say, "O My servants who have been prodigal to the detriment of their own souls! Despair not of God's Mercy. Truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address to the Ruined**

*Qul yā ʿibādiya alladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati Allāh* — "Say: O My servants who have been prodigal to the detriment of their own souls! Despair not of God's Mercy." Many scholars count this among the most hopeful verses of the Qur'an, alongside 4:48, 116; 24:22; 33:47; 42:19, 22; 93:5. Al-Ṭabarī calls such verses among "the most joyous of the Qur'an." Compare:

> *"Whosoever does evil or wrongs himself, and then seeks forgiveness of God, he will find God Forgiving, Merciful."* (Qur'an 4:110)

*Isrāf* is prodigality, exceeding bounds — here, against one's own soul. The addressees are not a polite middle. They are people who have damaged themselves. And they are still *My servants*. The possessive is not withdrawn by the prodigality.

**Occasion: Waḥshī, the Forced, and the Despairing Sinners**

Al-Ṭabarī notes that the verse addresses all humanity, while many reports give first occasions. Some Makkans feared they could not enter Islam because they had worshipped idols, killed those God had made inviolable, or fornicated (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī). *Isrāf* would then include the crimes of jāhiliyyah. Others said it was revealed about Muslims whom the Makkans prevented from emigrating and forced to recant, who then feared their repentance would not be accepted (al-Ṭabarī). A third view: it corrected Muslims who claimed that every major sinner was already of the Fire (al-Ṭabarī). The reports about Waḥshī — who killed Ḥamzah and later feared no path remained — belong to this cluster, whether the verse is dated Madinan on that account or not. The wording is wider than any one biography.

Thawbān reported that the Prophet ﷺ said he would not wish the whole world in exchange for this verse. A man asked about the one who associates partners with God; the Prophet ﷺ was silent, then said three times, "And why not one who associates partners with God?" (al-Ṭabarī). That forgiveness, the commentators immediately add, is contingent on leaving shirk, because:

> *"Truly God forgives not that any partner be ascribed unto Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 4:48; 4:116)

The door is as wide as repentance. It is not a door that lets a person keep the partners and collect the pardon.

**"God Forgives All Sins"**

*Inna Allāha yaghfiru al-dhunūba jamīʿā* — "Truly God forgives all sins." *Jamīʿā* is altogether. After repentance and the abandonment of shirk, no sin is too large to be named unforgivable. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"If you did not sin, God would remove you and bring a people who would sin and seek forgiveness from God, and He would forgive them."* (Muslim 2749)

Forgiveness is not God's reluctant exception. It is intrinsic to how He deals with a species that sins and returns. *Innahu huwa al-Ghafūru al-Raḥīm* — "Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful": the two Names seal the verse as character, not as a one-time amnesty.

**Despair as Itself a Sin against Mercy**

*Lā taqnaṭū* — do not despair. Qunūṭ is the closing of hope, the decision that the future is already Hell. The verse treats that decision as unauthorized. A servant may know his sins exactly and still may not issue the final verdict on himself. That verdict is God's, and God has announced mercy. In an age of both shamelessness and self-destruction, the verse cuts both ways: it does not license persistence in prodigality, and it does not license the suicide of hope. Verses 54–55 will immediately attach the mercy to turning, submitting, and following the revelation — before punishment arrives.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:54

> **Turn unto your Lord and submit to Him before the punishment comes upon you, whereupon you will not be helped.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy Has a Door, and the Door Has a Time**

*Wa anībū ilā rabbikum wa aslimū lahu min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu al-ʿadhābu thumma lā tunṣarūn* — "Turn unto your Lord and submit to Him before the punishment comes upon you, whereupon you will not be helped." Verse 53 forbade despair; verse 54 forbids delay. *Inābah* is turning in repentance (al-Ṭabarī): returning to obedience, withdrawing from disobedience. *Aslimū lahu* is submit to Him — the verb of islām, handing the self over. Together they recall the sūrah's opening command to worship with religion devoted entirely to Him (vv. 2, 11, 14), which al-Ṭabarī links to the primordial ḥanīf state:

> *"Truly I have turned my face toward Him Who created the heavens and the earth, as a ḥanīf, and I am not of the idolaters."* (Qur'an 6:79)

And:

> *"Know they not that God accepts repentance from His servants, and receives the charitable offerings, and that God is the Relenting, the Merciful?"* (Qur'an 9:104)

**Before, Not After**

*Min qabl* — before the punishment. Help after the punishment's arrival is denied: *thumma lā tunṣarūn*. The window is the window of this life, of health, of the uncollapsed city, of the unstilled breath. Verse 8's man turned during harm and forgot during blessing. Verse 54 asks for a turning that does not wait for harm, and a submission that does not wait for the fire's canopy. The next verse will specify what to follow in that remaining time: the most beautiful of what has been sent down.

**The Clock Attached to Mercy**

Verse 53 without verse 54 would be misread as a hammock. Verse 54 attaches a clock. *Min qabl* — before — is the most important preposition in the repentance literature. God accepts repentance until the death-rattle, as the ḥadīth says:

> *"God accepts the repentance of the servant so long as he has not begun to gurgle (at death)."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537; Ibn Mājah 4253; graded ḥasan)

And He does not accept it at the sight of the sun from the west (Muslim 157). "Before the punishment" includes those horizons. *Thumma lā tunṣarūn* means that after the window, help is not withheld out of stinginess. It is withheld because the exam has been collected. Turning and submitting now is the only help the verse will still name.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:55

> **And follow the most beautiful of that which has been sent down unto you from your Lord, before the punishment comes suddenly upon you while you are unaware,"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Following, Not Sampling**

*Wa ittabiʿū aḥsana mā unzila ilaykum min rabbikum* — "And follow the most beautiful of that which has been sent down unto you from your Lord." As in verse 18, "most beautiful" is not a license to discard parts. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī: it means act in accord with the revelation — obedience, not disobedience; what is enjoined, not what is forbidden. The purpose of sent-down injunctions is achieved only when they are practiced. Some (Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī) read it as the Qur'an being the best of the books sent down, as verse 23 called it the most beautiful discourse (al-Rāzī). Both readings agree on action.

**Sudden, While Unaware**

*Min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu al-ʿadhābu baghtatan wa antum lā tashʿurūn* — "before the punishment comes suddenly upon you while you are unaware." *Baghtatan* is all at once, by surprise. *Lā tashʿurūn* is the same unawareness as verse 25's destroyed nations. The two "befores" of verses 54–55 (before punishment; before sudden punishment in unawareness) are one mercy: you are being told that surprise is the default of the unprepared, and that preparation is still possible. Verses 56–58 will now script the three laments of the soul that waited.

**Practice as the Meaning of "Most Beautiful"**

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī's insistence that *aḥsan* means acting on the revelation, not ranking its parts, is a fence against a familiar abuse: the "spiritual" anthology that keeps consolation verses and deletes law, warning, and the Fire's canopies. The most beautiful of what was sent down includes verse 16 and verse 53 together, as verse 23's pairing of quiver and softness already taught.

*Baghtatan wa antum lā tashʿurūn* binds this counsel to verse 25. Unawareness is not an excuse; it is the predicted condition of the unprepared. Preparation is following. The suddenness of punishment is the ordinary weather of a life that postponed verses 54–55. The three laments that follow are the script of that postponement, written down now so they will not have to be improvised then.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:56

> **lest any soul should say, "Alas for me, for what I neglected of my duty to God! Indeed, I was among the scoffers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Lament: Negligence Confessed Too Late**

*An taqūla nafsun yā ḥasratā ʿalā mā farraṭtu fī janbi Allāh* — "lest any soul should say: Alas for me, for what I neglected of my duty to God!" *Ḥasrah* is utmost regret (al-Rāzī). *Farraṭtu* is I fell short, I wasted, I neglected the deeds enjoined (al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī). *Fī janbi Allāh* is literally "in the side of God." Commentators gloss: God's right (al-Bayḍāwī); obedience to God (al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī); God's reward. In Arabic, *janb* (side) can mean closeness — being at someone's side. The neglected thing is nearness itself, or what leads to nearness (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarsī).

**The Scoffer's Uniform**

*Wa in kuntu la-mina al-sākhirīn* — "Indeed, I was among the scoffers." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī: those who scoffed at the Qur'an and the Messenger. Verse 48 said the mocked thing would beset them. Here the soul admits it wore the mockers' uniform. The lament of verse 56 is at least honest: it names the shortfall and the tone of life that produced it. Honesty after the door closes does not reopen the door. It documents why the door was there.

**Ḥasrah as a Teacher While Time Remains**

The verse exists so that this sentence will be said *now*, in a smaller voice, while following (v. 55) is still possible — not then, when help is not given (v. 54). A believer who can already say "I have neglected the side of God" and then stand in the night (v. 9) has used ḥasrah as fuel rather than as a final epitaph.

**Using Ḥasrah While It Still Moves the Limbs**

*Yā ḥasratā* is among the most human sounds in the Book. The verse exists to move that sound forward in time: say a smaller version of it tonight, then follow (v. 55). The *sākhirīn* — scoffers — are not only the loud mockers of a Messenger. They include the cultured smile at seriousness, the joke that keeps a warning from landing (v. 48). A life can be spent in light scorn and still be the life this soul admits.

*Fī janbi Allāh* as nearness neglected is a particularly searching gloss. Not only prayers missed, but a side left empty — the companionship of God unchosen while other sides were crowded. The soul's "alas" is the discovery that the neglected side was the only one that would have lasted.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:57

> **Or lest it say, "If only God had guided me, I would have been among the reverent."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Lament: Blame Shifted Upward**

*Aw taqūla law anna Allāha hadānī la-kuntu mina al-muttaqīn* — "Or lest it say: If only God had guided me, I would have been among the reverent." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note that the lament is, in one sense, formally correct: God guides whom He wills to a straight path (2:213; 24:46). In another sense it is a false excuse, because signs were offered and refused. Verse 59 will answer: *Nay, My signs did indeed come unto you*. The soul that says verse 57 is trying to turn a withheld gift into an alibi for a refused gift.

**Responsibility Inside Decree**

The sūrah has already held both truths: whom God leads astray has no guide (vv. 23, 36); whom He guides none leads astray (v. 37); and whoever is guided, it is for his own soul (v. 41). Decree does not cancel the sending-down of the Book, the parables, the night's possibility, or the call not to despair. The one who ignored these cannot, at the end, indict the Guide for not having dragged him. Al-Ṭabarsī: they chose not to look at the signs, turning from the Qur'an toward the world.

**A Present-Tense Misuse**

The same sentence is sometimes said now, as fatalism: "If God wanted, I would pray." Verse 57 is preserved so that we will recognize that sentence as a lament of the damned, not as a theology of the living. The living are told to turn, submit, and follow (vv. 54–55). Those instructions would be cruel if verse 57 were a valid present excuse.

**Decree Is Not an Alibi for Refusal**

The formally correct half of the lament — God guides whom He wills — is the half the Qadarī and the fatalist both mishandle. The fatalist says it now, as an excuse not to pray. The Qadarī denies it later, as if guidance were a human product. Verse 59 will answer both by pointing to arrived signs. The Book, the parables, the night, the call not to despair: these are guidance already given in the sense of showing (90:10). The guidance of *tawfīq* — being made to walk — is asked for daily in *ihdinā*. What cannot be done is to refuse the showing and then indict the Giver of tawfīq.

The living who hear this verse are meant to retire the sentence "If God wanted, I would..." as a present theology. It is archived here as a sentence of the damned.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:58

> **Or lest it say, when it sees the punishment, "If only I had another chance, I would be among the virtuous."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Lament: A Request for Replay**

*Aw taqūla ḥīna tarā al-ʿadhāba law anna lī karratan fa-akūna mina al-muḥsinīn* Unlike verse 57's blame of God, this lament admits some fault and asks for a second life. Compare 32:12: *"Send us back that we may work righteousness. Truly we are certain."* The answer of the Book is that the request is not treated as sincere:

> *"And if they were sent back, they would return to the very thing they had been forbidden. Truly they are liars."* (Qur'an 6:28)

They have had revelation, and a reprieve until death (v. 42). The "other chance" was the first chance, unrecognized.

**Why Seeing Punishment Does Not Equal Reform**

Reform that requires the sight of Hell is not the iḥsān of verse 34. Iḥsān is excellence when the punishment is still unseen — worship as though you see Him. The soul of verse 58 wants to become *muḥsin* under conditions that abolish the test. That is not virtue. It is surrender to force. The Day is not a classroom for new character; it is the disclosure of the character already made. Verse 59 will now answer all three laments with one fact: the signs came.

**Virtue Under Force Is Not Iḥsān**

Iḥsān, as the ḥadīth of Gabriel defined it, is excellence when the unseen is still unseen. Asking to become *muḥsin* at the sight of punishment is asking to sit an exam after the answers are published. 6:28's "they are liars" is not a claim that they would not feel sorry. It is a claim that the character that produced the life would, if replayed under the same veil, produce the life again.

The "other chance" was the first chance: sleep returned (v. 42), harm that taught prayer (v. 8), a Book without crook (v. 28), a verse that said do not despair (v. 53). The soul of verse 58 is not underinformed. It is a soul that wanted a world in which virtue is unnecessary until fire is visible. That world was never the test.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:59

> **Nay, My signs did indeed come unto you; yet you denied them, waxed arrogant, and were among the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Reply to the Three Scripts**

*Balā qad jāʾatka āyātī fa-kadhdhabta bihā wa istakbarta wa kunta mina al-kāfirīn* *Balā* is the particle that contradicts a negative: it is not the case that guidance never came, that no chance was given, that neglect was unavoidable. *Āyātī* — My signs — includes the recited revelation and the signs on the horizons and in the selves (41:53). The three verbs then match the three diseases: denial (*takdhīb*), arrogance (*istikbār*), and the settled identity of kufr.

**Arrogance as the Engine**

The Prophet ﷺ defined the arrogance that bars Paradise:

> *"No one will enter Paradise in whose heart is an atom's weight of arrogance... Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people."* (Muslim 91)

Verse 59's *istakbarta* is that rejection when the signs have already arrived. The laments of 56–58 sounded almost pious. This verse discloses the biography underneath them: denial, pride, disbelief. The next verse will show that biography on the face.

**Three Verbs, One Biography**

Denial, arrogance, disbelief: the verse does not list three unrelated crimes. Denial is the mouth's refusal of arrived signs. Arrogance is the heart's reason for the refusal — the truth would require descent from a height (Muslim 91). Disbelief is the settled identity that those two produce. The laments of 56–58 sounded like piety, theology, and aspiration. This verse is the biography that makes them false.

*Jāʾatka āyātī* is in the second person singular: the signs came *to you*. The address personalizes what the damned would prefer to keep generic. Not "humanity was warned," but "you were." The unawareness of verse 55 is hereby denied as innocence. Unawareness was chosen by denial and pride.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:60

> **And on the Day of Resurrection thou wilt see those who lied against God having blackened faces. Is there not in Hell an abode for the arrogant?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faces as Records**

*Wa yawma al-qiyāmati tarā alladhīna kadhabū ʿalā Allāhi wujūhuhum muswaddah* — "And on the Day of Resurrection thou wilt see those who lied against God having blackened faces." Compare 3:106–107 (whitened faces and blackened faces); 80:38–42 (shining faces and faces covered with dust and darkness); 10:27 (faces covered with patches of night). The lie against God (v. 32) migrates to the skin. What was hidden in the breast (v. 7) becomes a color on the countenance. The face that would not soften at remembrance (v. 23) is darkened at the Meeting.

**Hell as the House of Arrogance**

*A-laysa fī jahannama mathwan li'l-mutakabbirīn* — "Is there not in Hell an abode for the arrogant?" Verse 32 asked if Hell was an abode for the disbelievers. Here the residents are named by the vice of verse 59: *mutakabbirīn*, those who make themselves great. Greatness is God's cloak, as the ḥadīth qudsī says:

> *"Glory is My izār (cloak) and majesty is My ridāʾ (garment). Whoever contends with Me in either of them, I shall cast him into the Fire."* (Muslim 2620; Abū Dāwūd 4090)

Hell as *mathwā* (abode) of the arrogant is not an arbitrary assignment. It is the place where false greatness is housed after it has been stripped. Verse 61 will name the opposite housing: those who are reverent, saved by their triumph.

**The Face as a Published Secret**

Verse 7 said He knows what lies within breasts. Verse 60 says the breast's lie against God will be worn on the face. 3:106's whitened and blackened faces, 80:38–42's radiance and dust, are the same publication. There is no remaining makeup. The arrogant, who lived by managing faces, are housed in a *mathwā* that matches the vice: Hell, where false greatness is stored after it has been stripped.

The ḥadīth qudsī of glory as God's cloak (Muslim 2620) explains why *mutakabbirīn* are named rather than a more generic "sinners." Contending with God for greatness is a specific theft. The abode fits the theft. Verse 61's reverent, who did not contend, are saved by a triumph rather than housed by a claim.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:61

> **And God saves those who are reverent by their triumph; evil will not befall them; nor will they grieve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Salvation as Deliverance with a Prize**

*Wa yunajjī Allāhu alladhīna ittaqaw bi-mafāzatihim* — "And God saves those who are reverent by their triumph." *Najāh* is rescue. *Mafāzah* is a place or state of winning, a desert crossed to safety, a triumph. Al-Ṭabarī: their virtues or deeds. Al-Ṭabarsī: their being delivered from the Fire. Others: the triumph of spiritual concerns over worldly ones. Attaining the Garden is called *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*, the great triumph, in over a dozen verses (e.g., 5:119; 44:51–57). The reverent are not merely "not punished." They are saved *by* a triumph — their taqwā has already been a crossing.

**No Evil, No Grief**

*Lā yamassuhumu al-sūʾu wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* — "evil will not befall them; nor will they grieve." Al-Ṭabarī: the torments of Hell will not touch them, nor will they grieve over the world that has passed. Ibn Kathīr: they are spared punishment on the Day of Resurrection. The pairing of *sūʾ* (evil, harm) and *ḥuzn* (grief) is the pairing of fear of the future and sorrow for the past — the two human weights that 2:38, 62, 112, 262, 274, 277 repeatedly lift from the successful. Verse 10 promised the patient a wage without reckoning; verse 20 promised *ghuraf*; verse 34 promised whatever they will; verse 61 gathers the negative side: no touching evil, no grief.

The next two verses will ground this salvation in who God is: Creator and Guardian of all things, Holder of the keys of the heavens and the earth.

**Triumph as the Manner of Rescue**

*Bi-mafāzatihim* can be read as "by means of their triumph" or "to their place of triumph." Either way, the reverent are not extracted as an afterthought. Their taqwā was already a crossing — the dangerous-road attention this commentary has used as the image of taqwā — and the rescue is the arrival that such attention was for. The great *fawz* of the Garden verses in the Book is the same word-family.

*Lā yamassuhumu al-sūʾu wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* lifts the two weights every human being actually wants lifted: harm ahead and grief behind. The Fātiḥah asked to be among those blessed; this verse describes that blessing from the inside of the Day. Fear and grief, which verse 9 carried as wariness and which verse 16 used as warning, are here ended for the people who let those fears do their work in time.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:62

> **God is the Creator of all things, and He is Guardian over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creator of All — Including the Scene Just Named**

*Allāhu khāliqu kulli shayʾ* — "God is the Creator of all things." After Hell's abode and the reverent's triumph, the sūrah lifts the camera to the One who made every actor and every outcome. Creation here is total: heavens, wombs, cattle, night, day, the Fire as a created justice, the Garden as a created mercy. Nothing in the two processions of the coming end (vv. 71, 73) is outside His making.

**Guardian over All**

*Wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin wakīl* — "and He is Guardian over all things." *Wakīl* is the trustee, the one in charge, the sufficient manager. Verse 41 told the Prophet ﷺ he is not *wakīl* over the people. This verse names Who is. Compare 11:12: *"Thou art only a warner, and God is Guardian over all things."* The human being is relieved of a job he could not do and is forbidden to despair of a job God does not fail to do.

The verse is a resting-place in the argument. Before the keys (v. 63), the rebuke of ignorant worship (v. 64), and the warning that shirk nullifies works (v. 65), the servant is told: the One you are being asked to worship alone is the One who already creates and already superintends everything. Exclusive worship is not a promotion of a tribal god. It is alignment with fact.

**Resting the Argument on Who He Is**

Before the keys, the rebuke of the ignorant, and the nullification of works by shirk, the sūrah pauses on two predicates: Creator of all, Guardian over all. Worship of such a One is not a tribal preference. It is agreement with fact. The Fire and the Garden, the two *zumar*, the trumpet, the womb, the cattle, the night — all are inside *kulli shayʾ*.

*Wakīl* also relieves the Prophet ﷺ of verse 41 and every later caller of a job they cannot do. The Guardian over all things is already on duty. Exclusive worship is alignment with that guardianship, not the promotion of a local god to a vacancy. There is no vacancy.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:63

> **Unto Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth. And those who disbelieve in the signs of God, it is they who are the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Keys as Sovereignty, Treasuries, and Knowledge**

*Lahu maqālīdu al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "Unto Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth" (cf. 42:12). Al-Zamakhsharī: He has sovereignty over them and protects them. Al-Ṭabarī: the keys of the treasuries. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī: an allusion to the treasuries themselves:

> *"Naught is there but that its treasuries lie with Us, and We do not send it down save in a known measure."* (Qur'an 15:21; cf. 63:7)

He opens provision and mercy for whom He wills and closes them as He desires. The keys may also refer to knowledge:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen. None knows them but He."* (Qur'an 6:59)

On a spiritual reading (Sahl al-Tustarī and others), He possesses the keys of all hearts. All readings agree: nothing needed — food, guidance, rain, unlocking of a breast (v. 22) — is stored in the rivals' houses.

**The Losers Named by Their Denial of Signs**

*Wa alladhīna kafarū bi-āyāti Allāhi ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn* — "And those who disbelieve in the signs of God, it is they who are the losers." Al-Ṭabarī: having denied the signs, they lose any share of the good God measures from His treasuries, here and hereafter. Verse 15 defined the manifest loss as losing souls and families. Here the loss is tied to refusing the signs that were the keys' public notice. To deny the signs is to stand outside the unlocked door and call it locked.

**Living as People Who Do Not Own the Keys**

The verse humbles the planner and comforts the poor. Careers, states, and savings are not the keyring. They are among the things unlocked or left locked. Tawakkul (v. 38) is the practical inference: ask the Keyholder, work with what He has already opened, and do not worship the locked doors as if they were lords. Verse 64 will now express amazement that anyone would still invite the Prophet ﷺ to worship other than the Keyholder.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:64

> **Say, "Do you bid me to worship other than God, O ignorant ones?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Amazement**

*Qul a-fa-ghayra Allāhi taʾmurūnnī aʿbudu ayyuhā al-jāhilūn* — "Say: Do you bid me to worship other than God, O ignorant ones?" After the keys, the Creator-Guardian, the Sufficer, the Originator, the question is almost incredulous. Al-Rāzī's line of thought: they ask him to worship those who have none of the keys alongside the One who has them all. Compare 38:9: do they have the treasuries of your Lord's mercy? The vocative *al-jāhilūn* is not a schoolyard insult. *Jahl* in this register is the jāhiliyyah's ignorance: knowledge of God as Creator (v. 38) without knowledge of His exclusive right to worship.

**The Pressure to Blend**

Quraysh's offers to the Prophet ﷺ included compromise: worship our gods a year, we worship yours a year (the background of Sūrah 109). This verse is the refusal in one line. Every later pressure to "include" a partner — political, academic, social — is the same bidding. The answer remains the question: do you command me to worship other than the Keyholder? The next verse will state what happens to works if the partner is accepted.

**Ignorance After Knowledge**

They would say God created the heavens and the earth (v. 38). They are still *jāhilūn* because jahl in the Qur'an's moral lexicon is not empty-headedness. It is the refusal to let knowledge govern worship. The question "Do you bid me...?" is amazement that such a bid could still be made after the keys have been named.

Compromise always arrives as sophistication: "include, be larger, do not be so absolute." Sūrah 109 and this verse treat that sophistication as ignorance. The Keyholder is not enlarged by partners. He is measured wrongly (v. 67). The Prophet ﷺ's refusal is therefore not narrowness of culture. It is accuracy of measure.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:65

> **Surely it has been revealed unto thee and unto those before thee that if thou dost ascribe partners [unto God], thy work will surely come to naught and thou shalt be among the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Warning Addressed Even to Prophets**

*Wa la-qad ūḥiya ilayka wa ilā alladhīna min qablika la-in ashrakta la-yaḥbaṭanna ʿamaluka wa la-takūnanna mina al-khāsirīn* The second person is the Prophet ﷺ, and the warning is extended to all previous messengers. The point is not that prophets commit shirk. The point is that the law is universal: no office exempts works from being nullified by partnership with God. If even the addressed Messenger is spoken to this way, no saint, scholar, or nation is immune.

**Ḥubūṭ al-ʿAmal: Works That Fall**

*Yaḥbaṭanna ʿamaluka* — your work will become void, fall, miscarry. The Qur'an repeats that without correct belief, deeds earn no lasting reward (2:217; 3:22; 5:5, 53; 6:88; 7:147; 9:17; 11:16; 18:105; 33:19; 47:9, 28, 32). The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Deeds are only in accord with their intentions, and every person has only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

Shirk is the intention that reroutes the deed to other than God; the deed then does not arrive. The losers of verses 15 and 63 include those whose portfolios of work were large and whose destination was cancelled by a partner in the intention.

**Why This Severity Is Mercy**

If mixed works counted, the pure religion of verses 2–3 would be optional. The severity protects *ikhlāṣ*. It also protects the sinner of verse 53: the way back is not to pile more mixed deeds, but to purify the object of worship so that deeds can stand. Verse 66 will state the positive replacement: worship God and be among the thankful.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:66

> **Rather, worship God and be among the thankful!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Replacement, Not a Vacuum**

*Bali Allāha fa-uʿbud wa kun mina al-shākirīn* — "Rather, worship God and be among the thankful!" *Bal* is correction: not the partners of verse 64, but God. The fronted Name is again restriction. Al-Ṭabarī: be thankful for His guidance in worshipping Him alone and repudiating idols, and, in the context of the keys, for what He has measured from His treasuries.

**Thankfulness as the Occupying Virtue**

A heart from which shirk is emptied will fill with something. The verse names the occupant: *shukr*. Verse 7 said God is pleased with gratitude for the servants' sake. Verse 8 showed ingratitude after blessing. Verse 49 showed knowledge-arrogance after blessing. Verse 66 commands the stable alternative: worship plus thanks — not worship plus self-congratulation, not thanks to the means as if they were gods.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The one who does not thank people does not thank God."* (Abū Dāwūd 4811; al-Tirmidhī 1955)

And he ﷺ stood in prayer out of thanks (al-Bukhārī 4836). Thankfulness here is not a mood. It is a membership: *kun mina al-shākirīn* — be among that people. Verse 67 will now show what ingratitude and partnership look like when measured against God's true measure: the earth a handful, the heavens enfolded in His right hand.

**Fill the Space Shirk Vacates**

A heart told only "do not" will fill with something else — often with self. *Kun mina al-shākirīn* names the people to join. Thankfulness occupies the space from which partners were expelled. It looks at the treasuries (v. 63), the womb (v. 6), the returned soul after sleep (v. 42), and the door of verse 53, and it answers with *shukr* rather than with Korah's diploma (v. 49).

Al-Ṭabarī's pairing of thanks for tawḥīd and thanks for provision keeps the verse from becoming either dry doctrine or mere table-manners. Worship without thanks becomes grim duty. Thanks without worship becomes a toast to the means. The verse wants both, in that order: *uʿbud*, then *kun mina al-shākirīn*.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:67

> **They did not measure God with His true measure. The whole earth shall be but a handful to Him on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens will be enfolded in His right Hand. Glory be to Him, exalted is He above the partners they ascribe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wrong Measure as the Root Error**

*Wa mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrih* — "They did not measure God with His true measure" (cf. 6:91; 22:74). Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī: they did not honor Him as He should be honored. Al-Bayḍāwī: they described Him by attributes that do not pertain to Him. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī: they did not believe He has complete power over all things. Almost all commentators relate the verse to Makkah's idolaters. Some also transmit that a Jew (in some reports a rabbi) said to the Prophet ﷺ that God would put the heavens on a finger, the earths on a finger, the mountains on a finger, the rest of creation on a finger, then say, "I am the King." The Prophet ﷺ smiled until his molars showed and recited this verse (al-Bukhārī 4811, 7414–7415; Muslim 2786; Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī). Some said the smile was confirmation of the shared monotheistic image against the idolaters; less reliable accounts treat the verse as a rebuke of the speaker. The verse's first audience remains those who assigned partners.

**The Earth a Handful, the Heavens Enfolded**

*Wa'l-arḍu jamīʿan qabḍatuhu yawma al-qiyāmah wa'l-samāwātu maṭwiyyātun bi-yamīnih* Al-Bayḍāwī: the ease with which God can bring creation to an end. The image is of utter scale: what fills our sky and our maps is, to Him, a closed fist and a folded cloth. Compare:

> *"On the Day of Resurrection, God will seize the earth and fold up the heavens in His right hand, then He will say: 'I am the King. Where are the kings of the earth?'"* (al-Bukhārī 7382; Muslim 2787)

The Attributes of hand and grasping are affirmed as He affirmed them, without likening God to creatures and without stripping the words of meaning — the rule of the early imams. The point of the verse is majesty, not anatomy.

**Glory above Partners**

*Subḥānahu wa taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn* (cf. 10:18; 16:1; 30:40). After the handful and the folding, partnership is not only false. It is grotesque — like ascribing rivals to One who folds the sky. Verse 4's *subḥānahu* denied a child; this *subḥānahu* denies partners at the moment the cosmos is shown as small in His grip. Verse 68 will now blow the trumpet over that small cosmos.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:68

> **And the trumpet will be blown, whereupon whosoever is in the heavens and on the earth will swoon, save those whom God wills. Then it will be blown again, and, behold, they will be standing, beholding.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Blasts, Two Worlds**

*Wa nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-ṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti wa man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa Allāh thumma nufikha fīhi ukhrā fa-idhā hum qiyāmun yanẓurūn* Other verses mention the trumpet (6:73; 18:99; 20:102; 23:101; 27:87; 36:51; 50:20; 69:13; 74:8; 78:18). This verse is distinct in naming two blowings by (as the commentators specify) the angel Isrāfīl. The first produces *ṣaʿaq* — swooning, the collapse of all creation. The second produces resurrection: *qiyām*, standing, *yanẓurūn*, looking, beholding.

**Who Is Excepted**

Those exempted from the first swoon are said by some (al-Ṭabarī, al-Ṭabarsī) to be the archangels Gabriel, Michael, Isrāfīl, and the Angel of Death. Others say eventually everything swoons, then God raises Isrāfīl to blow again (Ibn Kathīr). The second blast is the single cry of 79:13–14 and 30:25, after which they are upon the open expanse.

The Prophet ﷺ said the period between the two blasts would be forty — without specifying hours, days, months, or years — then:

> *"God will send down a rain from the sky, and the body will sprout as a green plant sprouts. Every part of the person will deteriorate except one bone, the coccyx. From it creation will be assembled on the Day of Judgment."* (al-Bukhārī 4814, 4935; Muslim 2955; Ibn Kathīr)

The field of verse 21 yellows and becomes chaff; the human field is rained on after the trumpet and stands.

**Standing and Beholding**

*Qiyāmun yanẓurūn* — they will be standing, looking. The posture of prayer (qiyām) becomes the posture of resurrection. The looking is the looking of people whose cover has been removed (50:22). The next verses will fill what they behold: a shining earth, a Book set down, prophets and witnesses, full payment.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:69

> **The earth will shine with the Light of its Lord, the Book will be set down, and the prophets and the witnesses will be brought forth. Judgment will be made between them in truth, and they shall not be wronged.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Earth Lit by Its Lord**

*Wa ashraqati al-arḍu bi-nūri rabbihā* — "The earth will shine with the Light of its Lord." It is not this earth's sunlight. On that Day the earth shall be changed into other than the earth (14:48). God will leave it a barren plain without crookedness or curvature (20:106–107). Human beings, and some say all creatures, come from the graves in throngs (78:18). A ḥadīth describes them gathered "barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised," sweat reaching the nostrils, yet not looking at one another, because for every man that Day his affair shall suffice him (80:37; Muslim 2859). The light is the Lord's light — disclosure, not a lamp.

**The Book Set Down**

*Wa wuḍiʿa al-kitāb* — "the Book will be set down." Each is presented the book of deeds that leaves out nothing small or great (18:49; 17:71; 69:19–29). The sūrah that began with the sending-down of the Book (v. 1) ends with a book set down as evidence. Revelation and record meet.

**Prophets and Witnesses**

*Wa jīʾa bi'l-nabiyyīna wa'l-shuhadāʾ* — "and the prophets and the witnesses will be brought forth." Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarsī: the recording angels (43:80; 50:17–18; 82:10–11). Others: those who witness that the prophets delivered and the people denied; or the martyrs; or everything that testifies, including limbs (41:20). The court of verse 31 is now staffed.

**Judgment in Truth, Without Wrong**

*Wa quḍiya baynahum bi'l-ḥaqq wa hum lā yuẓlamūn* — "Judgment will be made between them in truth, and they shall not be wronged." The same *bi'l-ḥaqq* as the Book's sending and the world's creation. The scales:

> *"We shall set the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged in aught. Even if it be the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it."* (Qur'an 21:47; cf. 2:281; 6:160; 10:47; 16:111; 36:54; 45:22; 46:19)

Verse 70 will complete the payment.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:70

> **Every soul will be paid in full for that which it has done, and He knows best what they do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Full Payment, Not Approximate Justice**

*Wa wuffiyat kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat* — "Every soul will be paid in full for that which it has done" (cf. 16:111; 2:281; 3:25). *Tawfiyah* is payment without remainder or shorting. The commercial language of the sūrah — loss (v. 15), earning (vv. 24, 48, 51), ransom (v. 47) — reaches its settlement. Good and evil done, or earned (2:281), are paid.

**He Knows Best What They Do**

*Wa huwa aʿlamu bi-mā yafʿalūn* — "and He knows best what they do." The payment is not based on the public version of the deed. Verse 7 already said He knows what lies within breasts and will inform them of what they used to do. Iḥsān's hidden night-worship (v. 9) and shirk's hidden rivals are both in the knowledge that sets the wage. No soul is underpaid by ignorance of the Judge, and no soul is overpaid by a costume.

The next four verses will sort the paid souls into two throngs: driven to Hell, driven to the Garden.

**The Wage Matches the Hidden Deed**

*Wuffiyat* is the verb of paying a debt in full. No remainder of good is uncredited; no remainder of evil is uncollected — except as verse 35's mercy erases the worst for the virtuous and pays them at their best. The general rule of this verse is full payment; verse 35 is the extra kindness inside that court for those who came with truth.

*Aʿlamu bi-mā yafʿalūn* blocks the last courtroom trick: the public version of the deed. Night-worship no one saw (v. 9), mixed intention no one audited (v. 2), mockery in private (v. 48), prodigality later regretted (v. 53) — He knows them better than the doers do. The two *zumar* of the next verses are not a tribal sorting. They are this knowledge, published as two processions.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:71

> **And those who disbelieve will be driven unto Hell in throngs, till when they reach it, its gates will be opened and its keepers will say unto them, "Did not messengers from among you come to you, reciting unto you the signs of your Lord and warning you of the meeting with this your Day?" They will say, "Yea, indeed!" But the Word of punishment has come due for the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Zumrah: Driven to Hell**

*Wa sīqa alladhīna kafarū ilā jahannama zumarā* — "And those who disbelieve will be driven unto Hell in throngs." *Sīqa* is driven, herded. *Zumar* are companies, the word that names the sūrah. Those whose scales are light (23:102–103; 101:6–9) are driven and thrust into the Fire with violent thrusting (52:13). The processional image is ordered, not chaotic: ranks of ruin.

**Gates Opened, Keepers Asking**

*Ḥattā idhā jāʾūhā futiḥat abwābuhā wa qāla lahum khazanatuhā* The keepers are said to be nineteen (74:30). Their question is rebuke (cf. 40:50; 67:8–9): *a-lam yaʾtikum rusulun minkum yatlūna ʿalaykum āyāti rabbikum wa yundhirūnakum liqāʾa yawmikum hādhā* The messengers were *from among you* — not alien species. The signs were recited. The Day was named. The three laments of 56–58 are already answered before they can be re-spoken.

**The Confession That No Longer Saves**

*Qālū balā* — "They will say: Yea, indeed!" Compare 67:9–10: a warner came, they denied, and they admit that had they listened or understood they would not be in the Blaze. *Wa lākin ḥaqqat kalimatu al-ʿadhābi ʿalā al-kāfirīn* (cf. 10:33; 22:18; 36:7; 40:6). Affirmation after arrival at the gate is not repentance. It is the closing of the file. Verse 19 asked who could save one for whom the Word had come due. Verse 71 shows them admitting the Word at the threshold.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:72

> **It will be said, "Enter the gates of Hell, to abide therein." How evil is the abode of the arrogant!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Entry as Sentence**

*Qīla dkhulū abwāba jahannama khālidīna fīhā* — "It will be said: Enter the gates of Hell, to abide therein." After the keepers' question and the confession, the imperative of entry. *Khālidīn* — abiding. The gates that opened in verse 71 now receive. The arrogant of verse 60, whose abode was asked about, are told to enter it.

**How Evil a Mathwā**

*Fa-biʾsa mathwā al-mutakabbirīn* — "How evil is the abode of the arrogant!" *Mathwā* again: a dwelling, a settled place. Arrogance chose height in this world; its house is the pit. The contrast with verse 20 is architectural: *ghuraf* stacked above rivers for the reverent; gates of Hell as an evil abode for those who made themselves great. Verse 73 will drive the other *zumar* to the opposite gates.

**Entry After Confession**

The confession of verse 71 (*balā*) does not commute the sentence. It completes the evidence. "Enter" is then only justice made audible. *Khālidīn* answers every hope of a temporary shame, a purgatory of pride that will later graduate. For the arrogant who died upon kufr, the abode is evil and it stays.

The naming of *al-mutakabbirīn* at the threshold binds this verse to 39:60 and to the ḥadīth of God's cloak of glory. Hell is not merely hot. It is the house of a stolen attribute returned to its Owner. The architectural contrast with the *ghuraf* (v. 20) and with the greeting of peace (v. 73) is now complete: two gates, two speeches, two kinds of abiding. The sūrah's title is about to be fulfilled in the second procession.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:73

> **And those who reverence their Lord will be driven to the Garden in throngs, till when they reach it, its gates will be opened and its keepers will say unto them, "Peace be upon you; you have done well; so enter it, to abide [therein]."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Zumrah: Driven to the Garden**

*Wa sīqa alladhīna ittaqaw rabbahum ilā al-jannati zumarā* — "And those who reverence their Lord will be driven to the Garden in throngs." The same verb *sīqa* — driven — is used of both companies, a linguistic mercy and severity together: both are conducted by a power not their own. The difference is destination and reception. Ibn Kathīr: one throng after another, the best, then the next. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"I will be the first to enter Paradise."* (Muslim 196; Ibn Kathīr)

And the first group "appearing like the moon on the night it is full, followed by a group like the brightest star" (al-Bukhārī 3246; Muslim 2834). In other reports they number seventy thousand or more who enter without reckoning (al-Bukhārī 5705; Muslim 216). Several aḥādīth mention eight gates of the Garden (al-Bukhārī 3257; Muslim 286).

**Gates Already Opening, Keepers Greeting**

*Ḥattā idhā jāʾūhā wa futiḥat abwābuhā* — "till when they reach it, and its gates will be opened." Many grammarians note the extra *wāw* ("and its gates will be opened") as a mark of honor: the gates are opened in welcome before or as they arrive, unlike Hell's gates opened upon arrival as a shock. The keepers say: *salāmun ʿalaykum ṭibtum fa-dkhulūhā khālidīn* — "Peace be upon you; you have done well; so enter it, to abide." Compare 13:23–24: angels entering from every gate, *"Peace be upon you because you were patient."* Other greetings of peace in the Garden: 7:46; 10:10; 16:32; 19:62; 25:75; 33:44; 36:58; 56:25–26.

**Ṭibtum: You Have Become Good**

*Ṭibtum* is you have become pure, fragrant, good. The taqwā of the sūrah — night-worship, sincerity, patience, repentance, following the most beautiful — is here named as a quality of the people themselves. They are not only going to a good place. They have been made good. Abiding (*khālidīn*) matches Hell's abiding in duration and infinitely exceeds it in content. Verse 74 will give them speech: praise for the Promise kept.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:74

> **They will say, "Praise be to God, Who was faithful to us in His Promise, and has caused us to inherit the land, that we may settle in the Garden wheresoever we will." How excellent is the reward of the workers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Praise as the First Sentence of Arrival**

*Wa qālū al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī ṣadaqanā waʿdah* — "They will say: Praise be to God, Who was faithful to us in His Promise." The keepers greeted with peace; the residents answer with ḥamd. Verse 20 said God will not fail the tryst. Here the tryst is kept, and the tongue's first work is praise. The Promise includes 10:4 (the return is true) and 31:8–9 (Gardens of bliss, God's Promise in truth), and 4:122. The disbelievers had scoffed, *"When will this promise come to pass?"* (10:48; 21:38; 36:48; 67:25). The residents know.

**Inheriting the Land**

*Wa awrathanā al-arḍa natabawwaʾu mina al-jannati ḥaythu nashāʾ* The righteous inherit Paradise (7:43; 23:10–11) and inherit the earth (21:105). Inheritance here means they may settle *ḥaythu nashāʾ* — wherever we will — matching verse 34's whatsoever they will. The land is not a national spoil. It is the earth of the Garden as a homeland finally matching the will that has been healed.

**The Wage of the Workers**

*Fa-niʿma ajru al-ʿāmilīn* — "How excellent is the reward of the workers!" *ʿĀmilīn* are those who worked — the night-standing, the sincere worship, the patience of verse 10, the following of verse 18 and 55, the turning of verse 54. The Garden is a wage (*ajr*), not a random jackpot. Work did not purchase God; God promised a wage and paid it. The next and last verse will widen the camera from the residents to the angels around the Throne, and will end as the Fātiḥah began: praise to the Lord of the worlds.

## Sūrah al-Zumar 39:75

> **And thou shalt see the angels encircling all around the Throne, hymning the praise of their Lord. Judgment shall be made between them in truth, and it will be said, "Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Widest Circle**

*Wa tarā al-malāʾikata ḥāffīna min ḥawli al-ʿarsh yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim* — "And thou shalt see the angels encircling all around the Throne, hymning the praise of their Lord." The human throngs have been housed. The camera lifts to the Throne and to those who encircle it. 40:7–8 describes those who bear the Throne and those around it, supplicating for the repentant and asking Gardens of Eden for them and their righteous families. Whether those and these are the same choir or overlapping choirs, the last image of al-Zumar is not human triumph as self-display. It is angels in *tasbīḥ* with *ḥamd* — declaring God far above, with praise.

**Judgment in Truth — the Refrain Completed**

*Wa quḍiya baynahum bi'l-ḥaqq* — "Judgment shall be made between them in truth." The same clause as verse 69. Differences (v. 3, v. 46), disputes (v. 31), and deeds (v. 70) are finished in truth. The sūrah that opened with a Book from the Mighty, the Wise, closes with a verdict from the same truth.

**The Last Sentence of Creation's Story**

*Wa qīla al-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — "and it will be said: Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds." Ibn Kathīr: uttered by the angels or by God Himself. Commentators note that another account of creation's beginning opens the same way:

> *"Praise be to God, Who created the heavens and the earth, and made darkness and light."* (Qur'an 6:1)

And the people of Paradise end their call with it (10:10), and the Fātiḥah begins with it (1:2). Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarsī take the echo to mean that creation begins and ends with God's praise. The first content sentence of the Qur'an is the last sentence of history.

**What It Means to Recite This Verse Now**

To say *al-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn* in this world's prayers is to rehearse the end. The servant who has walked this sūrah — from sincere worship, through the parable of one master, through death and sleep, through the prohibition of despair, through two processions — arrives at a praise that does not depend on his mood. It will be said, whether any given human tongue is ready or not. The wise join it early. ʿĀʾishah's report returns at the end: the Prophet ﷺ would not sleep until he had recited this sūrah. He ended his night, often, at this verse — angels around the Throne, judgment in truth, praise to the Lord of the worlds — and then slept under the same Lord, who takes souls in sleep and sends them back until a term appointed.

Thus Sūrah al-Zumar completes its work: it has sorted the two companies in speech before it sorts them in fact, and it has left the tongue of the living with the sentence that the universe itself will close upon.
