# Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt (Chapter 37) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

A Makkan sūrah, al-Ṣaffāt is believed to have been revealed directly after Sūrah 6, al-Anʿām (JJ). It takes its name from the reference to those ranged in ranks in vv. 1 and 165, both of which refer to the angels, and the naming places the angels at the sūrah's centre from its opening oath to its closing refutation.

Ālūsī notes that the sūrah can be seen as connected to the previous one in that it tells the stories of several generations or civilizations that were destroyed for rejecting God's messengers, material upon which readers or listeners are enjoined to reflect in 36:31: *Have they not considered how many generations before them We destroyed, such that they return not unto them?* The connection is therefore one of shared subject, and the earlier sūrah supplies the instruction to reflect that this one carries out.

After a brief reaffirmation of God's Oneness and Omnipotence (vv. 1–5), al-Ṣaffāt describes the futility of the efforts of those who attempt to obtain knowledge of the Unseen through the jinn (vv. 6–10). It then chastises the disbelievers for rejecting the Oneness of God, the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ, and the Resurrection, warning of the ignominious end they will face in the Hereafter and of the manner in which they will challenge and question one another regarding the fate that has befallen them (vv. 11–39).

This is followed by a description of the rewards God's sincere servants receive in the Garden (vv. 40–49) and the exchange they will have with the disbelievers they had known in this world, who are now in Hell (vv. 50–60). A description of the punishments in the form of food and drink given to the denizens of Hell (vv. 61–68) is followed by an explanation of their fate (vv. 69–73).

The sūrah then provides accounts of several prophets and the blessings God bestowed upon them: Noah (vv. 75–82), Abraham (vv. 83–113, a passage in which the near sacrifice of his son is also discussed, vv. 101–8), Moses and Aaron (vv. 114–22), Elias, that is Elijah (vv. 123–32), Lot (vv. 133–38), and Jonah (vv. 139–48). In these accounts, it is only in the story of Jonah that the people repent (vv. 147–48); the people associated with all the others are destroyed for having rejected God's messenger.

That single exception is what gives the prophetic sequence its shape. Six accounts are given, and in five the rejection ends in destruction, so the repentance of Jonah's people stands out as the one case where the warning produced its intended effect.

The discussion of the prophets is followed by a challenge to the worldview of the idolaters of the Prophet Muhammad's time (vv. 149–63) and a refutation in particular of the Divine powers they ascribe to the angels (vv. 164–66). The refutation returns the sūrah to the angels named in its title, and closes the circle opened by the oath at verse 1.

The sūrah concludes with a final address to the disbelievers and a promise that the Prophet ﷺ and his followers will triumph, as did the other prophets mentioned in the sūrah (vv. 167–82). The promise is grounded in the accounts that precede it, and the grounding is what makes the conclusion an argument rather than an assertion.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:1

> **By those ranged in ranks,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath by Ordered Ranks and the Weight of Divine Attention**

In the opening of al-Ṣaffāt, the Qur’an does not begin with a direct command or a narrative of prophets, but with a solemn Divine oath—yāmīn—whose witness is the celestial order itself. The word ṣāffāt derives from the Arabic root ṣ-f-f, which means to arrange, line up, or stand in consecutive order. Here it describes angels, birds, or the ranks of worshippers, all positioned with disciplined precision before their Lord. The form ṣāffāt is the plural feminine active participle, conveying ongoing, continuous arrangement rather than a single moment of alignment. Muslims have long understood this as a reference to the angels who stand in ranks before God, as in 37:165: And indeed there are among them those who are ranged in ranks. But al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr also note that the same word appears in 67:19—by the birds spreading (ṣāffāt) their wings—suggesting that the oath may encompass both the angelic hosts and the avian creation, both manifestations of flawless order.

The theological implication is immediate: God is swearing by entities that exhibit absolute regularity, and in doing so He prepares the listener for the affirmation that follows—the Oneness of the Lord—because only One God can sustain such perfect arrangement across all realms. The oath is not decorative; it is a rhetorical bridge. When a human being swears by something precious, the listener is moved to take the following statement seriously. Here, God swears by His own ordered universe, and what follows is the foundation of faith: “Truly your God is One.” The connection is logical: if the cosmos is arranged with such precision, the One who arranged it must Himself be One, without partner, without contradiction.

**Modern Parallel:** In an age of disorder and noise, the image of “those ranged in ranks” speaks to the human need for structure—whether in prayer lines, in communities, or in the disciplined pursuit of knowledge. Taqwā, as scholars note, is not chaotic emotion but orderly awareness, like soldiers in formation who stay vigilant because they know the enemy is near. The verse teaches that spirituality begins with alignment: aligning one’s actions, intentions, and heart with the Divine order.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The angels are ranged in ranks in prayer, or in battle, or in every act of worship; this emphasizes the majesty of God and the perfection of His command.”

> **Cross-Reference:** See 37:165—“And indeed there are among them those who are ranged in ranks”—reinforcing that both angels and prophets are arranged in disciplined order.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:2

> **and the drivers driving,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Drivers, Rebuke, and the Movement of Divine Purpose**

The second element of the oath introduces zājirāt—those who drive or rebuke—using the Arabic root z-j-r, which carries a double meaning: to drive forward (as with camels or clouds) and to rebuke or restrain (as with sin or Satan). In classical exegesis, these “drivers” are interpreted as angels who drive the clouds across the sky, distributing rain with precise timing, or as angels who drive away the satans from approaching human beings and from intercepting heavenly discourse. Al-Qurṭubī emphasizes that the word zājarāt can mean “those who drive away,” referring to the angels who repel evil jinn, a theme developed explicitly in the very next verses (6–10) where God establishes guards against defiant satans.

The morphological pattern zājirāt is active participle plural feminine, mirroring ṣāffāt. This parallelism suggests that the oath is not listing unrelated phenomena but describing complementary functions of the same heavenly order: some are arranged in ranks (static, reverent), others are in motion (dynamic, protective). The cloud-drivers represent God’s providence over the physical world—rain, seasons, fertility—while the rebukers represent His providence over the moral world. Together they demonstrate that God’s governance operates in both domains without contradiction.

**Hadith Connection:** The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said: “The angels of God never cease making supplication for the believer as long as he remains in a state of prayer, saying, ‘O Lord, bestow upon him what You have bestowed upon Your righteous servants.’” While this hadith (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī) focuses on angels’ intercession, it reinforces that angels are not passive decorations but active agents—drivers, intercessors, guards—who serve divine purpose. The “drivers” in verse 2 are thus part of a broader angelological reality in which spiritual beings participate in the preservation of order.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings often feel that moral progress requires external pressure—someone or something driving them away from harm. The verse validates this intuition by showing that at the cosmic level, God Himself has assigned “drivers” to push back against evil. Applied personally, this means that a believer should not rely solely on internal willpower but should seek external structures—prayer, community, accountability—that act as spiritual drivers, rebuking laziness and guiding movement toward good.

> **Cross-Reference:** 67:5—“Truly We have adorned the lowest heaven with lamps and made them missiles against the satans” — the drivers rebuke and repel, just as the stars become missiles.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The drivers are the angels who drive the clouds; they drive them to wherever God wills, spreading rain and blessing across lands.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:3

> **and the reciters of a reminder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Recitation, Remembrance, and the Human Bridge to the Divine**

The third component of the oath shifts from celestial motion (drivers) to human or angelic speech: qāriʾāt—those who recite. The root q-r-ʾ relates to reading, reciting, and proclaiming aloud, most prominently in the context of the Qur’an as al-qurʾān, “the Recitation.” In this verse, the reciters are those who read or recite “a reminder” (dhikr), which classical scholars identify as either the Qur’an itself, the Torah and Gospel, or the general remembrance of God that should permeate life. Ibn ʿAbbās and others note that this may refer to angels who recite the divine revelation, or to human beings—prophets and scholars—who bear the message. Either reading reinforces the central theme: the universe is ordered, and within that order there is a spoken word that guides.

The morphological pairing of qāriʾāt with ṣāffāt and zājirāt creates a triad: arrangement (structure), movement (protection), and recitation (communication). No single element is sufficient. A society with order but no guidance becomes rigid; a society with guidance but no order becomes chaotic; a society with protection but no speech remains silent about truth. The oath, therefore, is not about angels alone but about the complete conditions of revelation: God’s order must be known, protected, and spoken.

**Religious Practice:** The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: “The best of you are those who learn the Qur’an and teach it.” This hadith (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī) connects to verse 3 because recitation is not a private luxury but a public obligation—those who recite become part of the celestial “drivers” of human consciousness, pushing people away from forgetfulness and toward remembrance.

**Modern Connection:** In a digital age saturated with noise, “reciting a reminder” is a revolutionary act. To open the Qur’an, to listen to its recitation, to teach it to children—these actions place the believer within the same order described in the oath. The reciter is not merely consuming information but participating in the divine arrangement, becoming one of the qāriʾāt whose voices, when sincere, carry the same weight as angelic ranks.

> **Cross-Reference:** 68:1—“Nūn. By the Pen and what they inscribe” — the written and recited reminder begins with revelation.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “The reciters are the angels who recite the Qur’an to the Prophet, and also the scholars who recite it to people; both are included in God’s oath because both maintain the chain of remembrance.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:4

> **truly your God is One,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Oath and the Absolute Unity of God**

After the threefold oath—ranged ranks, drivers, reciters—the verse delivers its answer: “Truly your God is One.” This is not a casual assertion but the logical conclusion of the oath. If the celestial order is perfect, if angels drive and recite with precision, the source of all this cannot be divided, duplicated, or contradictory. The word wāḥid (One) is used here not in the numerical sense of “one among many” but in the ontological sense of absolute uniqueness, completeness, and indivisibility. In Arabic, wāḥid can indicate uniqueness (aḥad), unity (tawḥīd), and singular ownership. The verse chooses wāḥid specifically because it bridges these meanings: God is the only object of worship, the only sovereign, and His essence is without partners or parts.

The grammatical structure is emphatic: inna (truly) introduces a nominal sentence with a predicate that is definitive. The addressee is “your God” (ilāhikum)—not an abstract deity but the personal Lord of the Prophet’s community. This personal framing is critical. Tawḥīd is not merely a philosophical conclusion about the cosmos; it is a relational reality. The God who arranged angels, who drives clouds, who sends recitation, is the same God to whom “you” turn in prayer, in grief, in hope.

**Aqīdah Dimension:** The verse establishes the first pillar of Islam—ṣaḥāda: La ilāha illa Allāh—before any mention of prophethood. This ordering is deliberate. Before the Prophet is mentioned, the Lord is affirmed. Before the message is delivered, the sender is confirmed as One. This prevents the common error of following a messenger while neglecting the unity of the source. It also counters polytheism not only by denying partners but by affirming that all the order described in verses 1–3 flows from a single origin.

**Logical Reasoning:** If there were two gods, each could command differently; the universe would contain contradictions rather than harmony. The very regularity described in “those ranged in ranks” is evidence of a single legislator. This is the Qur’anic argument from cosmic order to divine unity, an argument that resonates with modern physics’ search for unified theories—though the Qur’an’s unity is not scientific but theological, the principle is shared: order implies a single ordering intelligence.

> **Cross-Reference:** 5:73—“Surely they have disbelieved who say, ‘Indeed, God is the third of three’” — the affirmation of One directly opposes trinitarian and polytheistic claims.

> **Classical View (Ibn Taymiyyah):** “Tawḥīd is the foundation of the religion; without it, no other act of worship is accepted, because worship directed to other than God is false worship, and worship to God without recognizing His unity is incomplete worship.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:5

> **Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, and Lord of the easts.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scope of Lordship from the Lowest to the Highest Horizon**

The verse expands the meaning of “Your God is One” by defining the domain of His lordship: the heavens, the earth, and all that lies between them. The phrase “whatsoever is between them” (wa-mā bayna-humā) is not a vague filler but a deliberate inclusion of every intermediate realm—the atmosphere, the orbits of celestial bodies, the spiritual dimensions that human senses cannot directly perceive, and the human beings themselves who live suspended between the ground and the sky. By naming these three zones—above, below, and between—the Qur’an asserts that God’s sovereignty is total, leaving no space ungoverned, no realm outside His authority.

The second clause, “Lord of the easts” (rabb al-mashāriq), introduces a geographic and temporal dimension. The word mashāriq derives from the root sh-r-q, to rise, as in shurūq al-shams (the rising of the sun). In Arabic, the “east” is not a single point but a succession of rising points across the year—this is precisely why the plural mashāriq is used. It evokes the sun’s daily birth in the east and the seasonal shift of sunrise along the horizon. God is Lord of every point of rising, meaning He governs time as well as space, seasons as well as locations. For the Prophet’s audience, this would have been a powerful affirmation: the pagans worshipped celestial bodies—the sun, moon, stars—yet here God claims lordship over even the sun’s path.

**Scholarly Connection:** Al-Ṭabarī notes that “Lord of the easts” also implies that God is the one who causes light to emerge from darkness, a metaphor for revelation itself. Just as the sun rises to illuminate the physical world, God’s message rises to illuminate the heart. The two meanings—geographic and spiritual—are not contradictory but complementary.

**Psychological Implication:** Human beings often divide their lives into compartments—work, family, faith, entertainment—treating God as relevant to only some. The verse rejects this compartmentalization by stating that God is Lord of everything between heaven and earth, which includes every decision, relationship, and moment. Taqwā, therefore, is not a weekend activity but the continuous awareness that one is always within God’s domain.

> **Cross-Reference:** 55:17—“Lord of the two easts and Lord of the two wests” — Allah’s lordship extends in all directions, leaving no geographical escape from His authority.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The easts refer to the places from which the sun rises throughout the year; God is Lord of all of them, which shows that He alone creates and governs the celestial movements, not the sun itself, which is merely a created lamp.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:6

> **Truly We adorned the lowest heaven with an ornament, the stars,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Decoration of the Sky and Its Hidden Purpose**

The verse introduces zayyannā al-samāʾ al-dunyā—“We adorned the lowest heaven” (al-samāʾ al-dunyā, the nearest celestial realm). The word zayyanna comes from the root z-y-n, which means to beautify, decorate, or embellish. But decoration in the Qur’anic context is never purely aesthetic; it is functional. The stars are not just beautiful points of light but “ornaments” that serve a protective purpose, as the following verse clarifies. The phrase “lowest heaven” distinguishes it from the higher heavens (al-samāwāt al-ʿulā) mentioned in other verses, placing this realm closest to human observation, where its effects are most directly felt.

The Arabic word for stars here is kawākib, which can refer to all celestial bodies—sun, moon, planets, fixed stars—though the singular kawkab is often used for the larger, more prominent bodies. The ornamentation is metaphorical but also real: the night sky, when unclouded, is one of the most striking experiences of human life, inspiring awe, contemplation of mortality, and recognition of cosmic scale. The Qur’an does not suppress this aesthetic response; it validates it as part of divine design, while redirecting it from worship of the stars toward worship of the Star-Maker.

**Scientific Note (without overreach):** Modern astronomy confirms that stars are not randomly scattered but follow galactic structures, stellar populations, and orbital dynamics. The “ornament” is not chaos but organized beauty—galaxies, constellations, binary systems—echoing the “ranged in ranks” of verse 1. The Qur’an does not require scientific accuracy to be true, but where scientific discovery aligns with its descriptions, it strengthens rather than weakens the believer’s confidence in a purposeful Creator.

> **Cross-Reference:** 50:6—“And We adorned the lowest heaven with lamps” — the same verb (zayyanna) is used, confirming that stars are both beautiful and functional as lamps and missiles.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The ornament is the stars, which are beautiful to look at and which serve as a protection against the satans; without them, the sky would be empty and the jinn could freely listen to the angels.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:7

> **and a guard against every defiant satan.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guard, the Defiant Satan, and the Boundary Between Worlds**

Following the ornament of stars, the verse reveals their true function: ḥifẓ, protection. The word ḥifẓ means to guard, watch, preserve, and defend. It implies active vigilance, not passive existence. The stars are not merely decorative lights; they are guards positioned against every defiant satan (shayṭān marīd). The root m-r-d means to rebel, to be insolent, to reject authority with arrogance. A shayṭān marīd is not merely a fallen jinn who made a mistake but one who is actively hostile, refusing submission to God’s command. The Qur’an explicitly states that these defiant beings attempt to access the heavenly assembly (v. 8), so the stars serve as a barrier that prevents them from doing so.

The phrase “every defiant satan” (kull shayṭān marīd) is comprehensive. It does not say “some” or “many” but “every” (kull), indicating that the protection is absolute and universal, not selective. No matter how powerful or cunning a satan is, the guard holds. This is a statement of divine omnipotence: God’s defense is not partial, not dependent on human merit, but a fixed feature of the cosmos.

**Hadith Connection:** The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) is reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim to have said: “When Allah created the heavens, He stretched them, and He said, ‘O heavens, bring forth your rain,’ and they did; and He said, ‘O earth, bring forth your fruits,’ and they did. And when He created the jinn from fire, and the human beings from clay, the jinn began to eavesdrop, so God placed stars in the sky as missiles against them.” This hadith directly links the stars’ role to repelling jinn, confirming the verse’s meaning.

**Modern Analogy:** Just as a home security system is not installed for decoration but for active defense, the stars are God’s celestial security system—not to be admired only for beauty but respected for authority. The believer should recognize that every harmful spiritual influence is already bounded by divine limits; awareness of this boundary produces calm, not fear.

> **Cross-Reference:** 67:5—“We have adorned the lowest heaven with lamps and made them missiles against the satans” — confirming both ornament and protection.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The stars are guards because they are missiles of fire that strike the jinn when they try to climb to the heavens; without them, the jinn would freely listen to the angels’ conversations and deceive humanity.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:8

> **They listen not to the Highest Assembly, for they are repelled from every side—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Highest Assembly and the Exclusion of the Rebellious**

The verse begins with mā yasmaʿūn—“they do not listen”—referring to the defiant satans mentioned in verse 7. The object of their failed listening is al-naṣīb al-aʿlā, the Highest Assembly, which in Qur’anic usage refers to the gathering of angels around God’s Throne, where divine decrees are discussed, where the Prophet (peace be upon him) was asked about what they disputed (38:69), and where knowledge of the unseen is shared only by God’s direct permission. The phrase “they are repelled from every side” (yur'adūn min kulli jānib) describes the satans’ condition: they are pushed away, expelled, blocked, turned back—intensively and from all directions. The root r-ʿ-d means to repel, drive away, or refuse entry; combined with “every side,” it conveys total exclusion.

The logical progression from verse 6 to 8 is precise: stars are ornament (6), stars are guard (7), and because of this guard, the defiant cannot listen to the Highest Assembly (8). The chain is causal. Without the stars, the satans could access the assembly; because of them, access is denied. This is not a war of equals—God’s angels and celestial missiles are infinitely stronger than the jinn—but a demonstration of divine organization: God has set boundaries so that evil cannot corrupt everything.

**Theological Point:** The Highest Assembly is a reminder that there is knowledge beyond human reach. The Prophet himself said: “I do not know what they disputed, except that God asked me and I said, ‘I do not know.’ Then He said, ‘He who is close to me will tell you.’” This humility—admitting ignorance about the unseen—is the antidote to the satans’ deception, which offers false knowledge to fortune-tellers and sorcerers. The verse teaches that true knowledge is protected, not easily stolen.

> **Cross-Reference:** 38:69—“We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him; it is only a reminder and a clear Qur’an, to warn those who live, and that the Word may be justified against the disbelievers.” — the assembly’s knowledge is reserved for divine purposes.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “The Highest Assembly is the assembly of angels; they dispute about who will descend to earth, or about deeds of human beings, and God reveals to them what He wills; but the satans are turned away because they have no right to be among them.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:9

> **cast out, and theirs shall be a punishment everlasting—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Expulsion and the Permanence of Justice**

The verse completes the sentence begun in verse 8: the defiant satans are cast out (maqrūnīn—cast away, expelled, thrown out), and their punishment is everlasting (ʿadhāb dāʾim). The word maqrūn is derived from the root q-r-n, meaning to be tied, established, or—by metaphor—permanently cast into a state from which there is no return. It is not a temporary banishment; it is a fixed, eternal exclusion from the Highest Assembly and from any hope of restoration.

The phrase “theirs shall be a punishment everlasting” (lahum ʿadhābun dāʾim) is stark and unambiguous. The punishment is not merely long; it is permanent, without end, continuous in its severity. The root d-w-m indicates continuity and permanence, the same root used in describing the eternal nature of Paradise (jannāt ʿadn, 9:72) and Hell (jahannam khālidīn, 2:82). The parallel is deliberate: just as good is permanently rewarded, evil is permanently punished—not out of cruelty, but out of justice, because the satans’ rebellion was not accidental but continuous, not a single error but a sustained rejection of God’s authority.

**Psychological Insight:** The human tendency is to believe that punishment can be escaped, that time will heal, that eventually everyone will be forgiven if they try hard enough. The verse rejects this comfort for those who actively and defiantly reject God. It is not saying that God is unforgiving—many verses confirm His mercy—but that there is a point beyond which rebellion becomes fixed, a point where the heart becomes so hardened that turning back is impossible. The satire of this is healthy: it warns against assuming that one can toy with evil indefinitely.

> **Cross-Reference:** 41:25—“And they said, ‘Our Lord, indeed we have obeyed our lords and our grandfathers, and they led us astray.’” — even leaders are not exempt from permanent punishment when rebellion is persistent.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Their punishment is everlasting because they refused to submit when they could have; they chose rebellion over obedience, and God’s justice demands that their choice be honored with permanent separation from His mercy, though His mercy is vast for those who repent.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:10

> **save one who snatches a fragment as a piercing flame pursues him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Proves the Rule and the Danger of Partial Knowledge**

After describing total exclusion, the verse introduces one exception: illā man khaṭafa al-khaṭfah—except one who snatches a fragment. The root kh-ṭ-f means to seize quickly, to snatch away, as a predator snatches prey. The word khaṭfah (fragment) refers to a small, partial piece of what was said in the Highest Assembly—a word, a phrase, a secret. The one who snatches this fragment is then pursued by a shihāb thāqib (piercing flame, meteoric fire). The root sh-h-b means to shoot, hurl, or flash; thāqib means piercing, penetrating, burning through. The image is vivid: a jinn seizes a word, but before he can deliver it to a fortune-teller, a flame strikes him, burning him away.

This exception is critical because it explains how false prophecy and sorcery operate. The jinn do not learn everything; they learn only fragments—half-truths, distorted messages, fragments of real knowledge that they mix with lies. This is exactly what the Qur’an describes elsewhere: in 6:121, the shayāṭīn inspire disbelievers with false promises; in 15:16–18, they listen to the heavenly discourse but are struck before they can fully comprehend it. The verse teaches that any “knowledge of the unseen” obtained through jinn is not only partial but dangerous—because the jinn who delivers it is pursued by fire, and any human who receives it is receiving from a source that is already being burned.

**Practical Application:** The believer should be cautious of any source that claims hidden knowledge—fortune-tellers, astrologers, dream interpreters who claim certainty—because true hidden knowledge belongs only to God (see 27:65, 31:34). The “fragment” is a useful analogy: even if a false source seems accurate sometimes, it is only because they have snatched a fragment; the rest is distortion. The fire pursuing them is God’s reminder that such knowledge cannot survive the truth.

> **Cross-Reference:** 15:16–18—“Indeed, We have made the stars in the nearest heaven ornaments for those who observe them, and We have protected them from every rebellious devil—except one who snatches a fragment, and a piercing flame pursues him.” — the exact same sequence confirms this is a repeated divine law.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The one who snatches is a jinn who listens briefly, then is struck; occasionally he manages to send something to a soothsayer before being struck, which is how soothsayers obtain partial truths mixed with lies.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:11

> **So ask them, are they more difficult to create, or those whom We created? Truly We created them from a viscous clay.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge of Resurrection and the Humility of Creation**

This verse shifts from cosmic description to direct address to the Prophet: “So ask them” (fa-sal hum). The pronoun hum refers to the disbelievers mentioned earlier—those who deny Resurrection, those who mock the Prophet, those who claim that returning to life after death is impossible. The question is rhetorical and logical: “Are they—those who deny—more difficult to create, or those whom We created?” The answer is embedded in the comparison: if God could create the complex, multi-layered human being from something as humble as clay, then creating them again after death is not a challenge to His power but an expression of it.

The phrase “truly We created them from a viscous clay” (inna khalaqnāhum min ṭīn lazib) introduces the material origin of human beings. The word ṭīn means clay, mud, or earth; lazib means viscous, sticky, adhesive, like wet mud that clings to the hands. This is different from the “dried clay, like earthen vessels” of 55:14 or the “molded mud” of 15:26. The variety of descriptions reflects different stages of creation—dust, clay, dry clay, viscous clay—each representing a phase of human development, from the first formed being to the developing embryo. The viscous clay captures the moment of formation when life begins to cohere, when the inanimate becomes animate, when the inert takes on form.

**Logical Reasoning:** The disbelievers’ objection to resurrection is not a scientific objection but a psychological one—they cannot imagine themselves as they were before, and they assume the body is too complex to be rebuilt. The verse answers this by showing that the original creation was already from something simpler and weaker. If God could create a human from sticky mud, He can certainly re-create one from scattered bones and dust. The argument is from lesser to greater (argumentum a minori ad maius), a classical form of reasoning recognized in both Arabic rhetoric and philosophical logic.

> **Cross-Reference:** 40:57—“Surely the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind; but most of mankind know not” — the creation of the universe is greater, yet God creates both easily.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The Prophet is commanded to ask them: is it harder to create from nothing, or to return to life what once lived? The answer is obvious to anyone with reason.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:12

> **Indeed, but thou dost marvel, while they scoff.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Marvelling at Revelation and Mocking Its Bearer**

The verse presents two parallel reactions: the Prophet marvels (taʿjab), and the disbelievers scoff (yastahziʾūn). The verb taʿjab comes from the root ʿ-j-b, which means to be astonished, amazed, or perplexed—usually in a positive sense, as when one encounters something beautiful, profound, or beyond expectation. The Prophet’s marvel is not weakness but the natural response of a pure heart to divine revelation. He is amazed at the Qur’an’s precision, at its answers to objections, at its ability to address both the heart and the mind. At the same time, the disbelievers’ laughter is a defensive mechanism: they cannot engage with the message, so they mock it, reducing it to poetry, sorcery, or madness (see 15, 36).

The contrast is sharp: one response is inward, contemplative, and respectful; the other is outward, aggressive, and dismissive. The verse does not ask the disbelievers to understand the Prophet’s marvel; it simply notes that these two reactions occur simultaneously, as if to say: “Look—while you laugh, he is moved to wonder, and that wonder is itself proof that what he brings is not from himself but from beyond him.” A false prophet might seek attention, might crave approval, might adjust his message to please the crowd. The Prophet, by contrast, marvels at the revelation’s truth regardless of how it is received.

**Psychological Insight:** In modern life, people often confuse critical thinking with cynicism. The verse teaches that true critical thinking produces astonishment when confronted with truth—not mockery. Mockery is not a sign of intelligence but of fear: the disbelievers mock because the message threatens their power, their traditions, and their self-image. The Prophet’s marvel is a model for the believer: when you encounter the Qur’an with sincerity, you should feel awe, not boredom; wonder, not fatigue.

> **Cross-Reference:** 50:2—“Indeed, they marvel that there has come to them a warner from among themselves; so the disbelievers say, ‘This is a magician, a madman.’” — the same pattern of marvel versus mockery.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The Prophet marvels at the Qur’an and at the disbelief of his people; they scoff because they have no response to its evidence.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:13

> **And when they are reminded, they do not remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Failure of Remembrance and the Closure of the Heart**

The verse describes a paradox: when the disbelievers are reminded (idhā dhukkirū), they do not remember (lā yadhkarūn). The root dh-k-r means to remember, to mention, to call to mind—both in the sense of mental recall and in the sense of being reminded by an external source (the Qur’an, a prophet, a sign). The term “when they are reminded” is passive: someone else is doing the reminding, usually God through revelation or through natural signs. The response is active refusal: they do not remember. The verb yadhkarūn is in the imperfect tense, indicating continuity—they consistently refuse, over time, habitually.

This is not ordinary forgetfulness—the kind that happens when a person is busy or distracted. It is a willful closure of the heart. The Qur’an distinguishes between forgetting due to weakness (which is human and forgivable) and refusing to remember due to arrogance (which is the disbeliever’s condition). In 18:101, God says: “We have not given them any knowledge; they have only the ear, and they do not use reasoning with it”; in 47:24: “Do they not then meditate on the Qur’an? Or are there locks upon the hearts?” The “lock” is not an external force but an internal choice: they hear but do not heed, they see but do not understand, they are reminded but do not remember.

**Social Implication:** This verse explains why some people attend lectures, read books, or hear sermons yet remain unchanged. The problem is not information deficiency but retention refusal. The heart, like a container, can only hold what it chooses to open itself to. The believer’s duty is not only to deliver reminders but to pray for hearts to be open—because ultimately, remembrance is a gift from God: “Indeed, with hardship comes ease” (94:5) and “We have made the Qur’an easy for remembrance” (54:17).

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:219—“They ask you what they should spend; say, ‘What you spend of good is for parents and relatives and orphans and the needy and the wayfarer; and whatever good you do—indeed, God is Knowing of it.’” — good spending requires remembering God.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “When reminded by the Qur’an or by signs, they turn away and do not remember; this is because of their arrogance and love of worldly life, which prevents them from benefiting from truth.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:14

> **And when they see a sign, they seek to scoff,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reaction to Signs: From Evidence to Mockery**

This verse continues the sequence of disbelievers’ responses: first they do not remember (v. 13), then when they see a clear sign (āyah)—a miracle, a natural wonder, a prophetic proof—they seek to scoff at it (yastahziʾūn). The verb yastahziʾ comes from the root h-z-ʾ, to mock, ridicule, or scorn. It is not passive disagreement but active derision. The phrase “seek to scoff” (yastahziʾūn) implies that they actively pursue mockery as a strategy, as if mocking the sign is their only available response when evidence overwhelms their objections.

The “sign” (āyah) can refer to any clear proof—miracles like the splitting of the moon (54:1), the resurrection of dead animals, the precision of the Qur’an, or natural phenomena like the birth of a child, the growth of plants, the cycles of the sun and moon. The disbelievers do not deny the existence of the sign; they see it, they observe it, but they choose to respond with laughter rather than submission. This reveals that their disbelief is not based on lack of evidence but on refusal of implication. They see the moon split, but they say, “Magic!” They see the Prophet’s moral excellence, but they say, “He is a sorcerer!”

**Logical Principle:** This is the fallacy of ad hominem combined with special pleading. When evidence is presented, instead of addressing it, the disbeliever attacks the messenger or invents an alternative explanation that requires more belief than the original claim. The verse exposes this pattern so that believers can recognize it in themselves: do we ever dismiss evidence because it demands change? Do we mock what we do not understand? The defense is humility—the willingness to say, “I do not fully understand, but I will not mock what is clearly from God.”

> **Cross-Reference:** 54:1—“The Hour has come near, and the moon has been split” — a sign they sought to mock as sorcery.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “They see the signs of God—His miracles, His creation—yet instead of believing, they seek to mock; this is the sign of a heart that is dead, for a living heart fears God when it sees His signs.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:15

> **and say, "This is naught but manifest sorcery."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accusation of Sorcery and Its Historical Pattern**

This verse captures the disbelievers’ spoken response to signs: they say, “This is nothing but manifest sorcery” (in hādhā illā sḥirun mubīn). The word sḥir refers to sorcery, magic, or enchantment—the use of hidden forces or tricks to deceive the eye and mind. The adjective mubīn means clear, evident, manifest—so they are not saying “perhaps it is magic” but “it is clearly, undeniably magic.” This is a refusal to consider that the miracle could be divine; instead, they attribute it to human or supernatural deception.

The accusation of sorcery is a recurring theme against prophets. Moses was called a sorcerer (7:106, 10:76, 26:34); Jesus was accused of sorcery (5:110, 61:6); Muhammad himself was called a sorcerer (10:2, 11:7, 34:43, 38:4, 46:7, 51:52). The consistency of this charge reveals a psychological pattern: when a message threatens established power structures, the messenger is not engaged but delegitimized. If the prophet can be labeled a sorcerer, his miracles can be dismissed without investigation, and his moral demands can be avoided.

**Hadith Connection:** The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said: “The Quraysh said, ‘You are a sorcerer,’ and I said, ‘No, I am not a sorcerer.’ They said, ‘You are a madman,’ and I said, ‘No, I am not mad.’” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). This confirms that the accusation was real, persistent, and personally directed at him. Yet the Qur’an responds not with anger but with logic: “Nay! He has come with the truth and confirmed the messengers” (v. 37).

> **Cross-Reference:** 46:7—“Yet when Our signs are recited unto them as clear proofs, those who disbelieve say to the truth when it comes to them, ‘This is manifest sorcery.’” — identical wording confirms this is not an isolated response.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They accused him of sorcery because they could not match the Qur’an with poetry or oratory; when they failed to defeat it intellectually, they resorted to labeling it magic, which was a known charge against prophets.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:16

> **What! When we have died and are dust and bones are we to be resurrected,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection of Physical Decay and Its Emotional Weight**

This verse expresses the disbelievers’ direct, emotional objection to bodily resurrection: “What! When we have died and are dust and bones, are we to be resurrected?” The exclamation “What!” (a-wa) is an expression of disbelief, shock, and rejection. The image is visceral: death reduces the human body to dust (turāb) and bones (ʿiẓām). Dust signifies utter dissolution—what remains when the body returns to earth; bones signify the hard, inanimate structure that once held life. Together they represent the total loss of identity, the erasure of the self that human beings fear most.

The question is not purely intellectual; it is psychological. The disbeliever is not asking “How can God rebuild a body?” but “How can I, who have become nothing, become something again?” This is the terror of annihilation, the fear that personal existence is so fragile that death is final. The Qur’an answers this fear not by denying the reality of decay but by affirming that God’s power exceeds it. If God could create the first human from sticky clay (v. 11), He can certainly recreate him from scattered bones.

**Cross-Reference:** 36:78–79—“And he strikes an example for Us, saying, ‘Who will revive bones while they are disintegrated?’ Say, ‘He will revive them who produced them in the first instance, and He is Most Knowing of all creation.’” — the same objection, the same answer.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “They ask in astonishment: how can bones that have become dust be brought back to life? The answer is that God who created them from nothing can return them to life without difficulty.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:17

> **and our fathers of old?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Weight of Ancestors and the Fear of Lost Continuity**

This short verse extends the objection of verse 16: not only will we be resurrected, but will our ancestors—those who lived long before us—also be resurrected? The word ābāʾunā (our fathers) refers to previous generations, ancestors, and forefathers. The disbelievers’ reasoning is that if God is just, then He must also hold previous generations accountable; but they assume that previous generations have been lost forever, that their bones have turned to dust, that there is no record of them, that justice is impossible because the past is irretrievable.

This reveals a deeper anxiety: the fear that time is a one-way street, that the past cannot be recovered, that death is a permanent archive. The Qur’an answers this by affirming that God is not bound by time. He knows every soul, governs every age, and brings all generations together on the Day of Judgment. The “fathers of old” are not forgotten; they are part of the same human family that will stand together, some saved, some punished, all accounted for.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings often feel disconnected from ancestors, treating them as historical abstractions rather than real persons who shared our nature, our struggles, and our responsibility to God. The verse teaches that resurrection is not an individual privilege but a collective reality—families, communities, generations will be reunited and judged together. This should motivate both humility (we inherit the choices of ancestors) and responsibility (our choices affect descendants).

> **Cross-Reference:** 18:49—“And the Book has been placed, and you will see the criminals fearful of what is in it, and they will say, ‘Woe to us! What is this Book that leaves nothing small or great but that it has enumerated it?’” — the book records all generations.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They say, ‘Our fathers too?’ This is because they consider it impossible that those who lived long ago could be brought back; but God answers that He is able over all things.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:18

> **Say, "Yes, and debased will you be."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Affirmative Answer and the Shame of Rejection**

The Prophet is commanded to respond: “Say, ‘Yes’” (qul naʿam)—a simple, direct affirmation that leaves no room for ambiguity or negotiation. The word naʿam (yes) is an oath-like agreement, confirming the truth of resurrection without hedging. Then comes the consequence: “and debased will you be” (wa hum muhdariṛūn). The word muhdariṛ comes from the root h-d-r, which means to be lowered, humbled, or brought to shame. It describes the state of the disbelievers when they realize that their denial was wrong—not only will they be resurrected, but they will be in a state of humiliation, because they mocked what was true, rejected evidence, and wasted their lives in arrogance.

The sequence is important: first the affirmation (yes), then the consequence (debasement). This mirrors the justice of the Hereafter: truth is confirmed, falsehood is exposed, and the result is not merely punishment but shame—the deepest form of psychological suffering, being shown to have been wrong in the presence of those one tried to impress. The disbelievers who called the Prophet a sorcerer, who laughed at the Qur’an, who mocked the signs, will realize that their mockery was not strength but weakness, not intelligence but ignorance.

**Spiritual Lesson:** The believer should not fear being debased, because debasement comes only to those who reject truth with arrogance. For the believer, the same resurrection brings not shame but honor, because they accepted the message, lived by it, and will be recognized by God and by the righteous. The contrast is clear: the disbeliever is debased; the believer is elevated.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:78—“So when We say, ‘O earth, swallow your water, and O sky, withhold [your rain],’ and it dries up, and they are not brought back to life” — the dead are brought back, and then judged.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “He says ‘Yes’ because God can restore them; and they will be debased because they denied after seeing the proof, which is the greatest humiliation.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:19

> **Then there will be but a single cry. And they will then**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Single Cry That Transforms Existence**

This verse begins a new section describing the moment of resurrection. The phrase “Then there will be but a single cry” (fa-inna hādhā yaṣīḥu wāḥidatan) describes the second blast of the trumpet (ṣayḥa) that will bring the dead back to life. The word pāʾish (or sayḥa) means a shout, cry, or blast—loud, sudden, overwhelming. The phrase “single cry” (wāḥidah) emphasizes that this is not a gradual process but an instantaneous, universal event. One sound, one moment, and all who have died since the beginning of time will be restored.

The verse is cut off in the source, continuing in verse 20, but the meaning is already clear: when the cry comes, all will suddenly be alive, and the disbelievers who denied resurrection will see what they had denied. The shock will be total: they will not only be resurrected but immediately aware that they were wrong, that their mocking was futile, and that the reality they rejected is now inescapable.

**Theological Point:** The “single cry” demonstrates God’s absolute power over time and life. There is no process, no delay, no need for gradual reconstruction—God speaks, and the dead live. This is not a miracle of nature but a miracle of will: God says “Be,” and it is. The believer should reflect on this and recognize that the same God who can restore billions of people with one sound is fully able to judge, forgive, and reward according to perfect justice.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:29—“Indeed, it is but one cry; then behold, they are looking on” — the same single cry is mentioned in the previous sūrah, confirming continuity.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The single cry is the second blast at which everyone is resurrected; at first they will be stunned, and then they will see what they had denied.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:20

> **They will say, "Oh, woe unto us! This is the Day of Judgment."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Woe, Regret, and the Reality of the Final Day**

The verse captures the disbelievers’ first cry upon resurrection: “Waylatanā!” (Oh, woe unto us!). The word wayl is an expression of deep, existential regret—not mere sadness but a crushing awareness of lost opportunity and inevitable punishment. It is used elsewhere when people realize they have missed the truth (5:31, 21:47, 25:28) and when they see their companions in Hell (36:52, 68:31). Here, the exclamation is directed at themselves—“unto us”—because they recognize that their own choices have led them to this moment.

“The Day of Judgment” (yawm al-dīn) is not a new concept to them—they heard it announced, mocked it, denied it—but now it is not an abstract idea but a lived reality. The shift from denial to recognition is instantaneous, because the single cry of verse 19 restores both life and awareness simultaneously. There is no time to prepare, no chance to repent. The judgment is immediate and total.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings often fear the unknown more than the known. The disbelievers feared death because it seemed final; now they fear life because it reveals their error. The verse teaches that the greatest fear is not death but the moment of truth—when reality becomes undeniable and all self-deception collapses. The believer avoids this by accepting truth before the final day.

> **Cross-Reference:** 44:43—“Indeed, the Tree of Zaqqūm is food for the sinful; it boils in the bellies like molten metal, like the boiling of liquid” — the punishment they now face.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “When they see the Day, they cry ‘Woe unto us!’ because they never expected it; now they know that their mockery was foolish and their denial was wrong.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:21

> **This is the Day of Division that you used to deny.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day of Division and the Exposure of All Choices**

The disbelievers now identify the day: it is the Day of Division (yawm al-faṣl), also called the Day of Judgment, the Day of Separation, or the Day of Reckoning. The root f-ṣ-l means to separate, divide, or decide—here it refers to God’s decisive judgment that separates the righteous from the wicked, truth from falsehood, success from failure. The disbelievers used to deny this day (qad kuntum tuṅkarūn), and their denial was continuous, not occasional. The verb munkar indicates active rejection, not passive ignorance.

The phrase “that you used to deny” is a direct accusation: they are not being punished for a mistake they made once but for a sustained posture of denial that shaped their entire lives. This aligns with the Qur’anic principle that judgment is based not only on actions but on orientation—whether one accepted or rejected the reminder, whether one followed guidance or followed its opposite. The Day of Division is not arbitrary; it is the natural result of choices made in the world.

**Social Implication:** In modern society, division is often seen as negative—people seek unity without truth, harmony without justice. The Qur’an teaches that division is necessary: truth and falsehood cannot coexist in the final reckoning. The believer should not fear this division but welcome it, knowing that their side—truth, justice, humility—will be recognized and honored.

> **Cross-Reference:** 44:40—“Indeed, that is the Day of Separation, We have brought you together and the former generations; so if you have any scheme, scheme against Me!” — God confirms the day and challenges any false defense.

> **Classical View (al-Tabarī):** “The Day of Division is the day on which God divides between the righteous and the wicked, giving each what they deserve; the disbelievers deny it because they think they can escape justice.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:22

> **Gather those who did wrong together with their spouses and that which they used to worship**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gathering of the Guilty and Their Companions**

The verse commands angels: “Gather those who did wrong” (ḥash-shū l-ẓālimīn)—the oppressors, the polytheists, the those who rejected evidence. The word ẓālimīn (wrongdoers) is broad: it includes those who wronged others, those who wronged themselves by disbelief, and those who wronged God by denying His signs. They are gathered not individually but collectively: “together with their spouses” (wa-azwājahum) and “that which they used to worship” (wa-mā kānū yaʿbudūn)—their idols, their false gods, their companions in sin.

The phrase “spouses” (azwājahum) can mean literal spouses—men and women gathered together—or it can mean “those of their kind,” “their companions,” or “those they associated with in wrongdoing.” The multiplicity of meaning is intentional: the wrongdoers will be gathered with everyone who shared their error—partners, friends, leaders—so that none can blame the others individually; they will all be present, and all will realize that their collective error was shared.

The command “Gather them” is addressed to the angels of judgment, emphasizing that this is not a chaotic mob but an ordered assembly under divine authority. The wrongdoers are not free to scatter; they are detained, brought together, and presented for accountability.

> **Cross-Reference:** 43:36—“And whosoever turns blindly away from the remembrance of the Compassionate, We assign to him a satan who is then a companion unto him” — the companions of sin are also gathered.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They are gathered with their spouses—those who followed them in error—and with their idols, which will be shown to be powerless; this is the ultimate defeat of false reliance.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:23

> **apart from God, and guide them unto the path of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Path to Hell and the Exclusion of God**

The verse continues: “apart from God” (min dūni Allāh)—their worship was directed to others, not to God. These “others” include idols, false deities, and even human desires that have become objects of worship. The phrase “apart from God” is critical because it reveals that the wrongdoers’ error was not merely neglecting God but actively replacing Him with substitutes. They did not worship nothing; they worshipped something—money, power, pleasure, ancestors, leaders—making these substitutes into gods.

“Guide them unto the path of Hellfire” (wa-hdū-hum ilā ṣirāṭ al-jahīm) is a command to the angels: not to lead them by force, but to show them, demonstrate to them, the path they have chosen. The word hdū (guide) can mean “direct,” “show,” or “lead”—here it implies that the path is already their own, and the angels are only revealing its destination. This is justice, not cruelty: they followed false leaders, now they are shown where that path ends. The “path of Hellfire” (ṣirāṭ al-jahīm) is not a single road but the collective trajectory of rebellion, pride, and denial—every choice that moves away from God is part of this path.

**Logical Reasoning:** The verse answers the disbeliever’s claim that “we were just following our ancestors” (43:23–24). The answer is that following false leaders is not an excuse but evidence of guilt; if they had followed God instead, they would have been guided to Paradise. The path they chose is now fully revealed, and they cannot deny that they walked it willingly.

> **Cross-Reference:** 17:82—“We reveal of the Qur’an that which is healing and mercy for the believers, and it increases the wrongdoers in loss.” — the same path increases loss.

> **Classical View (al-Turṭushī):** “They are guided to Hell not by coercion but by the revelation of their own deeds; what they thought was freedom was actually slavery to false gods.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:24

> **And stop them. Surely they are to be questioned:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Detention of the Guilty and the Inevitability of Questioning**

The command “stop them” (wa-qifū-hum) is addressed to the angels of judgment: halt the wrongdoers, do not let them proceed, detain them at the threshold of judgment. The verb qafa means to stop, hold back, or stand firm; here it implies a complete cessation of movement, as if the wrongdoers have been running toward their false hopes and are now blocked by divine authority. They cannot escape, cannot scatter, cannot hide.

The phrase “surely they are to be questioned” (innamā hum muʾakhkharūn) uses the term muʾakhkhar, which can mean “delayed,” “held back,” or “brought to account.” The root ʾ-kh-r relates to delay and delay in judgment, but here it means they are now brought to the moment of reckoning that was always approaching. The questioning is not a casual inquiry but an interrogation that will expose every deed, every intention, every hidden thought. The wrongdoers thought they could avoid this moment; now they realize it is unavoidable.

**Psychological Insight:** Many people live as if accountability is distant—“I will deal with it later,” “God is merciful,” “No one will know.” This verse destroys that illusion. The stop is absolute, the questioning is certain, and the delay is over. The believer should live with this awareness—not in fear, but in responsibility, knowing that every choice will be examined.

> **Cross-Reference:** 40:30—“Indeed, those who disbelieve will say, ‘Our Lord, show us those who misled us of jinn and humans, so we may put them under our feet, that they may be among the lowest.’” — the questioning reveals all roles.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They will be stopped because they have nowhere to go; the path to Hell is open only for those who choose it, and now they are detained for questioning. The question is the key to justice: each will be asked about what they did, and no one will have any excuse.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:25

> **"What ails you that you do not help one another?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rebuke of Mutual Abandonment and the Failure of Solidarity**

The question “What ails you?” (mā lakum) is not a request for information but a rebuke of failure. The disbelievers had claimed in life that they would help one another—“Take us with you; we will be with you” (6:129-130)—but now, in judgment, they do not help one another (lā tansurūn). The verb nasara means to help, support, defend, or assist; its absence here reveals that the false solidarity of the world is dissolved by truth. Each person stands alone with their own deeds.

The question is rhetorical: there is no answer. They cannot say “We tried but failed,” because they never truly tried. They followed their leaders, mocked the prophet, rejected the reminder, and now find themselves without allies. The rebuke is severe because it exposes hypocrisy: they claimed brotherhood in error but have no brotherhood in accountability.

**Ethical Lesson:** True help is not emotional agreement but moral support—encouraging others to do right, correcting them when they err, standing with them in difficulty because of truth, not because of shared error. The believer should build solidarity around God, not around tradition, status, or convenience.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:243—“Have you not considered those who left their homes in thousands, fearful of death? So God said to them, ‘Die.’ Then He revived them.” — true solidarity is with God, not with false security.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They are rebuked for not helping one another because they followed false leaders in life; now they see that those leaders have no power to save them, and they are left alone.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:26

> **Nay, but this Day they will surrender.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Surrender of the Disbelievers and the End of Resistance**

The word balā (nay) is a strong denial of any possibility that they will help each other. It is followed by “this Day they will surrender” (al-yawma yuslimūn). The verb aslama means to submit, surrender, or enter into Islam—not necessarily as a choice of faith but as an acknowledgment of defeat and submission to God’s decree. Here it means they will be forced to submit to God’s judgment, not because they have accepted His message but because they have no power to resist it.

The phrase “this Day” emphasizes the immediacy: the day they denied, mocked, and rejected has now arrived, and with it comes total surrender. They are no longer in control; they are being judged, detained, and directed to Hell. The surrender is not voluntary; it is the inevitable result of their own choices meeting divine authority.

**Philosophical Point:** Human beings often believe they are free until they encounter an absolute limit. The Day of Judgment reveals that freedom is not the absence of consequence but the responsibility to choose before consequence arrives. The disbelievers chose rebellion; now they must submit to the result of that choice. This is justice, not tyranny—because the choice was theirs.

> **Cross-Reference:** 6:163—“Indeed, I am of those who submit to God in Islam” — the believer’s voluntary surrender is the opposite of the forced surrender of the disbeliever.

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** “They will surrender because they have no power; they thought they could resist God, but now they see that He is the Sovereign of all things, and they must submit to His judgment.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:27

> **And they will turn to one another, questioning each other,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mutual Questioning and the Collapse of False Alliances**

The verse describes the disbelievers turning to one another (yatalāawana), questioning each other (yatasāʾalūn). The word yatalaawa means to turn around, to face one another, to look at each other—suggesting a circle of confrontation where each is searching for an answer from the others. The questioning is mutual: they ask, they answer, they blame, they defend. This is the opposite of the solidarity they claimed; now they are in a state of mutual accusation.

The progression is logical: first they do not help (v. 25), then they surrender (v. 26), then they turn to question one another (v. 27). Each step reveals deeper failure: they cannot assist, they cannot resist, and now they can only ask—“Why? How? What did you know?” The questions have no answers because the answers were always available in the reminder, which they rejected.

**Social Parallel:** In modern life, when groups that shared false beliefs face crisis, they often turn on each other—blaming leaders, blaming members, blaming circumstances. The verse teaches that false solidarity is fragile; only truth can sustain mutual support under pressure.

> **Cross-Reference:** 7:38–39—“Say, ‘Our Lord has gathered us together, then He has judged between us with justice. We are His family, and to Him we return.’” — the gathering is real, but the judgment reveals that false claims of family are empty.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They turn to one another because each seeks to blame the other for their fate; they ask, ‘Why did you not warn us?’ and the answer is that they knew but refused.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:28

> **saying, "Truly you used to come to us from the right."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accusation of Those Led Astray and the Role of False Leaders**

The disbelievers, those who were weak and led astray, say to their leaders: “You used to come to us from the right” (innakum kuntum taʾtūnanā min al-yamīn). The phrase “from the right” (min al-yamīn) means from a position of authority, power, influence, and leadership—those on the right hand are the honored, the respected, the ones whose word carries weight. The accusation is that these leaders came to them with false authority, making falsehood attractive and concealing truth.

This is a profound psychological observation: people often follow false leaders not because they are stupid but because the leaders present themselves with confidence, status, and social approval. The “right” is the side of honor and influence, and the leaders used this to deceive. The disbelievers now realize that their trust was misplaced—not because they were forced, but because they chose to follow those who appeared to be on the right side.

**Ethical Lesson:** The believer must examine sources of influence—who do we follow, why do we respect them, and do they lead to God or away from Him? The “right” in Islamic context is not social status but divine truth; those who claim authority without truth are not on the right side, no matter how respected they appear.

> **Cross-Reference:** 33:66–68—“O people of faith, be not like those who harmed Moses; for God absolved him of what they said, and he was honorable in God’s sight.” — even prophets can be accused by those they led.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The followers say to their leaders: you came to us with authority and influence, and you led us astray; this shows that they recognized the leaders’ power but failed to recognize their falsehood.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:29

> **They reply, "Nay, but you were not believers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Leaders’ Repudiation and the Rejection of Shared Responsibility**

The leaders respond to their followers: “Nay, but you were not believers” (bal inna-kum kuntum lā tuʾminūn). The word bal (nay) is a direct denial—the leaders refuse to accept blame for the followers’ choices. They say: “We did not force you; we only presented falsehood; but you chose to follow it. Your disbelief was your own decision, not our imposition.”

This is the same argument made by Satan in 14:22: “And I had no authority over you, save that I called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me, but blame yourselves.” Both Satan and false human leaders use the same logic: they offered, the followers accepted, and the responsibility lies with the followers. The Qur’an confirms this: “Each soul bears its own burden” (6:164, 35:18), and those who lead others astray will carry additional burden (29:13), but those who followed willingly are not excused.

**Psychological Insight:** This exchange reveals how false leaders protect themselves by shifting blame. It is a pattern visible in history—leaders of oppressive regimes, cults, and corrupt organizations often deny responsibility by saying “my followers chose me.” The verse teaches that while followers are responsible for their choices, leaders are also responsible for what they present and how they use authority. Both are judged, both are punished, and both must face the truth.

> **Cross-Reference:** 14:22—Satan’s exact words, confirming this is a universal pattern of false authority.

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** “The leaders deny that they forced anyone; they say you were not believers, meaning you already had inclination to disbelieve, and we only made it attractive; but this does not absolve them, because they knew and encouraged it.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:30

> **And we had no authority over you. Rather, you were a rebellious people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Denial of Authority and the Confession of Rebellion**

The leaders continue: “We had no authority over you” (wa-mā kāna lanā ʿalaykum min sulṭān)—no power, no proof, no compulsory force that could make them follow. The word sulṭān means authority, dominion, or proof; its absence means that the followers followed by choice, not by coercion. This is important because it rejects the excuse of “we had to follow because of social pressure or fear.” The Qur’an consistently teaches that every person has free will; no one is forced to disbelieve.

Then comes the final charge: “Rather, you were a rebellious people” (bal kuntum qawman ṭāghīn). The word ṭāghī means rebellious, transgressive, arrogant—someone who exceeds limits, who refuses submission, who chooses pride over humble acceptance. The leaders say: “You were already rebellious; we did not create your rebellion; we only gave it a direction.” This is a devastating admission: the leaders acknowledge that they did not need to force anyone because the followers’ hearts were already inclined toward rebellion.

**Moral Lesson:** This verse is a reminder that internal disposition matters. One cannot blame environment, upbringing, or social pressure for disbelief; the root is a heart that refuses submission. The believer must examine their own heart: is it humble, open, and willing to accept guidance, or is it rebellious, proud, and resistant to truth?

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:286—“God does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity” — free will is real, and responsibility is proportionate to capacity.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They say ‘we had no authority’ because they cannot claim to have forced anyone; but they admit the followers were rebellious, which shows that the leaders chose to lead rebellion rather than guide to truth.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:31

> **So the Word of our Lord has come due for us; truly we shall taste [it].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word of Judgment and the Certainty of Consequence**

The followers now concede: “So the Word of our Lord has come due for us” (fa-la-qad jaʾa kalimatu rabbina ʿalaynā al-ḥaqq). The word kalima (word) refers to God’s decree of judgment, the decision pronounced against them, the promise that has now been fulfilled. The phrase “has come due” (jaʾa) means it has arrived, it has reached them, it is no longer distant or theoretical. The word ḥaqq (truth) confirms that this is not arbitrary punishment but just, real, and undeniable.

Then comes the acceptance of consequence: “Truly we shall taste it” (inna-nā la-dhāʾīqūn). The verb dhāqa means to taste, experience, feel directly—not just observe from outside but personally suffer or enjoy the result. They will taste the punishment because they rejected the warning; they will taste the fire because they followed false leaders. The word lam (truly) is emphatic: this is certain, not possible but inevitable.

**Theological Reflection:** The phrase “the Word of our Lord has come due” teaches that God’s promises—both of mercy and of judgment—are not empty words. They are real, active, and fulfilled at the appointed time. The believer should trust both the promise of Paradise and the warning of Hell, knowing that God’s word is always true.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:7—“We have not sent to them any messenger except that We have seized him by the revelation, but some of them would mock others” — the word of judgment comes to all.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They confess that God’s word has come true against them; this is the final admission that they were wrong, and now they must taste what they denied.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:32

> **We caused you to err for truly we were errant.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confession of Shared Error and the Reality of Leading Astray**

The leaders now confesses: “We caused you to err” (innā kunnā naduʾukum) — we led you astray, we guided you to error, we were the cause of your misdirection. The verb ada (or dāʾa) means to mislead, lead into error, or cause to go wrong. It does not deny that the followers chose; it confirms that the leaders actively directed them toward error, made falsehood attractive, and concealed truth.

Then comes the reason: “for truly we were errant” (innā kunnā khāṭiʾīn). The leaders admit that they themselves were wrong, that they were not innocent guides but guilty parties. This is significant: false authority cannot claim innocence; those who lead others to error are doubly guilty—once for their own error, and once for leading others to share it. The Qur’an confirms: “And those who do not believe and avert others from the path of God, for them We will add punishment to punishment” (16:88).

**Ethical Lesson:** The verse teaches that influence is a serious responsibility. Those in positions of authority—parents, teachers, leaders, media figures—must examine whether they are guiding others toward truth or error. The confession “we were errant” is the first step toward reform, but in judgment it is too late. The believer should seek truth not from those who claim authority but from those who have evidence and humility.

> **Cross-Reference:** 29:13—“Surely they will bear their own burdens, and others’ burdens along with their own” — leaders carry additional burden.

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** “They confess both that they led others astray and that they themselves were wrong; this is the complete admission of guilt, leaving no excuse for anyone.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:33

> **Verily that Day they will share in the punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shared Punishment and Collective Responsibility**

The verse states: “Indeed, that Day they will share in the punishment” (inna-humā li-shurakāʾ fī l-ʿadhāb). The word shurakāʾ means partners, companions, or sharers—those who will experience punishment together, not because they are innocent but because their errors were intertwined. The punishment is not divided; it is shared. They suffer together, blame together, and realize together that their collective error was real.

The word “they” (humā) can refer to both the leaders and the followers—the two groups mentioned in the previous verses—confirming that both are punished, both are responsible, and both will suffer. There is no separation of punishment: the weak and the strong, the follower and the leader, will all be in Hell together. This is justice that is both precise (each for their own deeds) and collective (shared experience of error).

**Psychological Insight:** The human desire is to separate from those who led us wrong—to say “I was just following them, they are the real criminals.” The verse denies this separation: they are partners in punishment because they were partners in error. True repentance requires recognizing not only one’s own fault but also one’s complicity in collective wrongdoing.

> **Cross-Reference:** 29:13—“Surely they will bear their own burdens, and others’ burdens along with their own” — the shared burden is real.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They will share punishment because they shared wrongdoing; the followers followed willingly, and the leaders led willingly, so both are partners in the consequence.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:34

> **Thus do We deal with the guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Pattern of Justice and the Treatment of the Guilty**

The verse concludes the dialogue: “Thus do We deal with the guilty” (kadhālika naʿmalu bi-l-mujrimīn). The phrase “thus” (kadhālika) refers to the entire sequence: gathering, questioning, blaming, confessing, and sharing punishment. This is not an exception but the pattern—God’s consistent way of dealing with those who reject truth, deny evidence, follow false leaders, and refuse to accept responsibility.

The word mujrim (guilty, criminal, wicked) is broad: it includes not only those who commit physical crimes but those whose crime is disbelief, arrogance, and rejection of God’s signs. The treatment is fair: they are gathered, questioned, and punished according to their deeds. Nothing is hidden, nothing is overlooked, and no one is exempt.

**Theological Point:** The verse teaches that God’s justice is not arbitrary but patterned. It follows logical steps: evidence is presented, denial is exposed, blame is shared, and consequence is certain. The believer can trust this pattern: if they live with truth, they will not be treated as guilty; if they reject truth, they will experience this same sequence.

> **Cross-Reference:** 6:57—“Indeed, I am of those who submit to God in Islam” — the opposite of the guilty is the submitter.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “This is the manner of God with wrongdoers: they are gathered, questioned, and punished; it is a just and visible decree, not a hidden punishment.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:35

> **Indeed, when it was said unto them, "There is no god but God," they waxed arrogant,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Arrogance of Rejection and the Refusal of Tawdīḥ**

The verse shifts from judgment to the cause of judgment: the disbelievers’ response to the call to tawḥīd. When told “There is no god but God” (la ilāha illa Allāh)—the simplest and most profound declaration—they waxed arrogant (istakbarū). The verb istakbara comes from k-b-r, to be great, to become big, to be proud; with the form ista-, it means to consider oneself too great to accept a simple truth. Their arrogance was not accidental; it was the direct result of social status, tribal pride, and attachment to inherited practices.

This is the same pattern described in 2:34—Satan refused to bow to Adam out of arrogance—and in 7:111—Pharaoh rejected Moses due to pride. Arrogance is the root of disbelief: not lack of evidence, not confusion, but the refusal to submit because submission seems to diminish one’s status, autonomy, or power.

**Psychological Insight:** Modern society often equates humility with weakness and pride with strength. The Qur’an inverts this: true strength is the ability to say “I do not know everything, I need guidance, I will submit.” The disbelievers’ arrogance made them unable to accept that they needed a messenger, that they needed guidance, that they were not the ultimate authority.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:206—“And when it is said to them, ‘Worship God,’ they are arrogant (yastakbirūn)” — the same response to the same call.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They were arrogant because they thought they were superior; they refused to abandon their idols and their traditions for what seemed to them a simple, humble declaration.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:36

> **saying, "Are we to forsake our gods for a poet possessed?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mocking of the Messenger and the Reduction of Truth to Madness**

The disbelievers respond with sarcasm: “Are we to forsake our gods for a poet possessed?” (a-fa-aḍāʿnā ālihatan wa-ilāhakum bi-shāʿirin majnūn?). The word shāʿir means poet; majnūn means possessed, mad, insane—someone whose mind is not fully in control. They accuse Muhammad of both being a poet (reducing Receipt to art) and being possessed (reducing it to madness). This is a double attack: first, they say he is not a prophet but a performer; second, they say he is not rational but delusional.

The accusation is historically significant because it reveals the disbelievers’ strategy: when they cannot defeat the message with logic, they attack the messenger with labels. If he is a poet, then the Qur’an is just beautiful language, not divine truth; if he is possessed, then he is not reliable, not trustworthy, and not worthy of obedience. Both accusations were false, but they were effective in maintaining the disbelievers’ resistance.

**Logical Reasoning:** The response also reveals a false dichotomy: they assume that choosing the Prophet means abandoning all tradition, all identity, and all social value. The Qur’an answers this by showing that the Prophet confirms previous messengers (v. 37) and brings truth that does not erase identity but corrects it. The “forsaking” is not of culture but of false worship; it is a gain, not a loss.

> **Cross-Reference:** 52:30—“Or have they not pondered over the Qur’an? If it were not from God, they would have found in it much contradiction.” — the accusation of madness is answered by the Qur’an’s coherence.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “They say ‘poet’ because they cannot match the Qur’an in poetry; they say ‘possessed’ because they have no other explanation for his moral excellence and courage.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:37

> **Nay! He has come with the truth and confirmed the messengers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Affirmation of Truth and the Continuity of Prophethood**

The response begins with “Nay” (bal)—a direct rejection of the accusation. The Prophet is not a poet possessed; he has come with al-ḥaqq (the truth)—the Qur’an, divine revelation, and the message of tawḥīd. And he has confirmed (wa-ṣaddaqa) the messengers—meaning he brings what they brought, does not contradict them, and continues their line of guidance. The root ṣ-d-q relates to truth, sincerity, and verification; the Prophet verifies previous revelation by bringing its fulfillment and its correct interpretation.

This continuity is central to Islamic belief: Muhammad is not a new religion but the completion and confirmation of the same message given to Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and all previous prophets. The Qur’an repeatedly states this: 2:41, 89, 91; 3:3, 81; 5:48; 6:92; 35:31; 46:30. The disbelievers’ accusation of novelty is false; the reality is continuity.

**Spiritual Lesson:** The believer should find comfort in continuity: we are not alone in history; we join a long line of truth-bearers who faced the same accusations, the same mockery, and the same victory through patience and trust in God.

> **Cross-Reference:** 5:48—“To you We have sent down the Book in truth, confirming what was before it; and He has revealed the Torah and the Gospel.”

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “He confirms the messengers because he brings the same message—worship God alone—and he confirms their books by not contradicting them and by revealing what they foretold about him.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:38

> **Truly you shall taste the painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning of Certain Punishment and Its Painful Reality**

The verse delivers a direct warning: “Truly you shall taste the painful punishment” (la-tumūṭū al-ʿadhāba al-alīm). The verb dhāqa (taste) is used again, emphasizing that punishment is not theoretical but personally experienced—felt in the body, the heart, and the soul. The adjective alīm (painful, bitter, severe) describes the intensity of the punishment in Hell—not only physical fire but psychological suffering: regret, shame, isolation, and the awareness of wasted opportunity.

This is not a threat made in anger but a promise made in justice: those who reject the truth, mock the messenger, and follow false leaders will experience the result of their choices. The punishment is “painful” because it is proportionate: they rejected a message that could have saved them, and now they suffer the loss of that salvation.

**Practical Application:** The believer should take this warning seriously—not with fear but with motivation. Knowing that punishment is real and painful should strengthen commitment to truth, to prayer, to avoiding false alliances, and to living with awareness of final accountability.

> **Cross-Reference:** 32:14—“And taste the punishment everlasting for that which you used to do” — the same verb, the same certainty.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They will taste the punishment because they denied after seeing proof; the pain is due to the realization that they wasted their lives in falsehood.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:39

> **And you shall not be requited, save for that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exactness of Justice and the Prohibition of Unjust Punishment**

The verse clarifies the nature of punishment: it is not arbitrary or excessive but precisely based on deeds—“save for that which you used to do” (illā bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn). The word illā (except) is restrictive: they will not be punished for anything beyond their actions, for any inherited guilt, for any mistake they did not commit, or for any evil they did not do. This is a profound statement of divine justice: God is not cruel, He does not exceed limits, and He does not hold anyone accountable for the choices of others.

The phrase “that which you used to do” (mā kuntum taʿmalūn) is in the past continuous tense—indicating repeated, habitual action, not a single mistake. The punishment is proportional to the habit of rebellion, the sustained denial, the continuous mockery. This aligns with the Qur’anic principle that “each soul bears its own burden” (6:164) and that “God does not wrong human beings in the least” (10:44).

**Psychological Comfort:** The believer can trust that God is just; the disbeliever cannot claim that punishment is unfair. The verse also teaches that if one’s actions are only good, the result will be only good—there is no hidden punishment for the sincere.

> **Cross-Reference:** 10:44—“Surely God does not wrong human beings in the least, but rather human beings wrong themselves.”

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They are only punished for what they did; God does not wrong anyone, and this is proof that judgment is fair and precise.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:40

> **Not so for God’s sincere servants.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception of the Sincere and the Contrast of Outcomes**

The phrase “Not so” (illā) introduces the exception that defines the entire judgment: not so for God’s sincere servants (ʿibād Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn). The word mukhlaṣ means pure, sincere, devoted, free from hypocrisy—someone who worships God only for Him, not for worldly benefit, social approval, or fear of punishment. These servants are not treated like the disbelievers; their outcome is different, their accounting is different, and their destiny is different.

The contrast is explicit: the disbelievers are gathered, questioned, blamed, punished, and shared in error (vv. 22–34); the sincere servants receive provision, honor, and bliss (vv. 41–49). This is the fundamental divide of the Hereafter: not between rich and poor, not between Arab and non-Arab, but between sincere and insincere, between those who submit to God alone and those who submit to others.

**Spiritual Reflection:** The believer should ask: is my worship sincere? Is it for God alone, or is it mixed with the desire for social status, family approval, or personal benefit? The verse teaches that sincerity is the key to being among those who are excluded from punishment—not because they are perfect, but because they are devoted.

> **Cross-Reference:** 39:10—“Indeed, those who are patient shall be paid their reward in full without reckoning” — the sincere receive reward without detailed accounting.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “The sincere servants are those who dedicate worship to God alone, without partners; they are spared from punishment and given the highest rewards because of their purity of intention.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:41

> **For them there shall be a known provision,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Certainty of Heavenly Provision and Its Distinction from Worldly Uncertainty**

The verse begins the description of paradise: “For them there shall be a known provision” (la-hum rizqun maʿlūm). The word rizq means provision, sustenance, livelihood, or gift from God—everything needed for life, comfort, and flourishing. The adjective maʿlūm (known, certain, fixed) distinguishes heavenly provision from earthly provision, which is uncertain, temporary, and often deceptive. On earth, riches come and go; health is fragile; security is conditional. In Paradise, the provision is fixed, guaranteed, and appropriate to each person’s need and merit.

The word “known” also implies that the servants know what they will receive; there is no anxiety about tomorrow, no fear of loss, no competition for resources. The provision is not only sufficient but also personal and appropriate—each receives according to what God has decreed for them.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings experience anxiety because the future is unknown. The verse teaches that in God’s presence, anxiety is replaced by certainty. The believer should cultivate this certainty now through trust in God’s provision, knowing that the same God who promises Paradise also promises sufficient rizq in this life.

> **Cross-Reference:** 3:159—“And if they had been patient, [it would have been] better for them; and God is Almighty, Wise” — patience with earthly provision leads to heavenly certainty.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The provision is known because it is certain and fixed; unlike this world where provision is uncertain, in Paradise it is guaranteed and never changes.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:42

> **fruits. And they will be honored**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fruits, Honor, and the Fullness of Heavenly Life**

The verse continues: “fruits” (fākihah)—the produce of Paradise, diverse, abundant, beautiful, and never-ending. Fruits in the Qur’anic description of Paradise represent both physical nourishment and aesthetic pleasure; they are not merely food but symbols of life, growth, and fulfillment. The word fākihah can refer to any fruit—dates, grapes, pomegranates, and others mentioned specifically in different verses—indicating variety and abundance.

“And they will be honored” (wa-hum mukramūn): the word mukram refers to those who are honored, respected, given dignity, treated with generosity. In Paradise, honor is not earned through social status or tribal identity but through sincerity and devotion to God. The sincere servants are honored by God Himself, by the angels, and by all other inhabitants of Paradise. There is no humiliation, no exclusion, no ranking by worldly standards.

**Social Parallel:** In human societies, honor is often competitive, conditional, and exclusionary—based on wealth, beauty, power, or appearance. The Qur’an replaces this with divine honor: those who are sincere to God are honored by God, and this honor is permanent, universal, and without competition.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:55–56—“Indeed, for them are fruits, and they will be honored in Gardens of pleasure, upon thrones facing one another” — the same themes of fruits and honor.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The fruits are the best of Paradise’s provisions; the honor is the greatest of rewards, because God honors those who honored Him.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:43

> **in Gardens of bliss,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Gardens of Bliss and the Environment of Eternal Joy**

The verse describes the location: “in Gardens of bliss” (fī jannāti naʿīm). The word jann means garden, paradise, or hidden place; naʿīm means bliss, pleasure, ease, comfort. The phrase “Gardens of bliss” is repeated throughout the Qur’an (5:65, 10:9, 22:56, 31:8, 56:12, 68:34) to describe the environment of the righteous—not a place of mere comfort but of active, joyful, and expansive life.

The gardens are not static; they are filled with trees, fruits, rivers, and companions. The word jann can also evoke the hidden quality of Paradise—its beauty and wonders are beyond full description, hidden from the eyes and hearts of those who have not believed. The believer knows of Paradise by revelation, but the full experience is reserved for those who enter it.

**Spiritual Reflection:** The description of Paradise is not intended to satisfy mere curiosity but to motivate sincerity. When a person understands that the alternative to rejection is not just absence of punishment but active, beautiful, communal joy, the choice between disbelief and belief becomes clearer.

> **Cross-Reference:** 56:12—“In Gardens of bliss, upon thrones facing one another” — the same environment.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The Gardens of bliss are the highest Paradise, where the sincere servants enjoy every kind of pleasure, both physical and spiritual, without any fatigue or end.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:44

> **upon couches, facing one another,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Couches, Facing One Another, and the Social Dimension of Paradise**

The verse describes the physical arrangement: “upon couches” (ʿalā surur) and “facing one another” (muttaqilūn or mutanāẓirūn). The word surur is the plural of sarīr, a cushion or couch—something comfortable, elevated, and suited for rest and conversation. They are “facing one another,” which means no one turns their back on anyone, no one is behind another, and all have equal status and mutual respect.

This social arrangement is significant because it replaces the hierarchies of the world—rich over poor, powerful over weak, leader over follower—with equality in God’s presence. The sincere servants are not ranked by worldly status but by their sincerity, and in Paradise, all are honored together. The “facing” also suggests dialogue, friendship, and mutual enjoyment—not isolation but community.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings need both personal comfort and social connection. The verse addresses both: couches provide comfort; facing one another provides community. Paradise satisfies both needs fully, without the conflicts, jealousies, or exclusions that characterize earthly relationships.

> **Cross-Reference:** 15:47—“And We will remove any bitterness from their breasts—rivers will flow beneath them, and they will say, ‘Praise to God, who guided us to this! And we would not have been guided if God had not guided us.’” — the removal of bitterness enables mutual face-to-face joy.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “Facing one another is a sign of honor; no one is behind another, because all are equal in Paradise according to their sincerity, and there is no pride or superiority.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:45

> **with a cup from a gushing spring brought round,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cup, the Gushing Spring, and the Service of Paradise**

The verse describes the drink of Paradise: a cup (kaʾs) from a “gushing spring” (ʿayn ʾāniya) that is “brought round” (yuqallab). The spring is not still but gushing—continuously flowing, never drying, always fresh. The cup is brought around by servants, indicating that the inhabitants of Paradise are served, not because they are lazy, but because honor and ease are part of the reward.

The wine of Paradise is different from earthly wine: it does not cause intoxication or headache (v. 47). It is pure, white, delicious (v. 46), and brings joy without harm. The presentation is elegant: cups are served, not consumed in haste; the atmosphere is one of gracious hospitality, not desperate need.

**Spiritual Meaning:** The cup represents God’s generosity—not minimal provision but abundant, beautiful, and served with honor. The believer should trust that God’s generosity is not limited to this life; it is a foretaste of the generosity to come.

> **Cross-Reference:** 76:15—“Therein will be vessels of silver, and cups of crystal” — the beauty of heavenly vessels.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The spring is gushing because it never runs out; the cup is brought around because the servants of Paradise serve with honor and humility.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:46

> **white, delicious to those who drink thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purity of Heavenly Drink and Its Sensory Perfection**

The verse describes the drink: “white” (bīḍ) and “delicious” (ladhdhat li-l-shāribīn). The color white is associated with purity, cleanliness, and beauty—unlike the dark, murky drinks of Hell (v. 65–67). The word ladhdhat means pleasure, sweetness, enjoyment—something that is delightful to the taste and to the soul. The drink is not merely nourishing; it is enjoyable, beautiful, and satisfying in every sense.

The comparison with earthly wine is implicit: earthly wine can be beautiful in color but causes harm; heavenly wine is beautiful and harmless. This is the perfection of creation in Paradise—everything that is good in this life is retained, and everything that is harmful is removed.

**Psychological Insight:** Human beings often associate pleasure with risk—food can poison, drink can intoxicate, beauty can deceive. The verse teaches that in God’s presence, pleasure and purity are not opposites; they are united. The believer can enjoy the beauty of this life without fear of harm, because true pleasure comes from God and is completed in Him.

> **Cross-Reference:** 56:18—“There will be young men of perpetual youth around them; and when you see them, you will think them as dispersed pearls” — beauty without harm.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “White describes the purity of the drink; delicious describes the pleasure it brings, which is greater than any earthly pleasure because it is without harm and without end.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:47

> **No headiness lies therein; nor are they intoxicated by it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Absence of Intoxication and the Purity of Heavenly Experience**

The verse states clearly: “No headiness lies therein” (lā yusaddaru minhu) — there is no madness, no confusion, no loss of control, no foolishness caused by the drink. And “nor are they intoxicated by it” (wa-lā hum yaskarūn) — they do not become drunk, do not lose awareness, do not say foolish things, do not commit errors under its influence. This is the complete opposite of earthly wine, which can lead to both pleasure and harm.

The word yusaddar means to cause headiness or confusion; yaskar means to become drunk or intoxicated. Both are negated, confirming that heavenly pleasure does not diminish human dignity, reason, or awareness. The inhabitants of Paradise remain fully conscious, fully respectful, fully attentive—able to enjoy fully without any negative consequence.

**Spiritual Lesson:** The believer should seek pleasures that do not diminish the soul. True joy—whether in prayer, in community, in knowledge, or in worship—does not cause confusion; it clarifies, strengthens, and elevates. The verse teaches that God’s gifts are always pure, always beneficial, always complete.

> **Cross-Reference:** 37:47—“No headiness lies therein; nor are they intoxicated by it” — repeated for emphasis, confirming this is a defining feature of Paradise.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The drink of Paradise does not cause headiness because it is pure; it does not cause intoxication because the inhabitants are always conscious of God and of their blessing, which increases their appreciation rather than reducing their awareness.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:48

> **And theirs shall be maidens of modest gaze with lustrous eyes,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Chaste Companions and the Purity of Heavenly Relationships**

The verse describes the companions in Paradise: “chaste women” (ḥūr ʿīn) — a phrase that describes women of exceptional beauty, purity, and modesty. The word ḥūr refers to those with large, beautiful eyes; ʿīn means wide-eyed, pure, and chaste. These companions are “limited to [their] glances” (qaṣirāt al-ṭaraf) — they look only at their husbands, they do not wander with their eyes, they are exclusively devoted, modest, and faithful.

This description is not merely physical; it is moral. The companions of Paradise represent the perfection of relationship—complete trust, complete loyalty, complete satisfaction. Those in Paradise will not experience jealousy, suspicion, or betrayal, because every relationship is pure and mutual.

**Psychological Insight:** Human relationships in this life are often marked by insecurity, comparison, and fear of loss. The verse teaches that in God’s presence, these insecurities are removed—not because people change into perfect beings, but because God creates an environment where trust and honor are natural and complete.

> **Cross-Reference:** 52:20—“And We have made for them companions of perfect beauty” — the same theme of pure companionship.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The chaste women are pure in body and heart; they are limited to their glances, meaning they do not look at anyone except their spouses, because they are fully satisfied and fully devoted.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:49

> **as if they were pearls well protected.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pearls, Protection, and the Preciousness of Heavenly Companions**

The verse completes the description: the companions are “as if they were pearls well protected” (ka-annahunna al-yāqūt wa-l-marjān). The word yāqūt refers to rubies or precious gems; marjān refers to pearls. Together they represent the highest value, beauty, and rarity. The phrase “well protected” (makhfūẓa) means they are guarded, preserved, and treasured—nothing can damage them, nothing can diminish them, and nothing can take them away. They are secure, pure, and eternal.

The comparison is significant: pearls are formed slowly in darkness, through patience and endurance; they are beautiful precisely because they have survived difficulty. The companions of Paradise are like pearls—not because they are hard, but because they have been purified through believer’s patience, and now they are preserved in eternal beauty.

**Spiritual Meaning:** The believer should see every trial as a process of formation—like a pearl in darkness—knowing that God will preserve and honor what was pure and patient. Nothing is lost; everything is transformed.

> **Cross-Reference:** 52:20—“Companions of perfect beauty” — the same concept of precious, pure companionship.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “Pearls and rubies are the most precious of jewels; the companions are preserved and protected like them, meaning they are safe from any harm or change.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:50

> **And they will turn to one another, questioning each other.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Recompense, Reward, and the Just Exchange of Deeds**

The verse introduces the reason for the rewards: “As a recompense for what they used to do” (jazāʾan bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūn). The word jazāʾ means recompense, reward, repayment, or just return—exactly proportionate to what was done. It is not a gift of grace (though God’s grace is vast) but a just exchange: good deeds are rewarded with good consequences, and the sincerity of intention is matched by the perfection of reward.

The phrase “what they used to do” (mā kānū yaʿmalūn) is continuous: their actions were consistent, their worship was sustained, their sincerity was habitual. The reward is not given to those who did one good action but to those who lived in obedience, who made righteousness a way of life.

**Theological Point:** The verse teaches that God’s justice is not only punitive (as in vv. 22–34) but also rewarding. The same God who questions the guilty also rewards the sincere. The balance is perfect: neither reward nor punishment is arbitrary.

> **Cross-Reference:** 53:39—“And that man will have nothing but that for which he strives” — the principle of exact recompense.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The recompense is exactly for their deeds; they are rewarded not because of their lineage or status but because of their sincere actions in this life.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:51

> **One among them will say, “I had a companion**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Absence of Vain Talk and Sinful Call in Paradise**

The verse describes what the sincere servants do not hear in Paradise: “no vain discourse” (lā yasmaʿūna fī-hā lāghiyan) and “no call to sin” (wa-lā taʾthīm). The word lāghiy means foolish, vain, useless talk—gossip, slander, empty arguments, meaningless noise. The word taʾthīm means sin, wrongdoing, or forbidden action—anything that leads to harm, corruption, or separation from God.

In Paradise, there is no gossip, no slander, no false accusations, no arguments over status or wealth, no invitations to do wrong. This is the complete opposite of earthly life, where much of our communication is vain or harmful. The absence of these things is not a restriction but a liberation: when everything is pure, there is no need for defense, no need for competition, no need for deception.

**Psychological Insight:** The human mind is often filled with noise—social comparison, status anxiety, fear of judgment, desire for approval. The verse teaches that true peace is not just the absence of conflict but the absence of the conditions that create conflict. In God’s presence, the heart is quiet because it is secure.

> **Cross-Reference:** 19:62—“No vain discourse will they hear therein, nor any call to sin” — identical phrase, confirming this is a defining feature of Paradise.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They hear no useless talk because all speech in Paradise is beneficial and pure; they hear no call to sin because sin is impossible in Paradise, and all actions are good.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:52

> **who would say, ‘Are you among those who confirm?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Greeting of Peace and the Atmosphere of Paradise**

The verse completes the sentence: the only sound they hear is the greeting of “Peace, Peace” (salām, salām). The word salām can mean peace, safety, greeting, or submission; repeated twice for emphasis, it indicates that peace is not just present but abundant, continuous, and comprehensive. It is the greeting exchanged among the inhabitants of Paradise—between angels and servants, between companions, and between the servant and God.

This repetition is significant in Arabic rhetoric: doubling a word increases its intensity and completeness. They hear not one peace but peace upon peace—peace in body, peace in heart, peace in relationship, peace with God. The absence of vain talk makes room for this pure greeting; the absence of sin ensures that the greeting is sincere.

**Spiritual Meaning:** The believer should seek to bring “peace” into relationships—not as empty politeness but as real safety, respect, and harmony. The greeting of Paradise is both a gift and a model: if we want to experience peace in this life and the next, we must create it through sincerity, justice, and humility.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:58—“For the companions of Paradise—Peace be upon you—because you were patient” — the greeting of peace is part of the reward.

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “They hear only ‘Peace, Peace’ because all other speech in Paradise is useful and good; the repetition emphasizes the completeness and continuity of peace.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:53

> **What! When we have died and are dust and bones, are we to be requited?’”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mockery of Resurrection**

The verse completes the speech of the companion begun at verse 51. He asks whether his former friend is among those who confirm the Resurrection, and then supplies his own scoffing answer: *What! When we have died and are dust and bones, are we to be requited?*

Ibn Jurayj, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabarī note that the first part of the verse reflects a question posed repeatedly across the Qur'an, citing 13:5, 17:49 and 98, 23:35 and 82–83, 27:67, 36:78–79, 37:16–17, 50:3, 56:47–48, and 79:11.

The recurrence is significant. The objection is not an isolated quibble but the standard denial the Qur'an attributes to those who reject the Hereafter, and the accumulation of parallels shows how central the objection is.

The second part of the verse mocks belief in a final reckoning specifically. The reduction of the human being to dust and bones is offered as proof that requital is impossible.

**Modern Analogy:** The argument assumes that what has decomposed cannot be reassembled — a claim about mechanism offered as a claim about possibility. The Qur'an answers it not by describing the mechanism but by appealing to the first creation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:78–79 — *Who shall revive the bones when they have rotted away? Say: He shall revive them Who first brought them into being* — the direct answer to this objection.

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the companion's question is astonishment bordering on ridicule, and it is precisely this astonishment that the believer's vision at vv. 54–55 will overturn.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:54

> **He will say, “Will you look?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Removal of the God-Fearing and the Safety of Taqwā**

The verse introduces the protection of those who have feared God (man kana yattaqī): they will be “removed from Hellfire” (yuzḥalū ʿan al-nār). The word yuzḥal means to be removed, separated, taken away—indicating that they are not just saved from entering Hell but are actively removed from its presence, as if they were never close to it. This is the power of taqwā: it creates a distance, a protection, a separation that is both physical and spiritual.

The phrase “who have feared God” (man kana yattaqī) is general: it refers to anyone who lived with awareness of God, who avoided disobedience, who sought to please Him. The protection is not selective based on lineage or status; it is based on the state of the heart and the consistency of actions.

**Psychological Insight:** Fear of God is not terror but respectful awareness—like walking carefully on a dangerous path. The verse teaches that this awareness is not restrictive but protective: it removes us from the danger we might otherwise walk into.

> **Cross-Reference:** 52:18—“Indeed, those who fear God are in Gardens and springs” — the same protection is described positively.

> **Classical View (al-Qurṭubī):** “Those who feared God are removed from Hell because taqwā is a shield; it prevents the fire from reaching them by God’s mercy and their own actions.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:55

> **So he will look and see him in the midst of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**So He Will Look and See Him**

The verse records the second half of an exchange begun at verse 54, where the believer asks his companions in the Garden, *Will you look?* The asking is followed here by the looking and by what the looking discloses.

Al-Ṭabarī, along with Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Kathīr, records that the believer then sees his companion from this world in the midst of Hellfire in the Hereafter. The recognition is of a specific person, not of a category.

The specificity is what gives the verse its force. The one seen is a former companion, someone known in the worldly life, and the seeing is therefore a recognition rather than a survey.

The verse's brevity is deliberate. It records the look and the sight and nothing else, leaving the significance to the verses that follow.

**In the Midst of Hellfire**

The location is specified: in the midst of Hellfire. The companion is not at its edge but in its middle, and the specification is what the believer's companions are asked to witness.

The placement matters because it removes any ambiguity about the companion's state. He is not suffering incidentally but is situated within the punishment.

The verse thus completes the contrast the sūrah has been building. The one asking the question is in the Garden; the one seen is in the midst of the Fire.

The contrast is visual rather than argued. The verse does not explain why the two are where they are; it shows them where they are.

**What the Seeing Establishes**

The commentators explain that on seeing his former companion the believer knows that had he followed him, he would have suffered the same fate. The knowledge is counterfactual and personal.

The counterfactual is what distinguishes this from a general observation about punishment. It concerns what would have happened to this person had he taken a different course.

The commentators add that if not for God's Blessing in guiding him, he too would have been arraigned unto the punishment, citing 30:16 and 34:38.

The attribution to Divine blessing is what the seeing produces. The believer's own guidance is recognized as conferred rather than achieved, and the recognition follows from the sight.

**Ibn Kathīr's Gloss**

Ibn Kathīr glosses these verses with 7:43, where the believers say, *Praise be to God, Who guided us unto this. We would not have been rightly guided, had God not guided us.*

The gloss supplies the words the verse leaves unspoken. What the believer knows on seeing his companion is what 7:43 states explicitly.

The connection is thematic as well as verbal. Both passages locate guidance in God's initiative rather than in the believer's own attainment.

The gloss therefore completes the verse's meaning without adding to its content, and the commentators preserve it as interpretation rather than as text.

**The Sequence Within Verses 54–57**

The four verses form a single narrative unit, and the commentators treat them together. The believer asks, looks, sees, and then speaks at verse 56.

The sequence is what gives each verse its place. The asking at verse 54 requires the looking at verse 55, and the looking requires the speech that follows.

The verse's position within the unit is therefore structural. It supplies the disclosure that the question sought and that the following speech responds to.

The commentators' joint treatment of verses 54–57 reflects this unity, and the treatment indicates that no single verse carries the whole account.

**Recognition and Gratitude**

The verse's disclosure produces recognition, and the recognition produces gratitude. The believer sees what he was spared, and the seeing is what makes the sparing visible.

The visibility is what the verse accomplishes. Guidance that went unnoticed in the worldly life becomes unmistakable when its alternative is seen.

This is why the believer calls his companions to look. The sight is not private but shared, and the sharing is what the question at verse 54 requested.

The verse therefore records both the disclosure and the occasion of gratitude, and the two belong to a single movement.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Theme**

The verse belongs to the sūrah's sustained contrast between the outcomes of belief and rejection, and it presents the contrast through a particular case rather than a general statement.

The particular case is what makes the contrast concrete. Two companions are separated, and the separation is shown rather than described.

The showing is what the sūrah's method requires at this point, since the general principle has already been stated and what remains is its illustration.

The illustration is what the verse provides, and the provision is what connects it to the speech that follows at verse 56.

**What the Verse Leaves Unstated**

The verse does not explain why the companion is in the Fire, and it does not record any exchange between the two at this point. The explanation comes in the following verses.

The restraint is characteristic of the account. The sight is recorded, and its interpretation is left to the believer's speech, which occupies verses 56 and 57.

The division of labour between the verses is what the commentators' joint treatment reflects, and the treatment shows that the account is designed as a sequence.

The verse's contribution is therefore the disclosure itself, and the disclosure is what the following speech interprets.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:56

> **He will say, “By God! You did well-nigh destroy me;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**You Did Well-Nigh Destroy Me**

The verse records the believer's address to the companion he has just seen in the midst of Hellfire. Ibn Jurayj, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabarī treat vv. 54–57 as a single exchange: the believer asks his companions to look, sees his former companion, and then speaks.

The opening oath *By God!* gives the speech its weight. What follows is not a taunt but a recognition of how narrowly the speaker escaped, and the oath marks the seriousness of that recognition.

The phrase *you did well-nigh destroy me* identifies the companion's influence as nearly fatal. The danger was not physical but spiritual — the companion's denial had almost carried the believer with it.

Ibn Kathīr glosses these verses with 7:43, where the believers say, *Praise be to God, Who guided us unto this. We would not have been rightly guided, had God not guided us.*

**Psychological Insight:** The verse captures a familiar experience in retrospect — recognizing that a relationship once thought harmless was in fact corrosive, and seeing this only once the consequences are visible.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:166–167 — *Those who followed will disown those whom they followed, when they behold the punishment, and the ties between them are cut asunder.*

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** the address confirms that the companion of vv. 51–53 is the same person now seen in the Fire, binding the two passages into one narrative.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:57

> **and if not for the blessing of my Lord, I would have been among the arraigned.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**If Not for the Blessing of My Lord**

The verse completes the believer's speech, and its logic is counterfactual: *if not for the blessing of my Lord, I would have been among the arraigned.* The commentators cite 30:16 and 34:38 for the reckoning implied by *the arraigned*.

The attribution is the verse's point. The believer does not credit his own firmness but God's blessing, and the contrast with the companion makes the attribution concrete rather than abstract.

Ibn Kathīr's gloss with 7:43 — *We would not have been rightly guided, had God not guided us* — supplies the words this verse implies, locating guidance wholly in Divine initiative.

The pairing with verse 56 matters. The companion nearly destroyed him; God's blessing prevented it. Both halves of the outcome are named, and neither is attributed to the believer's own merit.

**Modern Analogy:** Like someone who learns that a decision once made casually determined everything after it, and who cannot claim to have seen the fork in the road at the time.

> **Cross-Reference:** 30:16 — *Then as for those who disbelieved and denied Our signs and the meeting of the Hereafter, those shall be arraigned unto the punishment.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the verse establishes that deliverance from the Fire is owed to guidance received, not to superiority earned, which is why the believer speaks with relief rather than triumph.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:58

> **Are we then not to die,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Purification Through Spending and the Transformation of Wealth**

Verse 58 introduces another category of the righteous: “He who spends his wealth to be purified” (man anfaqa mālahu li-yutazarrā). The word anfaqa means to spend, to distribute, to give away; the purpose is “to be purified” (li-yutazarrā), meaning to cleanse oneself from greed, attachment, and the corruption of wealth. Spending is not just charity; it is a spiritual practice that transforms the heart.

The phrase “to be purified” connects to the concept of zakāh (purification) and to the idea that wealth is a test. Those who pass the test by giving freely receive purification; those who fail by hoarding receive punishment. The verse teaches that the purpose of wealth is not accumulation but transformation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 9:103 — “Take from their wealth a charity by which you may purify them.”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “Spending to be purified means giving zakāh and voluntary charity so that the heart is cleansed from love of money.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:59

> **but our first death, and are we not to be punished?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**But Our First Death**

The verse continues the speech of the believer, or in the commentators' alternative reading, the words of all the believers in Paradise. Ibn Jurayj and al-Zamakhsharī treat vv. 58–60 as an expression of joy at two realizations: that they will not die again, and that they have escaped Divine punishment.

The phrase *but our first death* is the hinge. Death is acknowledged as having occurred once, and the exclusion of any further death is what the believers rejoice in.

The second half — *and are we not to be punished?* — is a question of delighted disbelief rather than doubt. The believers marvel that immunity has been granted them.

The commentators note that attaining the Garden is called the great triumph in over a dozen verses, and direct the reader to 5:119 and 44:51–57 for the blessings of the Garden.

**Psychological Insight:** The joy described is specifically relief — not the pleasure of acquisition but the cessation of a danger that was real and is now permanently past.

> **Cross-Reference:** 44:56 — *They shall not taste death therein, save the first death; and He shall protect them from the punishment of Hellfire.*

> **Classical View (al-Zamakhsharī):** the two clauses correspond to the two great fears of the human condition — annihilation and condemnation — and the verse declares both dissolved.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:60

> **This indeed is the great triumph!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seeking Only the Face of God and the Ultimate Intention**

The verse completes the description: “For he seeks only the Face of his Lord, the Most High” (illā btighāʾa wajhi rabbihi al-aʿlā). The phrase “Face of his Lord” (wajh Allāh) is a metaphor for God’s presence, approval, and pleasure—not a physical face but the direction toward which one turns in worship and in life. The spenders do not seek the praise of people or the rewards of this world; they seek only God.

The word al-aʿlā (the Most High) emphasizes God’s exalted nature: He is above all limitations, above all competition, and above all need for human approval. Those who seek Him seek the highest possible goal, and in return they receive the highest possible reward.

> **Cross-Reference:** 92:18-20 — “And those who give their wealth to be purified... seeking only the Face of God, the Most High.”
> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** “Seeking the Face of God means seeking His pleasure and approval alone; this is the purest intention and the cause of the best reward.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:61

> **For the like of this, then, let the laborers labor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Regret Over the Message and the Realization of Loss**

The verse captures the disbelievers’ cry of regret: “What was this message of ours?” (mā hādhā al-kitābu). The word kitāb here refers not to a book but to the record of their deeds—the message that has been written against them, the decree that has come true. They realize that the message was not a distant prophecy but a personal reality that they ignored.

The “woe” (wayl) expresses their realization that they wasted their lives in denial, and now the record is undeniable. The verse teaches that every action is recorded, and at judgment the record speaks with clarity.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:66 — “Indeed, We have decreed death for you, and We are not to be outdone; and We shall raise you in a gathering We know well.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They ask about the message because they realize it was true and they denied it; now they see the record of their deeds and regret everything.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:62

> **Is this a better welcome or the tree of Zaqqūm?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Disobedience of the Messenger and the Personal Responsibility**

The disbelievers now identify the source of their loss: not only they rejected the message, but they disobeyed the messenger (man ʿaṣaynā al-rasūl). The word ʿaṣā means to disobey, to oppose, to reject authority. They admit that they not only ignored the message but actively opposed the one who brought it.

This is a critical admission: they recognize that the messenger was not a magician or madman but a true guide whom they chose to reject. The regret is deep because they understand that their disobedience was not accidental but deliberate.

> **Cross-Reference:** 5:92 — “Obey God and obey the Messenger” — the opposite of disobedience.
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They confess they disobeyed the messenger, which shows that they knew he was true but chose pride over submission.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:63

> **Truly We have made it a trial for the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trial for the Wrongdoers**

The verse states that the tree of Zaqqūm has been made a trial, and al-Zamakhsharī explains that the rhetorical question at verse 62 is posed as a rebuke to the disbelievers for the course they chose, since it is known that there is no good in Zaqqūm.

The trial is thus not an uncertainty about the tree's nature but a test of what people do with a warning. The tree's horror is announced in advance, and the choice remains open.

The contrast with the believers is explicit in the surrounding verses. Against the fruits given as a known provision in the Garden at vv. 41–42 stands Zaqqūm, which al-Zamakhsharī says bears pain and sorrow.

The commentators direct the reader to 44:43–46 for the tree's effects: *Truly the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinner, like molten lead boiling in their bellies*, and also 56:51–55.

**Modern Analogy:** A hazard that is fully documented and clearly signposted, so that encountering it later cannot be pleaded as unforeseen.

> **Cross-Reference:** 17:60 — *And We appointed the vision We showed thee as a trial for the people, and the tree accursed in the Quran.*

> **Classical View (al-Zamakhsharī):** the tree is a trial in this world before it is a punishment in the next, since belief in it is itself a test of whether the warning is taken seriously.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:64

> **Truly it is a tree that emerges in the depths of Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Tree in the Depths of Hellfire**

The verse locates Zaqqūm, and Ibn Kathīr explains that its emerging in the depths of Hellfire means its roots grow from the bottom of Hell and it is nourished by fire.

Al-Zamakhsharī records a related account: that its roots reach the depths of Hell while its branches rise through its other levels. The two descriptions agree on the root and differ on the extent of the growth.

The nourishment by fire is the striking element. A tree sustained by what destroys ordinary life inverts the natural order, and the inversion is what marks the tree as belonging to the Fire rather than to the world.

The commentators preserve both descriptions rather than choosing between them, so the reader is given the range of the tradition about the tree's form.

**Modern Analogy:** An organism adapted to an environment lethal to everything else — the image works because it inverts the expectation that fire consumes rather than sustains.

> **Cross-Reference:** 44:43–44 — *Truly the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinner.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** that the tree grows in Hell is itself a sign of the Fire's inversion of the natural order, since fire is ordinarily what prevents growth rather than what feeds it.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:65

> **Its spathes are as the heads of satans.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Spathes as the Heads of Satans**

The verse describes the tree's spathes, and the commentators offer two readings. Al-Zamakhsharī takes the comparison to the heads of satans as indicating that the tree bears sheer evil; Ibn Jurayj understands the spathes as being like snakes.

The two readings differ in whether the comparison is moral or visual. On the first, the likeness conveys the tree's nature; on the second, its appearance.

Both readings are preserved, and the preservation matters: the simile is explained neither as pure metaphor nor as literal description, and the tradition declines to settle which it is.

Al-Zamakhsharī adds that the disbelievers eat of it because they are commanded to, citing 56:53, and that they fill their bellies either from severe hunger or because they are forced to eat despite despising it.

**Modern Analogy:** Something whose appearance signals danger accurately, so that consuming it requires either compulsion or a refusal to read the signal.

> **Cross-Reference:** 56:52–53 — *You shall surely eat of a tree of Zaqqūm, and shall fill therewith your bellies.*

> **Classical View (al-Zamakhsharī):** both interpretations allude to the fact that their own avaricious natures force them to continue eating from Zaqqūm, so the compulsion is partly internal.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:66

> **They will eat thereof and fill their bellies therewith.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The State of Entry and the Completeness of Peace**

The verse describes the entry: “Entering them with peace and security” (dākhilīna fī-hā bi-salāmin āminīn). The word salām is peace—internal, external, and with God; āmin is security—freedom from fear, danger, and anxiety. Together they describe the perfect state of the sincere servant: they enter Paradise not with fear, not with doubt, but with complete peace and absolute security.

This is the fulfillment of the “Peace, Peace” greeting (v. 52): they enter into an environment where peace is not only spoken but lived, not only heard but experienced fully. The entry is not a struggle but a welcome; not a test but a gift.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:58 — “Peace be upon you, because you were patient.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They enter with peace because they have been purified; they are safe because God has protected them from Hell; their entry is the completion of taqwā.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:67

> **Then on top of it they will have a brew of a boiling liquid.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Brew of Boiling Liquid**

The verse adds a drink to the food, and al-Zamakhsharī explains the sequence: if the disbelievers are satiated by Zaqqūm, thirst overwhelms them, and they are given a drink that burns.

The phrase *on top of it* indicates that the brew follows the eating rather than accompanies it, and the sequence is what makes the punishment cumulative rather than single.

The relief that eating would ordinarily bring is thus withdrawn. Satiety produces thirst, and the drink offered does not quench but scalds, so each stage generates the next need.

Al-Zamakhsharī's account of the cycle — hunger, eating, satiation, thirst, scalding drink — is what gives the verse its place within the sequence of vv. 62–67.

**Modern Analogy:** A remedy that reliably produces the next symptom, so that treatment and affliction become indistinguishable.

> **Cross-Reference:** 56:54–55 — *And shall drink thereon of boiling water, and drink as the thirsty camel drinks.*

> **Classical View (al-Zamakhsharī):** the alternating punishments are not redundant; each addresses a need created by the last, which is why the sequence has no resting point.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:68

> **Then their return shall be unto Hellfire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Brothers, Couches, and the Perfection of Relationship**

The verse completes the description: they are “as brothers” (ʿalā sururin mutaqābilīn) — actually the phrase is “brothers facing one another upon couches.” The word ikhwān (brothers) indicates equality, shared identity, and mutual love—not competitive siblings but harmonious partners. They sit “upon couches facing one another” — the same arrangement described in v. 44, confirming continuity and completeness.

This is the social perfection of Paradise: no hierarchy of status, no exclusion, no hidden resentment—only brotherhood, mutual respect, and shared enjoyment. The believer should seek to build such relationships in this life, knowing that they are a foretaste of Paradise.

> **Cross-Reference:** 15:47 — “And We will remove bitterness from their breasts; below them rivers flow.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They are brothers because they shared the same faith and the same sincerity; their relationship is pure because God has removed all bitterness.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:69

> **Truly they found their fathers astray;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Found Their Fathers Astray**

The verse gives the reason for the disbelievers' rejection, and al-Ṭabarī explains that because their fathers had followed a different set of beliefs, they waxed arrogant when told to bear witness that there is no god but God.

Al-Rāzī adds that they preferred the way of their forefathers to the one the Prophet ﷺ called them to, and cites 2:170: *Nay, we follow that which we found our fathers doing. What! Even though their fathers understood nothing, and were not rightly guided?*

The commentators supply a further list of parallels — 5:104, 7:28, 11:109, and 43:22–23 — showing that appeal to ancestral practice is a recurring objection rather than a local one.

The verse is diagnostic rather than merely accusatory. It identifies inheritance of belief as the mechanism by which the rejection is transmitted across generations.

**Psychological Insight:** The objection is not that the fathers were shown to be right but that they were the fathers. Authority is located in lineage rather than in evidence, which is why counter-evidence does not reach it.

> **Cross-Reference:** 43:22–23 — *Nay, but they say: Truly we found our fathers following a religion, and we are guided by their traces.*

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** the arrogance mentioned by al-Ṭabarī is the refusal to examine an inherited position, and the refusal is what the verse identifies as the root of the denial.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:70

> **yet they hasten in their footsteps.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet’s Denial of Unseen Knowledge and the Limits of Human Wisdom**

The verse is spoken by the Prophet: “Say, ‘Had I known the unseen, I could have acquired much good, and no harm would have touched me.’” The word ghayb (unseen) refers to knowledge of future events, hidden realities, and divine secrets—things that only God knows. The Prophet denies having such knowledge, affirming that he is a human messenger, not a divine being with omniscience.

This is important because it protects the Prophet from the accusation that he was a sorcerer or fortune-teller (which requires hidden knowledge). He does not claim to know the future; he only delivers the revelation he received. This humility is part of his credibility: he admits his limits, and in doing so, proves that his message comes from God, not from himself.

> **Cross-Reference:** 7:188 — “Say, ‘I hold not for myself any harm or benefit, except what God has willed; if I knew the unseen, I could have acquired much good.’”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The Prophet says he does not know the unseen; this is proof that he is not a sorcerer, because sorcerers claim hidden knowledge, but he only delivers what God reveals.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:71

> **And before them most of those of old had indeed gone astray,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Most of Those of Old Had Gone Astray**

The verse extends the diagnosis of vv. 69–70 backward through history. Al-Rāzī explains that these verses affirm that most human collectivities have gone astray and rejected the prophets sent to them.

The scope is deliberate. The failure is not peculiar to the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ contemporaries but is the general pattern of human communities, and the generality is what the verse asserts.

Al-Rāzī notes that this aspect of history may be cited as counsel for the Prophet ﷺ to be patient and to continue calling people to religion, since the prophets before him — whose stories occupy the remainder of the sūrah — were also rejected but ultimately prevailed.

The verse therefore functions as consolation before the narratives begin. The pattern of rejection is established first, so that the accounts that follow can be read as instances rather than anomalies.

**Modern Analogy:** Establishing a base rate before assessing a single case, so that an individual setback is not mistaken for evidence that the enterprise has failed.

> **Cross-Reference:** 22:44 — *And if they deny thee, so did the people of Noah, and ʿĀd and Thamūd, and the people of Abraham and the people of Lot, and the people of Madyan.*

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** the verse prepares the Prophet ﷺ for the accounts that follow by showing that rejection is the historical norm and that it has never been the final word.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:72

> **though We had indeed sent warners among them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Though We Had Sent Warners Among Them**

The verse supplies the crucial qualification to verse 71, and the concessive *though* is what gives the passage its force. The going astray occurred despite warning, not in its absence.

Al-Rāzī's reading connects the two verses: most of the ancients went astray, and they did so after warners had been sent to them. The warning removes ignorance as an excuse.

This is what makes the historical pattern a warning rather than a mere observation. The communities failed with full notice, and the notice is what the verse emphasizes.

The verse also completes the consolation begun at verse 71. The prophets were sent and were rejected, yet the sending was not futile — the outcome of each account shows that the warning was ultimately vindicated.

**Psychological Insight:** The verse identifies a specific human tendency: that being warned does not produce attention, and that the presence of a warning can even harden the position it was meant to correct.

> **Cross-Reference:** 6:112 — *And so for every prophet We appointed an enemy — satans among mankind and jinn, inspiring one another with beguiling speech.*

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** the sending of warners is what converts the historical record from a catalogue of failures into a counsel of patience, since the warners prevailed despite the rejection.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:73

> **So behold how those who were warned fared in the end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Behold How Those Who Were Warned Fared**

The verse turns from statement to invitation, and al-Rāzī notes that it is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ while also serving as a warning to the disbelievers.

Al-Rāzī further identifies the verse as a segue to the stories of Noah, Abraham, Lot, and the other prophets in the remainder of the sūrah, which is what gives it its structural role.

The commentators note that this verse about those who were warned is repeated as part of 10:73, and list further verses enjoining consideration of final ends: 3:137, 6:11, 16:36, and 43:25 for the deniers; 7:84 and 27:69 for the guilty; 7:86 and 103 and 27:14 for the workers of corruption; 10:39 and 28:40 for the wrongdoers.

The accumulation of parallels shows that the invitation to look at outcomes is a settled Qur'anic method. History is offered as evidence rather than as illustration.

**Modern Analogy:** Pointing to a set of completed cases and asking what happened in them, rather than arguing from first principles about what should happen.

> **Cross-Reference:** 10:73 — *So behold how those who were warned fared in the end.*

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** the verse is a hinge: it closes the argument about rejection and opens the prophetic narratives that will supply the evidence the invitation calls for.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:74

> **Not so for God’s sincere servants.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah’s Call and the Excellence of God’s Response**

The verse begins the story of Noah: “And Noah called to Us” (wa-nāḥin nādā-na) — Noah prayed, supplicated, and called upon God with urgency and sincerity. The word nāda means to call loudly, to cry out, to seek help—indicating that Noah was in genuine need, facing the destruction of his people, and turning fully to God.

“Excellent are the Responders” (wa-niʿma al-mujībūn) — God is the best of those who respond to prayer, the most generous, the most just, and the most quick to answer those who call to Him with sincerity. This is a reassurance to every believer: when you call God with sincerity, He responds—not always with what you expect, but with what is best.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:76 — “So We saved him and his family from the great distress.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Noah called to God because he was desperate; God is excellent in responding because He answers with mercy and saves those who rely on Him.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:75

> **And indeed Noah cried out to Us. How excellent are those who respond!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rescue of Noah and His Family from the Great Distress**

The verse confirms God’s response: “We saved him and his family” (wa-najja-nāhu wa-ahlahu) — Noah, his wife, his sons (except the rebellious one), and those who believed with them. The word najjā (saved) is active and complete: God did not just protect them but fully removed them from danger.

The “great distress” (al-gabar) refers to the flood, the destruction of the people, and the overwhelming nature of the punishment. It is “great” because it was universal—everyone except the saved was destroyed. The verse teaches that God’s protection is not partial when the danger is total; it is complete.

> **Cross-Reference:** 7:64 — “So when Our command came, and the oven overflowed, We said, ‘Load upon it of each [pair] two and your family.’”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “God saved Noah and his family because they were sincere; the great distress was the flood that destroyed all others.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:76

> **We saved him and his people from great distress,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Saved from Great Distress**

The verse records the deliverance of Noah and his people, and the commentators gloss *his people* as those who responded to his call to follow God. According to traditional accounts, though Noah called people to God for hundreds of years — see 29:14 — few followed him.

The smallness of the group is what gives the deliverance its significance. The saved are few, and the verse names them as *his people* rather than as a multitude.

Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Kathīr explain *great distress* as the insults Noah suffered, while Ibn Jurayj also records the reading that it refers to his being separated from his people.

The two readings differ in whether the distress was inflicted by the people or consisted in separation from them, and both are preserved without preference.

The commentators note that these verses give only the broad contours of Noah's story, directing the reader to 11:25–48, 23:23–30, 26:105–21, and Sūrah 71 for fuller accounts, with briefer ones at 7:59–64, 10:71–73, 21:76–77, 29:14–15, and 54:9–15.

> **Cross-Reference:** 29:15 — *But We saved him and the people of the ark, and We made it a sign for the worlds.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the distress is understood as the sustained mockery Noah endured across centuries of preaching, which is what makes the eventual deliverance a reversal rather than a rescue from a single crisis.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:77

> **and We made his progeny endure,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**We Made His Progeny Endure**

The verse states that Noah's progeny endured, and Ibn Kathīr explains that as the only line of human beings to survive the flood, his descendants are the continuation of the human race.

The claim is genealogical as well as theological. Every subsequent generation descends from those preserved in the ark, and the verse marks that fact as a Divine act rather than a coincidence.

Ibn Kathīr adds that Noah was blessed with being remembered well, in a manner befitting his status, by later generations, and that his story is left as a sign for the worlds, citing 29:15 and 54:15.

The double blessing is notable: survival of his line and honour in remembrance. The verse records both, and the second is what verses 78–79 develop.

**Modern Analogy:** A bottleneck event after which all subsequent diversity traces back to a small surviving group — the image works because it is both a biological claim and a claim about significance.

> **Cross-Reference:** 54:15 — *And We left it as a sign. So is there any who will heed?*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** that all later humanity descends from Noah's progeny is what makes the flood a renewal of the human race rather than merely its punishment.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:78

> **and We left [a blessing] upon him among later generations:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Blessing Among Later Generations**

The verse records what was left upon Noah among later generations, and Ibn Kathīr explains it as his being remembered well, in a manner befitting his status, by those who came after.

The remembrance is thus presented as a lasting gift rather than a reputation Noah cultivated. It is left upon him, and the leaving is God's act.

Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī explain the prayer for peace upon Noah throughout the worlds at verse 79 as meaning that all of mankind, the jinn, and the angels wish him peace.

The scope of the blessing is what the verse emphasizes. It is not confined to his own people or his own time but extends across the worlds, and the extension is what verse 79 states.

The commentators connect this to his story being left as a sign for the worlds at 29:15 and 54:15, so remembrance and signification are the same blessing described from two angles.

> **Cross-Reference:** 29:15 — *And We made it a sign for the worlds.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the honour consists in the manner of the remembrance as much as in its persistence — it befits his status rather than merely recording his name.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:79

> **“Peace be upon Noah throughout the worlds.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah’s Status as a Believing Servant and the Foundation of Faith**

The verse confirms Noah’s status: “Indeed, he was among Our believing servants” (inna-hu min ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn). The word muʾmin (believing) is not just belief in God but active trust, submission, and devotion. Noah was not only a prophet but a servant—he served God through prayer, patience, and obedience.

This is a model for all believers: to be a prophet is exceptional, but to be a believing servant is everyone’s calling. Noah’s life teaches that faith is not a single moment but a sustained commitment—through difficulty, through rejection, through destruction, and through preservation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:105 — “And We have written in the Zabūr, after [previous mention], that the righteous will inherit the earth.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Noah is praised as a believing servant because he believed in God, worshipped Him, called others, and was patient until the end.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:80

> **Thus do We recompense the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thus Do We Recompense the Virtuous**

The verse generalizes from Noah's case, and the commentators supply an extensive list of parallels: 6:84, 12:22, 28:14, and within this sūrah 37:105, 110, 121, and 131, together with 77:44.

The recurrence within Sūrah 37 itself is significant. The formula returns after each prophetic account, so the sūrah repeatedly draws the same conclusion from successive instances.

For *the virtuous* the commentators direct the reader to 29:69c, and they explain that God rewards those who do what is good and beautiful with greater beauty.

The support cited is 42:23 — *And whosoever accomplishes a good deed, We shall increase him in goodness thereby* — and 55:60: *Is the reward of goodness aught but goodness?*

**Modern Analogy:** A principle stated after a worked example, so that the reader can apply it to cases not yet given — which is exactly what the sūrah proceeds to do.

> **Cross-Reference:** 55:60 — *Is the reward of goodness aught but goodness?*

> **Classical View (al-Zamakhsharī):** the recompense is of the same kind as the deed but greater, so the verse states a proportionality that exceeds itself.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:81

> **Truly he was among Our believing servants.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Few Who Followed and the Many Who Rejected**

The verse summarizes the result of Noah’s mission: “Indeed, among them were those who followed him” — a few believed, entered the ark, and were saved; “but most of them were defiantly disobedient” — the majority rejected, mocked, and were destroyed. The word kāfirūn (disobedient, disbelieving) indicates active refusal, not passive ignorance.

This is a recurring pattern in prophetic history: the message is clear, but the response is small. The believer should not be discouraged by small numbers; the quality of faith matters more than the quantity. Noah’s few followers became the foundation of humanity; the majority’s destruction was a warning for all generations.

> **Cross-Reference:** 54:1 — “The Hour has come near, and the moon has been split; and if they see a sign, they turn away and say, ‘Continuous magic.’”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Only a few followed Noah; most were rebellious; this shows that truth is often rejected by the majority, and the believer should not seek validation from crowds but from God.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:82

> **Then We drowned the others.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Continuation of Prophethood and the Chain of Guidance**

The verse describes the continuation after Noah: “Then after him, We sent prophets to his descendants” (thumma baʿdahu arsalnā fī ʿaqibihi al-rusul) — God did not abandon humanity; He continued to send prophets, messengers, and guides through the generations. The word ʿaqib means descendants, offspring, or those who come after; the prophets were sent to Noah’s descendants, meaning that the message of monotheism was preserved and transmitted.

This is the continuity of revelation: from Noah to Abraham, from Abraham to Moses, from Moses to Jesus, and from Jesus to Muhammad—each prophet confirms the previous, and each message builds on the last. The believer should see themselves as part of this chain, inheriting the message and responsible for passing it on.

> **Cross-Reference:** 56:75-76 — “We have already sent to you messengers from among you, and from among your fathers and your mothers; so that they may be a witness against you.”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “God sent prophets after Noah to his descendants because they needed ongoing guidance; the chain of prophecy is continuous and never broken.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:83

> **And surely among his party was Abraham,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham’s Sound Heart and His Approach to God**

The verse begins Abraham’s story: “And Abraham, when he came to his Lord with a sound heart” (wa-ibrāhīma idh jāʾa rabbahu bi-qalbin salīm). The phrase “sound heart” (qalb salīm) means a heart free from disease, corruption, hypocrisy, and doubt—pure, sincere, and fully devoted to God. Abraham did not come with pride, with excuses, or with demands; he came with a heart that was already clean.

This is a model for all believers: the first step toward God is not performance but purification of the heart. Abraham’s heart was sound because he had rejected his father’s idolatry, because he had sought truth through reason, and because he had chosen God over everything else.

> **Cross-Reference:** 26:89 — “Except for those who have pure hearts and do righteous deeds.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham came with a sound heart because he had rejected false gods, reasoned about the Creator, and turned entirely to God.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:84

> **when he came unto his Lord with a sound heart,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**With a Sound Heart**

The verse describes Abraham as coming unto his Lord with a sound heart, and the commentators offer a range of readings that they preserve together rather than reduce.

Ibn Jurayj, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī take it to mean that he never inclined to idolatry. Al-Qumī and al-Ṭabrisī hold that his heart was free from doubt.

Al-Rāzī adds a third reading: that his heart was free from all vice and he desired for others only what he desired for himself. Al-Ṭabrisī records a fourth — that there was none but God in his heart.

All the readings agree that he was completely submitted to God, and the commentators direct the reader to 26:89c for the fuller treatment of the sound heart.

The plurality is instructive. The commentators do not treat the phrase as having one fixed sense but as describing a condition approached from several directions.

**Psychological Insight:** Each reading identifies a different absence — no idolatry, no doubt, no vice, no rival object of love — and together they describe integrity as the absence of division rather than as a positive achievement.

> **Cross-Reference:** 26:88–89 — *The Day when neither wealth nor sons avail, save him who comes unto God with a sound heart.*

> **Classical View (al-Rāzī):** the soundness of the heart consists in its being undivided, which is why the readings converge on singleness of orientation despite differing in emphasis.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:85

> **when he said unto his father and his people, “What do you worship?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Defense of Tradition and Its Failure**

The response is classic: “We found our fathers doing it.” The word wajadnā (we found) indicates inheritance, tradition, and social pressure—not personal conviction, not evidence, not reasoning. They defend idolatry not because they have proof but because it is familiar, comfortable, and socially approved.

Abraham answers this by showing that following the ignorant is itself ignorance. Tradition is not proof; social agreement is not truth; inheritance is not justification. The verse teaches that every belief must be tested not by history but by reasoning, evidence, and divine revelation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:166-167 — “Have you seen those to whom you call instead of God? Have they created anything in the heavens and the earth? Or have they a share in creation? Or did We give them a book? Or do they have knowledge?”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They defend with tradition because they have no rational defense; Abraham shows that tradition without truth is worthless.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:86

> **Is it a perversion, gods apart from God, that you desire?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Direct Judgment of Error and the Need for Clarity**

Abraham responds with directness: “Indeed, you and your fathers have been in clear error.” The word dalāl (error, misguidance) is not a minor mistake but a fundamental wrong direction—following false gods, rejecting the Creator, and wasting lives in false worship. The word mubīn (clear, evident) emphasizes that the error is not hidden; it is obvious to anyone who thinks.

This is the courage of the prophet: not to avoid confrontation, not to hide from conflict, but to speak clearly when lives are at stake. Abraham’s directness is not rudeness but responsibility—because the error is clear, he must say so clearly.

> **Cross-Reference:** 7:71-73 — Abraham’s direct challenge to his people’s idolatry.
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “Abraham says they are in clear error because they follow images that cannot speak, create, or protect; the error is obvious to anyone with reason.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:87

> **What, then, think you of the Lord of the worlds?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge to Abraham’s Sincerity and the Accusation of Mockery**

The people respond with suspicion: “Have you come with truth, or are you of those who mock?” The word istaʾlakum (have you come) questions Abraham’s intention; the word sāḥir (mocking) suggests that he might be mocking their traditions, making fun of their customs, or playing a trick on them.

This is a common response to reformers: their challenge is interpreted as mockery, and their sincerity is questioned. The people assume that because they are comfortable with their error, anyone who points it out must be mocking them or playing a game. The verse teaches that true reform often faces misunderstanding, not because the reformer is unclear, but because the audience is resistant.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:54 — Abraham breaks the idols and asks who did it, showing that the “mockery” is on the idols, not on the people.
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They accuse him of mocking because they cannot answer his reasoning; they prefer to attack his intention rather than address his argument.”

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:88

> **Then he cast a glance at the stars**

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Cast a Glance at the Stars**

The verse describes Abraham's glance, and the commentators explain that *cast a glance at the stars* is an idiomatic expression meaning to contemplate something, interpreted here as Abraham casting his glance upward while considering what to do.

Ibn Jurayj records that some say he feigned reading his own future, which then becomes the basis for his claim to be sick at the following verse.

The claim to sickness is itself explained in several ways. Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Kathīr hold that his heart was sickened by the worship of other gods; Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī hold that he feigned illness to avoid attending a religious festival where idol worship would occur.

The commentators note the reported saying of the Prophet ﷺ that Abraham did not lie save in three instances, which frames how the tradition handles the feigning.

**Modern Analogy:** Using an opponent's own framework to buy time — the gesture is read within their assumptions in order to create the space needed for what follows.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:57 — *And by God, I shall scheme against your idols after you have turned your backs.*

> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** the feigned illness is understood as a stratagem permitted by the circumstance, not as a falsehood told for its own sake, which is why the tradition records it without censure.

---
## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:89

> **and said, “Truly I am sick.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abraham’s Plan and the Expose of False Gods**

Abraham says: “By God, I will surely plan against your idols” — he will take action, not just speak. The word la-aʾtafilanna (I will plan) indicates deliberate, careful action; the word aʿmilan (I will do) confirms it will be done. Abraham does not wait for permission; he acts because truth demands action.

The phrase “after you have turned away and become supporters of them” describes the people’s state: they have turned from God and now support idols. Abraham’s plan is not violent against people but against false gods—breaking them, exposing their powerlessness, and showing that they are nothing.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:58 — “So he broke them into pieces, except for a great one among them.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham plans to break the idols to expose that they are false; he does not harm people but removes false objects of worship.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:90

> **But they turned their backs on him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Breaking of Idols and the Exposure of Powerlessness**

Abraham breaks the idols into pieces (ka-ṣābihīn) — except for one great idol — so that the people will return to it and realize it cannot help them. The word kabbalah (great) describes the largest idol, which remains intact, ready to be questioned: “Why did the others break? Did this one do it?” The answer is obvious: they are all powerless.

This is a masterful use of logic: instead of arguing with people, Abraham creates a situation where the evidence is visible. The idols cannot speak, cannot defend themselves, and cannot repair the damage. Their silence is their condemnation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:63 — “He said, ‘Nay, this, their great one has done it. So ask them, if they can speak.’”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “Abraham leaves one idol so that the people will ask it, and then they will realize that it is powerless; this is the best argument—proof by silence.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:91

> **Then he went quietly to their gods and said, “Will you not eat?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accusation of Abraham and the Response to the Evidence**

The people respond with shock: “Have you done this to our gods, O Abraham?” The word a-faʿalta (have you done) is accusatory; they are not asking for explanation but blaming him for the destruction of their objects of worship. Their focus is on the idols, not on the truth—showing that their attachment is to the objects, not to God.

This reveals the psychology of idolatry: people defend their false gods not because they are true but because they are familiar, comfortable, and socially significant. Breaking the idols does not convince them; it enrages them, because the idols represent their identity, their traditions, and their social order.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:64 — “Indeed, you know that these do not speak.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “They accuse him of destroying their gods because they value the objects more than the truth; this is the nature of false reliance.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:92

> **What ails you that you speak not?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge to Speak and the Silence of False Gods**

Abraham responds with a challenge: “Rather, this—their great one—has done it” (bal hādhā kabīruhum) — he points to the one remaining idol. Then: “So ask them, if they are able to speak” (fa-salūhum in kānū yantiqūn). The challenge is direct: ask the idols if they can speak, defend themselves, or explain what happened. The answer is silence.

This is the final proof: true gods must be able to speak, to respond, to act, and to protect. Idols cannot; they are inanimate objects made by human hands. Abraham’s challenge reveals not only the powerlessness of idols but also the intelligence of the questioner—he requires evidence, not just belief.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:65 — “So they turned to themselves and said, ‘Indeed, you are wrongdoers.’”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “The challenge is perfect: ask them to speak; since they cannot, they are proven false; this is the most logical refutation of idolatry.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:93

> **Then he turned upon them, striking with his right hand.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Striking with His Right Hand**

The verse records Abraham's action against the idols, and Ibn Kathīr explains the background: the people had placed food before the idols, and Abraham mockingly asked why the idols did not eat it.

Ibn Kathīr adds that he then destroyed all but the largest of them, which he accused of having smashed the others, as a means of mocking their beliefs — see 21:58–59c and 21:62–63c.

The commentators connect the verse to Abraham's earlier warning at 21:57: *And by God, I shall scheme against your idols after you have turned your backs.*

The phrase *with his right hand* is understood as indicating the strength and deliberateness of the blow, and the turning upon them marks the action as directed rather than incidental.

Al-Jawzī notes that from a spiritual perspective the passage alludes to how people should smash the idols of the things they rely upon and depend only upon God.

**Modern Analogy:** A demonstration designed to expose a contradiction — the largest idol is left standing precisely so that the claim made about it can be tested by its defenders.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:58 — *So he broke them into pieces, save the largest of them, that haply they might have recourse to it.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the leaving of the largest idol is the point of the stratagem, since it forces the people either to admit the idol's impotence or to accuse it of the destruction.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:94

> **Then they came to him rushing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Admission of Powerlessness and the Confession of Truth**

The people respond: “Indeed, you have known that these do not worship” (la-qad ʿalimta anna hāʾulāʾi lā yaʿbudūn). The word ʿalimta (you have known) addresses Abraham personally: he knows because he has reasoned, observed, and tested. The admission is forced: they must confess that the idols do not worship, do not respond, and do not have any power.

But they do not turn to God; instead, they remain attached to their idols because of tradition, pride, and social pressure. This is the tragedy of false belief: even when the evidence is clear, the heart refuses to change.

> **Cross-Reference:** 36:75 — “Do they have feet by which they walk? Or do they have hands by which they strike? Or do they have eyes by which they see? Or do they have ears by which they hear?”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “They confess that the idols do not worship, but they do not abandon them because they are attached to tradition and pride, not to truth.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:95

> **He said, "Then do you worship, apart from God, that which possesses for you neither benefit nor harm?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Challenge to the Usefulness of False Worship**

Abraham asks the ultimate question: “Then do you worship, apart from God, that which possesses for you neither benefit nor harm?” The word yanfaʿ (benefit) and yadurr (harm) describe the complete inability of idols to affect human life—positively or negatively. They cannot give, they cannot take; they cannot heal, they cannot hurt; they are completely powerless.

This is the final proof: if a god has no benefit and no harm, then it is not a god at all. Worship of such objects is not only false but absurd. The verse teaches that any belief must be tested by its practical effects, not by tradition or emotion.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:67 — “Indeed, if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will surely say, ‘God.’”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham asks if they worship things that can do neither good nor harm; this is the ultimate argument—worship requires power, and these have none.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:96

> **while God created you and that which you make?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Created You and That Which You Make**

The verse completes Abraham's question, and the commentators state its force: *Do you worship what you make rather than the One Who made you?*

Ibn Kathīr records a second reading in which the verse means that God creates human beings and all that they do, since He is the only one who can create.

On this reading the verse is connected to the affirmation of Divine Omnipotence in 3:128 — *Naught is thine in the matter* — 3:154: *The decision belongs entirely to God*, and 12:21: *And God prevails over His affair, but most of mankind know not.*

The two readings differ in scope. On the first, the contrast is between the maker and the made; on the second, even human making falls within Divine creation.

The commentators preserve both, and the second is the more demanding claim, since it denies that human agency is independent of Divine agency.

**Modern Analogy:** The difference between saying a tool was made by a person and saying the person's capacity to make anything was itself given — the second claim reaches further.

> **Cross-Reference:** 3:154 — *The decision belongs entirely to God.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** the verse can be read as a rebuke about misdirected worship or as an assertion of Divine Omnipotence, and both readings are sustained by the Arabic.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:97

> **They said, “Erect a building for him and cast him into the fire.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Erect a Building and Cast Him into the Fire**

The verse records the people's response to Abraham's argument, and the commentators direct the reader to 21:68–70 for the fuller account of the attempt to burn him and God's command, *Be coolness and peace for Abraham* (21:69).

The escalation is significant. Abraham argued with words, and the reply is not a counter-argument but a decision to destroy him. The building is erected specifically for the burning.

The commentators note that Abraham says *I am going unto my Lord* at verse 99 either when he decides to leave his people, declaring he will go where God commands, or before he is cast into the fire, meaning he is prepared to die.

Ibn Jurayj preserves both readings of when the statement is made, and the difference concerns whether the words express departure or readiness for death.

Al-Jawzī adds the spiritual reading: the passage alludes to how people should smash the idols of the things upon which they rely and depend only upon God.

**Modern Analogy:** An argument that cannot be answered being met with force — the shift from reasoning to violence marks the point where the stronger case is acknowledged by being suppressed.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:69 — *We said: O fire! Be coolness and peace for Abraham.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Jurayj):** the two occasions proposed for Abraham's statement both preserve its meaning, since in either case the words declare that his destination is God rather than the fire.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:98

> **They desired to scheme against him, so We made them the lowest.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Desired to Scheme, So We Made Them the Lowest**

The verse contrasts two schemings, and the contrast is the verse's whole content. The people schemed against Abraham, and God made them the lowest.

The reversal is stated without elaboration. The scheme is not described, only its failure, and the failure takes the form of the schemers being reduced to the lowest position.

The commentators connect the verse to the preceding account of the fire, so the scheming is identified with the attempt to burn Abraham and the lowering with its defeat.

The word *the lowest* is understood as a reversal of status: those who sought to elevate themselves over Abraham by destroying him are placed beneath him instead.

Al-Jawzī's spiritual reading applies the verse inwardly: the idols people rely upon are what must be broken, and dependence belongs to God alone.

**Modern Analogy:** A trap that closes on the one who set it — the verse's economy comes from not narrating the mechanism, only the inverted outcome.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:70 — *And they desired to lay a snare for him, but We made them the greater losers.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Jurayj):** the parallel at 21:70 confirms the reversal, and the two verses together establish that the schemers' plan produced the opposite of its intention.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:99

> **And he said, “Truly I am going unto my Lord. He will guide me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fear Not of False Gods and the Trust in God’s Will**

Abraham says: “I do not fear those whom you associate with Him” — he does not fear the idols, the people who worship them, or the social pressure to conform. His fear is not of human power but of God’s will: “except that my Lord should will something” — he fears only that God might decree something, and he accepts that decree with trust.

This is the balance of tawḥīd: freedom from fear of false powers, combined with deep reverence for God’s will. Abraham is not arrogant; he does not claim immunity from God’s judgment. He simply says that the false gods have no power over him, and God’s knowledge encompasses everything.

> **Cross-Reference:** 5:44 — “Indeed, God is swift in taking account.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham fears not the idols but God’s will; he trusts God’s knowledge and accepts His decree, which is the perfect balance of courage and humility.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:100

> **My Lord, give unto me from among the righteous.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Give Unto Me from Among the Righteous**

The verse records Abraham's prayer, and the commentators note that it is understood by most to be a prayer for a righteous son.

The commentators preserve an alternative reading in which it is a prayer asking God to allow Abraham himself to be among the righteous.

The two readings differ in the object of the request. On the majority reading Abraham asks for a righteous child; on the alternative he asks for his own inclusion among the righteous.

The alternative is theologically significant, since it presents even a prophet as requesting inclusion among the righteous rather than assuming it.

The commentators record both without preferring either, and the preservation leaves the prayer's object open.

**Psychological Insight:** The minority reading identifies a distinctive humility — the one who has just been called to sacrifice everything asks not for reward but to be counted among the righteous.

> **Cross-Reference:** 26:83 — *My Lord! Vouchsafe me wisdom, and join me with the righteous.*

> **Classical View:** the parallel at 26:83 supports the alternative reading, since there Abraham explicitly asks to be joined with the righteous rather than asking for a son.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:101

> **So We gave him glad tidings of a gentle son.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Glad Tidings of a Gentle Son**

The verse records the answer to Abraham's prayer, and Ibn Jurayj and al-Ṭabarī note that the son announced here is understood to be either Isaac or Ishmael.

The commentators preserve both identifications without settling between them, and the ambiguity matters for the account that follows at verse 102.

The commentators also observe a distinction in the manner of the announcement: here it is God who gives Abraham glad tidings, whereas in all the other accounts angels come to him with glad tidings of a son — see 11:69c, 15:53, 29:31, and 51:28.

They resolve the difference by noting that together these verses indicate God gave the glad tidings through the intermediary of the angels, so the two formulations describe one event at different levels.

The description of the son as *gentle* is what the following verse develops, since the son's response to the command of sacrifice is given in terms of patience.

**Modern Analogy:** A report attributed directly to a principal in one account and to his messengers in another — the tradition reconciles them rather than choosing.

> **Cross-Reference:** 51:28 — *So they came unto him and said: Peace! He said: Peace! A people unknown. Then he turned unto his household and brought a fatted calf.*

> **Classical View (Ibn Jurayj):** the identification of the son is left open in the tradition, and the commentators decline to resolve it here, which is why the sacrifice narrative that follows remains unnamed.

The phrase “I am not of those who associate others with God” is repeated for emphasis: Abraham’s identity is defined by what he rejects as much as by what he accepts. He is not just a submitter; he is a rejecter of false gods.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:103

> **But when they had submitted and Abraham had laid him upon his forehead,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Persistent Argument and the Unyielding Response**

The people argue again (jādalūhu): they do not accept Abraham’s reasoning, they do not follow his evidence, and they do not turn to God. Abraham responds with the same question: “Have you argued with me concerning God, and He has guided me?” The repetition shows that Abraham does not change his message; he does not compromise; he does not adapt to their rejection.

This is the strength of the prophet: not in convincing everyone, but in remaining faithful to the message regardless of response. Abraham knows that his duty is to deliver, not to force acceptance.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:68 — “So they said, ‘You have known that these do not speak.’”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:104

> **We called unto him, “O Abraham!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Continuation of Abraham’s Courage and Trust**

The verse repeats the statement from v. 99: Abraham does not fear those associated with God; he fears only God’s will. The repetition confirms that this is not a temporary feeling but a permanent state—a consistent, settled confidence that comes from knowing God’s knowledge and sovereignty.

The phrase “My Lord encompasses all things in knowledge” (wa-rabbī bi-kulli shayʾin muḥīṭ) is a powerful statement of divine omniscience: God knows everything—past, present, future, visible, hidden. Abraham trusts this knowledge, knowing that God will not abandon him or allow him to be defeated.

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:105

> **Thou hast been true to the vision.” Thus indeed do We recompense the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish for Fear of God and the Status of Protection**

Abraham expresses a wish: “Would that you had feared God” (law kuntum taddaʿūn) — if only you had been aware of God, if only you had submitted, if only you had chosen truth over tradition. The word law (if) expresses regret, not accusation: Abraham wishes they had made a different choice.

“Or indeed you had been of the protected” (aw kuntum min al-muḥ'sinīn) — he wishes they had been among those who do good, who are sincere, and who are protected by God’s mercy. The wish shows Abraham’s compassion: he does not want them destroyed; he wants them saved.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:69 — “Indeed, We have done thus to the doers of good.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham wishes they had feared God; his wish is for their salvation, not their destruction, showing his compassion even in rejection.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:106

> **Truly this was the manifest trial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dispute About the Sacrifice and Abraham’s Submission**

The verse describes the dispute (jabaramūhu) about the command to sacrifice his son: they argue, they resist, they ask questions. Abraham responds: “Indeed, I am of those who have submitted to God” — he is not debating; he is obeying. His submission is absolute: he does not ask for exceptions, he does not seek comfort, he does not demand explanation. He simply submits.

This is the pinnacle of faith: not just belief in God, but obedience to His command even when it seems impossible, painful, or irrational. Abraham’s submission is a model for all believers: true faith is tested by willingness to obey.

> **Cross-Reference:** 37:102 — Abraham’s declaration of submission.
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “Abraham disputes no more; he submits fully to God’s command, because his faith is complete and his heart is sound.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:107

> **Then We ransomed him with a great sacrifice.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fulfillment of the Vision and the Reward of Excellence**

The verse describes the moment of sacrifice: “Then We said to him, ‘O Abraham, indeed you have fulfilled the vision’” — God confirms that Abraham has completed the test. The word ruʾyā (vision, dream) refers to the command Abraham received in a dream to sacrifice his son; now Abraham has fulfilled it by preparing to obey.

The phrase “Truly thus do We reward the doers of good” confirms that Abraham’s excellence is recognized and rewarded. His obedience is not lost; it is transformed into a permanent blessing—both for him and for his descendants, and as a lesson for all humanity.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:127 — “And when We made the House a place of return for the people and [said], ‘Take, [from the examples], the station of Abraham.’”
> **Classical View (al-Ṭabarī):** “God confirms that Abraham fulfilled the vision; he is rewarded because he acted with excellence, not because he completed the sacrifice physically, but because he was ready to obey fully.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:108

> **And We left [a blessing] upon him among later generations:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Clear Trial and Its Meaning for All Believers**

The verse confirms: “Indeed, that was the clear trial” (inna hādhā la-huwa al-baḥth al-mubīn). The word baḥth means trial, test, examination—something that reveals the true nature of a person. Abraham’s trial was “clear” because it was direct, unambiguous, and demanding: he was asked to sacrifice the son he loved most.

The trial is not punishment but revelation: it reveals Abraham’s sincerity, his love for God over everything else, and his willingness to obey without understanding. For the believer, this trial is a model: faith is not just belief but readiness to obey when the command is difficult.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:124 — “And [remember] when His Lord tested Abraham with words, and he fulfilled them.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “The trial was clear because Abraham knew exactly what was required; he did not hesitate because his faith was complete.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:109

> **“Peace be upon Abraham.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ransom and the Substitute and the Mercy of God**

The verse describes the resolution: “And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice” (wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm). The word fadanā means to ransom, to replace, to substitute—God provided a substitute for the son. The “great sacrifice” (dhibḥ ʿaẓīm) is a large, valuable animal—usually understood as a ram—whose sacrifice fulfilled the command and saved the son.

This is the mercy of God: He does not require the destruction of the beloved; He requires the willingness to obey. When the willingness is complete, He provides the substitute, and the son is saved. The believer learns that God’s commands are not burdens but tests of willingness, and His mercy is greater than His trial.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:260 — “So We said, ‘Take four birds...’”; 37:107 — confirmation of fulfillment.
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr):** “God ransomed Isaac with a ram because Abraham was ready to obey; God does not require the destruction of the son when the heart is sincere.”

**The Lasting Peace and the Memory of Abraham**

His title is not only prophet but “friend of God” (khalīl Allāh), and his peace is a permanent blessing.

The phrase “among the later generations” (fī l-ākhirīn) emphasizes continuity: Abraham is not forgotten; he is remembered, honored, and followed by every generation that recognizes his truth. His legacy is not just a story but a model—of reason, submission, patience, and trust.

> **Cross-Reference:** 4:125 — “And God took Abraham as a close friend.”

**The Gift of Isaac and Jacob and the Continuation of Prophethood**

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:114

> **And We gave him Moses and Aaron as a gift,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moses and Aaron as a Gift and the Continuation of Guidance**

The verse describes God’s gift to Abraham’s descendants: “We gave him Moses and Aaron” — Moses (Mūsā) as a prophet and messenger, Aaron (Hārūn) as his helper and speaker. The word wahabnā confirms divine generosity: God does not just preserve the line; He elevates it with new prophets of exceptional importance.

**The Rescue of Moses and His People and the Power of Divine Protection**

**The Victory of the Believers and the Defeat of the Oppressors**

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:117

The verse states: “We made for him a covenant with his descendants” — God establishes a covenant not only with Abraham but also with his descendants: Isaac, Jacob, Moses, and all who follow. The covenant is inherited; it passes from generation to generation, not by blood alone but by faith and obedience.

This is the continuity of divine guidance: God does not abandon a family that remains sincere. The covenant is a promise that those who follow Abraham’s path will be guided, protected, and rewarded.

> **Cross-Reference:** 14:40 — “Our Lord, and grant us from among our wives and descendants those who are pleasing to You, and make us an example for the righteous.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr): “The covenant with descendants is a promise of continuity; those who follow Abraham’s path receive God’s guidance and protection.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:179

> **and observe; for they will soon observe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guidance of Isaac, Jacob, and All Descendants**

The verse lists the descendants: “We gave him Isaac and Jacob—all [of them] We guided.” The word “all” (kull) confirms that every descendant is guided—not only some, not only the famous, but all who choose to follow. This is the universality of God’s guidance: it is offered to all, and those who accept receive it.

The guidance is not just external (revelation, scripture) but internal (spirit, sincerity, purity of heart). God guides those who seek Him; those who reject are left to their own choices.

> **Cross-Reference:** 6:87 — “And We guided before him a people, and he was of those who are guided.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr): “All descendants of Abraham who follow him are guided; the guidance is not limited but extends to all who choose it.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:180

> **Glory be to thy Lord, the Lord of Might, above that which they ascribe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noah’s Guidance Before Abraham and the Continuity of Prophethood**

The verse confirms: “And Noah, We guided before” — Noah is included in the line, confirming that the covenant and guidance begin with Noah and continue through Abraham. The word “before” (min qablu) emphasizes that Noah’s guidance is earlier, establishing the foundation of monotheism before Abraham.

This continuity is important: God does not begin guidance with Abraham; He continues it from Noah, through Abraham, through Isaac, through Jacob, through Moses, through Jesus, and through Muhammad. The message is one, continuous, and unbroken.

> **Cross-Reference:** 21:76 — “And We saved him and his family from the great distress.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr): “Noah is guided before Abraham; this confirms the continuity of prophecy from the beginning of humanity to the final messenger.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:181

> **Peace be upon the message bearers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Prophets Among Abraham’s Descendants**

The verse lists the great prophets: David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, Aaron. Each is a major figure in history, each receives revelation, each guides people, and each is part of the continuous chain. The list is not exhaustive—it includes the most prominent—but it confirms that Abraham’s descendants include some of the greatest messengers in history.

This is a reminder to the believer: the covenant of Abraham is not small or limited; it includes the greatest prophets, the most important revelations, and the most powerful guidance. The believer is part of this heritage.

> **Cross-Reference:** 6:84 — “And We made from his descendants prophets and messengers...”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr): “The list includes the greatest prophets; this confirms that the covenant is not minor but includes all major guidance from God.”

## Sūrah al-Ṣaffāt 37:182

> **And praise be to God, Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Preference of Some Prophets and the Reward of All Good Doers**

The final verse of the sūrah: “Indeed, We have preferred some of them over others; but some are of those who have done good.” God has preferred some prophets over others—not because of their personal importance, but according to divine wisdom, the needs of the time, and the nature of their missions. Some received more revelation, some had greater miracles, some were given more authority.

But “some are of those who have done good” — all prophets are good, all are sincere, and all are rewarded according to their actions. The preference is not a dismissal; it is recognition that different missions require different strengths.

**The Conclusion of the Sūrah:** Al-Ṣaffāt ends with this reminder: God’s guidance is continuous, His covenant is eternal, His prophets are honored, and those who do good are rewarded. The believer should see themselves as part of this chain—receiving the message, following the guide, and trusting in God’s promise of salvation.

> **Cross-Reference:** 2:253 — “Those are the messengers; We have preferred some of them over others; to some God has spoken directly, and He has raised others in degrees.”
> **Classical View (Ibn Kathīr): “God has preferred some prophets over others according to wisdom; but all who have done good are rewarded; this is the final lesson of the sūrah: follow the path, trust the covenant, and do good.”
