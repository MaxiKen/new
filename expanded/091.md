# Sūrah al-Shams (Chapter 91) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Built Entirely of Oaths**

Sūrah al-Shams, "The Sun," is a short Makkan sūrah of fifteen verses, taking its name from the first word of the revelation: *wa'l-shams* — "By the sun." It belongs to the early Makkan period, when the Qur'an's address was compact, rhythmic, and aimed directly at the heart of a society that already knew the sun, the moon, the day, and the night as facts of its daily sky but had not yet been made to read them as evidence. The most cited opinion among the historians of revelation places it shortly after Sūrah al-Qadr (97) in the order of descent, which is to say it comes from the period when the Prophet ﷺ's recitation was still startling Mecca with short, oath-laden, picture-perfect chapters that ended on the destinies of destroyed nations. Its entire architecture is an oath: the first seven verses are a chain of no fewer than eleven things sworn by — the sun, its morning brightness, the moon, the day, the night, the sky and its building, the earth and its spreading, and the human soul with its fashioning — and the last five verses supply the weight that the oaths carry. No other sūrah of the Qur'an is so single-mindedly built from the grammar of swearing.

**The Grammar of the Oath and Why It Opens Here**

In Arabic rhetoric, an oath (*qasam*) has two limbs. The first is *al-muqsam bihi*, the thing sworn by; the second is *al-muqsam ʿalayhi*, the claim sworn about — the answer (*jawāb al-qasam*) that the oath guarantees. The classical commentators observed that this sūrah stretches the distance between the two limbs to its fullest extent: eleven created witnesses are called to the stand, one after another, before the claim is delivered. That delay is deliberate. Every added oath builds anticipation, and every sworn thing is itself a piece of the argument. When the answer finally arrives — *"Indeed, he prospers who purifies it; and indeed he fails who obscures it"* (91:9–10) — it lands on a reader who has been prepared, scene by scene, through the whole visible cosmos, to accept that the same polarity of light and darkness, disclosure and concealment, that governs the sky also governs the human soul. The sūrah, in other words, teaches its central doctrine by the structure of its own sentences.

**Eleven Witnesses in Seven Verses**

The commentators differ slightly on how to count, but the dominant reckoning finds eleven sworn things: the sun (v. 1); its morning brightness (v. 1); the moon (v. 2); the day (v. 3); the night (v. 4); the sky (v. 5); its being established (v. 5); the earth (v. 6); its being spread out (v. 6); the soul (v. 7); and its being fashioned proportionately (v. 7). Look at what God chose to swear by. He did not swear by the Kaʿbah, by a prophet, by a mountain, or by any object of human pride; He swore by the most ordinary and most universal phenomena of existence — the daily cycle of light and dark, the two luminaries, the two great planes of heaven and earth, and the one creature on whom the whole argument turns. The list moves in a deliberate order: from the greatest single light (the sun), to the light that follows it (the moon), to the day that discloses, to the night that covers, to the sky that stands above, to the earth that spreads below, and finally inward — to the human soul, the meeting point of all these opposites. The whole sweep of creation is gathered as evidence in seven breaths, and then the gaze is turned from the horizon to the interior.

**The Cosmos as a Set of Paired Opposites**

Everything in the first half of the sūrah comes in pairs: sun and moon, day and night, sky and earth, disclosure and enshrouding, spreading and building. This is not decoration. The Makkan Qur'an repeatedly points to polarity in the created world as the clearest signature of a single wise Maker — the same argument we find in the paired opposites of Sūrah al-Layl (92:1–4) and in the rhetorical questions of Sūrah 90: *"Did We not make for him two eyes, and a tongue, and two lips, and guide him upon the two highways?"* (90:8–10). A world built from balanced opposites cannot be self-made, because opposites do not organize themselves; they need an organizer who holds both ends. And the sūrah's deeper point is that this polarity is not only *out there* in the sky; it is reproduced *inside* every human being. The soul is the place where the two highways of 90:10 meet. The external cosmos is, in this sense, a mirror held up to the internal one: just as the day discloses what the night concealed, the purified soul discloses what heedlessness buries, and just as the moon borrows the sun's light, the soul borrows its light from its Maker.

**The Soul Between Iniquity and Reverence**

The hinge of the entire sūrah is the pair of verses on the soul: *"by the soul and the One Who fashioned it, and inspired it as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent"* (91:7–8). Here the Qur'an states, in the most compressed possible form, its entire anthropology. The human soul is not a blank slate awaiting external instruction; it arrives *fashioned* — proportioned, balanced, made upright in its primordial nature (*fiṭrah*) — and *inspired*, taught from within, the two directions that lie open to it: its capacity for iniquity (*fujūr*) and its capacity for reverence (*taqwā*). Both are known to the soul before any prophet or book arrives. The Creator did not leave the soul ignorant and then blame it; He built into it the very instrument — a discriminating moral conscience — by which it can be held responsible. This is why the next two verses can state success and failure so plainly: the material of the test is already installed. What remains is what the human being *does* with it — whether he grows the soul in purity or buries it in corruption.

**The Answer the Oaths Carry**

Verses 9 and 10 are the *jawāb al-qasam*, the point the eleven oaths were sworn to secure: *qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā, wa qad khāba man dassāhā* — "indeed he prospers who purifies it, and indeed he fails who obscures it." The sūrah's whole cosmology collapses into this single moral equation. Notice the economy: the universe is not described for its own sake but as the scaffolding of one decision. The sun, moon, day, night, sky, and earth all point to this — that the human soul, placed between disclosure and concealment, is either purified into its native brightness or buried under the darkness it permits. Some classical readers took the verbs of verses 9 and 10 as God's action ("he whose soul God purifies prospers"); others took them as man's action ("he who purifies his own soul prospers"). Both readings are true at different levels, and the sūrah deliberately leaves the grammatical door open — because, as the commentary on those verses will show, purification in the Qur'an is precisely the meeting of divine grace and human striving, each real, neither canceling the other.

**The Example of Thamūd: Purification Rejected**

The last five verses supply the concrete proof of the equation, drawn from the well-known story of Thamūd, the northern Arabian tribe that rejected the prophet Ṣāliḥ. The source commentary notes that this story appears in two of the earliest Makkan revelations (here and in 54:23–31) and is later expanded in the Madinan sūrahs (7:73–80; 11:61–68), which shows how deeply the tale was embedded in Arab memory. Thamūd did not fail for lack of clarity: a living sign — the she-camel of God, brought forth from the rock — was set before them, and they were told, in a single sentence, the terms of coexistence: *"the she-camel of God; give her drink"* (91:13). Their answer was to hamstring the sign itself, preferring the darkness they knew to the obligation the sign imposed. The sūrah's verdict is that this is what "obscuring the soul" looks like when it matures into a community's policy. And the closing verse — *"for He fears not the consequence thereof"* (91:15) — seals the lesson with the attribute that distinguishes the divine reckoning from every human one: God punishes without anxiety about consequences, because His judgment needs no justification before any higher court. The destroyer of Thamūd does not look over His shoulder.

**Why This Sūrah Is Recited and How It Was Prayed**

The early community treasured this sūrah not only as doctrine but as prayer. The most famous report attached to it concerns verse 8: it is transmitted that when the Prophet ﷺ recited the sūrah and reached the verse about the soul's iniquity and reverence, he would pause and supplicate, in the wording preserved by Muslim:

> *"O God, grant my soul its reverence (taqwāhā), and purify it — for You are the best of those who purify it; You are its Guardian and its Master."* (Muslim 2722)

The sūrah, that is, was read as a petition for the very thing it teaches — a recognition that purification is ultimately a gift to be asked for, even as it is a task to be performed. What follows treats each of the fifteen verses as an independent discourse, in strict order, separating the verses that the source commentary groups (5–7, 9–10, and 11–14) and giving every verse the full treatment: Arabic language, the classical commentators, the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the logic of the teaching, and its bearing upon the modern human being who still wakes each day beneath the same sun the sūrah swore by.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:1

> **By the sun and its morning brightness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Begins with the Greatest Visible Light**

The sūrah opens with the oath *wa'l-shamsi wa ḍuḥāhā* — "By the sun and its morning brightness." The particle *wāw* here is the *wāw al-qasam*, the oath-particle, and the sun is the first of the eleven created things God calls to witness. The choice of the sun to stand first is not accidental. Among everything a desert-dwelling Arab saw in his sky, the sun was the most overwhelming fact: the source of light, heat, growth, the measurer of the day, the clock of all labor, the unerring riser and setter whose course had never once failed since the world began. To open a chapter with the sun is to open it with the single most reliable and most beneficial object in the entire visible universe — and then to say that this object is a *witness*, not a god. The pre-Islamic Arabs, like many ancient peoples, had been tempted to divinize exactly this: the Qur'an elsewhere reports the self-destruction of a people who took the sun as lord (cf. the story of Abraham in 6:76–79, where he watches a star, the moon, and the sun rise, and rejects each in turn). The very first oath of this sūrah is therefore a quiet act of monotheism: the sun is summoned to testify, and in being summoned it is demoted from deity to evidence.

**Why God May Swear by His Creation — and We May Not**

The classical commentators pause here over a question the structure raises: may the Creator swear by His creatures? Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record the established answer. God is free to swear by whatever He wills of His creation, and His swearing by a thing is an act of honoring it and of drawing His servants' attention to its sign-value. But the creature is not free to swear by anything but God. The Prophet ﷺ drew the boundary in a saying preserved in the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"Whoever swears by other than God has committed shirk (association)."* (recorded by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd 3251, and al-Tirmidhī 1535, who graded it ḥasan; its meaning is corroborated by Muslim's wording from Ibn ʿUmar, "Whoever swears by other than God has disbelieved or associated partners")

The logic is precise: to swear by a thing is to invest it with the dignity of a guarantor of truth, and only God holds that office. When God swears by the sun, He is not honoring a rival; He is indicating to us that this creature is so evidently His work that it can stand as His own witness. The creature may look at the sun and say, "How great is the One who made it"; he may not look at it and say, "By it I swear." What is permitted to the Master is not permitted to the servant, because the servant's oath would be worship, while the Master's oath is instruction.

**The Word Ḍuḥā: More Than "Morning"**

The second sworn thing is *ḍuḥāhā* — "its morning brightness." The noun *ḍuḥā* in Arabic denotes the specific time after the sun has fully risen, when its light has spread and its heat has begun to pour out — the bright forenoon, as opposed to dawn (*fajr*), which is the first glimmer before sunrise, and to midday (*ẓuhr*), when the sun has passed its peak. The root sense of *ḍ-ḥ-w* carries the idea of becoming manifest and spreading out: the same root gives *ḍaḥā* in verse 6 of this sūrah (the spreading of the earth) and in 79:30. So *ḍuḥāhā* is not merely the sun but the sun in its characteristic act — the moment when its light fully discloses the world and its heat brings the earth alive. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse could have simply said "by the sun"; the addition of *ḍuḥāhā* names the sun's most beneficent and most glorious state, just as a person might swear by a king "and his generosity." It is the sun at the peak of its giving, not the sun at the edge of its rising or setting.

**The Sun as a Sign That Never Fails**

Why does the sun deserve to be an oath-witness? Because it is the created world's most perfect example of *constancy in service*. The Qur'an repeatedly appeals to the sun's unerring course as evidence of a governing will:

> *"And the sun runs its course to a fixed resting place; that is the decree of the Mighty, the Knowing."* (Qur'an 36:38)

> *"He it is Who made the sun a radiance and the moon a light, and measured out for it stations, so that you may know the number of the years and the reckoning."* (Qur'an 10:5)

The sun has risen, in obedience, billions of times without a single day's delay or a single degree's error. No human institution can claim a thousandth of that fidelity. The logical force of the oath is this: a creature so constant, so beneficial, and so beyond human control cannot be accidental, and the One who holds it to its course is the same One who will hold every human soul to its accounting. The sun is, in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, an *āyah* — a sign — and a sign points beyond itself. The same sūrah family makes the point negatively, warning against confusing the sign with the signified:

> *"And of His signs are the night and the day, and the sun and the moon. Do not prostrate to the sun or to the moon, but prostrate to God, Who created them, if it is Him you worship."* (Qur'an 41:37)

**The Sun Prostrates: A Prophetic Glimpse of Its Station**

The Prophet ﷺ gave his Companions a glimpse of what the sun's "obedience" means at the level of the unseen, in the long and authentic account of its daily prostration, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Dharr:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ said to Abū Dharr when the sun had set: 'Do you know where this goes?' He said: 'God and His Messenger know best.' He said: 'It goes until it prostrates beneath the Throne and asks permission, and permission is granted it; and soon it will prostrate and not be granted permission, and will ask permission and not be granted it — and it will be said: "Return from whence you came." So it will rise from its setting place.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3199; Muslim 159)

Two truths stand inside this report. First, the sun that seems to move by blind physics is, in reality, a creature performing an act of worship — its "course" is a prostration, its constancy a form of devotion. Second, even the sun's obedience will one day end, when its permission to rise is withdrawn and it rises from the west — one of the great signs of the approaching Hour, as the Prophet ﷺ taught elsewhere (Muslim 2941). And in the same register, al-Bukhārī records that on the Day of Resurrection the sun and moon themselves will be folded up:

> *"The sun and the moon will be rolled up (yukawwarān) on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 3203; cf. Muslim 2791 and Qur'an 81:1, "When the sun is rolled up")

The object of the oath is thus doubly instructive: it is the most dependable thing in the sky, and even it is mortal. If the sun's term is written, how much more is the term of every soul that walks beneath it. The oath by the sun is, hidden inside itself, an oath by the very impermanence that forces the question of the Hereafter.

**The Morning Brightness as Daily Resurrection**

There is a further reason the sūrah pairs the sun with its *ḍuḥā*. Every dawn is a miniature resurrection: the world dies each night into darkness and cold, and is brought back each morning into light and warmth. The Qur'an builds its central argument for bodily resurrection on exactly this daily, observable miracle — the revival of the dead earth:

> *"And you see the earth lifeless; then when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every kind of lovely plant."* (Qur'an 22:5)

The sun's morning brightness is the light-half of that same daily resurrection, and Sūrah 93 opens with precisely this pair — *"By the morning brightness, and by the night when it is still"* (93:1–2) — as comfort to a Prophet who had felt the darkness of revelation's pause. For the ordinary believer, the oath carries a quiet practical discipline: to witness the sunrise is to witness a promise kept. It is the Creator honoring a schedule with His whole creation, and the soul that begins its day by noticing this has begun its day with gratitude rather than with the half-conscious rush that forgets the sky. The sun has never missed a single morning; the human being, by contrast, forgets the Giver of the morning before the tea has cooled. The sūrah begins, then, by placing before the reader the most faithful servant of light in all creation — as a witness, a model, and a question.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:2

> **By the moon when following it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Witness: Light That Follows Light**

The second oath is *wa'l-qamari idhā talāhā* — "by the moon when it follows it." The pronoun *it* (the feminine suffix *-hā*) refers back to the sun, and the verb *talā* means "followed, came after, succeeded." Two readings of this "following" are recorded among the commentators, and both are true at their own level. The first, transmitted by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, is astronomical: the moon follows the sun in its circuit across the sky, and — at the start of each lunar month — the newborn crescent appears in the western sky near the setting sun and then sets shortly after it, as though walking in the sun's footsteps. The second, also from the classical commentaries, is luminous: the moon *follows* the sun in the sense of receiving its light, so that what the moon shines with at night is, in reality, borrowed sunlight. Either way, the moon is presented as the sun's follower — the second light, the reflected light, the light that waits on the greater light.

**A Borrowed Light and a Precise Distinction**

The second reading deserves careful attention, because the Qur'an's own vocabulary for the two luminaries is exact and deliberate. In 25:61 God is described as the One who *"made in the sky constellations and made therein a lamp (sirāj) and a shining moon (qamar munīr)"*; in 71:16 the moon is called *"a light (nūr)"* while the sun is *"a lamp (sirāj)"*; and in 10:5 the sun is *"a radiance (ḍiyāʾ)"* and the moon *"a light (nūr)."* The classical commentators read this distinction as precise science in theological idiom: the sun is a *lamp* — a source that burns with its own light — while the moon is a *light* — a body that *has* light without being its origin. The moon, in other words, receives and transmits; the sun originates and gives. This is why the verse says the moon *follows* the sun: its entire career is derived. What a modern astronomer would say about reflected sunlight and phases, the Qur'an had already said fourteen centuries earlier in two words of a single oath.

**The Crescent, the Calendar, and the Measuring of Time**

The first reading — the moon following the sun as the crescent follows the setting sun — leads directly to the moon's appointed function in human life. The Qur'an states it plainly:

> *"They ask you about the new moons. Say: They are appointed times for the people and for the pilgrimage."* (Qur'an 2:189)

> *"And the moon — We have determined for it phases, until it returns like an old date stalk."* (Qur'an 36:39)

The moon is the sky's calendar. Its monthly death and rebirth, its waxing from a hairline crescent to a full disc and its waning back to nothing, give human beings a way of counting time that the sun alone does not supply. The sun measures the day and the year; the moon measures the month. Between them — the day-counting sun and the month-counting moon — the entire architecture of human time is drawn from the two sworn witnesses of these opening verses. And the sūrah's later oaths by the day and the night complete the set: the *day* discloses, the *night* enshrouds, the *sun* rules the one, the *moon* adorns the other. The whole of created time is gathered into four oaths.

**"It Is Not for the Sun to Overtake the Moon"**

The relationship of sun and moon is governed by a further Qur'anic statement that the commentators bring to this verse:

> *"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor for the night to outstrip the day; each, in an orbit, is swimming."* (Qur'an 36:40)

Here the point is order without collision. Two great bodies cross the same sky, the one by day and the other by night, each on its own course, neither overstepping the other — and this mutual non-encroachment, sustained across all of recorded time, is itself the signature of a single director. Al-Rāzī makes the logical point explicit: two independent movers, left to themselves, would eventually collide or diverge; the fact that the sun and moon keep perfect, non-conflicting appointments is evidence that a single will assigns both their stations. The moon's "following" is therefore not servile imitation but disciplined coordination — a follower that never errs in following.

**The Moon as the Believer's Model: Reflection of Prophetic Light**

The spiritual commentators drew from this verse an analogy that has become part of the tradition's devotional language. The Prophet ﷺ is the sun — the source to whom revelation descends directly — and the community of believers is the moon, which has no light of its own but takes the Prophet's light and carries it through the darkness of the world's night. Where the sun has set, the moon still lights the way; so, after the Prophet's ﷺ departure from this world, his community carries his guidance to those who come after. The analogy rests on the verse's own grammar: the moon's glory consists precisely in *following*. It does not compete with the sun; it does not claim the sun's station; it does not pretend its borrowed light is self-made. Its entire excellence is receptivity and transmission. A believer, likewise, is not asked to originate light but to receive it — from the Book, from the sunnah, from the purified soul's own fitrah — and to reflect it faithfully into whatever night he happens to occupy. The moon teaches that reflected light is still real light; it illumines the traveler even though its source is elsewhere.

**The Followers of Light and the Followers of the World**

Finally, the verse's single verb *talā* — "followed" — reaches forward, in the sūrah's own logic, to the human drama of the later verses. Just as the moon's whole fate depends on *what it follows*, the human soul's whole fate depends on what it follows. The soul can follow the light — its fitrah, its prophets, its purification — and become luminous in its measure, or it can follow its lower impulses and be buried (the very word *dassāhā*, "obscured it," of verse 10, connotes concealment beneath the surface). The moon is offered at the head of the sūrah, then, not only as a witness but as an example: the blessed condition of every created thing is to follow its appointed light, and the ruin of every soul is to refuse that following. The night sky itself — sun, moon, day, night, in their mutual following — is a visible parable of the choice the sūrah is about to place before the human being.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:3

> **By the day when disclosing it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Witness: Disclosure in Broad Daylight**

The third oath is *wa'l-nahāri idhā jallāhā* — "by the day when it discloses it." The noun *al-nahār* is the day in its active, lit condition — the span between sunrise and sunset when the world is exposed to sight — as distinct from *yawm*, the twenty-four-hour unit, and from *ḍuḥā*, the forenoon brightness already sworn by in verse 1. The verb *jallā* (from the root *j-l-w*, "to make clear, to reveal, to polish until it shines") is the same verb used for removing a veil or burnishing a mirror: the day does not merely *have* light; it *discloses*, it brings things out into the open. The feminine suffix *-hā* (translated "it") is read by the commentators in three ways, each recorded by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī. It may refer to the sun — the day discloses the sun, revealing it in its fullness after the night had hidden it. It may refer to the earth or the world — the day discloses the world, laying bare every hill, road, and creature that the night had swallowed. Or it may refer to *the darkness itself* — in which case the meaning becomes "by the day when it disperses the darkness," the light scattering the night's cover. All three readings point the same direction: the day is the great revealer, the force that turns concealment into exposure.

**The Qur'an's Portrait of the Day as an Act of God**

The Qur'an never lets the day pass as a mere fact of astronomy; it presents the day's disclosure as a continuous divine act. The night is described as something God *strips* away to let the day through:

> *"And a sign for them is the night: We strip the day from it — and behold, they are in darkness."* (Qur'an 36:37)

And the whole alternation of night and day is named as a mercy and a means of livelihood:

> *"And We made the day for livelihood."* (Qur'an 78:11)

> *"Say: Do you see — if God made the night perpetual over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than God could bring you light? Will you not then hear?"* (Qur'an 28:71)

The last verse is the sūrah's own method in the interrogative mood: the day is not yours to command. You cannot summon it a minute early or hold it a minute longer. Its arrival, every single morning, is a fresh gift and a fresh argument — the Most Generous handing the world back its visibility. A human being who has ever spent a sleepless, dark night knows in his bones what disclosure means; the Qur'an is simply asking him to notice who performs it.

**Parallel Oaths: The Day in Sūrah al-Layl**

This verse has a direct twin in the very next sūrah, and the commentators place them side by side:

> *"By the night when it enshrouds; and by the day when it discloses."* (Qur'an 92:1–2)

There, as here, the pair is built of opposites — night and day, covering and disclosing — and there, as here, the pair is the scaffold for the sūrah's central contrast about human striving and its divergent ends (92:4–10). The recurrence of the identical image in two adjacent sūrahs tells us something about how the early revelation taught: it returned to the same cosmic vocabulary again and again, not because it had nothing new to say, but because these were the pictures a first hearer could never forget. Sun, moon, day, night — the four sides of the sky — became a moral alphabet, and once a hearer had learned to read light and dark as disclosure and concealment, he was ready to read his own soul the same way.

**Disclosure as the Law of Moral Life**

This is precisely the move the sūrah is making, and it is the reason the day stands third in the oath-chain. The whole chapter is a study in the ethics of light: what the sun and moon are in the sky, purification and heedlessness are in the soul. The day's *disclosure* has its exact moral counterpart in the purified soul's openness. Just as the day exposes what the night hid, sincerity exposes what hypocrisy buries; truthfulness exposes what falsehood conceals; and the purified soul lives in the light, with nothing it needs to hide, while the corrupted soul — the "buried" soul of verse 10 — lives in concealment, dreading disclosure. The Qur'an states the coming exposure as the very definition of the Last Day, whose other name is *yawm al-tanād*, the day of outcry, and of which it says:

> *"On that Day you will be exposed — not a secret of yours will remain hidden."* (Qur'an 69:18)

The believer who swears by the day is, therefore, being trained to *live as though already in the light*: to conduct himself so that, when the final disclosure comes, it will reveal nothing he is ashamed to have known. The sūrah's oath by the disclosing day is a rehearsal of the Day of Disclosure.

**The Spiritual Reading: Faith as the Soul's Sunrise**

The spiritual commentators, following the sūrah's own logic, read the day's disclosure of the sun as a figure for the light of faith disclosing what ignorance had darkened. Faith is to the soul what daylight is to the landscape: it does not create the truth; it reveals it — the truth of God, of human nature, of the soul's purpose, which had been there all along but invisible in the dark. Ignorance is not a condition of having no world; it is the condition of standing in a world one cannot see. When faith enters, the same life that seemed meaningless is disclosed as full of signs, the same duties that seemed arbitrary are disclosed as mercy, and the same soul that seemed a chaos of impulses is disclosed as a fashioned and inspired creation with a known path. This is why the Qur'an can say of the one it guides:

> *"Is one whose breast God has opened to Islam, so that he is upon a light from his Lord [like one who is not]?"* (Qur'an 39:22)

And the same image runs backward: the Prophet ﷺ described each new day's dawn as the world's daily opening — an invitation to begin again with gratitude, prayer, and disclosure rather than concealment. The day that discloses is, finally, a daily mercy and a daily summons: whatever the night hid, the day will show; whatever the soul buries, the Day of Judgment will show. The sūrah swears by both, back to back, so that no hearer can claim the darkness will last.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:4

> **By the night when enshrouding it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Witness: The Night That Covers**

The fourth oath completes the pair begun in verse 3: *wa'l-layli idhā yaghshāhā* — "by the night when it enshrouds it." The verb is *yaghshā* (from the root *gh-sh-y*), meaning "to cover, envelop, spread over, conceal." It is the same verb the Qur'an uses for the terror that will "cover" faces on the Day of Judgment and for the darkness that "covers" the one who drowns in the sea's waves. Here its object is, again, read three ways: the night enshrouds the sun, hiding the great light; or the night enshrouds the earth, wrapping the world in its cover; or the night enshrouds the light generally, extinguishing visibility. The image is one of a garment thrown over the world — the Qur'an's own word for the night elsewhere is *libās* (a garment) and *subāt* (a cutting-off, rest): *"And We made the night a garment, and made the day for livelihood"* (78:10–11), *"And We made your sleep a rest, and the night a garment"* (78:9–10). The night is not the day's enemy in the Qur'an; it is the day's counterpart, the covering under which creation rests and from which the day's disclosure will again emerge.

**The Twin Oath in the Next Sūrah**

As with the day, the night has its exact twin in Sūrah al-Layl, which opens:

> *"By the night when it enshrouds (idhā yaghshā); and by the day when it discloses."* (Qur'an 92:1–2)

The two sūrahs thus share the same opening picture, and the commentators observe that in both, the night-and-day pair is made to serve a moral contrast. In Sūrah al-Layl the contrast is between the giver and the withholder, the reverent and the denier (92:5–10); here it is between the purified soul and the buried soul (91:9–10). The recurrence is deliberate pedagogy: the same sky, read twice, yields the same verdict. The night, in both, is the principle of concealment — and concealment is the moral danger the sūrah is warning against.

**Why the Night Is a Mercy, Not a Misfortune**

Modern readers, living under electric light that has all but abolished the night, easily forget what the night was to the sūrah's first hearers: a gift. The Qur'an calls the alternation of night and day a mercy specifically because the night forces rest, stops labor, cools the heat, and hushes the world:

> *"And out of His mercy He made for you the night and the day, that you may rest therein and seek of His bounty."* (Qur'an 28:73)

> *"He it is Who made the night for you, that you may rest therein, and the day, giving sight."* (Qur'an 10:67)

So the night's *enshrouding* is not hostile; it is sheltering. The darkness that hides the sun also hides the laborer from his toil and gives the body its appointed season of repair. Even the night's concealment has a purpose within creation: growth that happens in secret — the seed germinating underground, the child forming in the womb, the sleeper's mind sorting the day — requires darkness. God has built concealment into the world as the necessary companion of every disclosure, just as He has built it into the human drama as the arena of moral choice. What matters is not darkness itself but what a creature *does* in the dark.

**The Moral Danger of Concealment**

And this is the turn the sūrah makes with the night. Darkness in the sky is a mercy; darkness in the soul is a disease. The same covering that lets the body rest lets the thief work, the hypocrite scheme, the sinner hide. The Qur'an's psychology is exact on this point: concealment is what allows evil to mature. Sin almost always prefers the night — not the astronomical night only, but the inner night of secrecy. The spiritual commentators read this verse precisely so: just as the night enshrouds the sun, persistent sin enshrouds the light of faith in the heart, covering it until the soul forgets it ever had light. The Prophet ﷺ described the process in terms the commentaries on this verse preserve — the heart that sins acquires a dark spot, and the heart that repents is polished back to brightness:

> *"When a servant commits a sin, a black spot is stamped upon his heart. If he leaves it and seeks forgiveness and repents, his heart is polished; and if he returns to it, the spot increases until it covers his heart. That is the 'stain' (rān) which God mentioned: 'No! Rather, what they used to earn has stained their hearts.'"* (recorded by al-Tirmidhī 3334, Ibn Mājah 4244, and Aḥmad; graded ḥasan; the quoted verse is Qur'an 83:14)

Here is the night of the soul in a single report: the sin that is not repented becomes a covering, and the covering, repeated, becomes a blindness. The sūrah's fourth oath is thus a warning dressed as a picture. The night that covers the sun is God's mercy; the night that covers the soul is the soul's own doing — and the difference between the two is exactly the difference the sūrah is about to name: purification versus burial.

**The Night as the Theater of Secret Devotion**

Yet the same concealment is also the believer's opportunity. The tradition treasures the night precisely because its darkness offers the soul privacy with God — a disclosure to the One who sees in the dark that cannot be made in the crowd of day. The Qur'an honors the ones who *"forsake their beds to call upon their Lord in fear and hope"* (32:16), and the Prophet ﷺ taught that the prayer most beloved to God after the obligatory is the night prayer (Muslim 1163), and that in the last third of every night God descends, in a descent befitting His majesty, calling:

> *"Who is calling upon Me, that I may answer him? Who is asking of Me, that I may give him? Who is seeking My forgiveness, that I may forgive him?"* (al-Bukhārī 1145; Muslim 758)

So the night is double-edged by design: the same darkness in which the sinner buries his sin is the darkness in which the worshipper finds his Lord. The sūrah swears by the night, then, because it is the created sign of both possibilities — the arena where concealment either becomes corruption or becomes intimacy. The day discloses what the night concealed, and the sūrah's whole question is: what have you been doing with your nights, and what will the day of disclosure find?

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:5

> **By the sky and the One Who established it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth Witness and the Grammar of Its Mystery**

With verse 5 the oath-chain turns from the two luminaries and the two halves of the day to the two great planes of the cosmos: *wa'l-samā'i wa mā banāhā* — "by the sky and that which established it," or, as some render it, "by the sky and the One Who established it." The word *al-samāʾ* (from the root *s-m-w*, "to be high, elevated") names everything above the earth — the heavens in the broadest sense, the visible vault that arches over the world. The word *banā* (from *b-n-y*) means "built, constructed, raised as a structure" — the same root as *bināʾ* (a building) and *bunyān* (edifice). But the real weight of the verse lies in the little particle *mā*, and it is here that the classical commentators record one of the most instructive grammatical discussions in the whole sūrah.

**Three Readings of the Word *Mā***

The particle *mā* in *wa mā banāhā* can be read in three ways, and al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qurṭubī all discuss them. First, *mā* may be a relative pronoun (*mā mawṣūlah*) meaning "the One Who" — so the oath runs "by the sky and the One Who built it," that is, God Himself. Second, *mā* may be a verbal-noun particle (*mā maṣdariyyah*) meaning "that which" — understood as "its being built," the act of building itself: "by the sky and its building." Third, some read it as "that which built it," taking the builder as an impersonal power. Al-Qurṭubī records that the reading preferred by the majority — and by himself — is the second, the infinitival reading: *wa mā banāhā* meaning *wa bināʾihā*, "and by its being established/built." The reason given by al-Rāzī for hesitating over the first reading is theologically interesting: the oath is being sworn *by* the created thing, and to make God the object of His own oath in this construction seemed to some scholars inappropriate — for an oath's weight comes from swearing by something great *other than* the speaker, and the sequence here is of created things: sun, moon, day, night, sky, earth, soul.

**Can God Swear by Himself? A Refinement of the Question**

The reader should not misunderstand this discussion. The Qur'an elsewhere has God swear by Himself in the plainest terms — *"By your Lord, We shall surely question them all"* (15:92), *"By your Lord, they do not believe until they make you judge"* (4:65) — so there is no question of whether God *may* swear by Himself; He does. The debate over *mā* in this verse is narrower and more precise: it is about what the *grammar* of this particular construction most naturally yields, and whether the symmetry of the oath-chain (created thing + created thing) favors the infinitival reading. Both readings are transmitted, both have their defenders, and both converge on the same ultimate meaning: the sky is not self-standing; it has a builder, and the oath draws attention to the sky *together with the act that established it*. What the verse refuses to allow, under either reading, is the one thing it is aimed against: the notion that the sky simply *is*, unbuilt, uncaused, eternal.

**The Sky as a Structure: The Qur'an's Building Language**

The choice of the verb *banā* — "built" — is itself a piece of the argument. A building implies a builder the way a footprint implies a foot. The Qur'an applies the same architectural vocabulary to the heavens repeatedly, and each time the point is that this vast, seamless vault is *constructed*, held, and proportioned:

> *"And the sky We built with strength; and truly, We are the Expander."* (Qur'an 51:47)

> *"Are you more difficult to create, or is the sky He built? He raised its ceiling and proportioned it."* (Qur'an 79:27–28)

> *"He raised the heavens without pillars that you can see."* (Qur'an 13:2)

> *"And We made the sky a protected ceiling; yet from its signs they turn away."* (Qur'an 21:32)

Al-Ṭabarī explains the rhetorical force: to "build" is to bring parts together into a coherent, ordered whole, and the sky's coherence — its balance, its distances, its non-collapse — is the strongest possible testimony that it did not assemble itself. A house is not built by its bricks, and the sky is not raised by its own clouds. The sūrah, swearing "by the sky and its building," is swearing by the whole argument of design at once — the single most ancient and most accessible argument for the Creator, restated in the vocabulary of the builder's craft that every hearer knew.

**The Height of the Sky and the Lowliness of the Skeptic**

The word *samāʾ* carries elevation inside it, and the Qur'an repeatedly pairs the sky's loftiness with the skeptic's smallness. It asks whether the denier has even looked up:

> *"Have they not looked at the sky above them — how We built it and adorned it, and it has no cracks?"* (Qur'an 50:6)

> *"Do they not look at the sky above them..."* — and the threat that hangs over every heedless head: *"and We could make fragments fall upon them from the sky"* (34:9).

The psychology of the verse is that of all awe: the sky is the one thing every human being sees and no human being controls. You cannot build it, touch it, paint it, or hold it up; you can only stand under it. Its sheer scale is the standing refutation of human self-sufficiency. The sūrah places this refutation in an oath because an oath is the language of ultimate seriousness — the language a person uses when his mere word is not strong enough and he must call something greater to guarantee his truth. God calls the sky itself as guarantor, and the sky, in all its height, is one more witness — and one more creature — under the single Builder to whom the sūrah is leading.

**From the Sky Above to the Soul Within**

Finally, the verse's position in the chain matters. The oaths have moved from the sun (the great single light) to the moon (the following light) to the day (disclosure) to the night (concealment), and now they rise to the sky — the widest, highest, most encompassing frame of all — before descending in the next verse to the earth, and then turning inward to the soul in verse 7. The movement is a deliberate narrowing and deepening: from the cosmos at its largest to the human interior at its smallest, as though the sūrah were a camera pulling back to the full vault of heaven and then diving into the single human heart. The sky's builder and the soul's fashioner are the same, and by verse 7 the reader will have been brought, through every scale of creation, to the one creature the oaths were always about: himself. The height of the sky is invoked so that the depth of the soul may be felt.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:6

> **By the earth and the One Who spread it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sixth Witness: The Ground Beneath Our Feet**

The sixth oath descends from the sky to the earth: *wa'l-arḍi wa mā ṭaḥāhā* — "by the earth and that which spread it," or "by the earth and the One Who spread it." The noun *al-arḍ* — the earth — is feminine in Arabic, like the sun and the sky before it, and the verb *ṭaḥā* (root *ṭ-ḥ-w*) means "spread out, laid flat, extended as a level expanse." The same grammatical ambiguity of *mā* that occupied verse 5 recurs here, and the commentators give the same threefold answer: *mā* as "the One Who" (God), *mā* as "that which" (the spreading itself, by the maṣdar reading *wa ṭaḥwihā*, "its spreading"), or, as some transmitted from the earliest exegesis, *mā* understood as "what He created in it" — the earth's contents. Under every reading the oath's core is identical: the earth did not lay itself out; its surface is a made surface, prepared and proportioned for habitation. The verse invites the hearer to do what he never does — to look at the ground he walks on as evidence.

**The Earth as a Spread: The Qur'an's Vocabulary of Preparation**

The Qur'an's word for what the earth was made into is a family of related images, all conveying the same mercy: the earth as a *bed*, a *cradle*, a *spread*, a *resting place* prepared for its inhabitants. Sūrah al-Nabaʾ gathers them:

> *"Have We not made the earth a spread (mihād), and the mountains as pegs?"* (Qur'an 78:6–7)

Sūrah al-Baqarah pairs the earth with the sky in the same breath as this sūrah does:

> *"Who made for you the earth a bed (firāsh) and the sky a canopy, and sent down water from the sky, bringing forth thereby fruits as provision for you."* (Qur'an 2:22)

And Sūrah Ṭā Hā calls the earth a *cradle*:

> *"He Who made for you the earth a cradle (mahd) and threaded for you therein paths."* (Qur'an 20:53)

The choice of images — bed, cradle, spread — is itself the commentary. A bed is not a thing that happened; it is a thing that was *made* for a sleeper. A cradle is not accidental; it is *prepared* for an infant. The earth, the Qur'an is saying, is not a random rock drifting in space; it is a provision, shaped in advance for the one creature that would stand on it, farm it, build on it, and be tested upon it. The same point appears in the verb's other Quranic kin: *"And the earth after that He spread out (daḥāhā)"* (79:30) — *daḥā* there and *ṭaḥā* here both mean "spread," and both carry the sense of a surface leveled and extended for use.

**The Pair of Planes and the Creature Between Them**

The structure of verses 5 and 6 taken together is deliberate: the sky is *built* above, the earth is *spread* below, and the human being stands in the space between. The Qur'an returns to this framing again and again, because it is the visual diagram of the human condition: heaven above, earth beneath, and the soul at the crossing point of both. The sky built and the earth spread are the two halves of one dwelling prepared by one Maker:

> *"And the sky We built with strength, and truly We are the Expander. And the earth — We spread it out; how excellent are the Spreaders!"* (Qur'an 51:47–48)

The source commentary draws exactly this parallel, and the exclamation is worth pausing on: *"how excellent are the Spreaders!"* — the plural of majesty, the Creator praising His own craftsmanship in the vocabulary of a craftsman, so that the hearer feels the delight of the maker in the made. The earth's spreading is not a bare fact; it is a finished work offered for admiration. Al-Ṭabarī adds the logical step: a spread surface, leveled and made passable, bearing soil and water and seasons, points to a spreader as surely as a smooth road points to the road-builder. The earth is the road, and the sūrah is about to name the journey.

**Subdued for Travel: The Earth as a Servant**

The Qur'an goes further than design; it declares the earth itself to be a *servant* placed at the human being's disposal:

> *"It is He Who made the earth subservient (dhalūl) to you; so walk in its paths and eat of His provision — and to Him is the rising."* (Qur'an 67:15)

The word *dhalūl* is used of a riding-camel that has been broken in and made gentle — the earth is pictured as a mount, tamed and readied for its rider. This is the sūrah's quiet democratizing of awe: the same earth that swallows the dead and shakes with the earthquake is also, by God's appointment, the docile ground under every foot. The one who swears the oath is the one who both spread the earth for the traveler and can fold it up at the end of the journey — *"On the Day when the earth will be changed to another earth, and the skies likewise"* (14:48). The oath by the spread earth is thus an oath by both God's mercy (the prepared dwelling) and God's sovereignty (the dwelling's owner, who reclaims it at will).

**Gratitude and Stewardship: Reading the Ground Morally**

The practical fruit of this verse is a specific kind of gratitude, and a specific kind of duty. First, gratitude: the most taken-for-granted thing in a human life is the ground. No one thanks the floor of his house each morning, and fewer thank the earth. The sūrah's oath is a corrective to exactly this blindness — it makes the earth a *sworn witness*, and a witness must be noticed. The believer who recites this verse is being asked to feel the sheer givenness of the ground: its firmness, its fertility, its capacity to receive a seed and return a harvest, its patience under every abuse. Second, duty: because the earth is God's spread and not man's invention, it is not man's to ruin. The Qur'an's appointment of the human being as *khalīfah*, a caretaker-vicegerent upon the earth (2:30), and its warning against *"corruption in the land"* (fasād fī'l-arḍ, e.g., 2:205, 7:56), grow directly out of this verse's premise. A bed you were given is a bed you keep; a road you were granted is a road you do not mine. In an age that has learned, the hard way, that the "spread" can be poisoned, eroded, and depleted, the oath by the spread earth reads not as poetry but as a charter of stewardship — the earliest and most concise statement that the ground is a trust.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:7

> **By the soul and the One Who fashioned it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Climax of the Oath-Chain: The Inward Turn**

Seven verses have been building, and the seventh turns the camera inward: *wa nafsin wa mā sawwāhā* — "by the soul and that which fashioned it," or "by the soul and the One Who fashioned it." After the sun, the moon, the day, the night, the sky, and the earth — after the whole visible cosmos has been called to the stand — the oath arrives at its true subject: the human soul. Everything before this verse was preparation for it. The commentators note the turn with emphasis: the oaths have descended from the widest heaven to the firmest earth, and now they enter the one creature for whose sake, in a sense, all the rest was arranged. The sky was built and the earth spread, and then, at the center of that prepared dwelling, was placed the soul — the only thing in creation that can read the sky and the earth as signs. The sūrah's entire cosmology is a frame around this single verse.

**The Word *Nafs*: The Breathing Self**

The Arabic *nafs* derives from the root *n-f-s*, whose basic sense is breath — *nafas* is a breath, *tanaffus* is breathing. The soul is named, in Arabic itself, as "that which breathes": the self is the living breath, the warm interior of a living thing as opposed to its outward shell. The same root yields *tanāfus*, mutual striving and competition — because souls, being alive and desirous, compete with one another for the objects of their desire (the Qur'an uses exactly this word in *"then let the strivers strive (al-mutanāfisūn)"*, 83:26). And it yields *nafīs*, precious — because the self is the most precious thing a person possesses, the one thing he can never replace. All these senses converge in the oath: God swears by the breathing, striving, irreplaceable self, because that self is the entire arena of the message the sūrah is about to deliver.

**Fashioned, Proportioned, Balanced: The Verb *Sawwā***

The verb *sawwā* (from the root *s-w-y*, "to be equal, level, even") means "fashioned, proportioned, made even, balanced." It is the verb of the craftsman who levels a scale, straightens a line, and removes every crookedness from his work. The Qur'an uses it for the creation of the human being in precisely this sense throughout its account of human origins:

> *"Who created you, then proportioned you (fa-sawwāka), and made you balanced (fa-ʿadalaka)."* (Qur'an 82:7)

> *"Who perfected everything He created, and began the creation of man from clay... then He proportioned him (sawwāhu) and breathed into him of His spirit."* (Qur'an 32:7–9)

> *"We have certainly created man in the best of statures (aḥsani taqwīm)."* (Qur'an 95:4)

The fashioning is thus twofold, and the classical commentators insist on both halves. Outwardly, the human being is proportioned in body — the upright frame, the balanced limbs, the hands and eyes and tongue that the Qur'an elsewhere lists (90:8–9; 76:2–3). Inwardly, the soul is proportioned in faculties — balanced between intellect, appetite, and anger, none of which is evil in itself, each of which has its right measure and its excess. The word *sawwā* denies the idea that the self is raw chaos; it is a *made*, balanced thing, and its balance is the very ground on which it can later be held responsible. A being with no proportion could not be judged; the soul's fashioning is the prerequisite of its accountability.

**The Grammar of *Mā* Again, and the Question of Who Fashions**

The verse carries the same threefold reading of *mā* as verses 5 and 6: the One Who fashioned (God), the fashioning itself (the maṣdar reading, "its fashioning," preferred by al-Qurṭubī), or, in some early readings, "what He created in it." Ibn Kathīr transmits the gloss that the soul here may allude to the soul of Adam or to the souls of all human beings. Either way, the conclusion is single: the self does not fashion itself. The deepest point of the verse is that the human soul has a *Maker* — that the thing you call "me," the thing you assume is simply given, is itself a creature, brought into proportion by the same hand that built the sky and spread the earth. The oath "by the soul" is therefore, hidden inside itself, the most personally arresting oath in the chain: it swears by *you*. When God swears by the soul, every hearer is being called as a witness against himself.

**The Fiṭrah: The Soul's Original Soundness**

The fashioning of the soul is the Qur'an's foundation for the doctrine of the *fiṭrah* — the primordial nature in which every human being is originated sound, upright, and inclined toward the truth. The pivotal verse is 30:30, which Ibn Kathīr and the commentators connect directly to this passage:

> *"So set your face to the religion as a ḥanīf — the primordial nature (fiṭrata Allāh) upon which He originated mankind. There is no altering of God's creation; that is the upright religion, but most people do not know."* (Qur'an 30:30)

The Prophet ﷺ grounded the same doctrine in a saying recorded in both authentic collections:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian — just as an animal gives birth to a whole animal. Do you see any of them born maimed?"* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658)

And in the ḥadīth qudsī recorded by Muslim, God declares:

> *"I created all My servants as ḥunafāʾ (upright, inclining to truth), but the devils came to them and turned them away from their religion."* (Muslim 2865)

The verse's "fashioning" is thus the theological ground of human dignity: no soul is born corrupt. The soul arrives balanced and inclined to truth, and whatever darkness later settles upon it is an addition, not an origin. This is why the sūrah can speak of "purifying" the soul in verse 9 — purification presumes an original purity to restore. You cannot restore what was never there.

**The Soul's Three Stations: Commanding, Blaming, at Peace**

Because the fashioned soul is balanced, it is not static; it moves, and the Qur'an names the three stations through which the self travels. The lowest is *al-nafs al-ammārah bi'l-sūʾ* — "the soul that commands to evil," the self under the sway of its appetites, which the Prophet Joseph identified with unflinching honesty:

> *"And I do not absolve my own self; indeed, the soul is ever commanding to evil, except what my Lord has mercy upon."* (Qur'an 12:53)

The middle station is *al-nafs al-lawwāmah* — "the blaming soul," which God Himself swears by in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah:

> *"And I swear by the blaming soul."* (Qur'an 75:2)

This is the self that has awakened: it sins, and then reproaches itself; it falls, and then feels the fall. Its very self-accusation is the sign of life and the seed of return. The highest station is *al-nafs al-muṭmaʾinnah* — "the soul at peace," which hears at last the call:

> *"O soul at peace, return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing; enter among My servants, and enter My Garden."* (Qur'an 89:27–30)

The sūrah's oath "by the soul and the One Who fashioned it" contains, folded into itself, this entire journey: the soul is *fashioned* (v. 7) so that it can be *inspired* with the knowledge of its two directions (v. 8), so that it can then be *purified* toward peace or *buried* toward ruin (vv. 9–10). The seventh verse is the hinge on which the whole human drama turns.

**The Honor of the Soul and the Duty of Self-Knowledge**

Two practical consequences follow from the verse, and the tradition drew both. The first is the soul's honor. The Qur'an repeatedly attaches the soul to God's own breath and spirit — *"then He proportioned him and breathed into him of His spirit"* (32:9) — and the classical scholars inferred from this that the human self is the most precious of the visible creation's contents, the one creature for whom the angels were commanded to prostrate (cf. 2:34, 7:11). A well-known saying, circulated widely in the spiritual literature, expresses the consequence in three words — *"He who knows himself knows his Lord"* — and though the saying is not established as a prophetic ḥadīth, its sense is the verse's own: the soul, being a fashioned sign of its Fashioner, is a window through which the thoughtful can read something of the One who made it. The second consequence is the duty of self-knowledge itself. If the soul is the arena, then the believer's first obligation is to know the arena — to study his own self, its strengths and its drives, its inclinations and its deceptions. The sūrah is about to teach that the soul can be purified or buried; but neither act is possible without first *looking* at the soul, and the oath "by the soul" is the command to look. After seven verses of sky and earth, the reader arrives at himself — and the sūrah's remaining verses will now tell him what he is holding in his hands.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:8

> **And inspired it as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Puts the Test Inside the Soul**

Verse 8 completes the soul's description and carries the sūrah's central doctrine: *fa-alhamahā fujūrahā wa taqwāhā* — "and inspired it as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent." The verb is *alhama*, "He inspired" — a word the Qur'an reserves, in distinction from *awḥā* (revelation), for the inward, silent teaching that God places in the human heart. The two objects are *fujūr* and *taqwā* — the two directions open to the fashioned soul. The translation in the source commentary renders it "as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent," and it records the alternative reading transmitted by Ibn Kathīr: *"He placed within it its iniquity and its reverence"* — God installed, in the very fabric of the soul, the knowledge of both. Either rendering yields the same astonishing claim: before any prophet, before any book, before any teacher, the human soul already *knows* the difference between its iniquity and its reverence. The test of life is not to discover right and wrong but to choose between two things the soul has known from the beginning.

**Two Words That Name the Two Roads**

The two words deserve their Arabic. *Fujūr* comes from the root *f-j-r*, "to burst open, to split through" — the same root as *fajr*, the dawn, because dawn is the moment light *bursts through* the darkness. A *fājir* is one who bursts through the bounds — who breaches the limits God has set the way water bursts a dam. Iniquity, in this vocabulary, is not a mere mistake; it is a *breaching*, an act of tearing through a boundary. *Taqwā*, by contrast, comes from the root *w-q-y*, "to protect, to shield" — and the word's built-in image is of a guard placed between oneself and a harm. The reverent one (*al-muttaqī*) is the one who takes a shield (*wiqāyah*) against what would destroy him, who walks through a dangerous place with attention and care, the way a driver through a storm keeps both hands on the wheel and his eyes on the road. The sūrah thus names the two roads with two exact pictures: bursting through, and shielding oneself. The soul is inspired with the knowledge of both pictures — and the whole question of a human life is which picture it will walk toward.

**Inspiration Is Not Revelation, and Why the Distinction Matters**

The classical commentators are careful to distinguish *ilhām* (inspiration) from *waḥy* (revelation). Revelation is the special address of God to His prophets — a certainty from without, descending in definite words. Inspiration is the general address of God to the inner constitution of every soul — a knowledge from within, installed at creation. The Qur'an shows God inspiring even things that are not prophets: He *inspired* the mother of Moses to cast her infant into the river (28:7, using *awḥaynā* there in the broad sense), and He *inspired* the bee to build its comb (16:68). The inspiration of the soul with its fujūr and taqwā is of this general kind: it is the moral compass built into human nature itself. This is why the verse is the Qur'an's foundation for the doctrine of the *fiṭrah* already discussed at verse 7: the soul is not merely fashioned in body but *informed* in conscience. God did not leave the human being to stumble in moral darkness; He illuminated the interior with the knowledge of the two roads before the journey began.

**The Qur'an's Own Testimony to the Innate Moral Sense**

The Qur'an returns to this inward knowledge so often that it is unmistakably a deliberate doctrine. Sūrah al-Balad, the sūrah immediately preceding this one, asks:

> *"Did We not make for him two eyes, and a tongue, and two lips, and guide him upon the two highways?"* (Qur'an 90:8–10)

And Sūrah al-Insān makes the same point from the side of free choice:

> *"Indeed, We guided him upon the way — whether he be grateful or ungrateful."* (Qur'an 76:3)

The Day of Judgment, in the Qur'an's telling, is therefore not the moment when the truth is first revealed to the soul, but the moment when the soul is made to *confront what it already knew*:

> *"Nay, man is a clear witness against himself, even if he casts forth his excuses."* (Qur'an 75:14–15)

> *"Each soul will know what it has sent forth and what it has left behind."* (Qur'an 82:5)

And the covenant of 7:172 — the verse the commentators bring to this passage — describes the primordial scene in which every soul already testified to its Lord:

> *"And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them bear witness upon themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yes, we bear witness.'"* (Qur'an 7:172)

The inspiration of verse 8 is this testimony made inward and permanent. The soul that denies God at the end does not deny in ignorance; it denies against a knowledge that was in it from the start.

**The Prophet's Own Testimony to the Inward Judge**

The Prophet ﷺ translated this verse into a rule of practical conscience in a report that belongs to the heart of Muslim ethics. Al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān asked him about righteousness and sin, and he answered:

> *"Righteousness is good character; and sin is what wavers in your soul and you dislike that people should find it out."* (Muslim 2553)

And to Wābiṣah ibn Maʿbad, who had come to ask about the same matter, he gave the instruction that has since been called the charter of the conscience:

> *"Consult your heart. Righteousness is that in which the soul finds rest and the heart finds rest; and sin is that which wavers in the soul and hesitates in the breast — even if people give you a verdict, and give you a verdict again."* (recorded by Aḥmad in his Musnad and by al-Dārimī; the hadith scholars have graded its chain ḥasan)

Both reports rest on exactly this verse's premise: there is an inward tribunal, installed by the Fashioner, that knows the difference between fujūr and taqwā before any mufti speaks. The conscience is the verse's living witness in every human breast. A person can silence it, starve it, drown it in habit and appetite — but he cannot, the Prophet is saying, erase it; and it is precisely its persistence that makes the final judgment just.

**The Great Debate: Decree, Will, and Responsibility**

Verse 8 has always stood at the center of Islam's most serious theological discussion — the relationship between God's creating and decreeing on one side, and human will and responsibility on the other — and the source commentary records both positions with precision. The question is unavoidable: if God *inspired* the soul with both its fujūr and its taqwā, and if God *creates* all things, then in what sense is the human being responsible for which road he takes?

One reading — preferred by most Ashʿarite theologians — takes the verse to indicate that God decrees all things for each human being, including the soul's final direction, while the human being nevertheless acquires his acts and is justly rewarded or punished for them. In support of this reading, Ibn Kathīr transmits the report of a man who asked the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"O Messenger of God! Do you consider the actions of mankind and their struggles to be something ordained (qaḍāʾ) for them and coming to pass from a previous measuring-out (qadar), or something written for them only after the Message came to them from their Prophet?"* He replied: *"Rather, it is ordained for them."* The man said: *"Then what is the point of our actions?"* The Prophet ﷺ answered: *"Whomsoever God created for one of the two positions [Paradise or Hell], He makes it easy for him [to attain it]. And the proof of that is in the Book of God: 'By the soul and the One Who fashioned it, and inspired it as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent.'"* (transmitted by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary)

The Prophet's fuller teaching on this subject, recorded in both authentic collections, is the famous reassurance:

> *"Act, for everyone is facilitated toward that for which he was created."* (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2647)

The other reading — associated with the Muʿtazilite school — takes the inspiration of the verse as God's *making known* of the two roads, leaving the soul's choice genuinely its own, since God does not compel anyone and does not wrong anyone. The two schools debated for centuries, and the mainstream Sunni position came to hold the two truths in a deliberate balance, refusing to surrender either: God is the Creator of everything, including human acts, so nothing escapes His will and knowledge; yet the human being possesses a real will and genuine capacity, is addressed by command and prohibition, and is justly rewarded and punished. The verse itself contains both halves — *"He inspired it"* (God's originating act) and *"its iniquity or its reverence"* (the soul's two real possibilities) — and the tradition's wisdom was to keep the whole verse rather than amputate either limb.

**A Map with Two Routes: The Logic of the Verse**

The reflective mind can see the coherence of the two truths in a simple image. A map-maker draws both routes — the road that reaches the city and the road that ends in the desert — and in drawing both he has *not* chosen the traveler's route. God, in inspiring the soul with both its fujūr and its taqwā, has given the soul the complete map; the giving of the map is mercy, and the choosing of the route is the test. This is exactly how the Qur'an frames the purpose of the whole arrangement:

> *"He Who created death and life to test you — which of you is best in deed."* (Qur'an 67:2)

A test with only one possible answer is not a test. A soul that knew only taqwā could not be virtuous; it would be an automaton. A soul that knew only fujūr could not be judged; it would be a victim. The soul is made *between* the two precisely so that its goodness can be real — chosen, striven for, earned. And the same verse that inspires the two roads also, in its very wording, points to the way back when the wrong road is taken: the sūrah's next two verses will say that the soul can be *purified* — and purification presupposes that even after the soul has tasted its fujūr, the road of return remains open.

**The Prophet's Prayer at This Verse**

It is fitting, then, that the tradition attached to this verse a prayer rather than a debate. It is recorded that the Prophet ﷺ would supplicate with these words, which Muslim collected in his Ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"O God, grant my soul its reverence (taqwāhā), and purify it — for You are the best of those who purify it; You are its Guardian and its Master."* (Muslim 2722)

Notice what the prayer does with the theology: the one who knows that God inspired the soul with both roads does not sit down and say "it is all written"; he *prays*. He asks God to grant the soul its taqwā and to purify it — because the knowledge of the two roads is God's gift, the purification of the soul is God's gift, and yet both are sought *by the soul's own asking*. The prayer is the verse's own answer to its apparent dilemma: the human being is not a passive recipient of a decree, nor a self-sufficient author of his virtue, but a servant who strives and, in the very act of striving, asks the Fashioner to complete what He began. This is the cooperation the next two verses will unfold in full.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:9

> **Indeed, he prospers who purifies it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer the Eleven Oaths Were Sworn to Secure**

After seven verses of oaths — after the sun and its brightness, the moon and its following, the day and its disclosure, the night and its enshrouding, the sky and its building, the earth and its spreading, the soul and its fashioning — the sūrah delivers its answer: *qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā* — "indeed, he prospers who purifies it." This is the *jawāb al-qasam*, the claim the entire oath-chain was sworn to guarantee. The particle *qad* before the verb is the particle of verification and emphasis: it marks the statement as *settled*, as certain as the sunrise. The subject *man*, "whoever," is deliberately open — the promise excludes no one, making purification the universal key to prosperity. And the pronoun *-hā*, "it," reaches all the way back to the soul of verse 7: the prosperity announced is not wealth, fame, or comfort, but the soul's own success. The eleven witnesses of the cosmos stand as guarantors of this one sentence: purify the soul, and you have prospered.

**The Word *Aflaḥa*: Success as Cultivation**

The verb *aflaḥa* (root *f-l-ḥ*) is worth its full weight. It means "to succeed, to prosper, to attain the desired end" — but its root is shared with *fallāḥ*, the farmer, and with *falāḥ*, the tilling of the soil. The Arabic ear, hearing *aflaḥa*, hears agriculture underneath it: success is pictured as *cultivation*, as the patient work of a farmer who breaks the ground, sows the seed, waters it, weeds it, and waits for the harvest. The Qur'an uses the word for its highest promises — the call to prayer's *ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ*, "come to success," and the verdicts of the Hereafter, *"the believers are the ones who prosper"* (23:1) — and it never loses the agricultural echo. Paired here with *zakkā* (whose root means *to grow*, as the source commentary notes from al-Shawkānī and al-Zamakhsharī), the verse is a complete farming metaphor: the soul is a field, purification is its cultivation, and prosperity is its harvest. A soul left unweeded does not stay neutral; it fills with whatever seed the owner permits. Success, the verse is saying, is not an accident that happens to a person but a crop that is grown.

**The Word *Zakkā*: Purity as Growth**

*Zakkā* (root *z-k-w*) holds two meanings that Arabic keeps fused and English splits. It means *to purify* — to cleanse from filth — and it means *to grow, to increase, to make thrive*. The two are one process in the word's logic: a thing grows precisely by being cleansed of what chokes it. This is why the obligatory alms are called *zakāt*: they are both a purification of wealth from the stain of greed and a cause of its growth in blessing. And it is why the Qur'an describes the Prophet's ﷺ entire mission as this single verb — *yuzakkīhim*, "to purify them":

> *"It is He Who sent among the unlettered a messenger from among themselves, reciting to them His verses, purifying them (yuzakkīhim), and teaching them the Book and the wisdom."* (Qur'an 62:2; cf. 2:129, 2:151, 3:164)

Purification of the soul — *tazkiyat al-nafs* — is thus, in the judgment of the tradition, nothing less than the purpose of revelation itself. The Qur'an came down, the scholars argued, to accomplish in the human interior what this single verse states in five words: to cleanse the soul of the low traits that bury it and to grow it into the character that saves it. The source commentary records that some have called this the entire object of the Book. Everything else — law, story, promise, warning — is in service of this one end.

**The Parallel Verse: Prosperity Defined Twice**

This verse has an exact twin in Sūrah al-Aʿlā, and the commentators read them together:

> *"Indeed he prospers who purifies himself (man tazakkā), and remembers the name of his Lord, and prays."* (Qur'an 87:14–15)

There the purification is reflexive — *tazakkā*, "purifies himself" — and it is paired with two visible acts, remembrance and prayer, as though the verse were answering the question "how does one purify the soul?" Here the object is the soul directly — *zakkāhā*, "purifies it" — and the sūrah's genius is that it leaves the *means* open, because the means are many and every soul's field needs its own care. Prayer, fasting, zakāt, repentance, remembrance, the company of the righteous, self-accounting before sleep, the restraining of the tongue and the eye, the forgiveness of the one who wronged you — these are the waters and the weeding of the soul's field. The Qur'an elsewhere fixes the individual's responsibility with total clarity:

> *"Whosoever purifies himself purifies himself only for his own soul; and to God is the journey's end."* (Qur'an 35:18)

Purification is work, it is *your* work, and its profit returns to no one but you.

**Whose Act Is Purification? The Cooperation of Grace and Striving**

But the verse's grammar holds a second reading, and the earliest commentators preserved it faithfully. The subject of *zakkāhā* can be read as *the human being* ("he who purifies it prospers") or as *God* ("he whose soul God purifies prospers") — the same ambiguity the source commentary records for both verses, from Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī. The ambiguity is not carelessness; it is the verse's precise theology. On the one hand, the Qur'an attributes purification to God:

> *"Rather, it is God Who purifies whomsoever He will."* (Qur'an 4:49)

On the other, it attributes it to the human being, as in 35:18 and 87:14 just quoted. And it states the governing law of the partnership between the two:

> *"Truly God does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves."* (Qur'an 13:11)

The tradition's resolution is a *both-and*: the human being strives — no purification happens without his effort, his turning, his asking — and God completes — no striving succeeds without His enabling, His acceptance, His gift. The sūrah's own next chapter of thought (verses 11–15) will show the failure of the one who refused to strive at all. The verse's two-faced grammar is the map of the whole spiritual life: *strive as though it all depends on you, and ask as though it all depends on Him*. This is exactly the posture the Prophet ﷺ modeled in his own prayer, already quoted at verse 8 — *"purify it, for You are the best of those who purify it"* — a man striving with all his might and, in the same breath, begging the Purifier to do what only He can do.

**The Prayer of Purification: Asking to Be Washed Clean**

The Prophet ﷺ made purification itself a daily prayer, and his words at the start of the ritual prayer remain the believer's charter of self-cleansing:

> *"O God, distance me from my sins as You distanced the East from the West; O God, cleanse me of my sins as the white garment is cleansed of filth; O God, wash away my sins with water, snow, and hail."* (al-Bukhārī 744; Muslim 598)

The image — a white garment, and the longing to be rid of every stain upon it — is the verse's own picture made supplication. And because the field of the soul is never weeded once for all, the Prophet ﷺ, forgiven though he was, prayed for it without ceasing:

> *"O people, repent to God and seek His forgiveness; for I seek God's forgiveness a hundred times a day."* (Muslim 2702; the number "more than seventy times" is in al-Bukhārī 6307)

Purification, in this light, is not a single decision but a daily discipline — the standing work of the one who has understood that the soul, like the field, either grows or fills with weeds, and that prosperity is the harvest of the field that was tended.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:10

> **And indeed he fails who obscures it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Verdict**

The sūrah's answer to its oaths is a pair, and the second verse states the counterpart: *wa qad khāba man dassāhā* — "and indeed he fails who obscures it." The structure mirrors verse 9 exactly — the same *qad* of certainty, the same open *man* ("whoever"), the same soul-bearing pronoun — but every content-word is its opposite. Where *aflaḥa* promised prosperity, *khāba* pronounces failure; where *zakkā* grew and purified, *dassā* buries and conceals. The two verses together close the oath-chain into a single, inescapable equation: the soul is the only capital a human being has, and it is either grown or buried. There is no third outcome. The sūrah has spent seven verses summoning the cosmos to testify, and its whole testimony reduces to this binary — because the soul's condition, in the end, admits of no middle state at the final disclosure.

**The Word *Khāba*: The Failure That Is a Loss**

*Khāba* (root *kh-y-b*) means "he failed, he was disappointed, he lost" — specifically the disappointment of one whose hopes have collapsed at the moment of outcome. The Qur'an uses the word and its cousin *khasira* (he lost) for the state of the soul that arrives at the Judgment to find it has nothing to show for itself:

> *"And he has failed (khāba) who buries it."* — and elsewhere: *"Indeed, the losers (al-khāsirūn) are those who lose their own souls and their families on the Day of Resurrection."* (Qur'an 39:15; cf. 42:45)

The failure named is not a passing setback but the total, final loss — the loss of the one possession that matters. A merchant may lose money and recover; the soul that arrives buried has lost *itself*, and there is no recovery because there is no second life in which to try again. The word is the Qur'an's most sobering, and it is delivered here with the full weight of eleven oaths behind it.

**The Word *Dassā*: Burial, Not Merely Corruption**

*Dassā* (root *d-s-s*) is the verse's sharpest instrument. Its root sense is "to hide, to insert secretly, to bury beneath the surface" — *dassa* is what one does to a thing one wishes to conceal underground. The source commentary records that most scholars take its literal meaning here as *to bury* (al-Shawkānī, al-Zamakhsharī), and the verse could thus be rendered, with full fidelity to the root, *"he fails who buries it."* The picture is exact and terrible: the soul is a living thing meant for light, and the heedless one *buries it* — under sins, under neglect, under the habits that cover it the way earth covers a buried body. Ibn Kathīr glosses it as dulling the soul through heedlessness and disobedience. Notice the opposite of purification: purification *uncovers and grows* the soul into its native brightness; burial *covers and smothers* it in what is foreign to it. The day discloses; the buried soul is what the night of negligence has claimed and kept.

**The Hidden Wordplay the Ear Is Meant to Catch**

The sūrah's artistry reaches its peak in the pairing of *zakkāhā* and *dassāhā*. The two words rhyme — the ear hears the echo of one inside the other — and the rhyme is doing theology. In one breath, two nearly identical sounds carry opposite destinies: *zakkā* (to purify/grow) and *dassā* (to bury). The near-identity of the sounds dramatizes the nearness of the two roads: the difference between prosperity and failure is not a distance but a direction. The same soul, the same faculties, the same days — cultivated in one direction they flower, and in the other they are buried, and the two verbs, so close the tongue can barely separate them, are the sūrah's way of saying how fine the line is that a human life walks. The oaths built the contrast of sun and moon, day and night; the verdict seals it in two rhyming verbs that a listener could never again unhear.

**What Buries the Soul: The Mechanics of Self-Burial**

How does a soul get buried? The tradition's answer is that no soul is buried in a day. Burial is gradual, and it happens through the accumulation of the small, unchallenged choices — the sin repeated until it becomes habit, the habit defended until it becomes identity, the identity hardened until the conscience, that inspired witness of verse 8, is silenced. The Qur'an names the mechanism with a single word: *rān*, the stain that covers the heart of what it has earned (83:14) — the same covering described in the report quoted at verse 4, where each unrepented sin stamps a dark spot that spreads until it covers the heart. The buried soul is simply the heart on which the covering has completed its work. What makes the verse so searching is that the burial is always *self-inflicted* — the earth of the burial is one's own deeds. God does not bury the soul; the soul buries itself, and the Qur'an's whole argument is that no one else can be blamed for the earth.

**The Two Readings Again: Who Does the Burying?**

As with verse 9, the earliest exegesis preserved a second reading of *dassāhā*: the subject may be God, so that the verse reads "he whose soul God obscures has failed." The commentators transmitted both, and the same cooperation-logic applies in reverse. The one whose soul is buried has, by his own heedlessness, removed himself from the light that purifies; and God, in the Qur'an's language, *"lets him go astray"* whom He wills (cf. 14:27, 16:93) — the divine "abandonment" that is always the *consequence*, never the arbitrary cause, of the soul's own choice. The two readings do not compete; they describe the two ends of the same rope. The verse that promised purification as a joint work of grace and striving now warns that burial is likewise a joint work — of human neglect and its divine consequence. The door of the one and the door of the other stand side by side, and the sūrah's next move is to show, in the story of Thamūd, what the buried soul looks like when it has become a whole nation's policy.

**Thamūd as the Living Illustration**

The transition is immediate and deliberate. Verse 10's buried soul does not remain an abstraction; the sūrah turns at once to the tribe that made self-burial a collective act. Thamūd, the northern Arabian people of the prophet Ṣāliḥ, will be shown in verses 11–15 as the worked example of everything this verse has just said: a people whose souls knew the truth — a living sign was set before their eyes — and who answered that knowledge by burying it under their own transgression, preferring the darkness they controlled to the light that judged them. The sūrah's structure is thus complete before the story even begins: the cosmos testifies (vv. 1–7), the soul is made to know its two roads (v. 8), the verdict is pronounced (vv. 9–10), and now the courtroom exhibits its evidence (vv. 11–15). The reader is about to be shown, in the fate of a vanished tribe, exactly what *"he fails who buries it"* means when it is acted out at full scale.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:11

> **Thamūd denied in their rebelliousness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Case Study Opens: A Tribe Named by Its Crime**

With verse 11 the sūrah turns from principle to proof. The abstract verdict of verses 9–10 — prosper or fail, purify or bury — is now exhibited in the story the early Arabs knew by heart: *kadhdhabat thamūdu bi-ṭaghwāhā* — "Thamūd denied in their rebelliousness." Thamūd were a pre-Islamic northern Arabian people, the successors of the earlier tribe of ʿĀd, famed for carving their dwellings out of the rock-faces of the mountains (cf. 7:74, 15:82, 26:149, 89:9) — an engineering civilization of striking sophistication whose ruins stood along the caravan routes for every later Arab to see. To them God sent the prophet Ṣāliḥ, one of their own, with a clear message and a living sign. They answered with denial. The verb is *kadhdhabat*, the intensive form of "to deny" — not a passive failure to believe but an active, repeated repudiation, a denial hammered home. And the sūrah states, in a single clause, the *cause* of that denial: *bi-ṭaghwāhā* — "by their transgression," "through their rebelliousness."

**The Word *Ṭaghwā*: The Flood That Overflows Its Bounds**

The noun *ṭaghwā* (also *ṭughyān*) comes from the root *ṭ-gh-y*, whose concrete sense is *to overflow, to exceed the limit, to surge beyond the bank*. The same root gives *ṭāghūt*, the tyrant or the false object of worship — because tyranny is precisely the overstepping of every limit. The picture inside the word is of water: a river that stays within its banks is life; a river that overflows them is destruction. Transgression, in this vocabulary, is not a list of particular crimes but a *state of overflow* — the self swollen past the boundaries God set. And the grammatical point of the verse is decisive: the *bāʾ* in *bi-ṭaghwāhā* is the *bāʾ al-sababiyyah*, the particle of causation. Thamūd denied *because of* their transgression. The denial did not produce the transgression; the transgression produced the denial. The sūrah here states, in three Arabic words, a law of moral psychology that the rest of the Qur'an confirms and that human experience bears out on every street: people do not always reject the truth because they find it unconvincing; they reject it because it convicts them.

**The Root of Denial: Why Sin Precedes Unbelief**

This verse reverses the order the modern mind assumes. We tend to think disbelief comes first — a person examines the evidence, finds it wanting, and then lives accordingly. The Qur'an's diagnosis is often the opposite: a person lives a certain way, becomes attached to it, and then finds the truth *wanting* because the truth would cost him the life he has built. The truth threatens the transgression, and so the transgressor must deny the truth. The Qur'an says of exactly this tribe elsewhere:

> *"And as for Thamūd, We guided them, but they preferred blindness over guidance."* (Qur'an 41:17)

Note the sequence: guidance *came first* — they were shown the way — and then they *preferred* blindness, because blindness was more comfortable to what they had chosen to be. The same law is stated in its general form:

> *"Then, when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate."* (Qur'an 61:5)

Deviation is self-initiated; its hardening is the consequence. The sūrah is teaching, through Thamūd, the precise mechanism by which the buried soul of verse 10 is buried: first the overflow, then the denial that protects the overflow. This is why the verse opens the story with the *transgression* and not with the *argument* — the argument was never the problem.

**A Civilization of Carvers: Thamūd in the Qur'an's Wider Narrative**

The sūrah assumes its hearers know Thamūd, and the Qur'an elsewhere fills in the portrait this compressed account leaves unspoken. They were the people of al-Ḥijr (15:80), the rock-cut cities between the Ḥijāz and Syria; they carved their homes "out of the mountains, feeling secure" (26:149), and God had given them gardens, springs, and cultivated fields (26:147–148). Their prophet Ṣāliḥ reminded them of all of it:

> *"And remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the land — you take for yourselves palaces in its plains and carve homes from the mountains. So remember the favors of God and do not spread corruption in the earth."* (Qur'an 7:74)

The irony is the sūrah's quiet point: a people who *carved* the rock could not *read* the rock, and a people who shaped stone into homes could not recognize the Fashioner who shaped them. Their very skill became their arrogance. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators note that Thamūd's story was chosen because it was the Arabs' own history — the ruins of these rock-dwellers were visible along their trade routes, a standing object lesson in what happens when a people's engineering outruns their obedience. The tribe was not backward; it was advanced. And it is exactly the advanced, the comfortable, the self-made — the Qur'an warns — who are most tempted to the overflow that refuses the truth.

**The Warning for Every Later Power**

The verse, though it speaks of a vanished tribe, is aimed at every later audience — and the sūrah's closing verses will make that aim explicit. The Makkans who heard this sūrah were themselves a commercial elite, proud, comfortable, attached to the order their forefathers had built, and increasingly annoyed by a Prophet whose message threatened their standing. Thamūd is held up as their mirror: a prosperous people whose prosperity bred transgression, whose transgression bred denial, and whose denial ended in the leveled ground the next verses describe. The pattern is transferable to any age — to every individual, community, or civilization that has ever answered an inconvenient truth with the same inner sentence: *this must be false, because if it were true I would have to change.* The sūrah's first hearers were being told, before the story's details were even unfolded, that denial is rarely an intellectual event; it is usually a moral one, and the moral one always has a name: *ṭaghwā*, the overflowing self.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:12

> **When the most wretched of them was dispatched.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Man, One Tribe, One Shared Crime**

The second verse of the story zeroes in on the agent: *idhi inbaʿatha ashqāhā* — "when the most wretched of them was dispatched." The word *inbaʿatha* (from the root *b-ʿ-th*, "to send, to raise, to rouse") means "was dispatched, was sent forth, rose up" — the verb of mission, and even, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, of *resurrection* (the *baʿth* is the raising of the dead). *Ashqāhā* is the elative of *shaqī*, "wretched" — "the most wretched of them," the superlative of misery. The verse thus presents a dark mirror-image of a sacred idea: just as prophets are *sent* by God, this man *rose up* from the midst of his people as the emissary of their ruin; just as the Hereafter raises the dead, this "raising" of the most wretched man was the tribe's own summons toward its burial. The story's villain is not named — the Qur'an leaves him anonymous, as though his name deserved to perish with him — but the tradition knew him: he was the ringleader who stepped forward to hamstring the she-camel, the one whose act sealed the tribe's doom.

**The Superlative of Wretchedness and the Hadith That Honors It**

The Prophet ﷺ himself attached a saying to this verse, recorded in the commentaries and in the hadith literature, identifying the hamstringer of the she-camel as the most wretched of the first generations. In one transmission, collected by Aḥmad and al-Ḥākim, he is reported to have said to ʿAlī:

> *"Shall I not tell you of the two most wretched of mankind? The hamstringer of the she-camel of Thamūd — and the one who will strike you here"* — and he pointed to ʿAlī's head — *"until this is dyed from it."* (recorded by Aḥmad in his Musnad and al-Ḥākim in his Mustadrak)

The two "most wretched" are paired across the whole span of sacred history: the man who slew the sign of a prophet's truth, and the man who would one day slay a prophet's kinsman. The hadith's point is the verse's point: wretchedness is not poverty, illness, or obscurity — it is the *choice* to attack the truth when it stands before you. The most wretched man in history was not the most deprived; he was the most defiant. The superlative *ashqā* marks him not for his suffering but for his crime.

**The Collective Dimension: Why the Whole Tribe Was Destroyed**

The most striking feature of the story is this: one man hamstrung the she-camel, and the *entire tribe* was destroyed. The source commentary and the classical exegesis draw the lesson with care. The one who acted did so as the tribe's "dispatched" agent — *inbaʿatha*, sent forth — because the whole people had consented to the denial of verse 11. They denied as a community; they resented the water-sharing as a community; they wanted the she-camel gone as a community; only the final blow was delegated to the most eager hand. The Qur'an makes the collective nature of the crime explicit in the fuller account:

> *"Then they hamstrung her"* — the plural verb — *"and became regretful"* (26:157); *"But they hamstrung her, and he said: 'Enjoy yourselves in your homes for three days; that is a promise not to be belied'"* (11:65).

The plural verbs are the point: the hamstringing was a communal act performed through a single hand. A people is not innocent of the evil it applauds, funds, tolerates, or stays silent before. The "most wretched" was the tip of a spear the whole tribe was holding. This is the Qur'an's warning against the comfortable illusion of the bystander — the belief that one is excused from a community's sin because one did not personally swing the blade. Thamūd's fate says otherwise: when a people consents, the whole people is the criminal.

**The Psychology of the Mob and the Cowardice of the Crowd**

The verse also captures the sociology of evil with uncanny compression. Why does a community delegate its worst deed to its "most wretched" member? Because collective wrongdoing is almost always performed through the hands of the few whom the many secretly wish to see act. The mob needs a scapegoat for its appetite: it wants the deed done, but it wants someone else's name on it. The most wretched man was "dispatched" precisely because he was the one willing to do openly what the others desired silently. The tradition records that the tribe had agreed among themselves that whoever killed the she-camel would be the one to do it, and the ringleader rose to the task while the rest stood by — ready to share the outcome if it went well, and to point at him if it went badly. The sūrah's lesson cuts into every era of human cruelty: the man who strikes is wicked, but the crowd that dispatches him and then disowns him is not thereby clean. God's reckoning, as the next verses will show, made no such distinction.

**Wretchedness as a Destination, Not an Accident**

Finally, the word *ashqā* — "most wretched" — belongs to the Qur'an's great binary of *shaqāwah* and *saʿādah*, wretchedness and blessedness, the two terminal states of the human journey:

> *"On the Day it comes, no soul shall speak except by His leave; among them shall be the wretched and the blessed. As for the wretched, they shall be in the Fire..."* (Qur'an 11:105–106)

The verse is teaching that wretchedness, like blessedness, is *arrived at*, not assigned at random. The most wretched man did not become wretched in the single moment of the hamstringing; he became wretched across a lifetime of transgression, of which the hamstringing was only the harvest. The sūrah has already laid the groundwork: the soul is fashioned and inspired with its two roads (vv. 7–8), and the one who takes the road of burial (v. 10) is walking, step by step, toward the station this verse names. *Ashqāhā* is not God's insult against a man but the man's own terminal diagnosis. And the terrifying part — the part the sūrah means its hearers to feel — is that the road to "most wretched" is paved with the same ordinary choices every human being faces, and that its first step is only the small, quiet refusal of a truth one already knows.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:13

> **So the messenger of God said to them, "[This is] the she-camel of God; give her drink!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Command in a Single Sentence**

The story's turning point is a sentence of startling economy: *fa-qāla lahum rasūlu Llāhi nāqata Llāhi wa suqyāhā* — "so the messenger of God said to them: 'the she-camel of God — and her drink!'" or, as the source commentary renders it, *"[This is] the she-camel of God; give her drink!"* Two things stand out at once. First, the speaker is named by the most honorable title any human can bear — *rasūl Allāh*, the messenger of God. Even the prophet of a destroyed people, a man whose community rejected him, is dignified in the Qur'an with the full title of his office: the worth of a messenger does not depend on the response of his audience. Second, the command itself is compressed into two nouns — the she-camel, and her watering — with no verb at all. The entire obligation God laid on Thamūd through this sign is contained in those two words: recognize the she-camel as God's, and let her drink her share of the water. A whole test, reduced to its minimum.

**The She-Camel of God: A Possessive of Honor, Not of Essence**

The phrase *nāqat Allāh*, "the she-camel of God," deserves its grammar. The commentators are unanimous that the possessive here is a possessive of *honor* and *distinction*, not of essence or ownership in the ordinary sense — exactly as the Qur'an speaks of *bayt Allāh*, "the house of God," for the sacred mosque, or *"the servants of God"* for the believers. The she-camel is called "of God" because she is a *sign* (āyah) sent by God, a miracle bearing His mark, and therefore an object under His special protection. The fuller accounts make her origin clear: Thamūd, challenging Ṣāliḥ to prove his claim, demanded a sign, and God brought forth a pregnant she-camel from the rock itself (cf. 7:73; 26:154–155; 54:27–28) — a living creature emerging from dead stone, as clear a demonstration of the Creator's power as the human eye can bear. To touch her was to touch the sign; to harm her was to declare war on the God who sent her. The sūrah's compression assumes all this; its hearers knew the story, and the phrase "she-camel of God" carried the whole weight of the miracle behind it.

**Why a She-Camel? The Test Attached to the Thing They Loved Most**

The choice of the she-camel as the test is itself the commentary's lesson. The she-camel was, for a desert people, the most precious creature in existence — the "ship of the desert," the source of milk, transport, wealth, and status, the living capital of Arabian life. God attached His test to the very thing they prized most, and to the resource they guarded most jealously: water. The arrangement, as the fuller accounts record, was that the water of the wells was to be shared — the she-camel drinking on one day, the tribe's animals on the next:

> *"He said: 'This is a she-camel; she shall have her drink, and you shall have your drink, on a known day. And touch her not with harm, lest the punishment of a tremendous day seize you.'"* (Qur'an 26:155–156)

> *"And inform them that the water is to be shared between them — each drinking in turn."* (Qur'an 54:28)

The test, then, was not a demand for worship or sacrifice in the abstract; it was a demand for *justice and restraint over a shared resource*, for recognizing God's right in the water they considered wholly their own. This is the Qur'an's recurring method: God tests a people through the thing it loves most, because only there is obedience truly costly and therefore truly real. Thamūd could accept Ṣāliḥ's monotheism in the abstract; it was the she-camel drinking *their* water that made their acceptance concrete — and there, precisely there, the test was failed.

**The Minimal Command and the Maximal Lesson**

Observe also how small the command was. God did not ask Thamūd to feed her, house her, or give her more than her allotted share; He asked only that they *let her drink*. The obligation was a negative one dressed as a positive one: do not obstruct, do not monopolize, allow the sign its due. The tradition's wisdom here is universal: the commands that reveal a soul are rarely the grand ones. A person's obedience is not measured by the epic choices he imagines he would make but by the small, concrete, daily ones he actually makes — the share of water he is willing to release, the right he is willing to recognize in the thing he calls his own. Thamūd failed not at a great crossroad but at a watering-trough. The sūrah places the entire drama of a civilization's destruction on this one hinge: they could not bear that a she-camel, God's sign, should drink from what they regarded as theirs. The test of faith, in the end, is almost always a test of giving up something small.

**"The Messenger of God Said to Them": The Dignity of the Deliverer**

One more feature of the verse demands attention: the sūrah names Ṣāliḥ not by name but by office — *rasūl Allāh*. In the sūrah's compressed telling, the individual is absorbed into the mission. This is the Qur'an's way of saying that the message did not fail with the messenger: Ṣāliḥ delivered it, plainly and completely, and the record stands forever — *"so the messenger of God said to them"* — whatever they then chose to do. The phrase also plants a warning and a comfort at once. A warning: the people who rejected this messenger were rejecting not a man but an office, and behind the office stood the Sender. A comfort: the messenger's duty ended at the delivery; the outcome belonged to God, and the next verses show that the messenger who warned was spared and vindicated while the deniers were leveled. For every later bearer of truth — and every later hearer of it — the verse fixes the two sides of the transaction: deliver the message and stand by it; receive it or refuse it; but know that the office and its Sender are what they are, regardless of what anyone decides.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:14

> **But they denied him, then hamstrung her. So their Lord crushed them for their sin, then leveled it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crime, the Punishment, and the Verb That Links Them**

The verse gathers the story's climax and its consequence into one breath: *fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-damdama ʿalayhim rabbuhum bi-dhanbihim fa-sawwāhā* — "but they denied him, then hamstrung her; so their Lord crushed them for their sin, then leveled it." Four verbs in rapid sequence: denied, hamstrung, crushed, leveled. The structure is a chain of cause and consequence with no gap between the links. The denial of the messenger matured into the hamstringing of the sign; the hamstringing of the sign matured into the crushing of the tribe; the crushing of the tribe was followed by the leveling of the ground. The sūrah lets the grammar itself do the teaching: sin and its recompense are not two separate stories but one continuous movement, the second half of which is already folded inside the first.

**The Word *ʿAqarū*: Hamstringing the Sign of God**

*ʿAqarūhā* (from the root *ʿ-q-r*) means "they hamstrung her" — the technical word for the slaughter of a camel by cutting the tendons of its legs so that it collapses, then finishing it. The source commentary notes that some read it simply as "slaughtered her," and the fuller accounts use both descriptions (cf. 7:77). But the choice of *ʿaqr* here is precise and significant. Hamstringing is not merely killing; it is *crippling* — the cutting of that which supports, the deliberate destruction of a creature's ability to stand. It is, in miniature, the very crime the whole sūrah has been describing: the soul that "buries" itself (v. 10) is a soul that has had its supports cut; the transgression of verse 11 is the overflowing that tears down what holds a life upright. Thamūd's outward act against the she-camel is the perfect symbol of their inward act against their own souls: they cut the legs from under the sign God sent, even as they had already cut the legs from under their own standing. The crime against the creature and the crime against the self are one crime, viewed from two sides.

**The Word *Damdama*: The Sound of Total Destruction**

*Damdama ʿalayhim* is one of the Qur'an's most vivid verbs, and the commentators linger on it. It is onomatopoeic — a word whose very sound imitates its meaning, the repeated, trampling, crushing fall of something heavy upon something fragile — and it conveys, the source commentary notes, "the most severe of punishments that came upon them suddenly, annihilating them and covering them entirely." The doubling of the root (the pattern *faʿlala*) paints the punishment as *repeated and total*: not a single blow but a grinding down, blow upon blow, until nothing remained. The Qur'an's other accounts of Thamūd's end describe the same event through its different faces — the earthquake that seized them in their homes (7:78), the shriek that annihilated them (11:67; 54:31), the thunderbolt of abasing punishment (41:13, 17) — and the tradition reconciles them as one catastrophic event, a scream of the sky and a convulsion of the earth arriving together. What *damdama* adds to all of them is the *finality*: after this verb, there is no survivor, no appeal, no aftermath but the level ground.

**For Their Sin: Proportion, Not Vengeance**

The punishment is expressly *bi-dhanbihim* — "for their sin." The preposition *bi-* is causal, and the word *dhanb* (sin, guilt) is singular and collective: the tribe's one shared crime. The Qur'an is scrupulous in the justice of the scene. The punishment came *for* the sin, in exact correspondence to it, and not a particle more — the same Book that narrates Thamūd's end insists in every register that God *"does not wrong people at all"* (10:44) and that *"your Lord is not unjust to the servants"* (41:46). The correspondence is even written into the imagery: they cut the legs of God's she-camel, and their own world was cut down beneath them; they refused to share the water, and the ground they hoarded received them. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr emphasize that the destruction was not arbitrary wrath but measured recompense — the divine equivalent of the law that a people reaps what it sows, executed without the two corruptions that afflict all human punishment: excess and neglect.

**The Word *Sawwāhā*: The Sūrah's Deepest Wordplay**

The verse ends with the verb the sūrah has been saving: *fa-sawwāhā* — "then leveled it," or, as the source commentary records, "He distributed it equally." The verb is *sawwā* — the very same verb used in verse 7 for the fashioning of the soul: *"by the soul and the One Who fashioned it (sawwāhā)."* The repetition is the sūrah's most artful stroke, and the commentators make its meaning explicit. In verse 7, God *fashioned* the human soul with balance and proportion. In verse 14, God *leveled* the ground over the tribe that had unbalanced itself — and, in the reading transmitted by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, *"He made it equal among them,"* distributing the punishment evenly so that not one member of the consenting tribe escaped the fate all of them had earned. The same verb thus frames the two ends of the human story: the One who *proportions* the soul is the One who *re-levels* the ground when the soul destroys its own proportion. Creation and destruction, in the Qur'an's theology, are two movements of one hand. The source commentary draws the inference with precision: God can destroy human beings as easily as He created them, and His punishment is part of the continuing process through which He *fashions* the human soul — the leveling of Thamūd is not a departure from the work of verse 7 but its severest continuation. What the soul refused to let God shape by guidance, the earth was shaped by judgment.

**The Justice of the Even Punishment**

The reading "He distributed it equally" deserves a further pause, because it is the verse's rebuke to every later attempt to soften collective guilt. The punishment was *even* — *sawwāhā* — among them, as al-Qurṭubī transmits, because the crime had been even among them: the denial was collective, the consent was collective, and so the recompense was collective. No rank shielded the noble; no poverty excused the poor; no claim of "I only watched" divided the tribe into the guilty few and the innocent many. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of communal responsibility in its sternest form, and it stands as a permanent warning against the two great illusions of every society — that the powerful can escape the consequences their order produces, and that the silent can escape the consequences their silence permits. When the ground was leveled, it was leveled evenly, because the sin had been spread evenly. The verse will not let the reader exempt himself from his community by the thin plea of having held no knife.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:15

> **For He fears not the consequence thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Ends the Sūrah and the Case**

The sūrah closes on a single clause that hangs in the air like a final gavel: *wa lā yakhāfu ʿuqbāhā* — "and He fears not its consequence." The subject, in the reading adopted by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, is God: He does not fear the aftermath of destroying a people like Thamūd. The word *ʿuqbā* (from the root *ʿ-q-b*, "to follow after") means the consequence, the outcome, the thing that follows an act — and the verse's claim is that the One who leveled Thamūd looks at what He has done without a trace of anxiety about what may follow it. This is the note on which the sūrah ends, with no epilogue, no softening, no transition — because the silence after this verse is itself the message. The story of Thamūd is not a tragedy that ended badly; it is a judgment that ended *completely*, and the completeness of the ending is the point.

**Why God Does Not Fear: The Logic of a Judgment Without Appeal**

The word "fear" deserves its analysis, because the verse's whole force is in it. Fear, in the human case, is the emotion of one who faces a threat from something greater than himself — a superior power, a rival, a consequence he cannot control. A human judge fears the consequences of his verdict: the anger of the powerful, the revenge of the convicted man's kin, the review of a higher court, the verdict of history, the pricks of his own uncertainty. Human punishment is therefore always shadowed by anxiety — the punisher glances over his shoulder, hedges, spares, or overdoes, because he is not the final authority and he knows it. The verse sweeps all of that away in a single denial. God fears no consequence, because there is nothing above Him to threaten, nothing beside Him to rival, nothing after Him to review. The Qur'an states the principle in its clearest form:

> *"He is not questioned about what He does, but they will be questioned."* (Qur'an 21:23)

This is not, as a careless reader might suppose, an amoral claim of power. It is the foundation of the verse's *justice*. Because God's judgment is subject to no external pressure and no self-interested anxiety, it is the only judgment that can be perfectly just — untempered by fear and untempted by favor. The punishment of Thamūd was not the overreach of a frightened tyrant nor the underreach of a bribed magistrate; it was the exact sentence of the One before whom no one can appeal, and who therefore had nothing to lose or gain by the verdict. The verse, in denying God's fear, is affirming the purity of His justice.

**The Other Readings: The Fears the Verse Denies**

The source commentary preserves two further readings of the verse's subject, both transmitted by al-Qurṭubī, and both deepen rather than contradict the first. In the second reading, the subject is *the most wretched man* of verse 12: "and he did not fear its outcome" — the hamstringer who did not fear the consequence of his act, who struck the she-camel in the heedlessness of one who thinks the future will never arrive. On this reading, the verse closes the sūrah by contrasting the two parties of the story: the criminal who did not fear, and the Judge who has no need to fear. In the third reading, the subject is the prophet Ṣāliḥ: "and he did not fear its outcome" — the messenger who warned his people, fulfilled his duty of delivery, and therefore did not fear what came after, because he had done what was his to do and was spared the punishment. All three readings orbit the same center: fear, or its absence, is the measure of a party's standing in the drama. The criminal should have feared and did not; the prophet need not fear and was saved; God, who is above both, fears nothing — and the verse ends the sūrah by pointing, with unblinking calm, at the only one of the three whose fearlessness is absolute.

**The Warning Folded into the Ending**

The sūrah's first hearers in Mecca heard this ending as a warning aimed directly at themselves. The source commentary states it plainly: the verse is a warning to Quraysh that God has no need of them and could wipe them out as easily as He wiped out Thamūd. The Meccan aristocracy, confident in their wealth, their trade, their alliances, and their ability to outlast an isolated Prophet, were being told in this final verse that the One who had already leveled a greater tribe than theirs was not negotiating, was not anxious about consequences, and was not dependent on their consent. The threat was not an expression of rage but of *independence*: the Creator does not need His creatures' permission to hold them to account, and the security a people feels in its own strength is the security of the she-camel's hamstringers before the ground opened. This is why the sūrah ends where it does — on God's fearlessness rather than on Thamūd's fate — because the fearlessness is the point that reaches out of the story and touches the living hearer. The warning to every later power, individual or collective, is the same: you are not dealing with an equal, and your refusal changes nothing but your own standing.

**The Comfort Folded into the Same Ending**

Yet the same verse, read from the believer's side, is a profound comfort, and the tradition heard both notes in it. If God fears no consequence, then the believer's cause is safe in hands that cannot be shaken: the oppressor who seems unassailable, the injustice that seems permanent, the truth that seems to lose — all of them stand under a Judge who will settle every account without hesitation and without fear. The believer who is wronged is told, in this final verse, that the reckoning is not uncertain because the Reckoner is not anxious; the delay of justice in this world is not the absence of justice, but the forbearance of One whose timing needs no counsel and whose verdict fears no appeal. The sūrah that began by swearing on the faithful sun and the prepared earth ends by placing the entire human story under the single attribute that makes final justice possible: the fearlessness of its Judge. The wronged can rest in that fearlessness; the wrongdoer should tremble at it.

**A Sūrah That Ends as It Began: Light, Judgment, and the Soul**

Look back, at the end, over the whole arc. The sūrah opened with the sun and its morning brightness — the disclosure of light — and it has moved through the whole created order to the human soul, to the two roads that soul is inspired to know, to the verdict that the soul is either grown or buried, and finally to the tribe that buried itself and the Judge who buried it without fear. The ending mirrors the beginning in a final, quiet symmetry: the sun's brightness *disclosed* the world each day, and the judgment of Thamūd *disclosed* the hidden truth of a people's hearts on the day of their ruin; the Fashioner who *proportioned* the soul (v. 7) is the same who *leveled* the ground (v. 14) and the same who *fears nothing* in the doing of either (v. 15). The sūrah's fifteen verses are a complete circle: from the light in the sky to the light and dark in the soul, and back to the Light by which all of it is judged. What remains, when the recitation ends, is the single question the sūrah has been asking since its first oath, and which it leaves, deliberately, unanswered — because the answer is the hearer's to give: *by the soul and the One Who fashioned it, will you purify it, or bury it?*
