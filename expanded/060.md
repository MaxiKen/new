# Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah (Chapter 60) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named After a Test**

Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah is one of the shortest of the long Madinan chapters — thirteen verses — and yet it carries a weight far beyond its length, because it legislates in the most delicate area of human life: where the heart's loyalties are allowed to rest when those the heart loves are on the other side. Its name is drawn from a single imperative in verse 10, *fa-mtaḥinūhunna*, "so examine them," from the root *m-ḥ-n*, which carries the sense of testing something to find out what it really is, as a metalworker tests ore or a merchant tests a coin. The passive participle *mumtaḥanah* means "the one who is examined," and by long-standing convention the sūrah is named after the woman it examined: Umm Kulthūm bint ʿUqbah, an Emigrant who walked out of Makkah alone and was tested when she arrived. It is worth pausing on that fact before a single verse is read. Of the 114 chapters of the Qur'an, this is the one whose title is taken from a woman, and the test that gave the chapter its name was a test of her sincerity, her safety, and her right to be believed.

**When It Came Down, and Why the Date Matters**

The sūrah is Madinan. The soundest position places its revelation after the sixth year of the Hijrah — that is, after the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah in 6/628 — because verses 10 and 11 legislate precisely for the situation the treaty created, and cannot be understood apart from it. Some early authorities held that the opening verse came down on the day Makkah was taken, in 8/630, and the difference between the two datings is not a contradiction but a window into how the chapter works: it was revealed in a decade whose central political question was the relationship between the new community and the old city that had driven it out. Read it against that background and every verse finds its temperature. Read it in abstraction and it becomes a slogan; read it in context and it becomes a legal and moral system.

Its position in the written copy of the Qur'an is itself instructive. It sits in the cluster of Madinan sūrahs that deal with community, loyalty, and inner division: al-Mujādilah (58) on the sanctity of private counsel and on the rule that a believer cannot embrace those who oppose God and His Messenger even if they are his own father or brother; al-Ḥashr (59) on exile, property, and the discipline of the collective; al-Mumtaḥanah (60) on loyalty and its limits; then al-Ṣaff (61), al-Jumuʿah (62), al-Munāfiqūn (63) and al-Taghābun (64) on rank, assembly, dissimulation, and the final settling of accounts. Verse 58:22 — *"You will not find a people who believe in God and the Last Day loving those who oppose God and His Messenger, even if they were their fathers, or their sons, or their brothers, or their kindred"* — is the seed; al-Mumtaḥanah is that seed grown into a full ruling, complete with exceptions, procedures, and financial settlement.

**The Four Names, and the Frame They Draw**

The chapter carries several titles, and each one points at a different center of gravity. It is al-Mumtaḥanah, "The Examined One," after verse 10. It is also called al-Imtiḥān, "The Examining," and by some "The Examiner," in which case the name describes the sūrah's own action rather than its subject: it is a chapter that examines and exposes the faults of human beings — a quality, as al-Qushayrī observed, that belongs to the Qur'an as a whole. And it is called al-Mawaddah, "Affection," after two phrases: *"you offer them affection"* in verse 1 and *"God will forge affection"* in verse 7. Those two phrases are not incidental. They form a frame around the whole chapter: at the beginning, an affection wrongly directed, a warmth handed to those who would use it against you; at verse 7, an affection rightly and miraculously created, a former enemy turned into family. Between the two poles every ruling of the sūrah is positioned. The chapter is, in a real sense, a treatise on the management of the heart's attachments.

**The Architecture of Thirteen Verses**

The sūrah falls into clear movements, and seeing them prevents the reader from mistaking its hard verses for its whole message. Verses 1–3 prohibit affectionate alliance with an enemy party, expose the psychology of such an alliance, and set it against the Day of Resurrection, when no family connection will be worth anything. Verses 4–6 set before the reader the concrete model of Abraham and those with him — a disavowal that is firm in doctrine yet tender in prayer, since Abraham's own words for his father are quoted and then expressly excepted from imitation. Verse 7 opens the door again: it may be that God will forge affection between you and those who are now your enemies. Verses 8 and 9 deliver the chapter's most cited and most frequently misunderstood pair of rulings: kindness and justice are commanded toward those who have not fought or expelled you; alliance is forbidden only with those who did. Verses 10 and 11 settle, with remarkable financial exactness, the case of believing women who emigrated and of believing men whose wives defected. Verse 12 records the pledge taken from believing women. Verse 13 returns to the opening prohibition and closes the circle. Al-Qushayrī noticed precisely this: the sūrah ends with the injunction with which it began — do not take God's enemies as intimates — so that the chapter is a ring, and the reader leaves it exactly where he entered, but instructed.

**The Hard Teaching and Its Guardrails**

Any honest reading must acknowledge that this chapter legislates *al-walāʾ wa-l-barāʾ* — loyalty and disavowal — and that the phrase has been used by some to justify contempt for the whole of humanity outside the faith. The sūrah itself is the strongest possible corrective to that use, and it supplies the corrective in its own body rather than leaving it to later interpretation. The disavowal it commands is not hatred of persons; it is the refusal of a political and military alliance against one's own community. It is bounded by three conditions named explicitly in verse 9 — fighting, expulsion, and support of expulsion. It is accompanied by a positive command in verse 8 — righteousness (*birr*) and justice (*qisṭ*) toward everyone else. It is softened by hope in verse 7 — today's enemy may be tomorrow's kinsman. And it is capped by the Day of Resurrection in verse 3, which removes the temptation to imagine that belonging to the right group will substitute for one's own deeds. A doctrine read together with these guardrails produces firmness without fanaticism; the same doctrine read without them produces something the Qur'an itself would condemn.

**The Women This Sūrah Protects**

A further feature deserves emphasis, because it is easy to miss. The last four verses of the chapter are almost entirely about women. They establish that an emigrating believing woman must be examined rather than assumed, that once her sincerity is established she may not be handed back to a hostile husband as a clause in a treaty, that the husband's financial loss must nonetheless be compensated in money, that a believing man must dissolve the marriage bond with a wife who remains in disbelief, that the settlement runs in both directions, and that believing women give their own pledge — in their own names, in their own voices, with their own responsibility before God. Verse 12 is the only place in the Qur'an where a pledge of allegiance is recorded in feminine grammatical form, addressed to women. The chapter that takes its name from a woman tested ends by giving women an independent covenant with the Prophet and, through him, with God.

**A Key to the Classical Voices Cited Below**

The commentary that follows draws on the source material in the workspace and on the internal scholarly tradition it represents: the great sequential exegesis of al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923), *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān*; al-Zamakhsharī's (d. 538/1144) linguistic *al-Kashshāf*; al-Rāzī's (d. 606/1210) rational and doctrinal *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb*; al-Qurṭubī's (d. 671/1273) jurisprudential *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān*; Ibn Kathīr's (d. 774/1373) *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm*, with its reliance on transmission; al-Qushayrī's (d. 465/1072) spiritual *Laṭāʾif al-Ishārāt*; al-Baghawī's (d. 516/1122) *Maʿālim al-Tanzīl*; al-Wāḥidī's (d. 468/1075) *Asbāb al-Nuzūl*, the classic collection of occasions of revelation; al-Ālūsī's (d. 1270/1854) *Rūḥ al-Maʿānī*; the concise *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn*; and, for questions of creed, the writings of Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 728/1328) and the reports of Ibn ʿAbbās (d. 68/687–88), the Companion whom the Prophet ﷺ called the interpreter of the Qur'an. Prophetic reports are cited with their collections identified.

**How to Read What Follows**

Each of the thirteen verses is treated below as an independent discourse, in strict order, quoted individually and expanded through the Arabic language, the occasions of revelation, the classical commentators, the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ, the logic of the ruling, and its bearing on modern life. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is short; if anything, the chapter's brevity invites the reader to slow down.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:1

> **O you who believe! Take not Mine enemy and your enemy as friends—you offer them affection, though they have indeed disbelieved in the truth that has come to you, expelling the Messenger and you for having believed in God your Lord—if you have gone forth striving in My way and seeking My good pleasure. You show them affection in secret, while I know best that which you hide and that which you disclose. And whosoever among you does so has indeed strayed from the right way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Law Revealed Over an Intercepted Letter**

Very few legal verses of the Qur'an come with their own documentary evidence attached. This one does. The occasion of its revelation is one of the most thoroughly transmitted incidents in the biographical and exegetical literature, and its details matter because the verse is not a general condemnation of contact with non-Muslims — verse 8 will forbid reading it that way — but a ruling about a specific kind of act by a specific kind of person.

The Prophet ﷺ was preparing to march on Makkah. A Companion named Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿah wrote to the Quraysh informing them of the movement, and sent the letter with a traveling woman. The Prophet ﷺ sent ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām, and al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad after her, with instructions to find her at a garden called Rawḍat Khākh. They did; she denied carrying anything; they made clear they would search her; and she produced the letter from her hair-braid. When Ḥāṭib was confronted, he did not deny the act — he explained the motive, and the explanation is the heart of the whole verse:

> "O Messenger of God, do not be hasty with me. I was a man attached by alliance to the Quraysh, but I was not one of them. Those with you of the Emigrants have relatives in Makkah who will protect their families and their property. I had no kinship there to protect me, so I wished to gain a hand with them by which my relatives would be protected. By God, I did not do it out of disbelief or apostasy, nor was I pleased with disbelief after Islam." (al-Bukhārī 3983, 4890, 6259; Muslim 2494)

The Prophet ﷺ accepted the explanation. "He has told you the truth," he said. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, with the severity that was his signature, demanded: "O Messenger of God, let me strike the neck of this hypocrite." And the Prophet ﷺ answered with the words that have been quoted ever since:

> "He witnessed Badr. And what makes you know? Perhaps God looked upon the people of Badr and said: *Do as you wish, for I have forgiven you.*" (al-Bukhārī 3983, 4028; Muslim 2494)

**Two Readings of One Act, Both Correct**

The incident is often cited as proof of the Prophet's clemency, and it is. But it is also proof of something else that is easy to overlook: the act itself was condemned. The Prophet ﷺ forgave the man and refused to execute him; the revelation that followed refused to call the deed acceptable. He separated the person from the act — a distinction that legal systems spend centuries learning. Mercy toward the actor did not require a redefinition of the action. Anyone who wants to use this story to argue that loyalty to God's enemies is a trivial matter has to explain why the verse was revealed at all, and why it uses the language of straying from the middle path.

There is a further subtlety. Ḥāṭib's motive was not treason in the sense of wanting the Muslims destroyed; it was the anxious calculation of a man without tribal protection trying to buy insurance for his family. The verse therefore addresses not only the deliberate traitor but the ordinary believer who quietly hedges his bets — who keeps a channel open with the other side "just in case." That is a far more common human failing than outright betrayal, and it is the more useful lesson for anyone living as a minority, an émigré, or a convert whose family is on the other side of a divide.

**What a Walī Actually Is: The Vocabulary of Alliance**

The prohibition is expressed with the plural noun *awliyāʾ*, from the root *w-l-y*, one of the densest roots in the Qur'an. Its basic sense is proximity — being next to, being close enough to touch — and from proximity come all the meanings that cluster around it: guardian, protector, patron, ally, intimate friend, master, and, in its divine form, the one who takes charge of a servant's affairs. God is called *al-Walī* (2:257; 42:28), and the believers are described as *awliyāʾ* of one another (9:71). So when the verse says *"do not take Mine enemy and your enemy as awliyāʾ,"* it is not forbidding friendship in the sentimental sense of liking someone; it is forbidding the transfer of a structural bond. A *walī* is someone you entrust with your security, your secrets, your future, and your side in a conflict.

Al-Ṭabarī is explicit that the word may be rendered "protectors," "helpers," or "allies," and the classical exegetes consistently read it in the register of political and military allegiance rather than personal warmth. This is why verse 8 of the same sūrah can command righteousness and justice toward non-hostile non-Muslims without contradicting verse 1: the two verses are speaking about different things. One is about *muwālāh*, the giving of alliance; the other is about *birr* and *qisṭ*, the giving of kindness and fair dealing. Confusing the two has produced both errors that plague modern readings of this chapter — the fanatic who treats every non-Muslim as an enemy, and the apologist who claims the Qur'an forbids nothing at all.

Note also the possessive order in the phrase: *"Mine enemy and your enemy."* God's enemy is named first. The verse teaches that the primary reason not to ally with the opponents of the message is that they are opponents of God, not merely opponents of the Muslims. Theological loyalty precedes political loyalty. That ordering also protects the believer from a subtle corruption: if the prohibition were based only on tribal interest, it could be renegotiated whenever interest changed.

**"You Cast Affection Toward Them"**

The participial clause that follows — *tudlūna ilayhim bi-l-mawaddah*, "you offer them affection" — uses a verb whose root *d-l-w* refers to lowering a bucket into a well, or casting something toward someone. The image is of a hand reaching out and throwing something across a gap. The affection described is not a passive feeling but a transmission: information, reassurance, confidence, access. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this verse, states plainly that the phrase describes conveying the community's secrets to its enemies. And in the case of Ḥāṭib it was literally true — the affection took the form of a letter.

This is the verse's sharpest psychological insight. People rarely betray a community by declaring enmity toward it. They betray it by small courtesies: a shared confidence, a warning passed along, a name mentioned, a schedule disclosed. The Arabic captures this because *mawaddah* denotes a love that expresses itself, that wants to be seen and reciprocated, as distinct from *ḥubb*, which can remain entirely interior. The verse indicts expressed affection — the outward gesture by which a person signals to the other side that he is not really against them.

**A Conditional That Is Really a Rebuke**

The clause *"if you have gone forth striving in My way and seeking My good pleasure"* has generated extensive grammatical discussion, because a prohibition followed by a condition of this shape is unusual. The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī, note that the conditional here functions as reproach rather than as permission: the sense is roughly, "How can you do this, if you have truly gone out in My way?" The condition is not a loophole through which the prohibition might be escaped; it is a mirror held up to the reader. If you really did emigrate, really did strive, really did seek God's pleasure rather than your family's safety — then this letter makes no sense. The two halves of your life contradict each other.

The rhetorical effect is severe and merciful at once. It does not merely say "this is forbidden." It says "this is beneath what you claim to be." Moral instruction that works this way is far more powerful than a bare prohibition, because it appeals to the person's own self-understanding rather than only to his fear. It is the same technique as 3:139 — *"Do not weaken and do not grieve, for you shall be the uppermost, if you are believers"* — where the "if" is not doubt but invitation.

**God Knows What You Conceal**

Then comes the verse's pivot from public to private: *"You show them affection in secret, while I know best that which you hide and that which you disclose."* The word for "in secret" is *sirr*, and the pairing of hidden with disclosed is a recurring Qur'anic formula for God's exhaustive knowledge (2:33; 27:74; 28:69). Placed here, at the end of a verse about a secret letter, it is almost unbearably pointed. The act that began as a private calculation, hidden in a woman's hair, is met by the statement that God's knowledge has no blind spots.

The verse thus performs the very thing al-Qushayrī said the whole sūrah performs: it examines. Before it legislates, it discloses. This is why the chapter is called "The Examining." A believer who reads verse 1 honestly is not primarily being warned against joining an enemy army; he is being shown that his heart has been audited. And the psychological consequence of that is not despair but clarity: the person who understands that God sees the small hedge knows that the hedge gains him nothing, since the God who sees it is also the God who controls the outcome he was hedging against.

**"He Has Strayed From the Level Path"**

The verse closes with *fa-qad ḍalla sawāʾa al-sabīl* — "he has strayed from the middle of the road." The word *sawāʾ* literally means the middle, the level, the even part of something; *sawāʾ al-sabīl* is the center of the path, the part where a traveler can see what is coming from either side. To lose it is not merely to leave the road but to be off-balance on it. The Qur'an uses the same phrase in 2:142 of the Muslim community itself, made *"a middle nation"* — *ummatan wasaṭan* — so that the concept carries an added resonance here: the community's defining quality is its centeredness, and the man who splits his loyalty loses exactly that quality.

The logic is straightforward and worth stating plainly. A person who owes his protection to one side and his affection to the other has, in practice, no side. He cannot be relied on by either. He is the element in any structure that makes the whole unpredictable. The verse is therefore not only theological; it is a statement about the mechanics of trust, which is why it applies equally to a marriage, a partnership, a workplace, or a nation.

**Where This Verse Lands Today**

Stripped of its seventh-century particulars, verse 1 addresses a condition that has become more common, not less. The convert whose parents still oppose his faith; the immigrant who fears for relatives under a hostile regime; the employee with access to information his community depends on; the activist whose funding comes from those who despise his cause; the ordinary person who maintains a discreet friendship with people who mock everything he holds sacred. In each case the verse asks the same question: are you transmitting to the other side things that belong here?

And the verse's own mercy is that it names the failure without erasing the person. Ḥāṭib was not executed, not expelled, and not stripped of his standing; he remained a Companion of Badr. What the revelation did was draw a line and say, clearly, that he had crossed it. Believers have often wanted the second thing without the first — forgiveness without naming — but the two together are what make repentance possible. A sin that is never named cannot be repented of; a sinner who is never forgiven cannot come back.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2

> **Were they to come upon you, they would be enemies unto you and would stretch forth their hands and their tongues against you in evil. And they wish you to disbelieve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Test Verse 1 Deliberately Skipped**

Verse 1 forbade an alliance; verse 2 explains why the prohibition is not paranoia. The argument is a counterfactual, and its grammar does the work: *in yathqafūkum*, "if they were to come upon you / get the better of you." The conditional particle *in* here introduces a real possibility, and the verbs that follow — *yakūnū*, *yabsuṭū*, *waddū* — are cast so that the reader is asked to imagine a situation in which he is not the stronger party. That is the whole point. The believer who is considering a discreet friendship with the other side is, almost by definition, imagining a world in which he is safe. The verse removes the assumption: consider the world in which they have the upper hand. What then?

The verb *yathqafūkum* comes from the root *th-q-f*, which means to find and seize, to catch up with, to overpower — the same root that appears in 2:191, *"and slay them wherever you come upon them,"* and in 33:61, of the hypocrites, *"wherever they are found, they will be seized and slain."* It is a verb of capture. The verse is not describing a difference of opinion; it is describing capture.

**Hands and Tongues: The Order of the Two Weapons**

*"They would stretch forth their hands and their tongues against you in evil."* The commentators — al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭabarī among them — explain that stretching forth the hands means physical assault, fighting, and killing, while stretching forth the tongues means insult, revilement, and defamation. What deserves attention is that both are named, and that the hands are named first.

The sequence is not accidental. It describes the normal order of hostility: first the physical or material harm, which is the more visible and the more legally actionable, and then the verbal harm, which is easier to dismiss as "just words." The verse refuses to dismiss it. In the Qur'an's moral universe, the tongue is an instrument of injury comparable in kind to the hand, because both aim at destroying a person — one destroys his body or property, the other his standing, his reputation, his peace of mind. This is why the Qur'an legislates against slander (24:4), backbiting (49:12), mockery (49:11), and the spreading of shameful reports (24:19) with the same seriousness it legislates against theft.

There is also a second, more uncomfortable reading of the order. When a hostile party has power over you, the hand usually comes before the tongue because the tongue needs the hand's protection to be unleashed. People say the worst things about a group once that group can no longer answer. Anyone who has watched a public figure's fall, or a minority's sudden vulnerability, or a workplace where a colleague lost his backing, recognizes the pattern: the moment the hand is free, the tongue follows, and what was said behind closed doors becomes the public record.

**The Root That Runs Through the Sūrah**

The final clause — *"and they wish you to disbelieve"* — is *wa-waddū law takfurūn*. The verb *waddū* and the noun *mawaddah* in verse 1 share the root *w-d-d*, and this is one of the most economical pieces of wordplay in the Qur'an. In verse 1 the believer is described as casting *mawaddah* toward the enemy. In verse 2 the enemy is described as *waddū* — wishing, longing — for the believer's disbelief. The same root that names the believer's affection names the other side's appetite.

The exegetical conclusion drawn from this, and it is a hard one, is that the affection is not reciprocal in the way the person offering it assumes. The believer imagines a relationship; the other party is managing an opportunity. Where the believer offers warmth expecting warmth, the verse says the warmth being received is a means toward a specific end: your return to disbelief, your moral compromise, your abandonment of the community. This is not a claim that every non-Muslim harbors that wish — verse 8 explicitly denies it, commanding kindness toward those who have not fought or expelled the believers. It is a claim about the party under discussion in verses 1 and 2: those who disbelieved in the truth, expelled the Messenger and the believers, and would seize them if they could.

The same observation about asymmetry appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. *"O you who believe! Do not take as intimates anyone other than your own people; they spare no effort to corrupt you; they love that you should suffer"* (3:118). And 2:109 — *"Many of the People of the Book wish, out of envy in themselves, that they could turn you back into disbelievers after you have believed, after the truth has become clear to them."* The Qur'an treats this as a documented feature of hostile parties, not as a universal prejudice.

**Why Realism Is Not Cynicism**

A reader may object that the verse teaches suspicion, and that suspicion corrodes community life. The objection confuses two things. Cynicism is the habit of assuming the worst of everyone without evidence. Realism is the refusal to assume the best of people who have already shown you what they do. The Qur'an legislates the second and condemns the first — indeed, verse 8 of this very sūrah commands righteousness toward those who have given no cause for hostility, which is the opposite of cynicism.

The distinction can be stated as a principle: judge parties by what they have done and what they have said they want, not by what you need them to be. Ḥāṭib needed the Quraysh to protect his family. What the verse asks is that he weigh what they had actually done — expelled him, driven out the Messenger, fought three wars — against what he hoped they would do. Hope is a legitimate motive in supplication; it is a poor foundation for security arrangements.

**The Modern Shape of Hands and Tongues**

Translated into present conditions, the two weapons of the verse are instantly recognizable, and the tongue has become the more powerful of the two. Physical persecution still exists in much of the world, but in most societies the primary harm an individual suffers from a hostile majority is reputational and economic: being reported, doxxed, misquoted, caricatured, denied employment, stripped of professional standing. The verse names that harm in the same breath as violence, and thereby forbids the believer to console himself that "it is only talk."

It also warns against the specific form of self-deception that dominates minority life: the belief that if one is agreeable enough, discreet enough, and unobtrusive enough, the hand will stay down and the tongue will soften. The verse's counterfactual is a direct answer. Their posture toward you is not a function of your agreeableness; it is a function of their power and their aims. That is why the Qur'anic strategy for the vulnerable believer is not camouflage but *taqwā* — careful awareness of the actual terrain, the way a driver slows on an icy road not because he fears the road but because he respects what it can do.

**The Verse Ends on Their Wish, Not Your Fear**

One final detail deserves notice. The verse closes not with a description of your danger but with a description of their desire: *wa-waddū law takfurūn*. The danger is instrumental; the desire is the goal. What they ultimately want is not your wealth, your land, or your silence but your unbelief. That reframing matters, because it tells the believer what is actually at stake in the transaction he is contemplating. He is not merely risking embarrassment or exposure. He is being asked, slowly and through a thousand small accommodations, for the one thing the verse says his counterpart most wants.

And that is why the answer in verse 3 is not a call to fight but a call to remember the Day of Resurrection. When the temporal stakes are set out, the eternal ones must be set beside them, or the calculation will always be won by the nearest danger.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:3

> **Your family relations and your children will not benefit you on the Day of Resurrection; He will distinguish between you. And God sees whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reason Ḥāṭib Wrote the Letter**

Verses 1 and 2 described the act and its risk. Verse 3 addresses the motive behind the act, and it does so with surgical precision, because the motive was family. Ḥāṭib's letter was not treason for gain or for revenge; it was an attempt to purchase protection for relatives he could not otherwise defend. Verse 3 answers him — and every believer who has ever compromised for the sake of a parent, a child, or a spouse — with the sentence that ends all such calculations: on the Day of Resurrection, your family relations and your children will be of no benefit to you.

The logic is worth setting out plainly, because it is not a dismissal of family but a correction of its limits. If you betray the covenant for the sake of your family, you are making an exchange in which the only thing you can offer is your standing with God, and the only thing you can receive is temporary worldly protection. The verse states that the temporary protection will not follow you into the place where standing actually matters. You will have traded the permanent for the perishable, and the perishable will not arrive.

**Kinship Is the Qur'an's Most Attacked Illusion**

The word used for family relations is *arḥām*, the plural of *raḥim*, the womb — and it shares its root with *raḥmah*, mercy. The Qur'an treats the ties of the womb with enormous seriousness: it commands their maintenance (4:1; 47:22), condemns their severance as a sign of corruption on earth (2:27; 13:25), and the Prophet ﷺ made their upkeep a condition of entering the Garden. Yet the same Qur'an refuses, in a dozen places, to allow those ties to become a substitute for one's own accountability. *"The Day when neither wealth nor children avail, save for him who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). *"And fear a Day when no soul will avail aught for another soul"* (2:48). *"Then when the Trumpet is blown, there will be no ties of kinship among them that day, and they will not ask one another"* (23:101). *"The Day a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his wife and his children"* (80:34–36). *"O mankind, reverence your Lord and fear a Day when no father will avail aught for his son, nor a son avail aught for his father"* (31:33).

The cumulative effect of these verses is a doctrine: in Islam, salvation is personal. There is no collective redemption through lineage, tribe, sect, or family name. This is one of the sharpest breaks the Qur'an makes with the tribal worldview of seventh-century Arabia, in which the individual was a limb of the clan and the clan bore his honor, his debts, and his blood.

**"He Will Distinguish Between You": Three Readings of One Word**

The phrase *yafṣilu baynakum* — "He will distinguish between you" — carries an important set of variant readings transmitted in the canonical recitations. The reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, the one used in most of the Muslim world, is *yafṣilu*, a simple form meaning "He separates." The reading transmitted from Ibn ʿĀmir, Ḥamzah, and al-Kisāʾī is *yufaṣṣilu*, an intensive form meaning "He separates thoroughly, in detail." And a third reading, attributed to Abū ʿAmr and Yaʿqūb, is *yufṣalu*, the passive, "they will be separated."

The intensive reading is theologically the richest, and it is the one most commentators dwell upon. It suggests not merely a division into two groups but a detailed, individual adjudication in which each person's case is examined on its own particulars. That reading is a direct answer to the tribal mindset: there will be no bulk judgments by clan. The father's faith will not be counted for the son, nor the son's for the father, nor the husband's for the wife — which, given that verses 10 to 12 concern precisely the situation of a believing woman married to an unbelieving man, is a deliberate placement.

The commentators also note a second syntactic possibility in the verse, which the translation renders by placing the phrase after a break: "Your family relations and your children will not benefit you. On the Day of Resurrection He will distinguish between you." On either reading the meaning is stable — separation and separate judgment — and the exegetes agree that the separation referred to is the dividing of believers from disbelievers.

**The Prophet Warned His Own Family First**

The most powerful commentary on this verse is not a commentary at all but an event. When the verse *"And warn your nearest kin"* (26:214) came down, the Prophet ﷺ stood at the mountain pass and called out tribe by tribe, and then, according to al-Bukhārī and Muslim, he addressed his own household with words of extraordinary bluntness:

> "O Banū Kaʿb ibn Luʾayy, save yourselves from the Fire. O Banū ʿAbd Manāf, save yourselves from the Fire. O Banū Hāshim, save yourselves from the Fire. O Banū ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, save yourselves from the Fire. O Fāṭimah bint Muḥammad, save yourself from the Fire — for I can avail you nothing against God. Ask me for whatever you wish of my property, and I can avail you nothing against God." (al-Bukhārī 2753, 4771; Muslim 204–206)

The Prophet ﷺ said this to his daughter, his aunt, and his uncle. He said it in front of the Quraysh, in a culture where a man's honor was inseparable from his clan, and he said it while he was himself the most powerful man in Arabia. He was not diminishing his family; he was refusing to sell them a lie. A leader who tells his own children that his position cannot save them has removed the most common source of religious corruption in history — the belief that proximity to holiness is itself holiness.

Muslim also preserves the summary principle, from Abū Hurayrah:

> "Whoever is slowed down by his deeds, his lineage will not hasten him." (Muslim 2699)

**The Two Men Who Bookend the Lesson**

Islamic history preserves two cases, both involving the Prophet's own uncle, that illustrate the verse's operation in both directions. Abū Lahab — an uncle, and the man of Sūrah 111 — was promised destruction despite being the Prophet's closest male relative. Abū Ṭālib — another uncle, the one who protected the Prophet through the worst years of persecution and without whom the mission might not have survived — died without professing faith, and the reports in al-Bukhārī record the Prophet's grief and the revelation that he could not intercede for him beyond what was permitted. The lesson drawn by the exegetes from both cases is identical: kinship with the greatest of men is not a currency on the Day of Resurrection.

The counterpart is the case of the believer inside a hostile family — Abraham in verses 4 to 6, the Companions whose parents remained in Makkah, and today the convert whose father will not speak to him. To that person verse 3 offers something that sounds like a loss and functions as a liberation. If your family cannot save you, then your family cannot damn you either. The convert is not carrying his parents' rejection into the next world; the estranged son is not carrying his father's displeasure; the emigrant woman of verse 10 is not carrying her husband's disbelief. Each stands alone, and each standing alone means each is judged on nothing but what he or she actually did.

**"God Sees Whatsoever You Do"**

The verse ends with *wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr* — "and God is Seeing of what you do." The name *al-Baṣīr* denotes perfect vision, and its placement here completes the movement begun in verse 1: *I know best that which you hide and that which you disclose.* The sūrah opens its argument with an epistemological claim before it makes a legal one. Before telling the believer what he must do, it establishes that he is already seen doing it.

This ordering has a practical consequence that moralists have noted for centuries. A prohibition that is not accompanied by the certainty of being witnessed is obeyed only where it is convenient. A prohibition that is accompanied by that certainty produces the inner quality the Qur'an calls *taqwā* — which is not fear in the sense of terror but the sustained, careful attention of a person walking a narrow path in the dark, aware that a misstep has consequences he cannot undo. Verse 3 does not threaten; it informs. And the information is the ground on which the rest of the chapter's rulings can be asked of ordinary human beings, who are otherwise quite capable of finding reasons.

**The Verse as an Answer to a Very Old Bargain**

Human beings have always tried to outsource their moral standing — to a family, a nation, a party, a church, a school, a following. Verse 3 closes that market. It does so not with cruelty but with a kind of severe dignity: you are important enough to be judged individually. There is no crowd to hide in and no sponsor to lean on, which means also that there is no crowd whose failure will be charged to you.

For the believer caught between two worlds — which is the condition this entire sūrah addresses — that is the single most stabilizing truth available. He does not have to keep one foot in each camp to guarantee his safety. His safety was never in the camps. It was in the sound heart of 26:89, which he carries alone, and which he will present alone.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4

> **There is indeed a beautiful example for you in Abraham and those with him, when they said to their people, “Truly we are quit of you and of all that you worship apart from God. We have rejected you, and enmity and hatred have arisen between us and you forever, till you believe in God alone”; save for Abraham’s saying to his father, “I shall assuredly ask forgiveness for you, though I have no power to avail you aught from God.” “Our Lord, in Thee do we trust, unto Thee do we turn in repentance, and unto Thee is the journey’s end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why Abraham, and Why Here**

After three verses of prohibition, the sūrah stops legislating and offers a portrait. This is a deliberate method, and it is one the Qur'an uses whenever it asks something hard of human beings: it does not merely command, it shows a person doing it. *Qad kānat lakum uswatun ḥasanatun* — "there has been for you a beautiful *uswa*" — and the same formula appears again in verse 6, so that the whole passage is framed by it.

The word *uswa* (also vocalized *iswa*) means a model, a pattern, something that is imitated, and in classical usage it can also carry the sense of a consolation or a solace — the thing that makes a hardship bearable because others have borne it. Both senses are active here. The example of Abraham is a pattern to copy and a consolation to lean on: you are not the first believer to be told to break with his own people.

Abraham is chosen because he is the Qur'an's archetype of the individual against the collective. He argued with his father (19:41–48), smashed the idols of his town (21:57–67), was thrown into a fire for it (21:68–70), debated with a king who claimed divinity (2:258), left his homeland with nothing (29:26), and was commanded, at the end of his life, to settle his wife and infant son in a waterless valley (14:37). He is the model of a man whose only allegiance was vertical. For a community that had just been told not to ally with the people who expelled them, no other example would have made sense.

**"Those With Him": Prophets or Followers?**

The phrase *wa-lladhīna maʿah*, "and those with him," was read in two ways by the early exegetes, and both readings are preserved by al-Ṭabarī. On the first, it refers to the prophets — meaning that the believer is directed to the example of all previous prophets, each of whom distanced himself from the disbelievers of his own community. On the second, favored by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī, it refers to those who followed Abraham — his believing household and companions.

The difference matters more than it appears. If *those with him* means the prophets, the passage is establishing a continuous line: every prophet has had to do this, and you are joining a chain. If it means Abraham's followers, the passage is establishing something more intimate: it is possible for a handful of ordinary people to stand with one man against a whole town. For a Muslim community in Madinah, which still had brothers and cousins in Makkah, the second reading was probably the more consoling. And in fact both are true, which is a recurring feature of Qur'anic ambiguity — the wider meaning does not cancel the narrower.

**"We Are Quit of You": The Vocabulary of Disavowal**

*"Truly we are quit of you and of all that you worship apart from God."* The Arabic *bariʾūna minkum* comes from the root *b-r-ʾ*, which means to be free of, clear of, released from — the same root as *al-Bārīʾ*, one of the divine names meaning the Maker who is free of all defect, and the same root used in the technical sense of a person being acquitted of a debt or released from an obligation. It is a legal and financial word as much as a religious one. To declare *barāʾah* from someone is to say: I no longer carry your liabilities, and you no longer carry mine.

This is the vocabulary the Qur'an uses throughout the chapter of al-Barāʾah (Sūrah 9), which opens: *"A disavowal from God and His Messenger, to those of the idolaters with whom you made a treaty"* (9:1). It is a formal, almost contractual severance — not a burst of feeling. That distinction is essential to reading verse 4 correctly. What is being declared is the end of a bond of mutual obligation, not the beginning of a program of harm.

And the verse is careful about the object. *We are quit of you and of all that you worship apart from God* — the disavowal is joined to the worship, not to the persons in isolation from it. The reason for the break is stated as theological, not tribal. Compare 60:8, which commands righteousness toward those who have not fought the believers, and the boundary becomes clear: the enmity declared here is a consequence of opposition to God, and it ends when that opposition ends.

**"Forever — Until You Believe"**

The most quoted clause in the verse is also the most frequently misread: *"enmity and hatred have arisen between us and you forever, till you believe in God alone."* The two halves must be read together, and the second half is not a decorative afterthought; it is the condition that governs the first. The word for forever is *abadan*, an unbounded duration — and immediately after it comes *ḥattā tuʾminū bi-Allāhi waḥdah*, "until you believe in God alone." An eternal enmity that terminates upon belief is, in logical form, not an eternal enmity at all; it is a suspended one. Its duration is defined by the other party's refusal, not by the believer's resentment.

The verse itself then demonstrates this in the life of the man it is citing. Abraham declared disavowal from his own father, and within a few generations his descendants were the people of God's covenant. The Prophet ﷺ declared disavowal from the Quraysh, and seven years later stood in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah with the same men, told them *"go, you are free,"* and married into their families. Verse 7 of this very sūrah — *it may be that God will forge affection between you and those of them with whom you are in enmity* — is the commentary on *abadan*. Anyone who quotes the "forever" without the "until" is quoting half a sentence.

**The Exception: The One Thing Not to Imitate**

Then the verse does something unusual: it quotes the model and then subtracts part of him. *Save for Abraham's saying to his father, "I shall assuredly ask forgiveness for you, though I have no power to avail you aught from God."* The commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qushayrī all say this — are unanimous that Abraham's seeking forgiveness for his father is not part of the beautiful example to be followed. It is cited precisely to be excepted.

The reasoning is set out in Sūrah 9:

> *"It is not for the Prophet and those who believe to ask forgiveness for the idolaters, even if they be kinsmen, after it has become clear to them that they are companions of the Blazing Fire. Abraham's asking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him; but when it became clear to him that he was an enemy of God, he repudiated him."* (Qur'an 9:113–114)

So the exception is explained twice, and the explanation is consistent: Abraham prayed for his father on the strength of a promise he had made — *"I shall ask my Lord to forgive you"* (19:47) — and when it became clear that his father would die in hostility to God, he withdrew the prayer. The Qur'an treats this with remarkable tenderness. It does not say Abraham sinned; it says he made a promise, kept it as long as keeping it was possible, and then desisted. And the very next verse of Sūrah 9 says, *"Abraham was indeed tender-hearted, forbearing"* (9:114) — the reason he had made the promise in the first place.

**What Intercession Can and Cannot Do**

The exception opens onto one of the Qur'an's most carefully bounded doctrines. Prophets and angels do pray for others: the angels pray for the believers, *"Our Lord, You encompass all things in mercy and knowledge; so forgive those who repent and follow Your way"* (40:7), and the Prophet ﷺ is told to seek forgiveness for the believers (3:159; 24:62; 47:19). Jacob agrees to seek forgiveness for his sons (12:98). But the Qur'an repeatedly states that such prayer cannot reach a person who has died in settled opposition to God:

> *"It is the same for them whether you ask forgiveness for them or do not ask forgiveness for them; God will never forgive them."* (63:6; see also 4:107; 9:80)

The boundary is drawn at death and at persistence. While a person lives, prayer for him is meaningful — indeed it is one of the last resources a believer has for an unbelieving relative, and the Qur'an nowhere forbids praying for the guidance of a living non-Muslim. After death, the matter is closed. The verse is thus a mercy in disguise for the believer with a dead unbelieving parent: it releases him from an impossible obligation and redirects his effort to what still works — his own deeds, and charitable acts on behalf of the deceased in the ways the tradition permits.

**"In You We Trust, To You We Turn, To You Is the Journey's End"**

The verse closes with the prayer of Abraham and his companions, and the prayer is a three-stage map of the spiritual life: *ʿalayka tawakkalnā* — upon You we rely; *wa-ilayka anabnā* — to You we turn in repentance; *wa-ilayka al-maṣīr* — and to You is the final destination.

*Tawakkul* is reliance combined with action, not reliance instead of action. Ibn Taymiyyah and the scholars of his school insist on the point repeatedly: the one who abandons the means has not trusted God, he has disobeyed Him, because God commands the means as well as the end. The classic illustration is the Bedouin who asked whether he should tie his camel or trust in God, and was told: *"Tie her and trust"* (al-Tirmidhī 2517, who noted the chain; the meaning is supported by the Prophet's words in al-Bukhārī 5730 about the plague and about fleeing a lion). Abraham is the model of *tawakkul* precisely because he acted at every step — he built, he migrated, he negotiated, he settled his family — and relied on God throughout.

*Inābah*, from *n-w-b*, means to return repeatedly, and it is the Qur'an's word for the posture of a soul that keeps coming back. It appears as a divine attribute too: God is *al-Tawwāb*, the One who returns to His servant again and again (2:37, 54; 49:12). The pairing is deliberate: reliance looks forward to what one needs, and return looks backward at what one has done. A spiritual life that has only one of the two is incomplete — reliance without return becomes self-satisfaction, and return without reliance becomes despair.

*Al-maṣīr* is the final place of arrival, from *ṣ-y-r*, to journey toward. Ending the prayer with it is the theological equivalent of verse 3. The believer who has just been told that his family will not benefit him on the Day of Resurrection is now given the words to say about it: *to You is the journey's end.* Not to my father, not to my tribe, not to my country. The disavowal of verse 4 and the consolation of verse 4 are the same sentence.

**Reading Abraham in a Modern Family**

The verse's most common modern application is not political but domestic. The convert, the young Muslim who has returned to practice after years away, the daughter who has stopped complying with her family's expectations — each of them is being told, by the most honored patriarch in three religions, that breaking with a family's belief is not the same as breaking with the family. Abraham disavowed his father's worship and continued to speak to him with the word *"O my dear father"* four times in the same passage (19:42, 43, 44, 45). He was told not to imitate the prayer for forgiveness; he was never told to imitate rudeness.

And the final instruction of the verse is where the modern believer should end: not with the declaration of enmity, but with the three words of the prayer. A believer who has been compelled to stand apart from his own people has been given a place to stand. He is not left in the rupture; he is moved into reliance, return, and destination.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:5

> **Our Lord! Make us not a trial for those who disbelieve, and forgive us. Our Lord! Truly Thou art the Mighty, the Wise.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Strangest Prayer in the Qur'an**

The prayer of verse 4 ended with destination; the prayer of verse 5 begins with something most readers do not expect to find in a believer's mouth — a petition for the sake of the disbelievers. *Rabbanā lā tajʿalnā fitnatan li-lladhīna kafarū* — "Our Lord, do not make us a trial for those who disbelieve." At first hearing this appears almost perverse: a persecuted community praying about the spiritual condition of its persecutors. Once the commentators unpack it, however, the prayer turns out to be among the most clear-sighted in the Qur'an, and one of the most urgently relevant to Muslims living in public view today.

The word *fitnah* is central and notoriously difficult. Its root *f-t-n* refers originally to the assaying of gold — putting metal into fire to determine whether it is pure. From that literal sense it acquired the meanings of trial, testing, persecution, temptation, sedition, civil strife, and apostasy. The Qur'an uses it in nearly all of these senses: *"Do the people think they will be left to say 'we believe' and will not be tried?"* (29:2); *"We will surely try you with something of fear and hunger"* (2:155); *"fitnah is worse than killing"* (2:191); *"beware a trial that will not strike only the wrongdoers among you"* (8:25).

To ask God not to make one a *fitnah* for others is therefore to ask not to become the fire in which someone else's faith or unbelief is assayed — not to become the reason another person goes wrong.

**Three Readings of the Petition**

The classical exegetes offer three main interpretations, and they are complementary rather than competing.

The first, favored by al-Baghawī, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Rāzī, is: *do not let the disbelievers defeat us, lest they conclude that they are on the truth.* The reasoning is psychological and precise. In the ancient world — and, honestly, in the modern one — military success was widely read as evidence of divine favor. If the believers are beaten, the disbelievers will say: had these people been right, God would not have abandoned them. The prayer asks God to protect the truth's credibility, not the believers' pride. The same petition is put in the mouth of Moses in Sūrah Yūnus:

> *"Our Lord, do not make us a trial for the wrongdoing people, and deliver us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people."* (Qur'an 10:85–86)

The second reading, which al-Rāzī also records, is: *do not let them overpower us, lest they be punished on account of what they do to us.* Here the petition is genuinely for the enemy — an implicit recognition that the disbelievers' own eternal fate is bound up with how they treat the believers, and that their cruelty will be reckoned to them.

The third reading, which follows from the second and which al-Qushayrī's spiritual commentary implies, is that the prayer expresses a desire that evil not befall the disbelievers at all, since persecution of believers is precisely the crime the Qur'an warns of in the severest terms:

> *"Truly those who persecute believing men and believing women, then do not repent, theirs shall be the punishment of Hell, and theirs shall be the punishment of burning."* (Qur'an 85:10)

A fourth, broader reading treats *fitnah* here as civil strife and asks that open conflict and chaos not break out between the two communities — a reading reinforced by the parallel in 25:20, where the Qur'an describes the prophets as tested by one another: *"We have made some of you a trial for others — will you be patient?"*

**The Frightening Symmetry: When the Believer Becomes the Argument**

Whatever the precise reading, the prayer encodes a principle that deserves to be stated in the plainest possible terms: the conduct of believers is evidence in the minds of unbelievers. A community that claims to possess the truth and then behaves in ways that obviously contradict it does not merely damage itself; it damages the claim. It supplies the skeptic with a ready-made argument that requires no research: *look at them.*

This is one of the most demanding ideas in the Qur'an's social ethics, and it is the reason the verse is not a curiosity but a burden. It means that a believer's honesty in business, fairness in divorce, temper in argument, and reliability in work are not private virtues with public side effects — they are, functionally, missionary acts. And the reverse holds with equal force. The dishonest merchant, the abusive husband, the fraudster who funds a mosque, the cleric with two lives, and the politician who quotes scripture while looting the treasury are each, in the precise language of the verse, a *fitnah* for those who disbelieve: an occasion for them to conclude that the faith is either false or irrelevant.

The Qur'an states the underlying logic explicitly elsewhere: *"Thus We have made you a middle nation, that you may be witnesses upon mankind"* (2:143). A witness is evaluated on his character. A community constituted as a witness has no option about being judged on its conduct.

**Why the Prayer Asks for This Rather Than for Victory**

It is worth noting what the prayer does not ask for. It does not ask for strength, or wealth, or conquest, or the destruction of the enemy — though all of these appear in other Qur'anic supplications, including the last verse of al-Baqarah. It asks first that the believers not become an occasion of misguidance for others, and only then for forgiveness for themselves.

That order is instructive. It places responsibility for the spiritual state of other people before the request for one's own pardon. It is a small instance of a wider Qur'anic pattern in which the believer's concern is not limited to his own salvation: the prophets plead for their communities even while being rejected (7:155; 11:47), and the angels of 40:7 pray for the believers before praying for themselves. A community that internalizes this prayer becomes one whose first question in a crisis is not *how do we win* but *what will our conduct prove to the people watching us*.

**"And Forgive Us"**

Then, after the petition for others, comes the petition for self: *wa-ghfir lanā* — "and forgive us." The commentators note that this is where the prayer should have begun by human instinct, and that its position second is deliberate. But there is also a causal link between the two halves. The reason a community becomes a *fitnah* for others is, in the ordinary course of things, its own sin — its injustice, its lying, its greed. Asking to be spared the first thing while leaving the second unaddressed would be incoherent. The prayer therefore moves from the symptom to the cause: do not make us a trial for them; and forgive us, because our faults are what would make us one.

This structure has a practical corollary for any believer who is distressed by the way his faith is represented in public. The verse suggests that the remedy is not primarily apologetics. It is repentance.

**"The Mighty, the Wise"**

The prayer closes with two divine names: *al-ʿAzīz*, the Mighty or Invincible, and *al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise. The pairing is standard in Qur'anic supplication and doctrinally precise: might without wisdom is tyranny, and wisdom without might is ineffectual. A believer praying to be protected from the fate of becoming an occasion of misguidance needs both — the power to prevent it and the wisdom to arrange affairs so that it does not occur.

Al-Rāzī and others observe that the two names also answer the two halves of the prayer. *Al-ʿAzīz* answers the request not to be given into the hands of the disbelievers, since it is God who controls victory and defeat. *Al-Ḥakīm* answers the request for forgiveness and for the ordering of affairs, since forgiveness and arrangement are matters of wisdom rather than mere power. Ibn Taymiyyah makes the same point in his treatment of the divine names: every name in the Qur'an is paired so that the believer's knowledge of God is not lopsided, and he warns against the error of those who fixate on power alone and lose sight of wisdom, or on mercy alone and lose sight of might.

**How This Prayer Is Used Today**

There are three situations in which a modern reader will find this verse almost uncomfortably exact.

The first is public controversy. When a Muslim community is at the center of a dispute — over a mosque, a school, a broadcast, a legal case — the verse asks a question that is rarely asked in the heat of it: will the way we conduct ourselves confirm or destroy the case we are making? The answer determines strategy far more than any press release.

The second is family life with non-Muslim relatives. A convert whose parents are watching him has been placed, whether he likes it or not, in the position the prayer describes. His parents' eventual judgment about Islam will be formed substantially by whether he became a better or a worse son after he believed. The verse tells him that this is not an unfair burden but a real one, and gives him the words to ask for help with it.

The third is leadership. Every person who represents a community — a teacher, an imam, a manager, a politician, a parent — is a *fitnah* in the technical sense for those who follow or watch him. The prayer is therefore appropriately made by anyone in a position where his failure will be charged to something larger than himself. That is why the verse belongs as much in the mouth of a manager as in the mouth of a prophet, and why the prophets made it first.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:6

> **You have a beautiful example in them for whosoever hopes for God and the Last Day. And whosoever turns away—truly God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Example Is Conditional on the Hoper**

Verse 4 said there is a beautiful example in Abraham and those with him. Verse 6 repeats the statement and adds a qualification that changes its character entirely: the example is *li-man kāna yarjū Allāha wa-l-yawma al-ākhira* — "for whoever hopes for God and the Last Day." The model does not change; the person who can use it does.

This is a subtle and important principle in Qur'anic pedagogy. An example is not a transferable object like a sum of money. It is a pattern that only becomes usable by someone who shares the example's horizon. Abraham gave up his father, his town, his homeland, and eventually his son's company, for a promise he would never see fulfilled in his lifetime. To a person who wants results in this world, that is not an example; it is a warning. To a person who expects God and the Last Day, it is a blueprint. The same data yields opposite conclusions depending on the timeframe of the reader — which is precisely why the verse names the timeframe before it names the model.

The commentators, al-Rāzī foremost among them, make the point directly: the example of Abraham and his people, disassociating themselves from the disbelievers of their own tribe, is the model for those who anticipate the meeting with God and hope for the reward of the Hereafter. Al-Zamakhsharī adds that *whoever turns away* refers to those who do not follow the injunctions of verses 1 to 4 and maintain ties with the enemies among the disbelievers.

**Rajāʾ: The Word Translated as "Hope"**

The verb *yarjū* comes from *r-j-w*, and *rajāʾ* in the Qur'an is not wishful thinking. It is expectation grounded in a known object. The Qur'an distinguishes it sharply from *tamannī*, which is empty longing (2:111; 4:123), and it almost always pairs hope with fear: *"they call upon their Lord in fear and hope"* (32:16); *"they hope for His mercy and fear His punishment"* (17:57).

The construction here is unusual and worth pausing on. The verse does not say "hopes for the reward of God"; it says "hopes for God" — *yarjū Allāh*. The object of the hope is God Himself. Al-Qushayrī's spiritual commentary seizes on this: to hope for God is to expect the meeting with Him, to want the encounter itself and not merely the gifts distributed at it. It is the difference between a servant who hopes for his wages and a servant who hopes to see his master.

This has a direct bearing on verse 6's function in the chapter. The command to break with hostile kin is unbearable unless the believer actually wants God — not God's benefits, but God. The Qur'an is honest about the psychology: a person whose ultimate desire is worldly security will always find a reason to hedge, as Ḥāṭib did. The cure for hedging is not more rules; it is a reordering of desire.

**"And the Last Day"**

The pairing of God with the Last Day is one of the most frequent formulations in the Qur'an — appearing in 2:62, 2:126, 5:69, and dozens of other places, usually in the phrase "whoever believes in God and the Last Day." Its recurrence in a verse about cutting ties with family is not incidental. The Last Day is the horizon at which family becomes irrelevant (verse 3) and at which one's own deeds become everything. To hope for it is to accept the terms on which verse 3 was stated.

There is also a structural point. The two halves of the pair correspond to the two poles of religious motivation: hope in God's presence and mercy, and expectation of the Day of reckoning. A person who has only the first becomes sentimental; a person who has only the second becomes hard. The Qur'an's balanced believer holds both, and it is that balance which makes the disavowal of verse 4 possible without cruelty.

**Abraham's Own Statement of the Same Principle**

The verse's model is confirmed by Abraham's own recorded words, which the commentators cite in parallel:

> *"Abraham said to his father and his people: Truly I am quit of that which you worship, save Him who originated me, for surely He will guide me. And he made it a word enduring among his posterity, that haply they might return."* (Qur'an 43:26–28)

Two features of that passage reinforce verse 6. First, the disavowal is again attached to the worship, with the explicit exception of the One who created him — the same structure as 60:4. Second, the phrase *"he made it a word enduring among his posterity"* means Abraham left the declaration as a legacy for his descendants, so that anyone who wanted the way back would find it preserved. Verse 6 is that preserved word reaching the seventh century and now the twenty-first.

**"Whoever Turns Away"**

Then the turn: *wa-man yatawalla* — "and whoever turns away." The verb is from *w-l-y*, the same root as the *awliyāʾ* of verse 1, and this is one of the Qur'an's characteristic uses of a single root to bind a passage together. The one who turns away is the one who refuses to be a *walī* of God and His Messenger; the root that named the false alliance in verse 1 now names the refusal of the true one.

The commentators specify that *whoever turns away* refers to those who decline the injunctions of verses 1 to 4 and keep their alliances with the hostile party. But the phrase is left general, and its generality is the point: it covers the person who turns away from any of the chapter's commands — who allies with an enemy, who refuses justice to a peaceful non-Muslim, who returns an emigrant woman to her persecutors, who breaks his pledge.

**"God Is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised"**

The consequence of turning away is stated in two divine names: *al-Ghanī*, the Self-Sufficient, and *al-Ḥamīd*, the Praised. Together they form a complete answer to the human instinct to think that God needs our allegiance.

*Al-Ghanī* means the one who is free of need, rich beyond dependence. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly to make a point that is as much logical as devotional: *"O mankind, you are the poor toward God, and God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised. If He willed, He could remove you and bring a new creation, and that is not difficult for God"* (35:15–17). *"And whoever is grateful is grateful for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — truly my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Generous"* (27:40). *"Whoever turns away — God is not in need of the worlds"* (3:97).

*Al-Ḥamīd* means the one who is praiseworthy in Himself, whether or not He is praised. The pairing teaches that God's being praised is not increased by human praise nor diminished by human ingratitude. This is the doctrine of divine *ghinā* — self-sufficiency — and it has a practical effect on the believer that is easy to miss: because God does not need you, your obedience is entirely for your benefit and your disobedience entirely to your loss. That is liberating rather than threatening, and the Qur'an says so: *"If you are ungrateful, you and all who are on earth — truly God is Self-Sufficient, Praised"* (14:8).

The rhetorical force in context is severe and calm at once. God is not wounded by your turning away. The loss is entirely yours. There is no bargaining position from which a human being can make God need him — which destroys the last excuse of the person who thinks his family, his tribe, or his country matters to God more than his own conduct does.

**The Two Verses Together as a Diagnosis**

Read verses 4, 5, and 6 as a unit and their structure becomes visible. Verse 4 gives the model. Verse 5 gives the prayer the model prayed. Verse 6 states who can follow the model and what happens to the one who will not. The middle verse — the prayer — is the hinge, and it explains why the sequence is arranged this way: nobody can imitate Abraham on willpower. The example is beyond unaided human capacity, which is why the example is shown praying, and why the reader is invited to use the same words.

The modern application follows directly. A believer asked to distance himself from a family, a social circle, a professional network, or a national identity that requires him to compromise his faith is being asked to do something Abraham did. He will not manage it by resolution. He will manage it by wanting the outcome Abraham wanted — God and the Last Day — and by asking, in Abraham's words, not to be made a trial for others and to be forgiven.

And if he turns away, the verse does not threaten him with divine anger so much as it informs him of a fact he may find more sobering: nothing will have changed for God. He will simply have arrived, alone, at the destination he named in verse 4 — *and to You is the journey's end* — having chosen, at every fork, the nearer comfort.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:7

> **It may be that God will forge affection between you and those of them with whom you are in enmity. God is Powerful, and God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Keeps the Door Open**

After six verses of disavowal, warning, and severance, the Qur'an inserts a single sentence of hope, and it is one of the most important sentences in the chapter for preventing it from being read as a permanent declaration of war on humanity. *ʿAsā Allāhu an yajʿala baynakum wa-bayna lladhīna ʿādaytum minhum mawaddatan* — "It may be that God will place affection between you and those of them with whom you are in enmity."

The particle *ʿasā*, when used of God, carries a special weight that the commentators consistently note. When a human being says "perhaps," he is expressing uncertainty about something he cannot control. When God says *ʿasā*, He is describing something He is fully able to bring about and is inclined toward. Al-Ṭabarī and the exegetes after him therefore read *ʿasā min Allāh wājib* — "a 'perhaps' from God is effectively a certainty" — a principle applied throughout the Qur'an, as in 94:5–6, *"with hardship comes ease,"* introduced in the same idiom.

**"Between You and Those of Them": The Qualifier That Saves Whole Peoples**

The most important word in the verse is the smallest: *minhum*, "of them" or "among them." The verse does not say God will forge affection between you and those with whom you are in enmity; it says between you and *those of them*. The enmity is with a party, and the party is not coextensive with the people.

This is the same principle that verses 8 and 9 will legislate explicitly — that a whole nation must not be treated as an enemy because part of it is hostile — and it appears here first, in the register of hope rather than law. The exegetes read *minhum* as a deliberate restriction. It teaches that the categories of the previous verses are not ethnic, racial, or civilizational. They are behavioral, and they can change, person by person.

**How God Forged the Affection: Three Historical Answers**

The commentators give concrete answers to the question of how this promise was fulfilled, and the answers are worth setting out because they show the verse was not a vague reassurance.

The first is marriage. Al-Qushayrī and al-Wāḥidī both note that God forged affection between the believers and their former enemies through marriage, and the paradigm case is Umm Ḥabībah bint Abī Sufyān. Her father was Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb, the leader of the Quraysh and the commander against the Muslims at Uḥud and the Trench; she had embraced Islam years earlier and migrated to Abyssinia, where her husband apostatized and she was left alone and destitute. The Prophet ﷺ sent a proposal to her through the Negus, who married them and gave a dowry of four hundred dinars on the Prophet's behalf — accounts preserved in the sīrah literature and cited by Ibn Kathīr. The daughter of the enemy's commander became a Mother of the Believers. Abū Sufyān himself, standing in Madinah, was told by his own daughter's household what had happened, and the enmity of two decades was undone by a marriage contract.

The second is guidance. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Jalālayn note that God forged affection by guiding the disbelievers to belief — which is what happened at scale at the conquest of Makkah in 8/630, when the Prophet ﷺ entered the city that had expelled him, stood at the Kaʿbah before the men who had fought him for twenty years, and, according to the sīrah accounts preserved by Ibn Isḥāq and cited by Ibn Kathīr, told them: *"Go, for you are free."* The verse had been revealed in 6 AH. Within two years, virtually every person the believers had been commanded to disavow in verses 1 to 4 was a brother in faith.

The third is kinship through offspring. The children of the Quraysh who embraced Islam became the next generation of the Muslim community — including Muʿāwiyah, the brother of Umm Ḥabībah, and ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar, whose father had been ʿUmar's companion. The genealogies of early Islam are a map of this verse being fulfilled.

**Hearts Are Joined Only by God**

The verse's theology is stated most clearly elsewhere in the Qur'an, in a passage that reads as its commentary:

> *"And He joined their hearts. Had you spent all that is in the earth, you could not have joined their hearts, but God joined them. Truly He is Mighty, Wise."* (Qur'an 8:63)

The verse was revealed concerning the Emigrants and the Helpers — two groups with a history of tribal warfare in Madinah, now bound into a single community — and its principle is general: affection between people who have no reason to love each other is not produced by human effort, negotiation, or money. It is produced by God.

That has an obvious corollary for the individual believer. If you cannot manufacture affection where there has been hostility, then the correct response to hostility is not to try harder at charm, appeasement, or self-erasure. It is to work on what you can control — your own conduct, your justice, your patience — and to leave the joining of hearts to the One who does it. This is why Ḥāṭib's letter was futile even in worldly terms: he was attempting by himself to produce the thing that only God produces.

The Qur'an uses the same word, *mawaddah*, for the affection between spouses: *"And among His signs is that He created for you mates from among yourselves, that you may find tranquility in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy"* (30:21). The choice of *mawaddah* in verse 7 is therefore charged: the affection God promises between former enemies is of the same quality as the affection between husband and wife — a settled, willing, expressive love, not merely a truce.

**"God Is Powerful"**

The verse closes with three names: *al-Qadīr*, the Powerful; *al-Ghafūr*, the Forgiving; *al-Raḥīm*, the Merciful. The sequence answers three objections a reader might raise.

*God is Powerful* answers the objection that such a transformation is unrealistic. Abū Sufyān was not going to become a Muslim. The Quraysh were not going to open Makkah. History says otherwise. The name is placed here precisely because the promise sounds impossible to anyone calculating from present conditions.

*Forgiving* answers the objection that the enemy's past is too heavy. The men who fought at Badr, who mutilated the dead at Uḥud, who besieged Madinah — God is Forgiving of their past deeds if they repent, as al-Jalālayn and others note. This is the theological ground of the general amnesty at Makkah. A believer who cannot imagine a person being forgiven has underestimated God's capacity, not the person's guilt.

*Merciful* answers the objection that the offer will not remain open. God continues to extend guidance; the door does not close while a person is alive. The Qur'an makes the point in its most expansive statement of hope: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (39:53).

**Why This Verse Is Where It Is**

The placement of verse 7 is a deliberate act of pastoral strategy. Verses 4 to 6 have commanded believers to declare enmity toward hostile kin — a command that, according to al-Wāḥidī's report, some Companions took so hard that they immediately declared their idolatrous relatives enemies. Knowing what that cost them, God revealed verse 7.

The lesson is general and should not be lost: hard commands in the Qur'an are consistently followed by relief, and the relief is not an afterthought. *"And whoever is patient and forgives — that is indeed of the matters requiring resolve"* (42:43) follows the permission to retaliate in kind. *"Indeed with hardship comes ease"* (94:6) follows the description of the burden. Verse 7 is al-Mumtaḥanah's instance of the pattern, and it tells the reader how to hold the chapter: the disavowal is real, but it is provisional; the categories are firm, but they are not final; and no human being should ever be written off, because the God who is being served is the One who changes hearts.

**The Practical Rule This Verse Establishes**

Reduced to practice, verse 7 gives the believer three rules.

Never treat any person as a permanent enemy. Enemies in this chapter are defined by conduct, and conduct can change — including yours.

Never treat any group as monolithic. *Minhum* — "among them" — is the Qur'an's standing prohibition on collective judgment, and it is repeated in verse 8 with legal force.

Never assume that reconciliation is your job to engineer. The joining of hearts is God's action. Your job is to be just, to keep the door unlocked, and to keep praying the prayer of verse 5. When the affection comes, it will not have come because you were clever. It will have come because God is Powerful, Forgiving, Merciful — and that is precisely why it can happen between people who, on any human calculation, should still be enemies.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8

> **God does not forbid you, with regard to those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes, from treating them righteously and being just toward them. Truly God loves the just.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Forbids Collective Guilt**

If verse 1 is the chapter most often quoted by its critics, verse 8 is the chapter most often ignored by its friends. It is a general, unqualified, positive instruction: toward those who have not fought you for your religion and have not driven you from your homes, God does not forbid you from acting righteously and dealing justly. It is the counterweight without which verses 1, 4, and 9 are unreadable, and it was revealed — the exegetes are unanimous — to correct an excess in the application of those verses.

The grammatical form deserves attention. *Lā yanhākumu Allāhu ʿan...* — literally, "God does not forbid you from..." — is a negation of prohibition, which in Arabic idiom functions as a permission, and here, as al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qushayrī all note, as something stronger: a positive exhortation, with the sense "God wishes you to do this." The Qur'an frequently understates its commands in this way (compare *"do not approach the property of the orphan except in the best manner,"* 6:152), and the understatement is rhetorical: a permission so evident that it did not need to be commanded is being commanded anyway, because people were getting it wrong.

**Birr and Qisṭ: The Two Words Chosen**

The verse names two obligations, and the choice of each word is deliberate.

*Birr*, from *b-r-r*, is the Qur'an's broadest word for goodness. It is the word used for the highest standard of righteousness: *"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but righteousness is belief in God... and giving wealth, out of love for Him, to kinsmen, orphans, the needy, the wayfarer, and those who ask"* (2:177). The same word describes dutiful kindness to parents (*birr al-wālidayn*), and the Prophet ﷺ defined it in the plainest terms:

> "Righteousness (al-birr) is good character, and sin is that which wavers in your heart and which you dislike people to know about." (Muslim 2553)

To command *birr* toward peaceful non-Muslims is therefore not to permit mere neutrality; it is to command the same category of goodness that Islam demands toward one's own parents.

*Qisṭ*, from *q-s-ṭ*, means equity — giving each person his due, measured and proportionate. It is the word used in *"O you who believe! Be steadfast witnesses for God in justice, and let not hatred of any people lead you to act unjustly. Be just — that is nearer to reverence"* (5:8). The Qur'an distinguishes *qisṭ* from *ʿadl* in that *qisṭ* emphasizes the distributive and measurable aspect of fairness: wages paid, prices honored, testimony given, contracts kept.

Together the two words cover both registers of human obligation: the warm and discretionary (*birr* — kindness beyond what is owed) and the cold and mandatory (*qisṭ* — exactly what is owed). The verse commands both. A Muslim who pays a non-Muslim employee fairly but is cold to him has fulfilled *qisṭ* and neglected *birr*. A Muslim who is warm in manner but cheats in the contract has done the reverse. Neither has obeyed verse 8.

**Asmāʾ and Her Mother**

The occasion of revelation is one of the best-attested in the sūrah, and it is worth telling at length because it shows the verse being applied to a real family in real time.

Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr — the daughter of Abū Bakr, the half-sister of ʿĀʾishah, and one of the earliest Muslim women — had a mother named Qutaylah bint ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā who remained an idolater. Her mother came from Makkah to visit her in Madinah, bringing gifts. Asmāʾ, uncertain whether receiving an idolatrous parent was permissible now that the sūrah's earlier verses had commanded disavowal, refused to admit her and sent to ask the Prophet ﷺ. Her report is in both Ṣaḥīḥs:

> "My mother came to me while she was unwilling regarding Islam, during the period of Quraysh's truce. I sought a ruling from the Messenger of God and said: 'My mother has come to me and she is averse — should I keep ties with her?' He said: 'Yes, keep ties with your mother.'" (al-Bukhārī 2620, 3183, 5978; Muslim 1003)

Some accounts preserved in the exegetical literature, including Aḥmad's collection as cited by Ibn Kathīr, add that the mother brought gifts of raisins and clarified butter, and that Asmāʾ would not accept them or admit her until the verse was revealed; she then received her into her house.

The incident is instructive on several levels. First, it shows that the Companions themselves were unsure how far the disavowal extended — which is exactly why verse 8 was needed. Second, it shows that the Prophet ﷺ's answer was immediate and unqualified: yes, keep ties with your mother. Third, and most important for the modern reader, the mother's religion was not an obstacle. Her idolatry did not suspend the daughter's duty, and the daughter's faith did not annul the mother's right.

The verse's specification — *those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes* — describes Qutaylah exactly. She had not fought; she had not expelled. She had come to visit her daughter. And the ruling for her was *birr*.

**"God Loves the Just"**

The verse ends with *inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-muqsiṭīn* — "truly God loves those who are just" — a formula repeated in 5:42 and 49:9. Its placement here is the theological key to the whole chapter. In the context of a sūrah about enemies, the statement means that God's love is extended to those who can discriminate — who can tell an enemy from a non-enemy and refuse to treat them alike.

Al-Qushayrī and the commentators draw precisely this conclusion: that God loves the just here indicates that God loves those who are able to discern between those who are enemies and those who are not, thus avoiding indiscriminate animosity. Indiscriminate hostility is not strength; it is a failure of the faculty of judgment, and the Qur'an treats it as a moral defect.

The same idea underlies the prohibition of *ghuluww*, excess in religion, and the Prophet's warning:

> "Beware of extremism in religion, for those before you were destroyed by extremism in religion." (al-Nasāʾī 3057; Ibn Mājah 3029; the wording is supported by Muslim 1718 on those who make things hard upon themselves)

A community that cannot distinguish between an armed enemy and a visiting grandmother has lost something that the Qur'an counts among the marks of faith.

**The Rule in Relation to 5:8 and 16:90**

Verse 8 does not stand alone; it is one of a set of Qur'anic texts that together form the Islamic law of dealing with non-hostile non-Muslims. Three are decisive.

> *"Let not hatred of a people who barred you from the Sacred Mosque lead you to transgress. Help one another in righteousness and reverence, and do not help one another in sin and aggression."* (Qur'an 5:2)

> *"O you who believe! Be steadfast for God, witnesses in justice; and let not hatred of any people lead you to act unjustly. Be just — that is nearer to reverence."* (Qur'an 5:8)

> *"Truly God commands justice, excellence, and giving to kinsmen; and He forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and aggression."* (Qur'an 16:90)

The last of these is the verse that ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz recited in his Friday sermon week after week until people memorized it, and it is the verse Ibn Masʿūd called the most comprehensive single verse in the Qur'an. Read together with 60:8, they establish that justice is not a concession granted to outsiders when convenient; it is an unconditional command whose scope includes precisely those the believer is most inclined to wrong.

The often-quoted maxim attributed to Ibn Taymiyyah in his treatise on public accountability (*al-Ḥisbah fī l-Islām*) states the same principle in political form: God upholds the just state even if it is unbelieving, and does not uphold the oppressive state even if it is Muslim. Whether or not every wording attributed to him is exact, the principle is squarely Qur'anic and is what 60:8 legislates at the level of individual conduct.

**What Verse 8 Covers in Ordinary Life**

The categories are not theoretical. *Those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes* describes, in most of the world today, almost everyone a Muslim meets: the neighbor, the classmate, the colleague, the landlord, the patient, the customer, the in-law, the parent, the fellow citizen. Toward all of them the verse commands *birr* and *qisṭ*, and the Prophet's own life supplies the content of those words: he returned a trust to the Quraysh who wanted him dead (he left ʿAlī behind at the Hijrah precisely to return their deposits); he visited a Jewish neighbor's sick son and invited him to faith; he stood up when a funeral procession passed, saying "is it not a soul?" (al-Bukhārī 1311; Muslim 961); he honored a treaty with people who broke it only after they broke it first.

The practical test the verse imposes is simple and uncomfortable. If a Muslim is kinder, fairer, and more trustworthy with his own people than with others, he has not obeyed verse 8; he has obeyed tribalism with a religious vocabulary. The verse's whole purpose is to break the automatic equation of loyalty with faith, and to replace it with a judgment that must be made case by case, on evidence, about actual conduct.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9

> **God only forbids you, with regard to those who fought you on account of religion and expelled you from your homes and supported your expulsion, from befriending them; whosoever befriends them, they are the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word "Only" Does the Work of a Legal Definition**

Verse 8 said what God does not forbid. Verse 9 says what God forbids, and the single word *innamā* — "only" — restricts the prohibition to a precise set of people defined by a precise set of acts. The particle *innamā* in Arabic functions as a restriction: it affirms what follows and negates everything else. The prohibition of *muwālāh* is therefore not a general rule about non-Muslims; it is a rule about a specific category, and the category is defined by three verbs.

*Qātalūkum fī al-dīn* — they fought you on account of religion. *Wa-akhrajūkum min diyārikum* — and they expelled you from your homes. *Wa-ẓāharū ʿalā ikhrājikum* — and supported your expulsion.

The commentators note that the three acts are joined by *wa-*, and discuss whether all three are required together or whether any one suffices. The stronger reading, and the one most consistent with the sūrah's structure, is that they describe a single hostile posture manifested in three ways: armed opposition motivated by religion, actual expulsion, and active collaboration in the expulsion of others. The person who merely disbelieves, merely disapproves, or merely stays out of the conflict is not in this category — verse 8 has already settled that.

This precision is what distinguishes Qur'anic legislation from collective animosity. The enemy is defined by conduct, not by identity, and the definition is written so that a reader cannot stretch it to include people he merely dislikes.

**Muwālāh Is Not Muʿāmalah**

The prohibition is *an tawallawhum* — "that you take them as *awliyāʾ*," the same verbal form and root as verse 1. Everything said in the commentary on verse 1 about the meaning of *walī* applies here: the prohibited act is the transfer of alliance, protection, confidence, and side-taking, not the ordinary dealings of life.

The classical jurists developed this into a clean distinction that modern readers frequently lose. *Muwālāh* — taking someone as a *walī* — means entrusting them with your community's security, your secrets, your political support, or your aid against your own people. *Muʿāmalah* — dealing with someone — means trade, employment, neighborliness, medical care, contracts, hospitality, and family ties. The first is forbidden in the circumstances of verse 9. The second is commanded in the circumstances of verse 8. Confusing them produces the two errors that have done the most damage to the reading of this sūrah: the extremist who refuses to buy bread from a non-Muslim, and the apologist who claims the Qur'an permits unrestricted political alliance with hostile powers.

The same distinction underlies the other Qur'anic texts on the subject, and they should be read together with this verse rather than in isolation from it:

> *"O you who believe! Do not take the Jews and the Christians as allies; they are allies of one another. And whoever among you takes them as allies, he is one of them."* (Qur'an 5:51)

> *"O you who believe! Do not take as intimates anyone other than your own people; they spare no effort to corrupt you."* (Qur'an 3:118)

> *"You will not find a people who believe in God and the Last Day loving those who oppose God and His Messenger, even if they were their fathers, or their sons, or their brothers, or their kindred."* (Qur'an 58:22)

Read against 60:8, all of these carry the same restriction: the prohibition attaches to *opposition*, *hostility*, and *corruption*, not to difference of belief as such. The exegetes from al-Ṭabarī onward are consistent in reading 5:51 and 3:118 in the context of active hostility, and 60:8–9 is the clearest internal evidence for that reading, since it is the Qur'an's own explicit statement of who is and is not in the prohibited category.

**Why the Rule Mattered Then**

The historical situation the exegetes describe explains the verse's urgency. In the years after the Hijrah, the Muslim community in Madinah was a small settlement surrounded by hostile powers, and its members were crosscut by kinship and treaty obligations to those powers. A Muhājir whose brother was in the Quraysh cavalry; an Anṣārī whose tribe was allied with a clan allied with Makkah; a merchant whose livelihood depended on a caravan route controlled by an enemy — each faced a daily choice between the safety of his family or his income and the security of his community.

Such clarifications, as the source commentary observes, were crucial at the beginning of the formation of the Islamic community. But the verse's language is not limited to that moment. Its terms — fighting on account of religion, expulsion from homes, support of expulsion — recur wherever a community of faith is persecuted, and the ruling travels with them.

**"Those Are the Wrongdoers"**

The verse ends with a verdict: *fa-ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūn* — "those, they are the wrongdoers." The word *ẓulm* means, at root, to place a thing in other than its proper place. The wrong described is not merely harm to others; it is the misplacement of loyalty. The person who gives his alliance to a party that fought his community and drove out his people has put his trust where it does not belong, and the Qur'an treats that as a disorder of the soul before it is a threat to the state.

The same word is used in the gravest context in the Qur'an — *"indeed association with God is a great wrong"* (31:13) — and its use here signals the seriousness with which the Qur'an regards divided allegiance. It is worth observing that the verse does not describe the offender as a disbeliever or an apostate. It describes him as a wrongdoer, which leaves the door open to repentance. This is consistent with the treatment of Ḥāṭib in verse 1, who committed the act and was not expelled from the community.

**The Boundary Cases the Verse Settles**

Because the category is defined by acts, several practical questions answer themselves, and the jurists worked them out accordingly.

A non-Muslim citizen who has not fought the Muslims and does not support their expulsion is under verse 8, not verse 9. His life, property, and honor are protected, and *birr* and *qisṭ* are owed to him. The Prophet ﷺ made this explicit:

> "Whoever wrongs a muʿāhid (one under covenant), or burdens him beyond his capacity, or takes something from him without his consent — I shall be his opponent on the Day of Resurrection." (Abū Dāwūd 3052; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

And in another report: *"Whoever kills a muʿāhid will not smell the fragrance of Paradise, though its fragrance is found from a distance of forty years"* (al-Bukhārī 3166).

A relative who is not hostile remains a relative. Verse 9 does not sever kinship; verse 8 preserves it. Asmāʾ's mother is the decisive case, and the Prophet ﷺ answered it directly.

A person who fights against the community because of its faith, or who works to remove it from its homeland, or who materially assists in doing so, is in the category of verse 9 regardless of his religion, his name, or his ancestry. The definition is behavioral, and it is therefore symmetrical: a Muslim who assists in the expulsion of Muslims has done the thing the verse describes, even if he shares the community's creed.

**The Modern Application, Stated Carefully**

The verse is most often misused in two opposite directions, and both misuses come from ignoring the three conditions.

The first misuse treats every non-Muslim state, employer, or neighbor as an object of hostility because it is not Muslim. The verse does not permit this. It requires evidence of the three acts. Where they are absent, verse 8 governs, and its command is positive.

The second misuse treats the verse as obsolete — a wartime ruling with no bearing on modern life. The verse does not permit this either. Every Muslim community facing armed persecution, ethnic cleansing, or forced displacement today is in the situation the verse legislates for, and the prohibition on transferring alliance to those who are killing them is not a historical curiosity. The believer in such a situation is not being asked to hate his neighbor; he is being asked not to hand his community's secrets, funds, or political support to the party that is destroying it.

The discipline the verse demands is therefore intellectual before it is emotional. It requires the believer to ask, in each case, whether the person or party before him has actually done the three things. Where the answer is no, kindness and justice are obligatory. Where the answer is yes, alliance is forbidden. In neither case is hatred required, and in neither case is the judgment made by category. That is the whole of the ruling, and it is precisely the reason the Qur'an stated it in two verses rather than one.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10

> **O you who believe! When the believing women come unto you as emigrants, examine them. God knows best their faith. Then if you know them to be believers, do not return them to the disbelievers. Those women are not lawful for those men; nor are those men lawful for those women. And give them what they have spent. There is no blame upon you if you marry them when you have given them their bridewealth. And hold not to the ties of disbelieving women. Ask for what you have spent, and let them ask for what they have spent. That is the Judgment of God; He judges between you. And God is Knowing, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Verse That Is an Entire Legal Chapter**

Verse 10 is the longest verse in the sūrah and, arguably, its legislative heart. In the space of one verse it establishes an evidentiary procedure, a rule of non-refoulement, a change in the law of marriage, a financial settlement running in two directions, and a rule for the dissolution of existing marriages — and it does so in the context of a treaty obligation that the Qur'an is here interpreting. It is the verse that gave the sūrah its name.

**The Women Who Walked Out of Makkah Alone**

The background is the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah, concluded in 6/628 between the Prophet ﷺ and the Quraysh. Among its terms was a clause of extraordinary asymmetry, which the Companions at the time found almost unbearable: any man from Makkah who came to the Muslims without his guardian's permission was to be returned to Makkah, but any Muslim who went over to the Quraysh would not be returned to Madinah. The Companions objected; the Prophet ﷺ accepted the terms, saying, in the report of al-Bukhārī, that whoever leaves us and goes to them — may God keep him away — and that God would provide a way out for whoever was returned (al-Bukhārī 2731–2732; Muslim 1783–1785).

Then women began to arrive. They came on foot, alone, without a male guardian, without provision, into a settlement that could barely feed itself, having abandoned husbands, homes, and property in a city that would punish them for it. Among them was Umm Kulthūm bint ʿUqbah — the woman after whom the sūrah is named. She was the daughter of ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ, one of the Quraysh's most violent persecutors of the Prophet in Makkah; she had embraced Islam early, endured persecution, and escaped to Madinah. Her brothers ʿUmārah and al-Walīd came to the Prophet ﷺ demanding her return under the treaty. Others came for their wives.

The verse was revealed in response. Its ruling was precise: the treaty's return clause did not apply to believing women.

**"Examine Them": A Test, Not a Suspicion**

*Fa-mtaḥinūhunna* — "so examine them" — is the imperative from which the sūrah takes its name. The commentators explain what the examination consisted of, and it is important to be exact, because the word can sound harsher than the procedure was.

Al-Zamakhsharī states the purpose: the Muslims had to establish that the women had migrated out of sincere belief — not out of anger at their husbands, not out of love for another man, not out of worldly motive. The concern was practical as well as spiritual: a woman fleeing a bad marriage might claim conversion to gain protection, and both she and the community would be harmed if that were not established.

The method was an oath, and its content is given in verse 12 of this same sūrah. ʿĀʾishah's report is decisive:

> "The believing women were examined by this verse: *O you who believe, when the believing women come to you as emigrants, examine them.* And ʿĀʾishah said: whoever affirmed this from among the women, she affirmed the test." (al-Bukhārī 4892)

That is, the woman was asked to affirm the pledges of verse 12 — no association of partners with God, no theft, no adultery, no killing of children, no slander, no disobedience in what is right — and her affirmation of them constituted the examination. Then the verse adds the sentence that limits the entire procedure: *"God knows best their faith."*

**"God Knows Best Their Faith": The Limit on Human Inquiry**

This clause is the verse's safeguard against inquisitorial excess. It means: your examination goes as far as the outward profession and the oath; the interior of the heart belongs to God and is beyond your jurisdiction. Once the woman has sworn, her word is accepted and the matter is between her and God.

The principle is foundational in Islamic law and appears throughout the Prophetic practice: judgments are made on apparent evidence, and God judges the hidden. The Prophet ﷺ said of the man who declared faith in battle and was killed by Usāmah: *"Did you look into his heart?"* (al-Bukhārī 4269; Muslim 96). He accepted the profession of people whose sincerity he may well have doubted. The verse establishes the same rule for the emigrant women: examine to the limit of what human beings can know, and stop there.

There is a pastoral point here that is easily missed. These women had walked hundreds of miles, alone, into poverty, for a faith that would cost them everything they owned. The examination was not a presumption against them; it was a form of record — a formal, public acknowledgment that they had come for God. The woman who swore it was thereby received into the community with a documented claim on its protection.

**"Do Not Return Them to the Disbelievers"**

*Lā tarjiʿūhunna ilā al-kuffār* — "do not return them to the disbelievers." The command is absolute in form and is the origin, in Islamic law, of what international law now calls non-refoulement: the prohibition on returning a person to a place where he or she faces persecution. The verse grounds it not in humanitarian sentiment but in the incompatibility of the woman's new status with the fate awaiting her.

The Prophet ﷺ implemented the ruling. He allowed the believing women who migrated to remain in Madinah, and — the exegetes note the symmetry — he returned to the Quraysh the men who came to the Muslims, in keeping with the treaty's terms regarding men (al-Qurṭubī and al-Wāḥidī record this). The verse did not break the treaty; it defined its scope. The treaty spoke of persons; the revelation clarified that a believing woman's status before God placed her outside the clause.

This is a model of how the Qur'an interacts with treaties. It does not authorize their violation; it requires their precise construction. A promise is kept according to what was actually promised, and no promise could have been intended to hand a believer over to persecution.

**"Those Women Are Not Lawful for Those Men"**

*Lā hunna ḥillun lahum wa-lā hum yaḥillūna lahunna* — the marriage between a believing woman and an unbelieving man is declared invalid, in both directions of the sentence for emphasis. The commentators connect this to the general rule:

> *"And do not marry idolatrous women until they believe; a believing slave-girl is better than an idolatress, even if she pleases you. And do not marry your women to idolaters until they believe; a believing slave is better than an idolater, even if he pleases you."* (Qur'an 2:221)

The distinction the jurists drew, following 5:5, is essential and must be stated carefully. Muslim men are permitted to marry women from the People of the Book — *"And the chaste women of those who were given the Book before you"* (5:5) — because the husband in that marriage recognizes the wife's prophets and her scripture, so her religious dignity is preserved within the household. A Muslim woman may not marry a non-Muslim man, because he does not recognize her prophet, and the structure of that household would place her faith in a position of subordination. This is not a judgment on individuals but a rule about the structure of the family unit, and the Qur'an states it as law rather than as preference.

The consequence for the emigrant women was immediate and severe in worldly terms: their existing marriages were dissolved. The verse handles this with a financial provision that deserves attention in its own right.

**"And Give Them What They Have Spent"**

*Wa-ātūhum mā anfaqū* — "and give them what they have spent." The unbelieving husband who loses his wife to her emigration is to be reimbursed the dower he paid. The Muslim community — in practice, from the treasury or the spoils — pays the enemy husband his money.

This is one of the most remarkable provisions in Qur'anic legislation, and its logic is worth stating plainly. The woman is protected absolutely; she is not returned. The marriage is dissolved absolutely; it is not preserved. But the man's financial loss, which he did not choose, is compensated. Islam does not enrich itself from the other side's loss, even when the other side is an enemy at war.

Ibn Kathīr records that the Muslims carried out this command, paying the dowers of the women who had left, while the idolaters refused to reciprocate in the parallel case — which is the situation verse 11 then addresses. The Qur'anic principle here is that one's own obligations are not conditional on the other party's performance. The command was *give them what they have spent*; it was not *give them what they have spent if they do the same.*

**"No Blame on You If You Marry Them"**

The verse then permits the believing women, once the settlement is complete, to marry Muslim men, provided they are given their own dowers: *wa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum an tankiḥūhunna idhā ātaytumūhunna ujūrahunna*. The condition of a fresh dower is stated because the dissolved marriage's dower has been returned to the previous husband; the woman therefore enters the new marriage with her rights fully constituted, owing nothing and owed everything.

The commentators also connect this to the requirement of the waiting period (*ʿiddah*), which the Qur'an legislates in 65:4 and which the case of Subayʿah bint al-Ḥārith al-Aslamiyyah clarified for the emigrant women: she emigrated to Madinah having recently given birth, and the Prophet ﷺ ruled that her waiting period had ended and she could marry (al-Bukhārī 5318; Muslim 1484). The point is that the verse's permission is embedded in a working legal system, not issued in the abstract.

These women — abandoned by husbands, stripped of property, examined on arrival, reimbursed by the community, and then married with full dowers — were not absorbed as dependents. They were received as persons with enforceable financial rights against a community that owed them nothing by kinship.

**"Hold Not to the Ties of Unbelieving Women"**

*Wa-lā tumsikū bi-ʿiṣami al-kawāfir* — "and do not hold to the marriage-ties of unbelieving women." The word *ʿiṣmah* is the technical term for the bond of marriage, from a root meaning protection and holding fast; to hold a woman's *ʿiṣmah* is to be her husband. The ruling runs in the other direction now: a Muslim man whose wife remains in disbelief, in Makkah, must let the marriage go.

Ibn Kathīr records that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb divorced two wives who were idolaters on the day this verse was revealed, and that they returned to Makkah. The verse thus imposed a real cost on the men of the community as well, and it did so symmetrically: neither sex was permitted to maintain a marriage across the line of faith once the line had been drawn by emigration.

**The Two-Way Accounting**

*Wa-sʾalū mā anfaqtum wa-l-yasʾalū mā anfaqū* — "ask for what you have spent, and let them ask for what they have spent." The settlement runs in both directions. If a Muslim man's wife defects to the disbelievers, he is entitled to reclaim his dower; if a disbeliever's wife emigrates to the Muslims, he is entitled to reclaim his. The verse establishes reciprocity as a legal principle even between parties at war.

*"That is the Judgment of God; He judges between you."* The phrase *ḥukmu Allāh* signals that what precedes is not a negotiated compromise but a revealed ruling; *yaḥkumu baynakum* — He judges between you — recalls verse 3's *He will distinguish between you*, and closes the legal section with the reminder that the same God who separates people on the Day of Resurrection is the One who separates their claims here.

The verse ends with *al-ʿAlīm*, the All-Knowing, and *al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise — knowledge and wisdom, the two attributes a judge requires. The commentators note their aptness: God knows what is in the hearts of the emigrant women, which human examination cannot reach, and He is wise in the arrangement of the settlement, which human judgment would not have devised. A law that protects a fleeing woman absolutely, compensates the man she leaves absolutely, and dissolves a marriage across a religious line absolutely, all at once, is a law whose internal coherence is part of its claim to be revealed.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:11

> **And if any of your wives should go over to the disbelievers, and then you have your turn, give those whose wives have gone the like of what they have spent. And reverence God, in Whom you are believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Direction**

Verse 10 dealt with the woman who came. Verse 11 deals with the woman who left — and its existence is one of the most telling features of the chapter's legal architecture. A legislator concerned only with the community's advantage would have stopped at verse 10. The Qur'an continues, because the case it now addresses is the one in which the Muslims are the injured party, and it legislates for that case too.

*Wa-in fātakum shayʾun min azwājikum ilā al-kuffār* — "and if any of your wives escape from you to the disbelievers." The word *fātakum*, from *f-w-t*, means to escape, to slip away, to be missed — the same sense as *"the Day when no soul will avail aught for another"* uses of things that pass beyond recall. It is not a neutral word for travel; it describes a wife leaving a marriage unilaterally and crossing to the other side.

The commentators explain that this happened during the Prophet's lifetime. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Qurṭubī record that six such cases occurred — women of the Muslim community who abandoned Islam and returned to the idolaters. The husband in each case lost his wife and the dower he had paid, and there was no treaty mechanism to recover it, because the Quraysh would not honor the reciprocal obligation that verse 10 had imposed on them.

**"Any of Your Wives": What the Word *Shayʾ* Refers To**

The verse uses an unusual construction: *"if any (shayʾ) of your wives escapes from you."* The word *shayʾ* means "thing," and the commentators offer two readings. It may refer to the wives themselves — a euphemistic way of speaking of a painful loss, as one says "we lost something" of a person. Or, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī note, it may refer to the dower that has been lost along with them — the property that went with the woman. Both readings point at the same reality: the husband is bereft in two ways at once, of a marriage and of the wealth he spent on it.

The euphemism is worth noticing. The Qur'an rarely dwells on the humiliation of the injured party. It names the loss in the flattest possible language and moves immediately to the remedy.

**"Then You Have Your Turn": Two Readings of *ʿĀqabtum***

The clause *fa-ʿāqabtum* — literally "then you took the ʿuqbah" — is the verse's most discussed phrase, and the two readings the exegetes give lead to different mechanisms for the same result.

The first reading, favored by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭabarī, is that *ʿāqabtum* means "you gained the turn" in the sense of obtaining spoils or victory over the disbelievers. On this reading the compensation comes from the war-booty: when the Muslims take spoil from the enemy, the man whose wife defected is paid from it before the general distribution. The word *ʿuqbah* here carries the sense of a turn in sequence — as when people take turns at a task — so the meaning is: when the turn comes to you in the form of gain from them.

The second reading takes it as "you were afflicted in turn," meaning that when the Muslims suffer the same loss the disbelievers suffered — a wife going over — the community bears the cost collectively. Al-Rāzī records both.

On either reading the practical outcome is the same, and the exegetes agree on it: the compensation comes from the public treasury or from the spoils of war, not from the individual defector and not from the injured husband's own pocket. The community absorbs the loss that its enemy refuses to make good.

**A Community That Pays What Its Enemy Will Not**

This is the verse's most striking moral content, and it should not be softened. The Quraysh did not pay. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record that the Muslims fulfilled the command of verse 10, returning the dowers of the women who had emigrated, while the idolaters refused the reciprocal payment for the women who had gone back to them. The Qur'an's response to that breach was not to cancel the Muslims' own obligation. It was to tell them to pay their own injured men from their own resources.

The principle is stated in the verse itself: *"give those whose wives have gone the like of what they have spent"* — *fa-ātū lladhīna dhahabat azwājuhum mithla mā anfaqū*. The word *mithl* — the like, the equivalent — establishes that the compensation must match the loss, not merely acknowledge it. And the obligation is unconditional. It does not say "if they pay you, pay your own"; it says pay.

This mirrors, at the level of law, the same ethic that verse 8 established at the level of conduct: your righteousness is not a function of theirs. The Qur'an consistently refuses to make the believer's obligations contingent on the other party's behavior. Compare:

> *"O you who believe! Be steadfast for God, witnesses in justice; and let not hatred of any people lead you to act unjustly."* (Qur'an 5:8)

And the Prophetic statement of the same principle in personal ethics:

> "Fulfill the trust to the one who entrusted you, and do not betray the one who betrayed you." (Abū Dāwūd 3535; al-Tirmidhī 1264, who graded it ḥasan gharīb)

A community that internalizes this becomes structurally different from every other kind of group. Its internal justice does not degrade when its enemies behave badly. That is a hard thing to sustain, and it is why the verse ends with the command to reverence God.

**"Reverence God, in Whom You Are Believers"**

*Wa-ttaqū Allāha lladhī antum bihi muʾminūn* — "and reverence God, in Whom you are believers." The construction is deliberate: the relative clause *"in Whom you are believers"* is attached to the command, so that the command is grounded in the faith already possessed. It is not "believe, then be reverent"; it is "you already believe — so act accordingly."

The exegetes note that *taqwā* is used here rather than a legal formula because the situation is one in which external enforcement is difficult. The man whose wife has defected is angry. The community's treasury is thin. Nobody is watching whether the payment is made in full or fudged. The only reliable guarantor of performance in such a case is the internal one — the awareness of being seen, which is what *taqwā* means at its root.

The analogy the tradition most often uses for *taqwā* is that of a man walking a path full of thorns: he gathers his garments, watches where he places his feet, and moves carefully, not because he fears the path but because he knows what carelessness costs. The man paying compensation to an enemy who will not reciprocate is walking exactly such a path, and the verse hands him the only thing that will keep his feet off the thorns.

**Why This Verse Belongs Where It Is**

Read in sequence with verses 8 to 10, verse 11 completes a moral argument. Verse 8: be righteous and just toward those who have not fought you. Verse 9: do not ally with those who have. Verse 10: protect the woman who comes, and pay her former husband. Verse 11: when the enemy will not pay, pay your own man from your own funds, and do not let their breach lower your standard.

The progression moves from the easy case to the hardest possible one. Kindness to a peaceful neighbor costs little. Refusing alliance with an aggressor costs something. Protecting a fleeing woman costs the community money and a treaty complication. Absorbing an enemy's breach of contract without retaliation costs the community money and pride. Verse 11 is the summit of the sequence, and the reason it ends with *taqwā* rather than with a threat is that no external sanction could produce it. Only reverence will.

**The Modern Shape of the Ruling**

The specific mechanism — dowers, spoils, emigration — belongs to a particular historical moment. The principle does not. In any community there are people injured by the defection or betrayal of others: a business partner who leaves with the client list, a spouse who abandons the family, a colleague who takes the work and the credit, a member who leaves and takes funds. The instinct in every such case is to make the injured party bear the loss, on the grounds that the one who caused it is beyond reach.

Verse 11 says the opposite. The community absorbs the loss of its own injured member, at cost to itself, without waiting for the offender to make amends. That is what it means to be a community rather than a collection of individuals with a common label. And the verse grounds the whole obligation in a single reminder: you believe in God. Act like people who do.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12

> **O Prophet! When believing women come unto thee, pledging unto thee that they will not ascribe any as partners unto God, nor steal, nor fornicate, nor slay their children, nor bring a slanderous lie that they have fabricated between their hands and feet, nor disobey thee in anything honorable, then accept their pledge and seek God’s Forgiveness for them. Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Pledge in the Qur'an Addressed to Women**

Verse 12 records the terms of the pledge — *bayʿat al-nisāʾ*, the pledge of the women — and it is unique in the Qur'an in its grammar. Every other covenant in the revealed text is addressed in the masculine plural, which in Arabic covers both sexes. This one is addressed in the feminine plural throughout: *an lā yushrikna*, *wa-lā yasriqna*, *wa-lā yaznīna*, *wa-lā yaqtulna*, *wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka*. Women are being addressed as women, in their own grammatical person, and are giving a pledge in their own names.

The commentators, Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī among them, note the significance directly: the Prophet ﷺ took an independent pledge from women, indicating that women remain spiritually independent of their husbands and responsible for themselves before God and the Prophet. A woman's Islam is not derived from her father's or her husband's. She does not enter the covenant as an appendage. She enters it by her own declaration, and she will be judged on her own performance of it.

That principle is revisited in Sūrah 66, where the Qur'an sets out four women — two believing and two unbelieving, none of them defined by the faith of their husbands — as examples for believers. Together the two chapters establish what the exegetes called the spiritual individuality of women in Islam, and verse 12 is its legal expression.

**The Order of the Six Clauses**

The pledge has six articles, and their sequence is not arbitrary. It begins with the vertical and moves to the horizontal; it begins with the greatest sin and moves downward in scale; and it moves, notably, from the private interior to the public exterior.

First: *an lā yushrikna bi-Allāhi shayʾan* — that they associate nothing with God. Association (*shirk*) is named first because it is the only sin the Qur'an declares unforgivable if a person dies in it: *"Truly God forgives not that anything be associated with Him, and forgives what is less than that for whom He wills"* (4:48). Everything else in the pledge is a consequence of getting this right or wrong.

Then four prohibitions that protect property, lineage, life, and truth: theft, fornication, the killing of children, and slander. And finally, the article that governs the whole: no disobedience in what is right.

The same six articles, in the same order, appear in the pledge taken from men at the Second Pledge of al-ʿAqabah, as narrated by ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit:

> "Come and pledge to me that you will not associate anything with God, will not steal, will not commit fornication, will not kill your children, will not bring a slander you invent between your hands and your feet, and will not disobey me in what is right. Whoever among you fulfills it, his reward is with God. Whoever commits any of these and is punished for it in this world, that is his expiation. Whoever commits any of these and God conceals it, his affair is with God: if He wills He will punish him, and if He wills He will forgive him. So we pledged him on that." (al-Bukhārī 18, 3892, 4894; Muslim 1709)

The identity of content between the two pledges is itself the point: the moral law is one, and it applies identically to men and women. What differs is only the grammatical person in which it is recited.

**"Nor Steal"**

The commentators, including al-Rāzī, extend *lā yasriqna* beyond the physical taking of goods to any acquisition by unlawful means — fraud, deception, misappropriation, taking what one has no right to. The breadth is deliberate, and it makes the clause applicable to the ordinary commercial life of a household: a wife concealing money from a joint account, an employee pocketing small amounts, a person claiming a benefit they are not entitled to.

The clause has a specific historical resonance given what the sources report about Hind bint ʿUtbah at the pledge — discussed below — and it establishes that the pledge is not merely about spectacular crimes but about the small daily honesty on which a community's economic trust depends.

**"Nor Fornicate"**

*Wa-lā yaznīna* — the prohibition of unlawful sexual relations. In a pledge addressed to women in a society where a woman's chastity determined her standing and her children's legitimacy, the clause is a public commitment made by the woman herself rather than a rule enforced on her by her guardians. That is a significant shift of agency. The pledge makes her the guarantor of her own conduct before God and the Prophet, not the object of others' surveillance.

The Qur'an legislates severely against this sin precisely because of what it destroys: lineage, inheritance, the child's right to a known father, and the trust on which the family rests. The Prophet ﷺ summarized the logic in a statement reported in both Ṣaḥīḥs:

> "The child belongs to the marriage-bed, and the adulterer gets the stone." (al-Bukhārī 2053; Muslim 1457)

That is, the child's legal paternity follows the marriage, and the transgressor gets nothing but loss.

**"Nor Kill Their Children"**

*Wa-lā yaqtulna awlādahunna* — and do not kill their children. The commentators are agreed that the primary reference is to the pre-Islamic Arabian practice of *waʾd*, the burying alive of newborn girls, motivated by fear of poverty and shame. The Qur'an condemns the practice in its most searching verse:

> *"And when the buried girl is asked: for what sin was she slain?"* (Qur'an 81:8–9)

And: *"Slay not your children for fear of poverty — We provide for them and for you. Truly the slaying of them is a great sin"* (17:31).

Ibn Kathīr records the view that the prohibition extends by analogy to the destruction of the unborn child, a matter on which the jurists have differed in detail — regarding stage, necessity, and circumstance — while agreeing that the deliberate termination of a pregnancy without cause falls under the general principle of the verse. What is beyond dispute is the direction of the ruling: a society that had been killing its daughters was, in the act of embracing Islam, pledging not to.

The placement of the clause inside a pledge given by women is powerful. The mothers were the ones who had been made instruments of the killing. The pledge made them its guarantors against.

**"A Slander Fabricated Between Their Hands and Feet"**

*Wa-lā yaʾtīna bi-buhtānin yaftarīnahu bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna* — "nor bring a slanderous lie that they invent between their hands and their feet." The phrase is unusual, and the commentators explain it precisely: it refers to a woman attributing to her husband a child conceived through adultery. The child is "between her hands and feet" because that is the position in which an infant is carried and born — the image is of a baby held at her chest and delivered at her feet, whom she then presents as her husband's.

*Buhtān* is the Qur'an's strongest word for a falsehood: a slander that stupefies its victim by its audacity. The Qur'an uses it of the calumny against Maryam (4:156) and of false attribution generally. The prohibition is thus about the deliberate falsification of lineage — an act that in a kinship-based society destroys not only a marriage but the identity of a child and the inheritance of a family.

The commentators extend the principle to all forms of fabricated attribution: claiming another's work, forging a document, giving false testimony, inventing a story that damages someone's name. The underlying wrong is the same in every case — making a false thing enter the public record where it will be acted upon.

**"Nor Disobey You in Anything Honorable"**

The final clause is the one that most needs careful reading, because it is the clause most often quoted out of context. *Wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka fī maʿrūf* — "and do not disobey you in what is *maʿrūf*." The word *maʿrūf* is a technical term in the Qur'an and the law: it means what is recognized as good by sound reason and revealed guidance, the known and acknowledged good. The clause is explicitly bounded by it.

The Prophet ﷺ himself supplied the governing principle, and it is reported in both Ṣaḥīḥs in the incident of the commander who ordered his soldiers to enter a fire:

> "There is no obedience in disobedience of God; obedience is only in what is right (innamā al-ṭāʿatu fī al-maʿrūf)." (Muslim 1840; al-Bukhārī 7257 in similar wording)

And the general formulation:

> "There is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator." (Aḥmad 1098, from ʿAlī; also al-Ṭabarānī; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī)

The clause therefore establishes a conditional, morally bounded obedience — the opposite of blind obedience — and it does so in the very text that commands it. Any reading of verse 12 that treats the final clause as a general command of unquestioning submission has to delete the word *maʿrūf* from the verse, and the word is in the Qur'an.

**"Accept Their Pledge and Seek Forgiveness for Them"**

*Fa-bāyiʿhunna wa-staghfir lahunna Allāh* — "so accept their pledge and seek God's forgiveness for them." The Prophet ﷺ is commanded to do two things, and the second is as significant as the first. He is to receive them — the pledge is accepted, the women are admitted — and then he is to pray for their forgiveness.

The sequence acknowledges human frailty without excusing it. The women have just pledged not to sin; the Prophet is immediately told to ask that their sins be forgiven. Islam does not pretend that a pledge makes a person sinless. It provides, alongside the standard, the mechanism for failing it — and the one praying for the pledger is the one to whom the pledge was given.

The verse ends with *inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm* — "truly God is Forgiving, Merciful" — the same pair that closed verse 7, and for the same reason: at every point where the Qur'an imposes a demanding standard on human beings, it names the attribute that makes failure survivable.

**A Hand Was Never Touched**

The manner in which the pledge was taken is recorded in the most explicit terms, and it has been a source of law for fourteen centuries. ʿĀʾishah's report is in Muslim and al-Bukhārī:

> "By God, the hand of the Messenger of God never touched the hand of any woman. He pledged them only with words." (Muslim 1866; al-Bukhārī 5288, 7214)

She added, in the same report, that when the women had affirmed the pledge he said to them: "Go, for I have pledged you" — words only, no physical contact, no gesture. The pledge was a verbal covenant witnessed by those present.

Umm ʿAṭiyyah's account adds a detail that shows the pledge being administered in real life:

> "When we pledged the Messenger of God, he recited upon us *'that they associate nothing with God,'* and he forbade us wailing. A woman among us withdrew her hand and said: 'So-and-so made me happy and I wish to repay her.' He said nothing to her. She went away, then returned, and he pledged her." (al-Bukhārī 4895; Muslim 912 in related wording)

Wailing for the dead — the professional lamentation with scratching of faces and tearing of garments — was added by the Prophet ﷺ to the pledge's content on some occasions, according to the reports the exegetes cite, because it belonged to the same category as the other clauses: a public performance that contradicted what the woman had pledged inwardly.

**Hind bint ʿUtbah's Two Questions**

The commentators preserve an exchange from the pledge that is worth recording because it shows the women arguing with the terms rather than merely accepting them. Hind bint ʿUtbah — the wife of Abū Sufyān, and the woman who had mutilated the body of the Prophet's uncle Ḥamzah at Uḥud — came to give the pledge, and she raised two objections.

On fornication she said, in the words preserved in the biographical sources and cited by Ibn Kathīr: "O Messenger of God, do free women commit fornication?" — an indignant question, asked by a woman of rank who considered the clause beneath her.

On theft she raised a matter of practical law:

> "Indeed Abū Sufyān is a stingy man. Is there any harm on me if I take from his property without his knowledge?" The Messenger of God ﷺ said: "Take what suffices you and your children, reasonably (bi-l-maʿrūf)." (al-Bukhārī 2211, 5364, 5370; Muslim 1714)

The Prophet ﷺ did not rebuke her for raising the question. He answered it, and his answer became a rule of Islamic family law: a wife may take from her husband's property what is reasonably needed for herself and her children when he fails to provide. The clause "nor steal" was thus interpreted, in the very hour of the pledge, in a way that protected the woman's entitlement rather than only the man's property.

**What the Pledge Means Today**

The pledge is recited nowhere in Muslim practice as a ritual, and it does not need to be. Its function is to state, in six clauses, the minimum moral content of Islam — and to state it in a form the individual says about herself. It is worth noting what is not in it: no ritual acts, no forms of worship, no dress, no formula of words. It is belief, honesty, sexual integrity, protection of children, truthfulness, and bounded obedience. A person who performs every ritual and breaks these six has not kept the pledge. A person who keeps these six has kept what the pledge actually required.

And the verse's last clause remains its most necessary. Obedience — to a spouse, a leader, a scholar, a state, a party — is legitimate only within *maʿrūf*. The moment a demand leaves the territory of the recognized good, the pledge has been satisfied by refusing it, not by complying.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:13

> **O you who believe! Do not befriend a people with whom God is wroth; they who have despaired of the Hereafter, just as the disbelievers have despaired of the inhabitants of graves.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Returns to Its Beginning**

Al-Qushayrī's observation about this sūrah's shape is confirmed by its final verse: it ends with the injunction with which it began. Verse 1 said *"take not Mine enemy and your enemy as friends"*; verse 13 says *"do not befriend a people with whom God is wroth."* The prohibition is repeated at the close, and the repetition is not padding. It is the structure of a ring, and the effect on the reader is that he leaves the chapter where he entered it — but having passed through the model of Abraham, the hope of verse 7, the justice of verse 8, the boundary of verse 9, the protection of the emigrant women, and the pledge.

A ring composition also signals that the prohibition is not a transitional ruling for a particular crisis. Had the sūrah ended at verse 12, the reader might have concluded that the opening verses were about Ḥāṭib's letter and the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah. Ending with verse 13 places the injunction outside the historical frame.

**The Occasion: Loyalty Sold for Provisions**

The exegetes — al-Qushayrī, al-Rāzī, al-Wāḥidī, and al-Zamakhsharī — report that the verse was revealed concerning some poor Muslims who would inform the Jews of Madinah about the activities of the Muslims in exchange for provisions. The detail is small and it is devastating. These were not statesmen negotiating an alliance. They were hungry people selling information for food.

That is why the verse is where it is, and why it is the chapter's final word. Verse 1 dealt with Ḥāṭib, a man of standing who wrote a letter out of anxiety for his family. Verse 13 deals with poor people who sold information out of hunger. The two together cover the whole range of the failure: the elite compromise motivated by affection and kinship, and the poor compromise motivated by need. The prohibition is not aimed at a type of person. It is aimed at a structure of the heart that no social position exempts.

The modern equivalents are obvious and require no elaboration: the employee who shares internal information for a side income; the community insider who briefs a hostile journalist in exchange for access or money; the person whose political positions shift with his funding; the student who reports on his own classmates to win favor with an authority. In each case the transaction is small, the payment is immediate, and the damage is done to people who trusted the seller.

**"A People With Whom God Is Wroth"**

*Qawman ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim* — "a people with whom God is angry." The commentators identify the reference variously: the Jews of Madinah, given the reported occasion; or, more broadly, all who have earned divine wrath by rejecting a messenger after the truth became clear to them. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī both note the general applicability, and the Qur'an uses the same phrase of the people of Pharaoh's court (79:21) and of those who earned wrath by opposing their prophets (5:80; 58:14).

The phrase does something important theologically. It moves the description of the other party from the political register of verses 1 to 9 — fighting, expelling, allying — into the register of God's own assessment. The believer is not being asked to make an independent judgment about who deserves hostility. He is being told that there is a divine judgment already in force, and that attaching himself to those under it is to stand in the wrong place.

This also explains the sūrah's opening words again: *"Mine enemy and your enemy."* The enmity originates in God's relation to the party, not in the believer's feelings. Which is why the believer's feelings are not a reliable guide — as verse 7 makes plain when it says God may yet forge affection between them.

**"They Have Despaired of the Hereafter"**

*Qad yaʾisū min al-ākhirah* — "they have despaired of the Hereafter." The verb is from *y-ʾ-s*, and *yaʾs* is the Qur'an's word for the despair that closes a door. It is the direct opposite of *rajāʾ*, the hope named in verse 6 as the quality that makes Abraham's example usable.

The commentators give three readings of the clause, and all three are instructive.

The first, favored by al-Qushayrī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, is that it refers to the Jews of Madinah, who will despair because they denied the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ despite having awaited him. The second is that it refers to the hypocrites, who will despair because of their conspiracy against the Prophet. The third, and the most general, is that it refers to all who despair of the Hereafter because they preferred this world and worked only for it.

The third reading is the one that makes the verse apply to every reader. Despair of the Hereafter is not necessarily an articulated doctrine. In most people it is a practical condition: a life arranged as though nothing follows it. And the Qur'an identifies that arrangement as despair, however optimistic the person feels about his worldly prospects. The man who has no expectation of God has, whether he names it or not, despaired — because he has conceded that there is nothing more than what he can see, and he has built accordingly.

The contrast with verse 6 is exact and deliberate. Verse 6 promised the example of Abraham to *"whoever hopes for God and the Last Day."* Verse 13 forbids alliance with *"those who have despaired of the Hereafter."* The sūrah's two closing movements define the human being by the direction of his expectation.

**"As the Disbelievers Despair of the Inhabitants of Graves"**

The simile in the final clause is the verse's most striking image, and the commentators offer two readings of it.

The first: the disbelievers despair of seeing their deceased relations again, because they hold that death is the end and that the dead do not return. The pagan Arab who said *"there is nothing but our life in this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time"* (45:24) has, by his own creed, no expectation of meeting anyone he has buried. The dead are gone. That is the despair being described.

The second: the disbelievers despair of any good coming to those who died in disbelief — they have no hope to offer their dead, no prayer that reaches them, no expectation that anything remains to be done.

Both readings converge on the same point, and the simile's function is to describe a particular quality of hopelessness: the despair of the inhabitants of graves is final. It is the one despair in human experience that has no strategy, no timeline, and no remedy, because the person it concerns can no longer act. The dead cannot repent, cannot work, cannot accumulate, cannot change their position. To compare a group's despair of the Hereafter to the dead's despair is to say that their condition has, in effect, already begun — they are living inside a closed system.

The Qur'an develops this elsewhere with equal directness:

> *"Until, when death comes to one of them, he says: My Lord, send me back, that I may do righteous deeds in what I have left behind. Never! It is only a word he speaks; and behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected."* (Qur'an 23:99–100)

> *"And those who disbelieve — their deeds are like a mirage in a desert; the thirsty supposes it to be water, until when he comes to it he finds it to be nothing."* (Qur'an 24:39)

**Why Despair Is the Reason for the Prohibition**

It is worth asking why the verse gives despair as the reason not to befriend, rather than hostility or harm. The answer is practical and psychological.

A person who has no expectation beyond this world has no reason to restrain himself by reference to anything beyond this world. He will keep promises when it pays and break them when it does not. He will be honest where honesty is profitable. His cooperation is a function of calculation, and calculation changes. The believer, by contrast, is constrained by a horizon that calculation cannot reach — which is why his word can be relied upon and his opponent's cannot.

That is not a claim that non-believers are immoral; verse 8 has already commanded kindness and justice toward them, and the Qur'an and the Prophet's own practice are full of covenants faithfully kept with non-Muslims. It is a claim about the foundation of trust in the specific context of the sūrah — the transfer of alliance in a situation of conflict. Alliance is a long-term, high-risk commitment, and the verse asks the believer to consider what the other party's ultimate expectations actually commit him to.

**The Contrast With the Qur'an's Own Command Against Despair**

One final point completes the verse's meaning. The Qur'an forbids despair in the strongest possible terms for the believer:

> *"And do not despair of God's mercy. Truly none despairs of God's mercy except the disbelieving people."* (Qur'an 12:87)

> *"O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God's mercy; truly God forgives all sins."* (Qur'an 39:53)

> *"Say: who despairs of the mercy of his Lord except those who are astray?"* (Qur'an 15:56)

Despair is therefore not a neutral mood in the Qur'an; it is a theological position, and it is identified as the position of unbelief. The believer is commanded to hope, and the one who has despaired is described in verse 13 as the person whose alliance the believer must refuse. Between those two poles the entire sūrah is situated.

**Where the Sūrah Leaves Its Reader**

Thirteen verses began with a letter smuggled in a woman's hair and ended with poor men selling information for food. The two incidents frame a chapter that is, at bottom, about the direction of the heart's dependence. Ḥāṭib leaned on the Quraysh because he feared for his family. The poor men leaned on the Jews because they feared hunger. Abraham leaned on God and lost his father, his town, and his homeland — and is held up in verses 4 to 6 as the beautiful example.

The sūrah does not ask the believer to be unfeeling. It commands righteousness and justice toward the peaceful in verse 8, and it holds out in verse 7 the possibility that today's enemy will be tomorrow's family. What it forbids is the one thing that undoes a person from the inside: leaning on a party that has no expectation beyond this world, while claiming to hope for God and the Last Day.

The final verse is therefore not a warning about other people. It is a diagnostic. If your security, your income, your standing, or your future depends on those who have despaired of the Hereafter, then whatever your profession of faith, your dependence is placed where your creed says it should not be. And the cure the chapter offers is not more rules but the prayer of verse 5 — do not make us a trial for those who disbelieve, and forgive us — followed by the three words of verse 4, which are the whole of the sūrah's answer: *in You we rely, to You we turn, and to You is the journey's end.*
