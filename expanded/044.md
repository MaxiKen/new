# Sūrah al-Dukhān (Chapter 44) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah of the Manifest Smoke**

Sūrah al-Dukhān, "The Smoke," is the forty-fourth chapter of the Qur'an and takes its name from the single image that dominates its opening movement — a day when the sky will bring forth a *dukhān mubīn*, a manifest, visible smoke (44:10). The naming is Qur'anic in origin: the word *al-dukhān* occurs in the sūrah itself, and from the earliest generations Muslims have referred to the chapter by this word, just as they call Sūrah 2 al-Baqarah from the cow commanded to the Children of Israel and Sūrah 18 al-Kahf from the People of the Cave. The name is therefore not an editorial label imposed from outside the text but a natural pointer to the chapter's central sign — a sign that the commentaries read both as an already-fulfilled historical warning to the idolaters of Makkah and as one of the great portents of the approaching Hour.

**A Makkan Address with One Exceptional Verse**

The overwhelming majority of scholars classify al-Dukhān among the Makkan sūrahs, revealed before the Hijrah, in the middle Makkan period when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his Companions were enduring the organized hostility of the Quraysh. Its voice is the voice of that period: short, ringing sentences; repeated oaths; urgent warning of punishment; consolation for the messenger; and the retelling of Moses' confrontation with Pharaoh as a mirror held up to the Prophet's own confrontation with the powerful men of Makkah. Some commentators, however, note the possibility that verse 15 — *We shall indeed remove the punishment a little. Surely you will revert* — belongs to the Madinan period, revealed after the event to which it alludes, and the internal evidence is real: the verse speaks of a punishment already lifted and of a reversion already observed, which fits the aftermath of the famine that struck the Quraysh after the Hijrah. The chapter's position in the written muṣḥaf places it directly after Sūrah al-Zukhruf (43) and before Sūrah al-Jāthiyah (45), completing with them a sequence of seven sūrahs — from 40 to 46 — that open with the separated letters Ḥā. Mīm., the collective known as the Ḥawāmīm.

**Where the Chapter Begins: Answering the Previous Sūrah's Sigh**

The classical commentators observe that al-Dukhān opens where al-Zukhruf ended. The final verse of Sūrah 43 turns the Prophet's grief into a resigned leave-taking: *So turn away from them and say, "Peace." For they will come to know* (43:89). What "they will come to know," in this reading, is precisely the reality of the *clear Book* with which Sūrah 44 opens (v. 2) — the Book that tells of the smoke, the Day of Division, and the tryst of punishment that awaits the deniers. Even more striking is the echo of the Prophet's lament recorded at the very end of al-Zukhruf: *O my Lord, truly these are a people who believe not* (43:88). That same complaint — *These are a guilty people* — is placed on the lips of Moses in the middle of Sūrah 44 (v. 22), when he has exhausted every argument and every sign before Pharaoh and finally turns to God. The symmetry is deliberate and deeply consoling: the Prophet of the later age was not the first messenger to face a people who would not believe; the pattern had been run before, with Moses and Pharaoh, and its outcome was deliverance for the believers and drowning for the tyrants. A reader who holds the two sūrahs together watches the Qur'an answer its own earlier cry of grief with a history lesson and a promise.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah's Argument**

The chapter is tightly organized in a sequence of five movements. It opens with an oath upon the clear Book and the mystery of its descent on a blessed night, when every wise command is made distinct as a mercy from the Lord of the heavens and the earth (vv. 1–8). The second movement turns to the doubters and their punishment: the manifest smoke, the desperate plea for relief, the removal of the punishment, and the warning of a terrible striking to come (vv. 9–16). The third and longest movement retells the trial of Pharaoh's people: Moses' summons, Pharaoh's arrogance, the exodus by night, the drowned host, and the sudden emptiness of all that Egypt's tyrants had accumulated (vv. 17–33). The fourth movement confronts the Makkan denial of the Resurrection with two arguments — the destroyed power of the people of Tubbaʿ and the purposive character of creation itself, which demands a Day of Division when no friend will avail a friend (vv. 34–42). The final movement paints the two ends of that division in stark contrast: the tree of Zaqqūm, molten lead, and boiling liquid for the sinner seized and dragged into Hellfire (vv. 43–50), and for the reverent a secure station amid gardens and springs, silk and brocade, wide-eyed maidens, and immunity from death itself — a bounty from their Lord, the great triumph (vv. 51–57). Two short verses close the sūrah, turning from the Hereafter back to the present mission: the Book has been made easy upon the Prophet's tongue so that people may remember, and he is told to wait — for the disbelievers too are waiting, and the two waits will not end the same way (vv. 58–59).

**The Central Contrast: Pharaoh's Gardens and the Gardens of the Reverent**

The rhetorical engine of the sūrah is comparison. The gardens and springs that Pharaoh's people left behind in a single morning (vv. 25–27) find their answer in the gardens and springs of the reverent, from which they will never be expelled (vv. 51–52). The death that the disbelievers claim is their only end (v. 35) is answered by the believers who taste no death after the first (v. 56). The food of the sinner, the bitter tree of Zaqqūm boiling like molten lead in the belly (vv. 43–46), is answered by the call for every fruit, secure, in Paradise (v. 55). Even the garments are contrasted: the finery the Egyptians abandoned (vv. 25–28) and the fine silk and rich brocade that the reverent wear forever (v. 53). The sūrah thus teaches its central lesson twice, once negatively and once positively: everything in this world is a loan that can be recalled in an hour, and everything in the Hereafter that God grants to the reverent is a gift that cannot be revoked. The wise reader, the sūrah implies, is the one who invests in the second economy while still managing the first.

**An Oft-Recited Sūrah and Its Reported Virtue**

Because of its vivid picture of the Hereafter and its use in prayer, al-Dukhān has long been a chapter Muslims recite frequently, particularly on the eve of Friday and in the night prayer. Later collections of hadith transmit — through chains that scholars of hadith regard as weak, although the classical commentators continue to cite them — words such as the report the commentators al-Ālūsī, al-Zamakhsharī, and others record:

> "Whosoever recites al-Dukhān during the night will greet the morning with seventy thousand angels asking forgiveness for him."

Ibn Kathīr, when discussing the recitation of this sūrah on the night before Friday, notes that the reports urging it are not established as authentic (the most frequently cited one being attributed to Abū Hurayrah with an unverified chain), while still acknowledging the widespread practice. What is authentically known is the general virtue of the Qur'an's recitation and reflection, and the Ḥawāmīm in particular are honored in the words reported from Ibn Masʿūd and cited by the commentators, including Ibn Kathīr in his introduction to this group:

> "The Ḥawāmīm are the brocade of the Qur'an." (reported from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd)

How to read what follows: in accordance with the method of this entire project, every verse of the fifty-nine is treated here as an independent discourse. Verses that the source commentary presents in groups — such as 3–5, 10–11, 17–31, 34–36, 38–39, 41–42, 43–46, 47–50, and 51–57 — have been separated, and each receives its own full commentary in strict numerical order, with the Qur'anic text quoted individually and then expanded through Arabic language, classical scholarship, prophetic narration, reasoned analysis, and connection to human experience. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is long; the sūrah of the smoke is read here unhurriedly, from Ḥā. Mīm. to the final watching verse.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:1

> **Ḥā. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth of the Seven Ḥawāmīm**

Sūrah al-Dukhān opens with two letters of the Arabic alphabet recited by their names — *Ḥā. Mīm.* — making it the fifth in a chain of seven consecutive sūrahs (40 through 46) that begin with exactly this pair. The seven are so distinctive as a family that the Qur'an's readers gave them a collective nickname: the Ḥawāmīm, from the letters that head them. In the written order of the muṣḥaf the family appears as a block — Ghāfir, Fuṣṣilat, al-Shūrā, al-Zukhruf, al-Dukhān, al-Jāthiyah, al-Aḥqāf — a rare case in which seven whole chapters share an identical opening formula, and the reciter feels the unity immediately: a deep pharyngeal breath at ḥāʾ, a closed hum at mīm, then the announcement each of the seven makes of the Book's descent, of God's majesty, or of the coming Division.

The Ḥā. Mīm. pair belongs to the larger class of Qur'anic openings called *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, "the separated letters," which stand at the head of twenty-nine sūrahs in various combinations — a single letter such as Qāf or Nūn, pairs such as Ṭā. Hā., Ṭā. Sīn., Yā. Sīn., triples such as Alif. Lām. Mīm. and Alif. Lām. Rā., and longer strings up to the five letters that open Sūrah 19. From the first generation of Muslims these openings were treated with reverent wonder precisely because their full meaning was not disclosed. The dominant position of the classical commentators — represented by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the majority of the Salaf — is that their ultimate significance is known only to God. Reports from the Companions reinforce this posture of reverent acceptance rather than speculative decoding; it is narrated from Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq that every book has a mystery and that the mystery of the Qur'an lies in its sūrah-openings, and from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib that these letters are a quintessence of the Book. The scholars of later ages, including al-Rāzī in his great commentary, catalogued the interpretive possibilities while rarely claiming certainty: the letters might be names of the sūrahs they introduce; they might be oaths by which God swears, since the Arabs swore by letters of their alphabet; they might be words of divine address meant to awaken the listener's ear before the message begins; or they might be a challenge in themselves.

**Letters of the Challenge**

That last possibility deserves attention, for it ties the letters to the argumentative core of the Makkan revelation. The Quraysh accused the Prophet ﷺ of producing speech no human could match, and the Qur'an repeatedly throws the challenge back at them — *Or do they say he invented it? Say: "Then bring a sūrah like it, and call upon whom you can apart from God, if you are truthful"* (10:38). The separated letters make that challenge sharper: the Qur'an is built, visibly, out of the very same Arabic letters the poets and soothsayers of Makkah used every day — twenty-eight ordinary sounds of their own tongue, yet arranged into a speech that confounded them. Al-Dukhān's Ḥā. Mīm. thus functions like an exhibitor holding up the raw materials of the miracle: here are your letters; here is what issued from them; now produce its like. The letters open the sūrah, and then the very next verses announce that this Book was sent down on a blessed night — as if to say: this arrangement of sounds did not arise from the culture of men, but descended from heaven.

**Reading the Letters as a Sound of Warning**

There is also an audible dimension that commentators of the Qur'an's recitation and rhetoric noted. Ḥā. Mīm. is a breath-like, aspirated sound followed by a resonant closure — and in al-Dukhān, the sūrah whose first great sign is a cloud of smoke and whose central promise is a terrible striking, the opening letters share the texture of the verses to come: compressed, urgent, exhaled. Whether or not one accepts such tonal readings, the placement does real work: the letters are followed without pause by an oath on the clear Book, and the whole sequence of sūrahs 40–46 is dominated by warnings of punishment and the fate of past nations. The seven Ḥawāmīm, as Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said and the commentators transmit, are the adornment of the Qur'an:

> "The Ḥawāmīm are the brocade of the Qur'an." (reported from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd and cited by Ibn Kathīr)

A brocade is fine, figured silk — the most precious fabric of the ancient world — and the image suggests that these seven sūrahs, though often recited for their vivid accounts of judgment, are counted among the most beautiful stretches of the revealed text. The believer who recites Ḥā. Mīm. at the head of al-Dukhān is therefore entering a family of sūrahs that tradition has dressed in honor, and a single sūrah whose subject — the clear Book, its descent, its warning — is announced in the very sound of its first breath.

**Why Not to Over-Decode**

For the modern reader the letters can be a temptation to esoteric arithmetic — counting letter values, constructing names from ḥāʾ and mīm, or mining the pair for hidden dates. The reverent discipline of the classical tradition counsels otherwise. When the Qur'an wished to teach that some things are deliberately withheld, it did not do so obscurely: *"And they ask thee about the spirit. Say: 'The spirit is of the command of my Lord, and you have not been given of knowledge, save a little'"* (17:85). The separated letters belong, in the mainstream view, to the portion of knowledge withheld — signs of the reality that divine speech exceeds human comprehension at its very threshold. The practical fruit of the verse is therefore humility: the reader begins the sūrah by acknowledging that God's Book contains depths no created mind has fully sounded, and this acknowledgment is itself a preparation for the warnings and promises that follow. One who cannot decode the letters can still be transformed by the verses — which is exactly the point: the message does not depend on cracking a cipher; it depends on an open heart.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:2

> **By the clear Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Whose Object Is Divine Speech**

The sūrah now swears an oath: *wa-l-kitābi al-mubīn* — "By the clear Book." Arabic oaths in the Qur'an are sworn over things of greatness — the dawn, the night, the fig and the olive, the charging horses — and here the thing sworn by is the Book itself, the very revelation being recited. God swearing by His own speech is a striking act of honor: it is as though the universe were summoned as witness that this Book is what the coming verses say it is. In Arabic rhetoric the oath does not imply that the oath-taker doubts the matter; rather it magnifies the object and rivets the attention of the audience upon what follows. The commentators note that the answer to the oath — its *jawāb al-qasam* — is the next verse: *truly We sent it down on a blessed night*. So the structure reads: By this clear Book — indeed, We sent it down on a blessed night. The verse that needed the heaviest possible support is the claim of heavenly descent, and God supports it with an oath upon the very Book whose descent is being announced.

**What "Clear" Means: The Root *Bāʾ Yā Nūn***

The word *mubīn* is an active participle on the pattern *mufʿil*, from the root *b-y-n*, whose core sense is separation and distinction — one thing made distinct from another. Hence *bayyinah* is a clear proof, *bayān* is eloquence that makes meaning distinct, and the verb *bāna* means "it became clear." As an active form, *mubīn* means not merely "clear in itself" but "that which makes clear": the Book that clarifies. The Qur'an applies the word to itself repeatedly — *Alif. Lām. Rā. Those are the signs of the clear Book* (12:1), *the clear Book* at 26:2 and 43:2, and of the Prophet that he is a clear warner (67:26) — and the sense is always double: the Book is manifest in its own truth, and it actively distinguishes truth from falsehood, lawful from unlawful, guidance from error. Al-Qurṭubī notes that *mubīn* gathers all of these: the Qur'an is clear in wording, clear in meaning, clear in its proof, and clear in the distinction it draws between the two paths. It is not a speech that hides its intent in riddles, nor a scripture whose commands require an elite to decode; its clarity is part of its mercy.

**The Family Phrase Across the Ḥawāmīm**

This exact oath occurs in the preceding sūrah as well — *By the clear Book* (43:2) — and Sūrah Yā Sīn opens *By the wise Qur'an* (36:2), while 37:2–3 swears by the ranks and the warners. The repetition across the Ḥawāmīm binds the family together: Ghāfir announces *The sending down of the Book is from God* (40:2); Fuṣṣilat opens *A sending down from the Compassionate, the Merciful* (41:2); al-Zukhruf and al-Dukhān both swear by the clear Book. When a phrase recurs in the Qur'an with this density, the commentators teach the reader to look for the emphasis behind it: the Makkan audience's central dispute was the source of this Book — a human invention, they said, or poetry, or sorcery — and the Qur'an answers by swearing, again and again, that it is a sending down. The oath also guards a deeper truth: because the Book is clear, no one on the Day of Judgment can plead that the message was unintelligible. Clarity closes the door of excuse.

**The Clear Book and the Mother of the Book**

Some commentators — among them al-Ṭabarī in his discussion of the phrase — take "the Book" here to be the Qur'an itself as it is recited; others understand it as the guarded tablet of the divine decree, which the Qur'an elsewhere calls *the Mother of the Book*: *God effaces whatsoever He will and establishes; and with Him is the Mother of the Book* (13:39). On the second reading, the oath is sworn upon the original of all scripture, the heavenly record from which every revealed book descends — an oath upon the very architecture of revelation itself. The two readings need not conflict: the earthly Qur'an is, for human beings, the visible form of that heavenly original, and the verses that follow move deliberately between the two — the Book is sent down (v. 3), commands are made distinct in it (v. 4), as a command from God (v. 5). The reader is being shown the chain of revelation from its source to its recitation, and the oath of v. 2 stands at the head of the chain as its authentication.

**Why God's Clarity Demands Our Attention**

There is a quiet practical logic in this verse for every reader. People complain of confusion — too many truths on offer, too many books, too many voices — and the Qur'an's answer is that one Book has been distinguished from all speech as clear, and that its clarity is a matter of divine oath. The verse therefore transfers responsibility: the seeker who is genuinely looking for guidance has been given a criterion that is not obscure. The Qur'an itself draws the same picture of the clarified path when it says, *"And that this is My path, straight; so follow it, and follow not the other paths, lest they scatter you from His path"* (6:153) — a path whose guidance is plain to anyone who walks it with an honest intention, exactly the quality this verse claims for the Book. The modern reader who opens al-Dukhān is being asked to treat the Qur'an as such a path: not as one more ambiguous voice in a noisy world, but as the clear Book by which everything else is measured.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:3

> **truly We sent it down on a blessed night — truly We are ever warning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Oath: A Nightly Descent**

The oath of the previous verse now receives its answer: *innā anzalnāhu fī laylatin mubārakah* — "truly We sent it down on a blessed night." The pronoun *hu* refers to the Book, and the verb *anzalnā* — "We sent down" — carries the emphatic royal plural of divine majesty, used in the Qur'an for acts that display God's greatness. Three truths are packed into this one clause. First, the Qur'an's origin is heaven, not the culture of the Arabs: it was *sent down*, an object moving from above to below, which is why the Qur'an elsewhere insists *"Had We sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, thou wouldst have seen it humbled, rent asunder by the fear of God"* (59:21). Second, the descent happened at a particular time, on a particular night — revelation is an event in history with a date. Third, that night is *mubārakah*, blessed — a word from the root *b-r-k*, whose sense is the settling and increase of good; a *barakah* is a divine goodness that grows, like water that gathers in a hollow and gives life to everything around it. The blessed night is thus not merely a time marker but a description of quality: the night carries an abundance of good that still flows to those who revere it.

**Which Night Is Blessed? The Two Classical Positions**

The commentators are divided into two main camps over the identity of this night, and al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ālūsī all record both. The first and majority view identifies it with the Night of Power (*Laylat al-Qadr*), which the Qur'an itself locates in Ramadan: *"The month of Ramadan is that in which the Qur'an was sent down as guidance for mankind"* (2:185), and to which an entire sūrah is devoted — *"Truly We sent it down on the Night of Power"* (97:1). On this reading, 44:3 and 97:1 describe one and the same event: the descent of the Qur'an on the night of decree, which the Prophet ﷺ urged the believers to seek in the last ten nights of Ramadan, saying:

> "Seek the Night of Power in the odd nights of the last ten [nights] of Ramadan." (al-Bukhārī 2017, and Muslim 1169, from ʿĀʾishah)

The second position, held by a number of earlier authorities and transmitted prominently in the sources, identifies the blessed night with the middle of Shaʿbān — the eve of the fifteenth night of the month that precedes Ramadan, which popular piety in many lands still honors. On this view the Qur'an descended whole from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven on that night. The two positions are reconciled by a process reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and cited across the commentaries: on the night of mid-Shaʿbān the whole Qur'an is sent down to the heaven of this world, and God's decrees for the coming year begin to be drawn up; then, on the Night of Power in Ramadan, the decrees are delivered to the angels who execute them and the Qur'an begins its piecemeal revelation to the Prophet ﷺ through Gabriel. As the commentators al-Ālūsī and others phrase it, what is made distinct on the eve of mid-Shaʿbān is handed over to its keepers on the Night of Power. The two nights are therefore stages of one annual administration of divine command, and the verse's "blessed night" can refer to either — since both are blessed — while the entire complex shows how sacred time in Islam is ordered around revelation.

**Sent Down Whole, Then Sent Down Piece by Piece**

Both camps agree on the underlying model, which the classical commentaries — and Ibn Kathīr's in particular — articulate from reports of Ibn ʿAbbās and others: the Qur'an was first sent down in its entirety, all at once, from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven on the blessed night; from there Gabriel brought it to the Prophet ﷺ in portions over roughly twenty-three years, as occasion required, exactly as the Qur'an says of itself: *"And it is a Qur'an that We have divided, that thou mayest recite it unto mankind at intervals; and We sent it down by sending down"* (17:106). This two-stage descent explains several features of the text: why the whole Book is spoken of as sent down on a single night although its revelation stretched over decades, why verses revealed years apart fit together as one seamless discourse, and why the Prophet ﷺ — who could not read or write — nonetheless received a book whose every portion presupposed the whole. The image is of a king's complete letter entrusted to a courier and delivered chapter by chapter to its readers across a long journey: the letter was finished in heaven before its first line reached the earth.

**The Purpose Clause: "Truly We Are Ever Warning"**

The verse ends with its purpose: *wa-innā kunnā mundhirīn* — "truly We are ever warning." Why was the Book sent down on a blessed night? Because God is a Warner, and the night descent is the beginning of the warning. The imperfect sense of *kunnā* (literally "We were/are ever") expresses an ongoing divine attribute: warning is not a mood God passes through but a constant stance of His mercy toward creatures who forget. The Qur'an describes itself as a warning — *"This is naught but a reminder to the worlds — to whoever among you wills to go straight"* (81:27–28) — and the Prophet ﷺ as *nadhīr*, a warner, sent *"with glad tidings and warning"* (18:56). The logic of the warning is justice itself, as the Qur'an states plainly: *"Messengers as bearers of glad tidings and as warners, so that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers"* (4:165). A warning given before the punishment is the difference between justice and ambush: no traveler can complain of a cliff he was never told about. The Makkan idolaters, the people of Pharaoh, every community that perished — none could say, "We did not know," because the warners had come and the warning had been entered into the record of history.

**The Psychology of Warning, Then and Now**

There is a profound psychological realism in the Qur'an's insistence on warning. Human beings are built to respond to the present and to ignore the distant: the danger that is visible moves us, the danger that is decades or centuries away leaves us unmoved — which is precisely why the Qur'an keeps dragging the future into the present with images like the smoke, the striking, and the boiling liquid. A warning works by *making the distant near*, transferring the future into imagination where it can influence choice. The blessed night is the point in history where God performed that transfer for the whole of humanity: He took the final account and displayed its outline in advance. For the believer who recites this verse, the practical fruit is double: gratitude for the night that brought the warning, and an honest self-check — am I living as someone who has been warned, or as someone who behaves as though no warning ever came? The verse quietly distinguishes the two humanities that the rest of the sūrah will separate: those who receive the warning and change, and those who receive it and return to play.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:4

> **Therein every wise command is made distinct,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night of Administration**

The verse continues its account of the blessed night: *fīhā yufraqu kullu amrin ḥakīm* — "Therein every wise command is made distinct." The verb *yufraqu* is from the root *f-r-q*, to split, separate, or distinguish — the same root as *furqān*, the Qur'an's own title meaning "the Criterion," that which separates truth from falsehood. What happens on the blessed night is an act of separation and specification: every command, every affair, every decree of the coming period is taken from its undifferentiated place in the divine knowledge and assigned its definite form — its time, its manner, its recipient. The noun *amr* spans command and affair, and *ḥakīm* — from *ḥ-k-m*, judgment and wisdom — can be read actively or descriptively: the command that is wise, or the command that is made firm and definitive, sealed against alteration. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note both senses: the decrees of that night are wise in their design and definitive in their execution. Nothing that happens in the coming year is random; each event has already been distinguished, weighed, and written.

**The Annual Decree and the Divine Administration**

This verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement of an annual decree, and the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ālūsī among them — explain it as the yearly administration of the divine will. On the blessed night, the affairs of the coming year are made distinct: which of God's servants will live and which will die, what provision each will receive, what trials and mercies will befall the nations, what commands will be revealed. The classical formulation, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and widely cited, is that God makes all of the decrees on the eve of the middle of Shaʿbān and then delivers them to their keepers — the angels who carry out the commands — on the Night of Power. The same idea appears in the hadith literature concerning the middle of Shaʿbān, though the reports urging special fasting on that specific night are, in the careful judgment of hadith scholars, weak in chain; what stands on firmer ground is the general teaching, confirmed by the Qur'an itself, that the night of decree sees the descent of the angels with every command: *"The angels and the Spirit descend therein, by the leave of their Lord, with every command"* (97:4).

The doctrine is easily misunderstood, and the classical theologians were careful with it. God's knowledge does not change with time; what changes is the *manifestation* of that knowledge to the created world. The decree written on the blessed night is not God learning something new in Shaʿbān — it is the translation of His eternal knowledge into the language of time, so that the angels who administer creation may act, and so that human beings may understand that their years are measured. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said, in a saying recorded in the commentaries on this very passage: "God makes all of the decrees on the eve of the middle of Shaʿbān; then He delivers them to their masters on the Night of Power."

**Wise Commands: The Content of the Decree**

The word *ḥakīm* deserves a full stop of its own. The affairs decreed are not merely decided; they are *wise* — each fitted to a purpose visible or hidden. This is the Qur'an's answer to the ancient complaint that events seem arbitrary: the believer is taught that the year's inventory of joy and grief, health and illness, gain and loss has been drawn up by wisdom. The verse does not say that every decree will appear wise to the one who suffers it — that would contradict the experience of every grieving person — but that every decree is wise in the one who decreed it. This is the difference between believing that everything happens *for* a reason and believing that everything happens *by* a reason: the first can shatter when the reason does not appear; the second holds because the reason is located in God, not in the event's visibility. Al-Rāzī draws out the connection with the verse's placement in a sūrah of warning: even the punishments that the sūrah is about to describe — the smoke, the striking, the drowning of Pharaoh's host — are included in the category of wise commands, because each serves the ultimate wisdom of justice and the reformation of hearts.

**Provision, Death, and the Practical Secret of Tranquility**

The commentators connect this verse with the Qur'an's broad teaching that provision and term are fixed — *"No soul dies save by the leave of God, as a term appointed"* (3:145) — and that the year's sustenance is distributed with the decrees. The practical fruit is a particular kind of tranquility, which the classical scholars describe as the heart's release from the two anxieties that most consume human energy: the anxiety of livelihood and the anxiety of the future. If provision is decreed with wisdom, then hoarding cannot add to it and generosity cannot subtract from it; if the term is decreed, then neither the physician's skill nor the enemy's arrow can move it by a moment. This is not fatalism, and the Qur'an is emphatic that means still matter — the believer prays, works, and treats illness — but it is the end of *terror* before the future. The person who believes in the blessed night's decree is like a traveler who has seen the map: he still walks, still climbs, still chooses his route carefully, but he does not lie awake fearing that the road itself is an illusion. Anxiety is, at bottom, the attempt to live the future before it arrives; the decree doctrine returns the future to God, where it belongs, and leaves the believer only the present — which is the only time in which obedience, love, and work are actually possible.

**Why "Every Wise Command" Includes the Reader's Own Year**

The verse's scope — *kullu amrin*, every command — is total, and the believer who recites it in the present year is being told that this year, too, is administered: its Ramadan, its blessed night, its portion of joy and sorrow, its encounters, its endings. The sūrah of the smoke is not ancient history; its calendar is the reader's calendar. What was made distinct on the blessed night is precisely the stretch of time in which the reader now lives and in which the reader will be asked, at the Day of Division, what he did with his share. This is why the verse sits between the sending down of the Book (v. 3) and the sending of messengers (v. 5): revelation, decree, and messenger are the three instruments by which God administers history with wisdom, and each human being stands inside all three — addressed by a revealed Book, living inside a decreed year, accountable to a sent message.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:5

> **as a command from Us — truly We are ever sending,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Origin Clause: Every Decree Traced to Its Source**

Verse 5 completes the sentence that began in verse 3: the blessed night's descent, the making-distinct of every wise command, and now the origin of it all — *amran min ʿindinā* — "a command from Us." The phrase *min ʿindi*, "from Our Presence," is the Qur'an's way of locating something in the immediate divine presence, unmediated by any creature. When the Qur'an says of Jesus that he was *"a mercy from Us"* (19:21), or of the spirit that it proceeds *"from the command of my Lord"* (17:85), the phrase marks the thing as issuing from God's own counsel rather than from the chain of secondary causes. Here it declares that the entire annual decree — every wise command made distinct on the blessed night — originates directly from God's presence, with no partner, no intercessor, no deputy who decides. Ibn Kathīr comments on this verse that all that occurs does so as a command that originates directly from God's Presence, as does all revelation; the decree of the year and the descent of the Book are one movement, issuing from one source.

**"Truly We Are Ever Sending": The Verb That Never Stops**

The verse ends with *innā kunnā mursilīn* — "truly We are ever sending." The pattern is identical to verse 3's *kunnā mundhirīn*, and the two endings frame the passage: God is ever warning, and God is ever sending. The sending in view is understood by the commentators in two complementary senses, both recorded by Ibn Kathīr, Ibn Juzayy, and the Jalālayn. The first sense is the sending of messengers: the same divine hand that sends down the Book sends the bearers of the Book, from Adam's era to the final Messenger — the entire history of prophecy is a sequence of sendings from the same Presence, and this verse declares that the sequence was never an accident of human history but a standing policy of divine mercy. The second sense is the sending of the Qur'an itself as a mercy to everyone who believes in it: the revelation continues to be "sent" in its recitation and its effect, as guidance descends into hearts whenever the Book is sincerely received. Al-Bayḍāwī observes that the continuous form (*kunnā*) expresses that sending is intrinsic to the divine way of dealing with creatures — not an isolated event in the past but a permanent relation: God is the kind of Lord who sends.

**The Doctrine of Risālah: Why God Sends**

Theologically, the verse plants a flag on the doctrine of prophethood (*risālah*) that the Makkan sūrahs defend on every front. The disbelievers' question was: why should a human being be God's messenger? The Qur'an's answer, built across the Ḥawāmīm and sharpened here, is that sending is the logical completion of creation and decree. If God creates beings who must choose, they must know what they are choosing between — hence warning (v. 3). If God decrees affairs with wisdom (v. 4), the creatures who live inside those decrees must be told how to navigate them — hence messengers (v. 5). A God who decreed but never sent would be a God who held creatures accountable for a road map they never received; the Qur'an blocks that possibility at every turn: *"We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15). Sending is therefore not an interruption of the divine government but its mercy-apparatus: the same wisdom that decrees the year appoints the messengers who explain the year's meaning.

**Prophets as the Delivered Commands**

There is a beautiful implication in calling the messengers themselves part of the "wise command." The prophets were not freelance reformers who happened to agree with heaven; they were decrees in human form, portions of the annual administration made flesh and voice. Each messenger arrived with the precise message his people's situation required: Moses with liberation for an enslaved nation, the Qur'an's warning to a trading people intoxicated with wealth, and so on across the centuries. This is why the sūrah can move, a few verses later, from the sending down of the Book to the sending of Moses against Pharaoh (v. 17) without any sense of a change of subject: both are sendings from the same Presence, governed by the same wisdom, aimed at the same end — that human beings should know their Lord and choose Him freely.

**Application: Living as One Who Has Been Sent To**

For the individual believer, the verse's practical yield is the recovery of a sense of personal address. If God is ever sending, then the message did not stop arriving at the death of the last prophet; the Qur'an remains an ongoing sending — *mursilīn* in the present continuous — and every recitation is a fresh delivery. The believer who reads this verse in prayer is being told: you are not studying an archive; you are receiving a dispatch. And because the sending is from *ʿind* God — His Presence — the seriousness of reception is scaled accordingly: a letter from a king is handled differently from a newspaper. The classical commentators drew exactly this lesson from the verse's placement: because the command is from God, the response to it is not a matter of taste or culture but of obedience — which is the note on which the next verse, with its mention of mercy, hearing, and knowledge, will close this opening movement of the sūrah.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:6

> **as a mercy from thy Lord. Truly He is the Hearing, the Knowing,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy as the Engine of Revelation**

The opening movement of the sūrah now reveals its deepest motive: *raḥmatan min rabbika* — "as a mercy from thy Lord." Grammatically the phrase can be read two ways, and the commentators exploit both. Read as an accusative of reason, it answers the question *why*: the sending down of the Book, the distinguishing of the commands, the dispatch of the messengers — all of it happened *for the purpose of mercy*, as an act of compassion. Read as a continuation of the previous verse, it says: We are sending a mercy from thy Lord — in which case the thing sent is itself named mercy, and the commentators note that this is a description that fits the Qur'an and its Messenger alike. The Qur'an says of itself: *"And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers"* (17:82), and of the Prophet ﷺ it says: *"And We sent thee not, save as a mercy unto the worlds"* (21:107). Al-Qurṭubī records the view that "a mercy" here refers to Muhammad ﷺ himself — the sending of the final Messenger being the greatest act of mercy in the divine administration of history.

**Why Mercy Must Precede the Warning**

The sequence of the passage is itself a lesson in the divine order. Notice what comes first and what comes second: the descent on the blessed night, the making distinct of commands, the sending — and then, only after mercy has been named, does the sūrah turn to warning (vv. 7–9) and to punishment (vv. 10–16). Warning is framed by mercy; justice is the servant of compassion; even the threatened smoke is introduced only after the reader has been told that the whole apparatus of revelation is an apparatus of mercy. This order mirrors a foundational principle of Islamic theology, expressed by the Prophet ﷺ in the famous hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> "When God decreed the creation, He wrote with Himself upon His Throne: 'Truly My mercy prevails over My wrath.'" (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751)

The same priority appears in the Qur'an's self-description: *"Thy Lord has prescribed mercy for Himself"* (6:54). A warning that did not proceed from mercy would be cruelty; a warning that proceeds from mercy is rescue. The sūrah's structure — mercy named before punishment threatened — teaches the reader how to hear everything that follows: the smoke, the striking, the Zaqqūm, the boiling liquid are not the revelations of an angry deity but the emergency signals of a merciful one, the fire alarms installed in a building whose owner loves its inhabitants.

**The Hearing, the Knowing: The Two Names That Close the Case**

The verse seals itself with two divine names: *innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-ʿalīm* — "Truly He is the Hearing, the Knowing." The pairing is precise and pointed. The Samīʿ, the Hearing, hears every plea — and the sūrah will soon present a plea: *"Our Lord! Remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers"* (v. 12). The ʿAlīm, the Knowing, knows every heart — and the sūrah will soon expose hearts that profess belief while planning reversion: *"Surely you will revert"* (v. 15). The two names bracket the entire dynamic of the middle of the sūrah: God hears what is said, and God knows what is meant. A deity who only heard would be deceived by flattery; a deity who only knew would never hear a plea; the combination of hearing and knowledge is what makes judgment both compassionate and incorruptible. Ibn Kathīr notes the connection of the two names to the sending itself: the mercy is sent by One who hears the need of every creature and knows precisely what each creature requires — no prayer is lost in the crowd, and no prescription is miswritten.

**The Personal Register: "From Thy Lord"**

The address shifts register at the very end of the movement: *min rabbika* — "from thy Lord." Throughout the opening verses the Qur'an speaks in the majestic plural — *We sent it down, We are ever warning, We are ever sending* — but at the moment of naming mercy it turns singular and intimate: *thy* Lord, the address of the Prophet ﷺ and, through him, of every reciter. The rhetorical effect is deliberate: the decrees of the year are overwhelming, the administration of history is vast, but mercy is personal. The same God who governs galaxies governs *thy* life; the mercy that descended on a blessed night is addressed to *thee*. Every reader of the Qur'an is meant to feel the shift as a hand placed on the shoulder: the warning is for the nations, but the mercy is for you — if you will receive it. The verse's closing names then guarantee the reach of that mercy: it is heard — however faint the voice that asks; it is known — however confused the heart that needs it. The movement that began with an oath upon the clear Book ends with the Book's true purpose stated in one word: mercy.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:7

> **Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them; would that you were certain.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Book to the Whole of Creation**

The seventh verse performs a sudden widening of the frame. The sūrah began with a Book, a night, a decree; now the horizon expands to its maximum: *rabbu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā* — "Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them." The One who sent down the Book is the One who owns the totality of existence; the Sender and the Lord of all things are identical. The phrase *mā baynahumā* — "whatsoever is between them" — is the Qur'an's formula for everything that fills the cosmos: the stars and the space between them, the unseen realms and the visible ones, whatever lies within the sweep of the heavens above and the earth below. It recurs across the revelation like a refrain — at 5:17, 19:65, 20:6, 25:59, 37:5, 38:66, 44:7 and 38, and elsewhere — always with the same function: no corner of existence falls outside the lordship being claimed.

**The Argument from Lordship to Revelation**

The logic of the verse is an argument, though it moves silently. The Makkan idolaters, as the commentators repeatedly note, did not deny that there was a creator and lord of the heavens and the earth; their denial was more precise — they refused to accept that this Lord had sent a Book and a Messenger to them. The Qur'an's reply, here and throughout the Makkan period, is that lordship and revelation cannot be separated. If God is Lord of the heavens and the earth — Owner, Sustainer, Governor — then His governance of human beings necessarily includes telling them what He requires; a lord who owned everything but communicated nothing would be an owner without a governor's care, and the Qur'an insists that the divine lordship is active: *"His are the creation and the command"* (7:54). Al-Ālūsī, commenting in this vein on our verse, argues that the acknowledgment of God's universal lordship, if truly known and not merely inherited, would compel the acknowledgment that He also sends revelation and messengers as a mercy — the very point made in verse 6. One who admits the Lordship of all things cannot rationally exempt himself from the claims of the Lord's message.

**"Would That You Were Certain": The Rebuke of Half-Knowledge**

The verse ends with a cry that is almost a sigh of exasperation: *laʿallakum tūqinūn* — rendered "would that you were certain," and more literally "perhaps you might be certain" or, as many commentators read the tone, an expression of yearning mixed with rebuke: if only you had certainty. The verb is from the root *y-q-n*, which in Arabic denotes a knowledge so firm that doubt cannot touch it — the settled conviction that the Qur'an calls *ḥaqq al-yaqīn*, "the truth of certainty" (69:51). The verse's target is the peculiar state of the idolaters: they knew enough to acknowledge a creator — their own language of oaths invoked Him, their crisis prayers reached for Him — but their knowledge never ripened into certainty, never produced the obedience that certainty would require. Their knowledge was, in the classical phrase, a knowledge of the tongue and the inherited habit, not of the heart's discernment. Al-Qurṭubī describes such people as those who say "God is our Lord" with their lips while their lives contradict every implication of the claim.

**Degrees of Knowledge and the Modern Epidemic of Half-Belief**

The psychological anatomy here is worth dwelling on, because the condition it names is not extinct. The verse identifies a state familiar to every observer of human beings: the gap between what people *know* and what people *live*. Smokers know the statistics and light up; the arrogant know humility is wiser and remain proud; the idolaters of Makkah knew God was Lord and worshipped idols. The Qur'an's diagnosis of this universal gap is not ignorance but the absence of *yaqīn* — that is, the absence of the kind of knowledge that has passed through reflection and reached the will. Information is not certainty; certainty is information that has become identity. This is why the Qur'an keeps pairing knowledge with action and why it describes faith as a knowing that transforms: *"And those who are given knowledge and faith say..."* (30:56). Half-knowledge is actually more dangerous than ignorance, because it inoculates: the half-knower believes he has already dealt with the question.

**Where Certainty Comes From**

The verse's implied question — how does inherited or superficial acknowledgment become true certainty? — is answered by the sūrah's own method. The Book was sent down on a blessed night (v. 3); the Book's warnings and histories are the instrument by which certainty is built; the evidence of lordship in the heavens and the earth is the second instrument, which the Qur'an elsewhere calls the signs on the horizons and in the souls: *"We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (41:53). Certainty, in the Qur'anic scheme, is not a mystical luxury awarded to a few; it is the normal fruit of sustained attention to two books — the revealed Book and the created world — and both are pressed upon the reader in this single verse: the Book from verse 2, the cosmos in verse 7. The believer who wants the certainty the verse longs for has been told where to dig.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:8

> **There is no god but He; He gives life and causes death, your Lord and the Lord of your fathers of old.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Testimony Verse of the Sūrah**

Verse 8 is the sūrah's *shahādah* — its testimony of divine unity: *lā ilāha illā huwa* — "There is no god but He." The formula is the exact negative-and-positive structure that runs through the whole Qur'anic proclamation: first the denial — no deity, nothing worthy of worship, no object of ultimate devotion — then the affirmation — except He. The two-part structure is not merely rhetorical; it is the logical skeleton of monotheism. A statement that only affirmed God would leave room for partners; a statement that only denied false gods would leave the heart without an object; the Qur'anic testimony does both at once, sweeping the field of all rivals and then fixing devotion on the One who remains. This verse stands in a family of testimony verses across the Qur'an — *"God, there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsistent"* (2:255) — and its function here is to ground everything that follows in the unity of the One who warns and punishes and rewards.

**Life and Death: The Two Verbs of Absolute Power**

The testimony is immediately demonstrated by two verbs: *yuḥyī wa-yumīt* — "He gives life and causes death." The pairing is the Qur'an's standard proof of exclusive divinity, recurring across the revelation (2:258; 3:156; 7:158; 9:116; 10:56; 22:6; 23:80; 40:68; 53:44; 57:2). The argument is simple and devastating: every other thing that people worship — idols, wealth, stars, kings, their own desires — either has never given life or cannot prevent death. The one power that every human being must one day meet, the power that gives existence at the beginning and takes it at the end, belongs to God alone; therefore worship, which is the response of the whole self to the source of its existence, belongs to Him alone. The sequence "gives life and causes death" is also deliberately ordered: life comes first, because existence precedes nonexistence in the order of experienced reality — we are alive before we die, and the One who holds the first also holds the last. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the pairing covers every phase of existence: the One who originates life is the One who terminates it, and the One who terminates it is the One who can restore it — which is precisely the ground of the resurrection the sūrah will defend against the deniers in verses 34–36.

**An Address That Crosses Generations**

The verse then turns personal and genealogical: *rabbukum wa-rabbu ābāʾikumu al-awwalīn* — "your Lord and the Lord of your fathers of old." The phrase is aimed like an arrow at the idolaters' most cherished argument: the religion of the ancestors. The Makkans defended their idols by appealing to the fathers — *"We found our fathers upon a community, and we are guided by their traces"* (43:22). The Qur'an's reply here is masterfully economical: the Lord who made you also made your fathers. The ancestors' religion is not an independent authority that must be honored against God; the ancestors themselves were creatures of this same Lord, and if the fathers were alive to be asked, they would be bound by the same testimony. Ancestral custom deserves respect as a social inheritance but not as a theological argument, because the chain of ancestry ends where every chain ends — in the Creator, who precedes all fathers. The verse also carries a gentler note beneath its rebuke: God is not a foreign deity imported to overturn tribal loyalty; He is the God the Arabs' own fathers knew in the age of Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl, before the idols intruded. The call of Islam was, in this sense, a restoration of the ancestral religion of pure monotheism rather than an innovation.

**The Unity That the Sūrah's Contrasts Presuppose**

The verse's placement is theologically essential for what follows. The sūrah is about to describe a Day of Division in which some are dragged to the Zaqqūm and others are settled in gardens and springs. Such a division is only just if one God — the same God — decides both fates by one standard: *"Truly the Day of Division is their tryst, all together"* (v. 40). If there were many gods, each with favorites, judgment would be a tribal affair; the unity of the Judge is the guarantee of the fairness of the judgment. The sūrah thus plants its testimony at the front so that its threats and promises at the back will stand on monotheistic ground: the same Lord who gives life and death to Pharaoh's host and to the Quraysh will raise them all and divide them all, with no intercessor's partiality and no idol's protection. The believer who recites *lā ilāha illā huwa* in this verse is therefore not merely mouthing a formula but accepting the entire architecture of the chapter: one Lord, one standard, one reckoning, two ends.

**Living the Testimony of the Verse**

There is a practical test embedded in the verse's two verbs. Whatever a person cannot live without, whatever he fears losing more than life, whatever he would sacrifice everything to keep — that thing stands in the position of a god in his practical theology, even if his tongue recites the testimony. The Qur'an says of such inverted worship: *"Hast thou seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?"* (25:43). The verse's remedy is the contemplation of its own two verbs: the thing you have made your god cannot give you life — it is itself dependent on the One who gives life; and it cannot save you from death — nothing you own or desire will accompany you through it. To say "there is no god but He" with full meaning is to audit the heart's actual devotions — money, status, approval, comfort — and to return each to its proper place as a creature, keeping the throne of the heart for the only One whose giving and taking are absolute. This is the daily work of the testimony, and the sūrah will spend its remaining fifty-one verses showing why the work matters.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:9

> **Nay, but they are playing in doubt.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn From Testimony to Accusation**

After seven verses of majestic declaration — the Book, the blessed night, the decree, mercy, lordship, unity — the sūrah wheels around abruptly and fixes its gaze on the audience: *bal hum fī shakkin yalʿabūn* — "Nay, but they are playing in doubt." The particle *bal* ("nay, but") is a rebuttal; it strikes from the speaker's throat like a verdict. Against the background of the testimony just given, the verse asserts: despite all this clarity, they are not seekers — they are players. The clause contains two words that reward slow reading: *shakk*, doubt, and *yalʿabūn*, they play. The doubt is not the honest uncertainty of a mind looking for evidence; it is the posture of people who have made a game of the question of truth, treating the Messenger and his warnings as something to toy with, jest about, and turn into sport. The Qur'an uses the same accusation against the deniers of other prophets — *"in play"* and *"jesting"* recur in its indictments — and the pairing of doubt with play is precise: doubt is the playground of those who do not want to decide, and play is what one does with questions one refuses to take seriously.

**The Anatomy of Playing Doubt**

What does it look like to "play in doubt"? The sūrah's own context supplies the answer. The Makkans acknowledged a creator (v. 7) yet served idols; they witnessed the Qur'an's inimitability yet called it the speech of a man taught by others (v. 14); they were warned of the smoke and the striking and answered with demands for their fathers' resurrection as a taunt (v. 36). Their doubt was not intellectual but moral: they moved from one position to another as convenience dictated, never settling, because settling would have cost them their privilege. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī, in their treatments of this verse, note that the doubters are described as *playing* because their engagement with the truth had the structure of a game — amusement without commitment, moves without stakes, endless deferral of the moment of decision. The verse names the deepest pathology of the arrogant: they treat the most serious question in existence — the question of their own eternal fate — as if it were a pastime, because seriousness would force them to change.

**Playing Versus Seeking: Two Relations to Truth**

The contrast with the reverent is implied and will be made explicit later in the sūrah: the *muttaqūn*, the reverent, are described in the final movement as those who *believed* and were secure. The difference between the two parties is not IQ or information; it is the difference between playing with a question and being played by it. The seeker stands before the truth as a patient stands before a diagnosis — frightened, perhaps, but wanting to know, because knowing is the condition of treatment. The player stands before the truth as a spectator before a spectacle — entertained, detached, free to walk away. The Qur'an describes the same division elsewhere: *"And among mankind is one who worships God upon a brink; if good befalls him, he is content thereby, but if a trial befalls him, he turns completely around"* (22:11). Doubt that plays is the luxury of those who feel they can afford to lose; the sūrah's next verses will demonstrate that no one can afford it.

**The Psychological Mechanism of Perpetual Suspension**

Modern psychology has a name for what the verse describes: willful suspension of judgment as a defense against inconvenient evidence. The doubters of Makkah did not lack evidence — they had the Qur'an itself, its beauty, its internal coherence, its fulfilled predictions, its effect on lives; they had the example of the Prophet's ﷺ known honesty, the very trustworthiness they had relied on for decades before his call. The verse's diagnosis is that their doubt was not a conclusion their minds reached but a posture their wills maintained. This is why the Qur'an so often speaks of *kufr* — covering — in connection with them: the truth was present, and something in them worked to keep it covered. The play element explains the maintenance costs: a serious doubt must eventually resolve or collapse; a playful doubt can be sustained indefinitely because it never commits to its own questions. Every evasion — changing the subject, mocking the messenger, demanding fresh miracles after every miracle, appealing to the ancestors — was a move in the game, and the game's object was to avoid the one thing a real question demands: an answer.

**The Warning Hidden in the Play**

Yet the verse is not merely an insult; it is a warning wrapped in a diagnosis. The sūrah is about to describe the moment when play ends: the smoke covers the people, the plea goes up, and the players discover that the game had stakes all along. The Qur'an states the general rule with terrible clarity: *"Warn them of the Day of Regret, when the affair will have been decided, while they are in heedlessness and do not believe"* (19:39). There is a time for playing with the truth — or rather, there is a season in which playing is still *possible*; the verse's implied message to the reader is that the season is finite. Every reciter of this verse is therefore asked a personal question: where do I stand — am I seeking, or am I playing? Do I engage the claims of revelation with the seriousness of a patient who wants to live, or with the detachment of a spectator who wants entertainment? The difference, the sūrah insists, will be visible on the day the sky brings forth its manifest smoke, when the players' laughter stops and the whole game is seen for what it was: the most expensive amusement ever purchased.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:10

> **So keep watch for a day when the sky brings forth manifest smoke,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Wait and Watch**

The sūrah now issues its first direct order to the Prophet ﷺ: *fa-irtaqib* — "so keep watch." The verb is from *r-q-b*, to watch for something expected, and it carries the sense of patient, vigilant expectancy — a sentry's waiting, not a lounger's. The Prophet is commanded to await a day, a *yawm*, on which the sky will produce something unprecedented: *dukhān mubīn*, manifest smoke. The command is double-edged: it consoles the Prophet ﷺ — the day of their punishment is coming; you need only wait — and it warns the disbelievers — the day is appointed; your game has a deadline. The Qur'an addresses the Prophet with the same verb in parallel passages when his people demand the punishment be hastened: *"Say: 'Wait, for I, too, with you, am of those who wait'"* (52:31, and cf. 6:158; 7:71; 10:20, 102; 11:122; 32:30). The waiting of the believer and the waiting of the disbeliever run on different clocks and end in different places: one waits for vindication, the other for exposure.

**The Manifest Smoke: What the Commentators Understood**

The image is stark: a smoke so dense and so visible that the sky itself appears to give it birth — *taʾtī bi-dukhānin mubīn*, "brings forth manifest smoke." Like the splitting of the moon and the rising of the sun from the west, the smoke belongs to the Qur'an's catalogue of the great portents of the end of time, and the classical commentators present two main readings of the verse. The first reading, favored by many of the earlier authorities and embedded in a famous hadith of Ibn Masʿūd, understands the verse as already fulfilled — a description of the famine that struck the Quraysh in the lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ, when he prayed against them with the prayer of Joseph's years and hunger pressed them so hard that a starving man saw smoke between himself and the sky. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Ibn Masʿūd that he was asked about the verse and replied that five signs had already passed — the smoke, the moon, the Romans, the great grip, and the inevitable event — and in the fullest version of the report, transmitted by Muslim:

> "The Quraysh were slow to accept Islam, so the Prophet ﷺ prayed: 'O God, help me against them with seven years like the seven years of Joseph.' So famine seized them until they ate bones and carcasses, and a man would look to the sky and see what appeared to be smoke between him and it." (Muslim 2798, from Ibn Masʿūd)

On this reading the verse was a prophecy delivered before its own fulfillment: the Prophet was told to watch for the day — and then lived to see it.

**The Second Reading: A Portent of the Hour**

The second reading, held by al-Qurṭubī among others and supported by the parallel in Sūrah al-Rūm and the general Makkan context, understands the manifest smoke as one of the great signs of the Hour that are still to come — the smoke that will precede the Day of Resurrection and cover all mankind, afflicting the believers like a cold while penetrating the bodies of the disbelievers with torment. The two readings are not necessarily rivals, and many commentators — including al-Qurṭubī and the Jalālayn — hold that the verse refers to both: a foretaste in the famine of the Quraysh, a final fulfillment at the end of time. This double reference is characteristic of Qur'anic prophecy: events are announced in a way that lets them land first on their immediate audience and later on history's horizon, so that each generation reads the sign at its own station. The famine was the smoke's first draft; the smoke of the Hour is its final edition.

**Why the Sky? The Logic of the Image**

There is a pointed logic in the sign's location. The sky, in the Qur'an's cosmology, is the seat of the idols' impotence — the moon and stars the Arabs worshipped as deities lived there — and it is the route of revelation: the Book descended from it on a blessed night (vv. 3–5). A punishment that comes *from the sky* declares that the One who sends down mercy from heaven also sends down wrath from heaven; the same direction carries both. The smoke also inverts the idolaters' experience of plenty: the sky that brought the rains that fed their caravans and their herds brings instead a suffocating cloud, the harvest of their own denial. And the smoke is *mubīn* — manifest, undeniable: no one can claim it was a trick of the light, an optical illusion, a misunderstanding. The punishment that comes in the open is the punishment that closes every excuse.

**Keeping Watch in the Present Tense**

For the modern reader the verse's command — *keep watch* — retains its full force, because the watch has not been cancelled. Every generation of believers has recited this verse and understood itself to be living inside the waiting period it describes. The verse trains the believer in a specific spiritual posture: alertness without anxiety, expectancy without paralysis. The sentry does not know the hour of the relief or the attack, so he keeps the watch constant; the Qur'an says the same of the Hour: *"They ask thee about the Hour, when it will come to pass. Say: 'The knowledge of it is only with my Lord'"* (7:187). The practical fruit of the watch is moral: people who believe the sky may bring forth its smoke today are people who cannot postpone repentance until tomorrow. The verse is thus a device for compressing time — making the distant day present enough to govern today's choices — and that compression is exactly what the disbelievers' play (v. 9) refused.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:11

> **covering the people. This is a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Blanket of Smoke: The Sign's Second Stroke**

The description continues without a pause for breath: *yaghshā al-nās* — "covering the people." The verb is from *gh-sh-y*, to cover or envelop, the same root used for the veil that covers hearts at 2:7 and for the overwhelming event of the Resurrection, *al-Ghāshiyah*, the "Enveloper," which gives Sūrah 88 its name. The smoke is not a distant cloud on the horizon; it is a blanket lowered over the population, wrapping the whole of society in a single affliction. In the famine reading of the verse, this is the smoke as the starving saw it — rising between them and the sky until it seemed to cover everything — and the commentators add that it afflicted the believers among them only as a discomfort, like a harsh cold, while it entered the bodies of the disbelievers and tormented them, a distinction preserved in al-Māwardī's and al-Qurṭubī's commentaries and drawn from the reports of the event. The covering is also a symbol: what covers people here — smoke, obscuring their sight of the sky — is the outward trace of what had covered their hearts, the *kufr* that veiled the truth from their perception.

**The Suffering That Speaks for Itself**

The verse closes with the sufferers' own verdict: *hādhā ʿadhābun alīm* — "This is a painful punishment." The phrase can be read, as the commentators note, in three ways: as the Qur'an's own description of the affliction; as the words of the disbelievers, recognizing the smoke for what it is; or as what is said to them by way of rebuke — the verdict spoken over their heads, as the dwellers of the Fire will be told, *"This is the Fire which you used to deny"* (52:14). All three readings converge on one point: the punishment is recognized as punishment. The smoke cannot be explained away as weather, coincidence, or politics; those it touches know, with the terrible clarity that suffering brings, that they are being punished. This recognition is itself part of the punishment — the difference between a calamity that can be rationalized and one that announces its own meaning. The verse thus completes the arc from verse 9: the players who played in doubt are now the people covered; the game has become pain.

**The Efficiency of the Warning**

There is a deep mercy embedded in the very severity of the description, which the sūrah's placement makes clear: the smoke comes *before* the final striking (v. 16), the Zaqqūm, and the boiling liquid. It is a warning punishment, calibrated to awaken rather than to finish — a first dose administered so that the full prescription may be avoided. This is the pattern of the Qur'an's dealing with the Makkans and with the guilty communities before them: *"And We shall make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater punishment, that haply they may return"* (32:21), where the commentators understand the "lesser punishment" as the afflictions of this world — famine, loss, defeat — and the "greater" as the punishment of the Hereafter or of final destruction. The logic is that of a physician's escalating treatment or a parent's graduated correction: mercy uses the smallest force that can still communicate. The people covered by the smoke are being addressed, not merely struck: the punishment is a sentence with a meaning, and the meaning is *return*.

**When Pain Becomes Language**

For the believer reading the verse in tranquility, the scene raises the question of how suffering functions in human life. The Qur'anic answer, developed across the revelation, is that pain is one of God's most effective messengers precisely because it bypasses the defenses that words cannot penetrate. The disbelievers of Makkah had heard the Qur'an's warnings for years and played in doubt; the smoke reached them where argument had not — through the stomach, the lungs, the eyes. A modern reader can recognize the same structure in personal life: warnings arrive softly for years — in conscience, in counsel, in the quiet consequences of small errors — and when they are not heard, life supplies louder ones. The verse does not glorify suffering; it reports its function. And it carries a quiet corollary for the believer: if pain is a language, then the believer's own trials are also messages to be read rather than merely endured — invitations to examine the road before the road gives way. The people of the smoke were told what their suffering meant; the wisdom is to read our own before we are told in a voice we cannot mistake.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:12

> **"Our Lord! Remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cry from Under the Smoke**

The smoke does its work: the people covered by it find their voices and their theology at once, and what comes out is prayer — *rabbanā ikshif ʿannā al-ʿadhāb innā muʾminūn* — "Our Lord! Remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers." The scene is one of the Qur'an's most piercing ironies. Throughout the sūrah the disbelievers had refused to address God except through the vocabulary of the ancestors and the game of doubt; now, under the blanket of smoke, the vocabulary of faith arrives instantly — Lord, belief, supplication. The verse does not tell us whether their prayer is accepted in the moment, only that it is uttered; and the reader familiar with the Qur'an knows the pattern: belief that arrives with the punishment is a belief of the last hour, whose sincerity God alone weighs. Al-Qurṭubī reads the sentence as conditional: they mean, "If You remove the punishment, we will believe" — the bargain of the desperate, the faith that negotiates instead of surrendering.

**A Pattern Repeating Across the Scripture**

The Qur'an records this cry repeatedly, always at the moment when the alternative has closed: the guilty in the Fire plead, *"Our Lord! Bring us out of it; if we revert, then we are wrongdoers"* (23:107); the idolaters at the brink say, *"We believe in Him!"* (10:90) as the water closes over Pharaoh; and the Qur'an comments on the general law: *"But their belief was of no avail to them when they saw Our might — the established way of God which has passed over His servants"* (40:85). The repetition is deliberate pedagogy: the Qur'an is showing the reader, in scene after scene, the shape of a repentance that comes too late, so that the reader may perform the real thing while there is still time. The difference between the faith of this verse and the faith of the believer is not the words — both say "we believe" — but the timing and its cause: one believes because it has become profitable, the other because it has become true.

**The Removal of Punishment and the Return of the Players**

The context makes the scene's sequel inevitable. The verse's plea stands between the description of the smoke (vv. 10–11) and the next verse's question: *"How can there be a reminder for them...?"* (v. 13). Even if the punishment is lifted — as the next verses say it will be, partially — the belief professed under duress will not survive the lifting. This is the psychological law of convenience-faith: what fear produces, relief dissolves. The same people who prayed with clasped hands will return to their idols when the sky clears, which is precisely why verse 15 says: *"We shall indeed remove the punishment a little. Surely you will revert."* The commentators connect the scene to the famine hadith: when the Quraysh were stricken, Abū Sufyān came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked him to pray for rain, acknowledging his Lord; the Prophet prayed, the rain came, and the people reverted to their denial — a living commentary on this verse performed in real time.

**Why God Tests the Sincerity of Emergency Faith**

The verse raises a subtle question about divine justice: if the people believe under the smoke, why does the Qur'an treat their faith with suspicion? The answer lies in the Qur'an's understanding of what faith is. Faith, in the Qur'anic sense, is not a verbal profession extracted by fear; it is the soul's orientation — knowledge of heart, trust in will, obedience in action — that can only be tested in conditions of freedom. A belief produced by torture or by catastrophe is, in the Qur'an's analysis, not yet a belief of the person; it is a reflex of the creature. This is why the Qur'an insists that faith must be chosen in ease as well as in hardship, and why it warns the believers themselves that their faith will be examined: *"Do people suppose they will be left to say, 'We believe,' and they will not be tried?"* (29:2). The sincerity of the smoke-prayer is tested precisely by being answered: when the punishment lifts, does the belief remain? The sūrah's answer — they will revert — exposes the prayer as a transaction. The believer's prayer, by contrast, is not a transaction with God but a relationship; it continues in ease and hardship alike, because it was never purchased by either.

**The Lesson for the Reader's Own Prayers**

Every reader of this verse has stood, in some form, where the people of the smoke stood: the crisis prayer, the midnight vow, the promise made to God in the hospital corridor or the examination hall — "if You get me through this, I will change." The verse does not condemn such prayers; it is a mercy that people pray at all, and the Qur'an itself says the disbelievers turn to God in ships' distress (10:22–23). But the verse exposes the fragility of the vow that is only crisis-deep, and it invites the reader to examine his own history: what happened to the last emergency promise? The difference between the smoke-prayer and the believer's prayer is not sincerity at the moment of utterance — both are sincere — but the quality of memory: the believer maintains the state that the crisis produced, so that the removal of the punishment becomes an occasion of gratitude rather than of reversion. The verse's quiet counsel: do not let your prayers be only the smoke's children; give God some words in the clear sky too, so that the next smoke — if it comes — finds you already His.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:13

> **How can there be a reminder for them, when a clear messenger had come to them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Question That Closes the Door**

The sūrah answers the smoke-prayer with a question that is itself the verdict: *annā lahumu al-dhikrā* — "How can there be a reminder for them?" The word *dhikrā* (from *dh-k-r*) is the Qur'an's term for the effective remembrance — the warning that lands, the admonition that changes conduct. The question is rhetorical; its implied answer is that no reminder can now avail them, and the verse immediately supplies the reason: *wa-qad jāʾahum rasūlun mubīn* — "when a clear messenger had come to them." The particle *qad* marks the past event as complete and settled: the messenger came, fully and finally. The logic is exact: a reminder is only possible where the message has not yet been delivered; these people were delivered the message in its clearest form by a messenger whose truthfulness they themselves had certified for decades — and they had turned away. The reminder's season ended not because God withheld it but because they exhausted it.

**Why Punishment Ends the Season of Reminder**

The verse encodes a principle that runs through the Qur'an and that the commentators — including the Jalālayn and Ibn Kathīr in their discussions of the passage — articulate: once the punishment has come upon a people, the door of acceptance closes, because the faith that follows punishment is no longer free faith. The Qur'an states the principle at the level of communities: *"But their belief was of no avail to them when they saw Our might"* (40:85), and at the level of individuals at death: *"Repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'Truly now I repent'"* (4:18). The reason is not that God withholds mercy vindictively but that the test itself requires freedom: a belief compelled by catastrophe is like an answer shouted under torture — it reveals nothing about what the person would freely choose. The smoke of verses 10–11 was the beginning of compulsion; with compulsion, the examination was over. The reminder could no longer function because the condition in which reminders function — open choice in ordinary time — had passed.

**The Clear Messenger Whom They Had Already Received**

The verse's sting is in its final phrase: the messenger who came to them was *mubīn* — clear, manifest — the same word applied to the Book in verse 2. The messenger's clarity was total: his lineage was known to them, his honesty was proverbial (they called him al-Amīn, the Trustworthy, before the call), his message was in their own language, and his miracles were witnessed. There was nothing ambiguous to excuse them: no obscure messenger speaking an obscure tongue, no distant prophet whose authenticity could be doubted in good faith. The Qur'an presses this point against the Makkans repeatedly: *"And We sent no messenger save with the tongue of his people, that he might make clear to them"* (14:4); *"Or do they say there is madness in him? Nay, he brought them the truth, but most of them hate the truth"* (23:70). The argument from clarity closes the avenue of excuse: when the messenger is clear, the refusal is no longer a cognitive problem; it is a moral choice, and moral choices are precisely what get judged.

**The Sorrow of the Closed Door — and Its Purpose**

There is a somber undertone in this verse that the believer should not hurry past. God is presented here as One who closes doors — not gladly, but necessarily. The Qur'an elsewhere reveals the divine reluctance behind the closure: *"And had We willed, We could have given every soul its guidance; but the Word from Me came true: I shall fill Hell with jinn and mankind together"* (32:13). Yet the closure is never arbitrary: it is the logical terminus of a series of refusals, each of which was freely chosen. The verse thus functions as the Qur'an's answer to a question every observer of history asks: why do warnings so often seem to fail, and why do the warned persist until destruction? Because a warning has a career: it is offered, received or refused, repeated, refused again — and at the point where refusal has become the person's settled identity, the warning itself changes function, from an invitation to a testimony. The reminder that can no longer save them will still be read in the record against them.

**The Reader's Own Season of Reminder**

The verse's practical translation is urgent and personal: the reminder has a season, and the season is now. Every believer knows the experience of postponed response — the intention to change deferred to "later," the repentance scheduled for Ramadan, the return promised for old age. This verse stands against every postponement: the dhikrā that is possible today may be structurally impossible tomorrow, not because God grows angry but because the self hardens — the door that is never opened loses its hinges, a point the sūrah will make in its own way when it describes hearts that can no longer receive. The believer's task is therefore to treat today's reminder as possibly the last one that can still function: to answer the messenger's clarity with the only answer that honors clarity — immediate, free, and complete acceptance — rather than with the smoke-prayer of verse 12, which comes too late to be a test of anything but regret.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:14

> **Then they turned away from him and said, "He is taught, possessed."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Accusations That Explain the Turning**

The verse narrates the Makkans' response to the clear messenger with brutal economy: *thumma tawallaw ʿanhu* — "then they turned away from him" — and their verdict: *muʿallam majnūn* — "taught, possessed." Two accusations, and each is an inverted testimony. *Muʿallam* ("taught") concedes that the Qur'an's speech exceeds what an unlettered man could produce — so it must have been learned from someone; the Qur'an answers this charge elsewhere directly: *"We know that they say, 'He has merely been taught by a human being'"* (16:103) — and exposes its absurdity: the alleged teacher was a foreigner while the Qur'an is in clear Arabic (16:103's sequel). *Majnūn* ("possessed") concedes that the Prophet's behavior does not follow ordinary patterns — so he must be driven by a jinn; the Qur'an answers: *"You are not, by the blessing of your Lord, possessed"* (68:2). The two charges are mutually contradictory — one says he is too clever, the other that he is deranged — but contradictions do not trouble those whose only goal is refusal. The pattern is ancient: the prophets were called liars, poets, sorcerers, and madmen by turns (51:52), whatever label best served the refusal of the moment.

**The Anatomy of Turning Away**

The verb *tawallaw* — from *w-l-y*, to turn one's back — deserves attention: it describes a physical and moral about-face, the deliberate presentation of the back to the one addressing you. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the disbelievers' flight from reminders: *"Then what is wrong with them that they turn away from the reminder — as if they were frightened donkeys fleeing from a lion?"* (74:49–50). Turning away is a more complete rejection than arguing: the arguer still faces the message; the one who turns away has ended the conversation. The verse records that the turning preceded the slander — first the back, then the labels — which reveals the true order of their psychology: the accusations were not the cause of their rejection but its weaponry. They had already decided; the labels were manufactured afterward to supply respectable reasons.

**"Taught, Possessed": The Sociology of Dismissal**

The two labels are worth examining as social phenomena, because every carrier of truth meets their modern equivalents. The charge of being "taught" is the accusation of inauthenticity — the claim that the reformer is someone else's mouthpiece, the movement a conspiracy, the revelation a plagiarism; it survives today as the endless hunt for the "real source" behind every prophetic voice. The charge of being "possessed" is the accusation of irrationality — the claim that the truth-bearer is unbalanced, fanatical, unstable; it survives as the clinical dismissal of conviction as pathology. Both charges share one function: they allow the audience to discredit the messenger without examining the message. The Qur'an's repeated answer is to insist on the message's self-evidence: *"Or do they say he invented it? Nay, it is the truth from thy Lord, that thou mayest warn a people to whom no warner came before thee, that haply they may be guided"* (32:3). A message that is true does not become false because its carrier is slandered.

**Why the Slander Is Recorded: Testimony and Consolation**

The verse's preservation of the exact slanders serves two purposes, both noted by the commentators. The first is testimony: on the Day of Division, the record of what was said will be read back — *"This is that which you used to doubt"* (v. 50) — and the record must be complete, for the judgment's fairness requires that the accused's own words be the evidence. The second is consolation for the Prophet ﷺ and for every prophet: the slanders were not new; the messengers before him had been called the same names, and their histories ended in vindication. When the Qur'an tells the Prophet that the people say he is taught and possessed, it is saying, in effect: this is the standard reception of the truth by those who have chosen against it; do not measure your mission by their labels. The believer who carries any truth — a parent guiding a child, a reformer addressing corruption, a scholar correcting error — will recognize the labels in his own reception and can draw the same consolation.

**The Weapon That Becomes a Witness**

There is a final irony the verse leaves for the reader to unwrap. The same people who said "taught" were the people who, under the smoke, cried "we believe" (v. 12); and the same Prophet they called possessed was the one they begged to pray for rain when the famine pressed them — as the hadith of Ibn Masʿūd records, with Abū Sufyān himself coming to the man he had called mad to ask his intercession. The slanderers' own history refutes their slander: they turned to the "possessed" teacher in their extremity, which is the clearest possible admission that they knew, underneath the labels, exactly who he was. The verse thus convicts them from their own conduct, and it teaches the believer a principle of enduring usefulness: never waste energy answering every label; live so that the labels contradict themselves, and let the slanderers' own need of you — or of the truth you carry — become the witness. The back that is turned today will beg tomorrow; the wise carry the truth in both directions of that journey.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:15

> **We shall indeed remove the punishment a little. Surely you will revert.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Partial Lifting: Mercy With a Diagnosis**

The divine reply to the smoke-prayer comes in this verse, and it is unlike anything the pleaders expected: *innā kāshifū al-ʿadhābi qalīlan* — "We shall indeed remove the punishment a little." The removal is real — God is not mocked by the prayer; He answers it — but the verse attaches two qualifications: the removal is *qalīlan*, little and temporary, and the reason is stated in the same breath: *innakum ʿāʾidūn* — "surely you will revert." The verse is a double-edged sentence: mercy in its first half, diagnosis in its second. God grants the relief and simultaneously declares that the relief will not produce what the pleaders promised. The removal is thus not the answer to their faith but the exposure of it: they said, "Remove the punishment; we are believers" — and God says, in effect: We will remove it, and you will show what your belief is worth. The lifting of the punishment becomes the test that the punishment itself could not administer.

**The Two Readings of "Revert"**

The commentators divide over the meaning of *ʿāʾidūn*, and both readings illuminate the text. The first reading, tied to the famine event, understands reversion as the historical return of the Quraysh to disbelief once the famine broke: the rain came, the land greened, and the promise made under the smoke evaporated — exactly as recorded in the report of Ibn Masʿūd, where the Prophet ﷺ recited this very verse after the people's deliverance and his listeners asked whether the punishment would be lifted on the Day of Resurrection; Ibn Masʿūd answered that it would not be. The second reading, favored by al-Māwardī, al-Qurṭubī, and others, connects the verse with the Hereafter: the disbelievers will return to the Fire over and over again — *ʿāʾidūn* as "returning" — as in the verse: *"Whensoever they desire, in their grief, to leave it, they shall be returned unto it, while being told, 'Taste the punishment of the burning!'"* (22:22). On this reading the "little" removal refers to the reliefs of this world — every respite, every recovered health, every averted disaster — and the reversion to the final return from which there is no second removal. The two readings converge: every partial lifting in this life is a sample of mercy, and the one who does not let mercy convert him will learn the meaning of a punishment that is not lifted.

**The Pattern of Relapse in the Divine Dealing**

The verse rests on a general law of God's dealing with guilty communities that the Qur'an states in several places: *"And We shall make them taste the lesser punishment before the greater punishment, that haply they may return"* (32:21); *"And if they turn away, say, 'I warn you of a thunderbolt like the thunderbolt of ʿĀd and Thamūd'"* (41:13). The pattern is graduated: warning, lesser punishment, relief — each stage designed to return the people before the final stage becomes necessary. The tragedy the verse records is that the design so often fails: the relief is received as the cancellation of the warning rather than as its confirmation. The psychology is universal — the child who mistakes the parent's relenting for permission, the patient who mistakes the remission for the cure, the nation that mistakes the averted war for the end of the causes of war. The verse's diagnosis of the Makkans is a mirror for every recipient of reprieve: relief is not acquittal; it is opportunity.

**The Grammar of a Certain Prediction**

The verse's structure carries a quiet claim about divine knowledge that deserves theological attention. God does not say "I fear you will revert" or "perhaps you will revert"; He says *innakum ʿāʾidūn* — "surely you will revert" — a categorical prediction of future free human choices. The verse is thus a small window into the Qur'an's doctrine of divine knowledge: God knows what the people will freely choose — the reversion is genuinely their act, and they are accountable for it — yet it is known and announced in advance with certainty. The classical theologians of all schools wrestled with exactly this coexistence, and their consensus formulations — whether the Ashʿarī emphasis on divine decree or the more rationalist framings of the Muʿtazila — all preserved the two data points the verse itself contains: the people are warned as responsible agents whose reversion is blameworthy, and God's foreknowledge of it is complete. The verse does not resolve the philosophical puzzle; it states the religious reality within which the puzzle is lived: creatures choose, God knows, and the record of both stands.

**Living in the Interval Between Removal and Reversion**

For the reader, the verse's permanent lesson is a discipline of relief. Every human life is a series of lifted punishments — the illness that passed, the financial crisis that eased, the relationship that survived, the war that did not come. The verse asks how the interval is spent: is the lifted punishment the end of the story of return, or its beginning? The Makkans spent their interval in reversion; the believer is invited to spend his in conversion — the ongoing turning that the Qur'an calls *tawbah*, which literally means "returning." The deepest reading of the verse may be personal: God says to every soul that has been spared, "I removed the punishment a little — now show Me what you did with the removal." The one whose relief deepens his faith has broken the pattern of the verse; the one whose relief restores his heedlessness is reliving it. The sūrah leaves no middle ground: the punishment that is lifted is either the door of return or the down payment of the striking that follows in the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:16

> **On the Day when We strike with a terrible striking, surely We are vengeful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day the Sentence Is Executed**

The partial lifting of verse 15 finds its sequel in this verse: *yawma nabṭishu al-baṭshata al-kubrā* — "On the Day when We strike with the terrible striking." The word *baṭsh* denotes the sudden, violent seizure of a culprit — the grabbing of the criminal at the moment of arrest, the pounce that allows no flight. The Qur'an uses it for God's destruction of the guilty nations: *"Thus is the seizing of thy Lord when He seizes the towns that do wrong; truly His seizing is painful, severe"* (11:102). The striking here is *al-kubrā* — the greatest, the terrible one — distinguished from the lesser blows that preceded it: the smoke was the smaller punishment (32:21's "lesser punishment"), and now comes the greater. The verse's clause *innā muntaqimūn* — "surely We are vengeful" — completes the scene with the divine name of retribution: the One who takes vengeance, *al-Muntaqim*, acting in perfect justice against those who exhausted every warning. The verse is the hinge of the sūrah's first movement: everything from verse 9 has been building to this single day, and everything after it will be commentary on what that day means.

**When Was the Great Striking? The Commentators' Three Answers**

The commentators present three readings of the verse's referent, and together they show how the Qur'an's warnings operate on multiple time horizons. The first reading, held by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Māwardī, and al-Ṭabarī among others, identifies the terrible striking with the Battle of Badr — the first armed confrontation between the Muslims and the Quraysh in the second year after the Hijrah, in which the leading men of Makkah were killed and their power broken. The identification rests on the famous report of Ibn Masʿūd, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, in which he said that five signs had passed — and when asked about "the terrible striking," he answered: the day of Badr. On this reading the whole sequence of verses 10–16 maps onto real history: the famine, the smoke, the plea, the removal, the reversion, and then the sword at Badr, which the Qur'an describes elsewhere in identical terms: *"And you did not slay them, but God slew them; and thou didst not throw when thou threw, but God threw"* (8:17).

**The Second and Third Horizons**

The second reading, favored by many of the same authorities in another register, understands the verse as referring to the Hereafter — the Day of Resurrection itself, when the striking will be the final and irrevocable one. The context supports this: the verses that follow the account of Pharaoh and the verses at the sūrah's end concern the resurrection and the division, and verse 40 will name the Day of Division as the tryst of them all. The third reading unites the two: the terrible striking occurred in miniature at Badr as a foretaste and a proof, and will occur in full at the resurrection — the same event in two editions, the first historical, the second eschatological. This layering is characteristic of Qur'anic prophecy, and its pedagogical function is constant: the fulfillment visible in history certifies the fulfillment still to come. The believer who saw Badr happen — and the readers who see, in their own centuries, the pattern of arrogance followed by collapse — receives the same certificate: the One who struck then will strike again, and the second striking will not be partial.

**Vengeance as Justice Completed**

The verse's closing claim — *innā muntaqimūn* — requires careful understanding, because "vengeance" in English carries a flavor of personal spite that the Qur'anic concept excludes. The root *n-q-m* in the Qur'an's usage denotes the infliction of just retribution by the one who holds legitimate authority — the punishment of a criminal by the state, the correction of a rebel by the sovereign. When God describes Himself as taking vengeance, the Qur'an ties it always to wrongdoing: *"So We took vengeance on them and drowned them in the sea, because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them"* (7:136); *"So We took vengeance on them, and behold the end of the deniers!"* (43:25). Divine vengeance is justice with the gloves off: the retribution that a good judge would impose but that human courts can never fully impose, because their knowledge is partial and their power is limited. God's vengeance is perfect because His knowledge of the crime is total and His power to execute the sentence is absolute. The verse thus reassures the oppressed as much as it threatens the oppressor: the wrongs that human justice cannot reach have not escaped; they are merely filed under a different court's docket.

**The Consolation Buried in the Threat**

The sūrah's placement of this verse is significant: it comes immediately after the account of the Makkans' reversion, and it is followed by the story of Pharaoh — the archetype of the tyrant struck down. The verse is thus aimed at two audiences at once, and the believer should hear both registers. To the oppressors it says: your relief is temporary, your reversion is known, your striking is appointed. To the believers it says: the God who heard the cry of the smoke will not forget the tears of the oppressed; the striking is delayed precisely because it is great — great punishments require great preparation, and great patience is the believer's portion while the preparation proceeds. The Qur'an says to the believers: *"Think not that God is heedless of what the wrongdoers do. He only grants them respite till a day when eyes will stare"* (14:42). The modern believer facing tyranny — personal or political — is invited by this verse to exchange the anxiety of impatience for the certainty of appointment: the striking has a day, the day has a name in the divine record, and no reversion, no power, and no delay can cancel it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:17

> **And indeed We tried the people of Pharaoh before them, and a noble messenger came unto them:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The History Lesson Begins: "Before Them"**

With verse 17 the sūrah opens its third movement: *wa-laqad fatannā qablahum qawma Firʿawna* — "And indeed We tried the people of Pharaoh before them." The pronoun *hum* points back to the Quraysh, and the word *qablahum*, "before them," sets the two nations side by side on a timeline: before the Makkans there were the people of Pharaoh, and the same divine method was applied to them — trial, messenger, warning, choice, and consequence. The connecting particle *wa-laqad* ("and indeed") binds the Pharaoh narrative to the verses just completed: the terrible striking threatened against the Quraysh (v. 16) is not an innovation; it is the standard procedure of divine justice, demonstrated on a people whose power dwarfed that of Makkah. The word *fatannā* — "We tried" — is from *f-t-n*, to test by fire, the root of *fitnah*; al-Rāzī comments that the trial of the people of Pharaoh lay in the test posed by the arrival of a messenger: how a people responds to the truth sent to them is the furnace in which their mettle is assayed.

**Why the Qur'an Keeps Retelling Moses**

The attentive reader of the Qur'an notices that the story of Moses and Pharaoh is the most frequently repeated narrative in the entire scripture — told in full in Sūrahs 7, 20, and 26, and in summary in more than a dozen others, including 40:23–46, 43:46–56, 51:38–40, and 79:15–25. The repetitions are not redundancy; each telling selects what its context requires. In al-Dukhān the telling is framed entirely by the situation of the Prophet in Makkah: a messenger of humble social standing confronting the most powerful man of his age; a tyrant whose resources were limitless and whose arrogance was absolute; a deliverance that came not by gradual reform but by sudden divine intervention; and an oppressor whose entire accumulated world was confiscated in a single morning. The parallel with the Prophet and the Quraysh is drawn so tightly that the commentators treat every element of the Pharaoh story in this sūrah as a mirror of the Makkan situation: the Quraysh are Pharaoh's heirs in arrogance, and the believers are the Israelites in patience. The story's function is not antiquarian history but living prophecy: what God did with Pharaoh is the template of what He does with every power that refuses His messenger.

**A Noble Messenger: The Description That Refutes the Slander**

The verse ends by naming the instrument of the trial: *wa-jāʾahum rasūlun karīm* — "and a noble messenger came unto them." The adjective *karīm* — noble, generous, honorable — is pointed. The Qur'an uses it of Gabriel (81:19), of the Prophet ﷺ (69:40), and here of Moses; in every case it signals a messenger of unimpeachable honor. The description answers, in advance, the slanders that messengers always face: the Makkans called the Prophet taught and possessed (v. 14); the Qur'an responds by placing Moses in the same office — a messenger who was noble — and inviting the comparison: if nobility of character is the mark of the true messenger, then the slanderers' own categories convict them, for the man they slandered was the one their own society had surnamed al-Amīn, the Trustworthy, before revelation ever came to him. Al-Bayḍāwī notes that Moses is called noble because of his honor before God and his excellence in conveying the message — the two qualifications of every true prophet, of which the second is proved by the first.

**The Trial of the Powerful: A Mirror for Every Age**

The verse's claim that the people of Pharaoh were *tried* by the arrival of Moses contains a lesson of permanent relevance. Power imagines itself exempt from trial: Pharaoh asked, in the Qur'an's account, *"O my people! Is not the kingdom of Egypt mine?"* (43:51) — as though sovereignty placed him beyond the reach of accountability. The verse's quiet insistence that Pharaoh's people were tried — tested, examined, held to a standard — is the Qur'an's universal refutation of the exemption of power: no throne is outside the furnace, no treasury exempts its owner from the messenger's question. Every human authority — the ruler, the corporation, the celebrity, the head of the family — stands, like Pharaoh's court, in the position of the tried, not the triers. The verse invites the powerful reader to hear Moses' knock before the striking, and it invites the powerless reader to patience: the trial of the powerful is underway in every generation, and the noble messengers of that generation — whether prophets or the believers who carry their truth — are the instruments by which the trial proceeds.

**How the Trial Works: The Method Hidden in the Word**

Finally, the word *fatannā* hides a complete theory of divine testing that the sūrah will now illustrate across sixteen verses. The trial of Pharaoh's people consisted not in some artificial ordeal but in the ordinary machinery of their lives: a messenger came; he spoke; he showed signs; he was believed by some and refused by others; the refusal produced escalating confrontation, and the confrontation produced the plagues, the sea, and the drowning. The trial was not an interruption of their history — it was their history, read correctly. The Qur'an generalizes the point: *"Every soul shall taste death. And We try you with evil and with good, as a test, and unto Us shall you be returned"* (21:35). The good that Pharaoh enjoyed — his kingdom, his Nile, his monuments — was as much part of the test as the plagues that followed; the question in both was the same: would he recognize the Giver and obey His messenger? The modern believer reading this verse is invited to see his own life — its prosperity and its adversity — through the same lens: not as random fortune but as *fitnah*, a testing by the same divine method, conducted by the same Lord, and leading to the same two possible verdicts.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:18

> **"Deliver God's servants unto me! Truly I am a trustworthy messenger unto you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Demand: Liberation**

Moses' opening word to Pharaoh is not about theology but about people: *an addū ilayya ʿibāda Allāh* — "Deliver God's servants unto me." The phrase *ʿibāda Allāh*, "God's servants," is the Qur'an's own title for the Children of Israel at that moment — a designation with a double edge. The Israelites were, in Egyptian law, Pharaoh's slaves; in the Qur'an's framing they were God's servants whom Pharaoh had illegally detained. The demand to "deliver" them is thus an assertion of jurisdiction: Pharaoh's claim over them was usurped, because servitude to God annuls every other absolute claim — the point the Qur'an makes universally: *"Say: 'O People of the Book! Come to a word common between us and you: that we worship none but God, and that we associate nothing as partner with Him, and that none of us takes others as lords apart from God'"* (3:64). Moses' mission begins as a liberation movement, and the Qur'an never lets the reader forget that the prophets' message of monotheism was always also a message of freedom: the worship of the One God unchains the servant of every other master.

**The Trustworthy Messenger: The Claim That Precedes the Signs**

Moses continues: *innī ilaykum rasūlun amīn* — "Truly I am a trustworthy messenger unto you." The word *amīn* — from *-m-n*, the root of faith and security — is the same title the Makkans gave the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ before his call, and the same self-description the Qur'an places on the lips of the prophets Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb, each of whom says to his people: *"I am a trustworthy messenger unto you"* (26:107, 125, 143, 162, 178). The repetition is a deliberate chorus: every prophet's first credential is not a miracle but a character. The messenger asks his people to entrust themselves to him — their souls, their worship, their liberation — and the only ground for such trust is the demonstrated honesty of the messenger's whole life. Al-Ālūsī comments that Moses' declaration that he does not conjecture and has been entrusted by God with revelation: the trustworthy messenger is one who delivers exactly what he was given, neither adding nor subtracting, neither softening the difficult nor exaggerating the easy.

**The Echoes of the Declaration Across the Scripture**

The verse's first clause echoes Moses' words elsewhere in the Qur'an with variations that show the consistency of his mission: *"So send forth with me the Children of Israel"* (7:105), and in Sūrah Ṭā Hā, where Moses and Aaron say together, *"So send forth with us the Children of Israel, and punish them not"* (20:47), the latter adding the plea against continued torment. The demand is made twice in the Qur'an — in Sūrahs 7 and 20 before the signs, here at the outset of the confrontation — because the demand itself was the message: before the staff became a serpent and the hand became white, before the plagues and the sea, the essence of Moses' mission was the liberation of an enslaved people. The commentators note that Moses' words here are the words of a messenger who has come to a tyrant in the tyrant's own court — a lone man, raised in that same palace, now standing before its master to demand the release of the labor force that built it. The courage of the demand is part of its proof: no one invents such an errand.

**The Logic of "God's Servants" for Every Reader**

The phrase *ʿibāda Allāh* carries a permanent theological charge that the Qur'an will spend the rest of the sūrah unpacking. All human beings are, in reality, God's servants whether they acknowledge it or not; Pharaoh's crime was not merely political oppression but theological theft — he had claimed as his own property those whom God had claimed as His servants, and every tyrant since has repeated the theft in some form, treating human beings as raw material for his projects rather than as servants of God with dignity and destiny. The Qur'an's reply, spoken by Moses, is the foundation of human dignity: because every person belongs ultimately to God, no person belongs ultimately to any other person. No state, no employer, no system, no family may make an absolute claim on a human being, because absolute claims belong to the One whose servants we are. This is the verse's liberation theology, and it explains why the Qur'an's message of monotheism was received by the powerful as a threat: if the slaves are God's servants, the slave-owner's title is void.

**Trustworthiness as the Messenger's Only Armor**

Finally, the verse models the stance of the truth-carrier before power: Moses enters Pharaoh's court with no army and no wealth, armed only with a demand and a self-description — "I am a trustworthy messenger." The order matters: he asserts his trustworthiness before performing any sign, because the signs would be meaningless if the sign-bearer were not believed. Al-Qurṭubī observes that trustworthiness is the prophet's first miracle in every age — the life so consistent that when the prophet speaks of God, the audience's knowledge of his character silences the accusation of invention. The lesson for the believer is practical: the truth is carried by trustworthy people, or it is not carried at all. A person who preaches honesty while practicing evasion, who summons others to God while betraying trusts, undermines the message more effectively than any enemy. The believer who would stand, like Moses, before the powerful Pharaohs of his own age — the tyrannies, the corruptions, the institutional evils — must first be able to speak the words Moses spoke: in my life, among those who know me, I am trustworthy. That claim, once true, makes the demand possible; and the demand — deliver God's servants — is the oldest work of the prophets.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:19

> **And rise not against God; surely I have come to you with a manifest authority.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition That Precedes the Proof**

Moses' second sentence is a prohibition: *wa-an lā taʿlū ʿalā Allāh* — "and rise not against God." The verb *taʿlū* is from *ʿ-l-w*, to be high, to rise, to exalt oneself; the construction is that of a warning against arrogance directed at God Himself — the refusal to submit, the haughty assertion of one's own supremacy over the claims of heaven. Ibn Kathīr reads the phrase as meaning: "Do not be too proud to follow Him and accept His proofs" — for the essence of rising against God is not open warfare but the quieter rebellion of the ego that cannot bend. Pharaoh's entire psychology was a rising: his courtiers called him exalted, he announced himself the lord of Egypt, and the Qur'an says of him and his kind generally: *"They exulted in the earth without right and said, 'Who is mightier than we in strength?'"* (41:15). Moses' prohibition strikes at the root of Pharaoh's disease before the cure is offered: the man who will not bend to God is the man who cannot be reached by any proof, because proofs require the humility to look.

**Manifest Authority: The Credential Produced on Demand**

Moses immediately offers the alternative ground: *innī qad jiʾtukum bi-sulṭānin mubīn* — "surely I have come to you with a manifest authority." The word *sulṭān* — from *s-l-ṭ*, to prevail — denotes authority, power of proof, warrant; in the Qur'an's usage it is the overwhelming evidence that compels assent, the credentials of the one who speaks for God. The adjective *mubīn*, manifest, is the same word applied to the Book (v. 2) and the messenger (v. 13): the authority Moses carries is not hidden, not esoteric, not dependent on the audience's goodwill — it is public, visible, examinable. The Qur'an lists the contents of that manifest authority in the parallel accounts: the staff that became a serpent, the hand that shone white, and the signs that followed — *"We gave Moses nine manifest signs"* (17:101), the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood (7:133). The verse thus establishes the correct order of the confrontation: first the claim (I am a messenger), then the warning (do not rise against God), then the proof (I have brought authority) — and the proof's manifestness leaves the refusal without intellectual cover.

**Why "Manifest" Matters: The Closure of Excuses**

The Qur'an's insistence on *mubīn* throughout this sūrah — the clear Book, the clear messenger, the manifest smoke, the manifest authority — is a single coherent policy: God's dealings with humanity are conducted in the open. Nothing in the Qur'anic method is secret, nothing is reserved for an initiated elite, nothing depends on information that was unavailable to the people being judged. The manifestness of the proof performs a specific judicial function: it closes the door of excuse on the Day of Reckoning. When Pharaoh's sorcerers, having witnessed the staff of Moses swallow their ropes and rods, declared *"We believe in the Lord of the worlds, the Lord of Moses and Aaron"* (7:121–122), they testified that the proof had been visible to experts — the very people most qualified to detect illusion. The authority was manifest to the practitioners of magic before it was manifest to the throne; that inversion — the experts converted, the monarch obdurate — is the permanent pattern of the manifest proof, and it demonstrates that the failure of the powerful to believe is never a failure of evidence.

**The Etiquette of the Truth-Carrier Before Tyranny**

The verse also models, with precision, the manners of prophetic address to power. Moses does not flatter Pharaoh — "rise not against God" is blunt — but neither does he insult, mock, or threaten him personally; the rebuke is directed at the sin, the proof is offered, and the choice is left open. The prophets' address to tyrants in the Qur'an is always this combination: complete fearlessness in stating the truth, complete absence of personal rancor, and complete willingness to let the tyrant's own response determine his fate. The modern believer who must speak truth to power — the employee before corrupt management, the citizen before an unjust state, the scholar before a fashionable error — is taught here that the truth-carrier's authority is not his own eloquence or position but the manifestness of what he carries: evidence, argument, and sincerity so public that the refusal of them is exposed as refusal rather than as doubt. When the message is manifest, the messenger can afford to be courteous; the burden of the encounter has shifted to the hearer.

**Arrogance as the Enemy of Evidence**

The verse's deepest psychological insight is its diagnosis of the relationship between arrogance and proof. Moses brought a manifest authority — and Pharaoh did not believe. Why? Not because the authority was insufficient but because *ʿulūw*, self-exaltation, had closed the perceptual channel through which evidence reaches decision. The Qur'an states the law in a single verse: *"Thus does God seal the heart of every tyrant, haughty one"* (40:35) — arrogance does not merely accompany disbelief; it manufactures it, by making the arrogant person's own supremacy the criterion against which all claims are measured. A man who has made himself the standard cannot accept a messenger who summons him to a higher standard. The verse's warning — rise not against God — is therefore also a piece of self-knowledge for every reader: the temptation to exaltation is universal, and its cost is the loss of the capacity to receive truth. The believer is invited to audit his own heart for the Pharaoh within — the region where he refuses to bend, resents correction, and demands that even God's claims accommodate his supremacy — and to practice, against that region, the daily discipline of the prostration: the physical act of placing the highest part of the body at the lowest level, in training for the soul's permanent posture.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:20

> **Truly I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord, lest you stone me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refugee's Declaration**

The confrontation takes a new turn: *wa-innī ʿudhtu bi-rabbī wa-rabbikum an tarjumūn* — "Truly I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord, lest you stone me." The verb *ʿudhtu* — from *ʿ-w-dh*, to seek refuge, to flee for protection — is the language of the sanctuary: in the ancient Arab world, a man pursued could enter the protection of a powerful patron, and the patron's honor then made the pursuer's attack an offense against the protector. Moses declares that his patron is the Lord of Pharaoh himself — the ultimate inversion of tribal refuge: the protection Moses claims is the protection of the very God whose authority Pharaoh is rejecting. The logic is exact and devastating: Pharaoh cannot stone Moses without striking at the One Moses names as Lord of both; the court that would harm the messenger must first answer to the Messenger's Sender, who is also the court's own Lord. Al-Rāzī notes the rhetorical power of *bi-rabbī wa-rabbikum* — "my Lord and your Lord": Moses does not appeal to a private deity known only to himself and his tribe; he appeals to the Lord whom Pharaoh's own tongue acknowledged in the crisis prayers of the Egyptians, the universal Lord.

**What Did Moses Fear? The Two Readings of "Stone"**

The commentators offer two readings of *an tarjumūn*, and each opens a different facet of the scene. The first reading understands stoning literally — execution by the crowd, the standard fate of the unwelcome reformer; the Qur'an records the same threat against other prophets, as when the people of Shuʿayb warned him, *"Were it not for your clan, we would have stoned you"* (11:91). On this reading Moses' refuge-seeking is a plain acknowledgment of mortal danger: he stands in the court of a tyrant who has already decided that the Israelites are his property, and the messenger who demands their release knows the price of the demand. The second reading, recorded by Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, understands stoning as verbal assault — the barrage of abuse and slander by which communities destroy the credibility of the truth-carrier; for the Arabs, *rajm* (stoning) is used for the hurling of words as well as stones, and the Qur'an says of the disbelievers who slandered the Prophet, *"They had well-nigh unsettled thee with their looks when they heard the Reminder, saying, 'Truly he is possessed'"* (68:51). On this reading, Moses seeks refuge from the tongue as much as from the hand.

**The Refuge That Every Prophet Takes**

The verse places Moses in a long line of prophets who declared their refuge in God against the hostility of their peoples. Noah prayed, *"My Lord! I seek refuge in Thee, lest I ask Thee that whereof I have no knowledge"* (11:47); the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was commanded to say, *"Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind, the King of mankind, the God of mankind, from the evil of the slinking whisperer"* (114:1–4); and the whole of the Qur'an's opening prayer of refuge in Sūrah 113 and 114 frames the believer's life between two refuges. The declaration of refuge is not weakness; it is the strategic recognition that the truth-carrier's survival is not his own business but his Sender's. Moses does not say "I am strong enough" or "I will fight you"; he says "I have protection" — and the protection he names is the only one Pharaoh cannot breach. The believer who carries truth in hostile territory is taught the same economy of strength: save your own force; claim the divine protection openly; let the enemy understand that attacking you means attacking through you to the Protector.

**The Psychological Power of Naming the Shared Lord**

There is a subtle psychological strategy in Moses' phrasing that commentators have admired for centuries. By saying "my Lord *and your Lord*," Moses refuses to let Pharaoh cast him as the worshipper of a foreign god. The refuge he takes is in the God Pharaoh himself would invoke in extremity — the Lord of the heavens and the earth that even the idolaters acknowledged (v. 7 of this sūrah). The effect is to strip Pharaoh of the last comforting frame: this is not a conflict between Egypt's gods and a stranger's god; it is a conflict between Pharaoh and Pharaoh's own Lord, mediated by a messenger. The tyrant can no longer dismiss the messenger as an alien; the messenger has located the dispute inside the tyrant's own conscience. Modern readers see the same strategy whenever a reformer addresses an oppressor in the oppressor's own moral vocabulary — appealing to the justice the oppressor claims to serve, the faith the oppressor professes, the law the oppressor administers. The most powerful challenge to a hypocrite is to take his own words seriously in his presence.

**Refuge as the Believer's Permanent Address**

The verse's final lesson is devotional and practical at once. Moses sought refuge *before* the stones flew — the mark of wisdom, for refuge sought after the blow is only treatment, while refuge sought before it is protection. The believer is taught to begin every vulnerable undertaking — every confrontation with a powerful wrong, every public stand, every act of truth-telling in a hostile environment — with the declaration Moses made: I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord. The believer's daily liturgies preserve the same gesture: the *istiʿādhah* that opens every recitation of the Qur'an — *"I seek refuge in God from Satan the outcast"* (16:98) — is the same movement of the soul that Moses performs here, the flight of the unprotected to the Protector before the engagement begins. And the Qur'an promises the effectiveness of the flight: *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of the darkness into the light"* (2:257). The stones may come — the prophets were stoned, literally and verbally, in every age — but the refuge holds what the stones cannot reach: the mission, the message, and the messenger's ultimate safety in the Lord of both worlds.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:21

> **But if you believe me not, then keep away from me."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Proposal of Peaceful Separation**

Moses' address to Pharaoh's court reaches its conclusion in a sentence of extraordinary restraint: *wa-in lam tuʾminū lī fa-ʿtazilūn* — "But if you believe me not, then keep away from me." The verb *iʿtazilū* — from *ʿ-z-l*, to withdraw, to keep apart — is the root of *iʿtizāl*, separation; Moses is proposing a truce of mutual non-interference: believe or do not believe, but do not harm, do not obstruct, do not pursue. The sentence is remarkable because Moses had the power of God behind him and the legitimacy of a liberator's cause; he could have demanded submission or threatened destruction. Instead he offers the minimal terms of coexistence: unbelief is your affair, but the persecution must stop. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī comment that the verse means, "Refrain from harming me, even if you do not believe me," and Ibn Kathīr adds the sense: "I have nothing to do with you, and you have nothing to do with me — let each of us live in peace until God judges between us."

**The Freedom of Belief in the Prophetic Method**

The verse enshrines a principle that the Qur'an defends throughout the Makkan period and that distinguishes the prophetic method from every totalitarian project: belief cannot be compelled, and the appropriate response to unbelief is not war but separation. The Qur'an states the principle in its most categorical form: *"There is no compulsion in religion; truth is clear from error"* (2:256), and commands the Prophet, *"Say: 'O disbelievers! I worship not what you worship... Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion'"* (109:1–6). Moses' proposal is the ancient ancestor of that declaration: he does not ask Pharaoh to believe; he asks Pharaoh to stop. The distinction is crucial for understanding the nature of Islamic mission: the prophets came to *invite* faith and to *end* oppression — the two tasks are different in kind, and the second does not wait upon the first. Even a Pharaoh who would not believe was offered terms: leave God's servants alone, and the dispute is adjourned to the divine tribunal.

**Why the Prophets Offered Terms, Not Ultimatums**

A modern reader may wonder why Moses, armed with manifest authority (v. 19), would offer so little — why not demand Pharaoh's immediate surrender? The answer lies in the Qur'an's doctrine of the divine timing of justice. The prophets were sent as warners and bearers of glad tidings, not as executioners: *"We sent thee not, save as a mercy unto the worlds"* (21:107). Punishment belongs to God's own calendar, and the Qur'an repeatedly instructs the believers to leave retribution to the appointed day: *"So be patient; truly God's promise is true. And let not those who have no certainty make thee light of heart"* (30:60). Moses' offer of separation thus performs several functions at once: it gives Pharaoh's court every opportunity to relent with dignity; it isolates the aggressor morally by demonstrating that the messenger sought only peace; and it transfers the responsibility for what follows — the plagues, the pursuit, the drowning — entirely onto the party that refused even the minimal terms. The history of the confrontation will show the pattern: the proposal of peace was made, the proposal was rejected, and the rejection, not the proposal, produced the catastrophe.

**The Harder Separation: Leaving What You Cannot Convert**

For the individual believer, the verse's demand has an inner application that the classical commentators develop in their ethical works. There are things in every life — habits, environments, relationships, ambitions — that will not be converted to faith no matter how long they are argued with, and the prophetic method counsels a clean separation where conversion fails: *fa-ʿtazilū*. The addict who keeps the substance in the house while praying for abstinence, the believer who keeps the corrupt partnership while praying for its reformation, the soul that keeps the door open to its own Pharaoh while praying for deliverance — each is living in a state the verse gently exposes: belief and non-belief have been offered coexistence, and coexistence is what the prophets refused to grant. The verse's wisdom is that some battles are won only by withdrawing from them: the fire is not argued with; one leaves the room. The believer's version of Moses' proposal is the decisive break — the *hijrah* of the heart and sometimes of the address — after which the dispute is left to God, and the believer's energy is freed for what belief does require.

**The Court That Refused the Truce**

The sequel, recorded in the coming verses, gives the verse its permanent resonance. Pharaoh refused both the faith and the separation; he chose instead to pursue — and the pursuit ended in the sea. The Qur'anic account thus leaves every tyrant and every believer a single question: when the messenger of truth offers you the two doors — belief, or at least peace — which will you take? Pharaoh took neither, and his name became the Qur'an's archetype of the third option that is not really an option: war against the truth. The believer who recites this verse is reminded of the mercy embedded in the prophets' minimal terms — God does not require the impossible; He requires only that His servants be free — and of the corresponding duty: to offer, like Moses, the door of peace even to those who will not enter the door of faith, and to reserve the battle for those who, like Pharaoh, close both doors and come with armies.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:22

> **Then he called unto his Lord, "These are a guilty people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The End of Argument, the Beginning of Prayer**

The confrontation has reached its turning point: *fa-daʿā rabbahu* — "Then he called unto his Lord." After the declaration of trustworthiness, the warning against arrogance, the offer of the manifest authority, and the proposal of peaceful separation, Moses has done everything a messenger can do with his audience; the only door remaining is the one that opens upward. The verse marks the precise moment when prophetic effort exhausts its horizontal dimension and turns vertical: *rabba inna hāʾulāʾi qawmun mujrimūn* — "My Lord, truly these are a guilty people." The sentence is simultaneously a report, a diagnosis, and a plea. It is a report to the Sender about the audience's final state; it is a diagnosis — *mujrimūn*, from *j-r-m*, to cut off, hence to commit crime — that the people have become criminals in the settled sense of the word, their guilt no longer a matter of isolated acts but of established character; and it is an implicit plea that the justice of heaven now proceed where the patience of earth has been exhausted.

**The Echo of the Prophet's Own Lament**

The commentators are unanimous in hearing in this verse the echo of the final verse of the previous sūrah: *"And he said, 'O my Lord, truly these are a people who believe not'"* (43:88) — the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ own sigh over the Quraysh. The parallel is the sūrah's structural secret: the same complaint, two prophets, two eras, one divine policy. Moses said it of Pharaoh's people, and within verses the sea closed over them; the Prophet ﷺ said it of the Quraysh, and the sūrah that follows his sigh opens with the Book, the smoke, and the terrible striking. The Qur'an is teaching its first audience — and every audience since — to read the two laments together: the messenger who has exhausted his means does not abandon the mission; he escalates it to its highest court. When the truth-carrier can no longer move the people, he moves the Lord of the people; and the Lord's response to the prophets' prayers of despair is the pivot on which the history of salvation turns.

**The Guilt That Qualifies for Divine Action**

The word *mujrimūn* requires unpacking, because the Qur'an uses it as a technical designation for those whose wrongdoing has reached the point of no return — the people described elsewhere as *"the guilty"* who will be recognized by their marks (55:41) and questioned: *"What brought you into Saqar?"* (74:42). Guilt in this Qur'anic sense is not the theological state inherited from Adam's fall — Islam has no such doctrine — but the accumulated product of free choices: each refusal of a messenger, each persecution of a believer, each hardening of the heart adds a layer until the person or the people is *mujrim*, a criminal by formation. Moses' complaint is thus not an outburst of frustration but a considered verdict reached across the whole history of his mission: the signs had come, the warnings had been delivered, the offers had been refused, the persecution had continued, and the case now stood ready for judgment. The believer learns from the precision of the verdict that divine action against evil is never hasty: it waits for the record to be complete, then proceeds on the record.

**When the Prophet's Prayer Is the Only Weapon Left**

There is a profound lesson for the powerless in this verse's sequence. Moses had no army in Egypt; his people were slaves; his enemy commanded the resources of a superpower. Every avenue of worldly redress — negotiation, appeal, protest — had been tried and had failed. What remained was what the Qur'an here records as the decisive act: *fa-daʿā rabbahu* — he called upon his Lord. The verse is the charter of the believers' strategy in every age of oppression: when the doors of human recourse close, the door of prayer stands open, and the prayer of the truth-carrier against the oppressor is not a cry of impotence but an act of power — the power of invoking the only court whose verdict cannot be appealed. The Qur'an elsewhere records the same turn in the prayers of the oppressed: the believer whose rights are devoured is told that his prayer is heard even if delayed, for the Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Beware the supplication of the wronged, for there is no veil between it and God." (recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The believer who has done everything humanly possible and still faces the Pharaoh of his age is invited to do what Moses did: name the situation to God in one honest sentence, and then wait — for the sentence was heard, and the hearing has consequences.

**The Turning of the Sūrah**

Structurally, this verse is the hinge on which the entire Pharaoh narrative turns. Everything before it — verses 17–21 — has been Moses' address to Pharaoh; everything after it — verses 23–33 — is God's response: the command to depart by night, the sea at rest, the drowned host, the empty gardens, the delivered Israelites. The pattern is the sūrah's deepest teaching about the relationship between human effort and divine action: the messenger's work is to deliver the message with complete fidelity and complete patience; when that work is done and rejected, the divine work begins — and the divine work is always more comprehensive than anything the messenger could have accomplished alone. Moses could not free a single Israelite by argument; God freed them all by a sea. The believer who has done his duty and seen it refused should therefore recognize the moment Moses recognized: the moment of the call — the moment when the affair passes from the hands of the messenger to the hands of the Lord of the messengers, with results that belong to the divine scale rather than the human one.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:23

> **"Set forth with My servants by night; you will indeed be pursued.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Arrives: A Command in the Night**

Moses' call receives an immediate answer, and the answer is not a speech but an order: *fa-asri bi-ʿibādī laylan* — "Set forth with My servants by night." The verb *asri* — from *s-r-y*, to travel by night — is a command to begin the exodus under cover of darkness. The divine response is striking for what it does not say: God does not promise Moses that Pharaoh will relent, nor that the road will be easy; the command itself announces the pursuit — *innakum muttabaʿūn*, "you will indeed be pursued." Deliverance is decreed, and the decree includes the terror of the chase; the night journey is the answer to the prayer, and its first clause is a warning of the enemy at the heels. The verse teaches a lesson that the believers of every age must learn: divine deliverance rarely arrives as the removal of all danger; it arrives as the strength to move through the danger — the sea will part, but only after the army has been seen on the horizon.

**"My Servants": The Title That Changes Everything**

Note the pronoun: *bi-ʿibādī* — "with *My* servants." God does not say "the Children of Israel" or "your people"; He claims them: they are Mine. The divine claim is the theological center of the exodus: the journey that begins tonight is not a flight of refugees from a political oppression only; it is the repatriation of God's servants from an illegitimate captivity — the recovery of divine property from a usurper. The same claim was the burden of Moses' original demand: *"Deliver God's servants unto me"* (v. 18). Now the Owner Himself speaks, and the command to set forth is the exercise of His ownership: no power on earth may detain those whom God summons. Al-Qurṭubī observes that the phrase dignifies the Israelites at the very moment of their greatest vulnerability — a nation of slaves on the run — with the highest title the Qur'an can confer: servants of God, the same title borne by the prophets in their prayers — *"My servants who believe! Be reverent toward your Lord"* (39:10) — and by the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ when he is described as *"the servant of God"* (72:19).

**Why at Night? The Method of the Divine Escape**

The specification of night is laden with meaning. Night was the time when the Egyptians' surveillance relaxed, when the slave-quarters could be emptied without alarm, and when the pursuit — however determined — would begin hours behind the fugitives. The Qur'an's God, who commands the sun and moon, is not above using the cover of darkness; providence works through the texture of ordinary circumstance. But there is a symbolic register as well: the exodus begins in darkness and ends in light — the sea parts at dawn, the pillar of fire lights the night, and the destination is a land flowing with milk and honey. The night journey of the Israelites is the archetype of every believer's passage: faith is often a movement begun in obscurity, when the outcome cannot be seen, taken on the strength of a command rather than a map. Moses is told to go by night — that is, to go when he cannot see the road — and the command tests the very trust it demands: will the messenger move on the word of the Sender alone?

**The Pursuit That Is Also a Decree**

The verse's second clause — *innakum muttabaʿūn*, "you will indeed be pursued" — is grammatically a promise as much as a threat, and its placement inside the divine command reveals the divine method. God could have removed the pursuit from the equation entirely — could have blinded the Egyptians, delayed their intelligence, or transported the Israelites beyond their reach. Instead He allowed the pursuit, because the pursuit was part of the deliverance: it would drive the Israelites to the very edge of the sea, to the moment of maximum despair, so that the parting of the water would be recognized for what it was — not a lucky tide but the intervention of the Lord. The Qur'an records the moment of maximum despair in the parallel account: *"And when the two hosts saw one another, the companions of Moses said, 'We are overtaken!' He said, 'Nay! Truly my Lord is with me; He will guide me'"* (26:61–62). The pursuit that seemed to seal their doom was the setup for the miracle: the sea does not part for those who can still escape by land; it parts for those whose only road is heaven.

**Deliverance as a Journey, Not an Event**

For the modern believer, the verse reframes the experience of divine help. The answer to Moses' prayer was not a single miraculous moment but a whole journey — the night march, the pursuit, the sea, the wilderness, the forty years — and the command to set forth preceded the sight of any miracle. Deliverance in the Qur'anic pattern is rarely the instantaneous cancellation of the problem; it is the divine escort through the problem: the strength for the night march, the presence in the pursuit, the path in the sea. The believer facing his own Egypt — addiction, oppression, grief, impossibility — is invited to hear the same command that Moses heard: set forth. Move. Begin the journey on the word of God before the road is visible. The pursuit will come — the verse promises it — but the pursuit is not the cancellation of the promise; it is the context in which the promise will be seen to hold. The One who commanded the night march is the One who will deal with the sea; the servant's part is the first step, taken in the dark, because the command was clear.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:24

> **And leave the sea at rest; truly they will be a drowned host.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Difficult Command in the Sūrah**

The divine instructions continue with a command whose strangeness has occupied the commentators for centuries: *wa-utruk al-baḥra rahwā* — "And leave the sea at rest." Moses, having struck the water with his staff and seen it part into two walls with a dry path between, is commanded to leave the sea as it is — *rahwā*, calm, open, motionless, from a root whose sense is stillness and expanse. The difficulty of the command lies in what it forbids: every instinct of the fugitive would urge Moses to command the sea to close behind him the moment the last Israelite crossed, to drown the pursuit while the drowning was easy. Al-Zamakhsharī suggests that Moses was inclined to do exactly that — to strike the water again and let it collapse on the Egyptians — and the command *leave it* restrains him: do not close the road; leave the gate open; let the host enter; and when they are fully within the channel, then — without any action of yours — the sea will return by its Lord's command. The verse's implication, drawn out by Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī, is that the timing of the sea's return belonged to God alone: Moses was to hold the miracle open until the divine moment, and the divine moment was the moment of the Egyptians' complete entry.

**The Drowned Host: A Promise Built on Perfect Knowledge**

The verse's second clause explains the command's purpose: *innahum jundun mughraqūn* — "truly they will be a drowned host." The word *jund*, host, marks the scale: an army, the full military power of Egypt, chariots and horses and ranks — the entire instrument of Pharaoh's oppression reduced to the single status the verse announces: drowned. The form *mughraqūn* is a participle of a passive promise — "a host that will be drowned" — and its certainty is the ground of the command to leave the sea open: Moses need not manage the miracle because the outcome is already decided. The verse is thus a lesson in the division of labor between the servant and the Lord: the servant's task is obedience to the present command — walk, strike, leave the sea; the Lord's task is the future — the entry of the host, the return of the water, the drowning. The Qur'an's account of the exodus repeatedly insists on this division: at the moment of crisis Moses says only, *"Nay! Truly my Lord is with me; He will guide me"* (26:62) — no strategy, no counterattack, no closing of the sea by his own initiative; the guiding is the Lord's.

**The Sea at Rest and the Theology of Forbearance**

There is a deeper theological register in the command to leave the sea *rahwā* that the classical commentators connect with the wider Qur'anic teaching on the divine timing of justice. The sea was left open — the Egyptians were allowed to enter the path of their own destruction — because God does not punish while there remains a possibility of return: the crossing of the sea was, for Pharaoh's army, the last opportunity to see the sign and turn back, and the record shows that the pursuit continued to its own end. The command *leave the sea at rest* is thus an image of the divine forbearance that runs through the whole Qur'an: *"And if God were to take mankind to task for their wrongdoing, He would not leave upon it a single creature; but He grants them respite till a term appointed"* (16:61). The sea stays open — the door stays open — until the appointed term; when the term arrives, the sea returns without needing to be commanded, because the returning was included in the original parting.

**The Parallel Account That Completes the Picture**

The fullest version of the scene is given in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ, where the sequence is narrated with a detail that illuminates this verse: *"Then We revealed unto Moses, 'Strike the sea with thy staff,' and it parted, and each part was like a mighty cliff. And there We brought the others near. And We saved Moses and those with him, all together. Then We drowned the others. Truly in that is a sign, but most of them were not believers"* (26:63–67). The pattern of the parallel — strike, part, save, drown — assigns every stage to the divine initiative, with Moses' staff as the mere instrument. In al-Dukhān's compressed version, the same pattern is present in embryo: the command to depart by night (v. 23), the sea left at rest (v. 24), the drowned host (v. 24) — and the reader who knows the fuller accounts supplies the parted walls, the dry path, the pillar of cloud, and the terror of the crossing. The Qur'an's repetition of the scene across sūrahs — 2:50, 7:136, 8:54, 10:90, 17:103, 20:77–78, 26:63–66, 28:40, 43:55, 51:40, and here — is the measure of its importance: the drowning of Pharaoh is the Qur'an's central demonstration that no power on earth can stand against the deliverance of God's servants.

**What the Believer Is Commanded to Leave Open**

The verse's permanent lesson is one of the most difficult in the spiritual life: the believer is commanded to leave the sea at rest — to resist the temptation to manage the timing of justice. When the believer has been wronged and has seen the beginning of vindication, the instinct is to press the advantage, to close the sea on the enemy, to complete the destruction personally. The command *leave the sea* counsels otherwise: the road that God opened, God will close in His own time; the enemy that God is dealing with does not require the believer's assistance in his own ruin. Moses was told to leave the sea open — and the sea drowned the host without Moses lifting a hand. The believer's part is the exodus — the departure from oppression, the journey of obedience — and the enemy's part is God's concern: *"So be patient — truly the promise of God is true — and let not those who have no certainty make thee light of heart"* (30:60). The sea that is left at rest today is the sea that will be seen, tomorrow or on the Day of Division, to have been a grave prepared by justice and opened by forbearance.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:25

> **How many gardens and springs did they leave behind,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory of Loss**

The narrative pauses at the moment of drowning to take an inventory of what was lost: *kam tarakū min jannātin wa-ʿuyūn* — "How many gardens and springs did they leave behind." The particle *kam* is the Arabic interrogative of quantity, and in this rhetorical use it means "how many!" — an exclamation of abundance: gardens beyond counting, springs beyond number. The verse is the Qur'an's most economical memento mori: the reader is shown Egypt at the moment of its disappearance — the green fields along the Nile, the irrigation channels, the orchards and vineyards that generations of slave labor had watered — and then is told, in the same breath, that all of it was *left behind*. The verb *tarakū*, "they left," is chilling in its passivity: they did not sell, transfer, or bequeath; they abandoned — a whole civilization's accumulated wealth rendered ownerless in a single morning. The gardens did not vanish; they remained, watered and fruitful, while their owners' bodies settled in the sea. That discontinuity — wealth surviving its owners — is the verse's subject, and it is the oldest subject of human reflection: *"Every soul shall taste death"* and everything else is a lease.

**The Rhetoric of Counting What Cannot Be Counted**

The Qur'an uses the same *kam* construction elsewhere with the same elegiac force — *"How many gardens and springs did they leave behind, and sown fields and noble dwellings, and prosperity in which they rejoiced!"* (44:25–27, this very passage, echoed at 26:57–58), and of the generations before: *"How many a town did We destroy that exulted in its life of ease! There are their dwellings, uninhabited after them, save a little"* (28:58). The rhetorical question is aimed at the living: the Makkan audience of the sūrah were merchants and landowners whose entire identity was bound up in property — the gardens of Ṭāʾif, the orchards of the Hijaz, the caravans of the trade routes. The verse invites each of them to perform the arithmetic of mortality: count your gardens; now count the gardens of Pharaoh; now observe that the second count ended in a morning — and ask what protects the first. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the lesson explicit: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world; but the enduring good deeds are better with thy Lord in reward and better in hope"* (18:46).

**The Gardens of Egypt: History and Symbol**

The historical setting gives the verse its local color. Egypt was, in the ancient world, the granary of the Mediterranean — its fertility legendary, its wealth proverbial; the Qur'an itself puts the boast in Pharaoh's mouth: *"Is not the kingdom of Egypt mine, and these rivers flowing beneath me?"* (43:51). The gardens and springs of the verse are not decorative detail but the precise content of Egyptian power: the irrigation agriculture that fed the empire, the vineyards that supplied its tables, the shaded estates of its nobility. When the Qur'an lists what the drowned host left behind, it is listing the actual economy of the oppression — the wealth built on Israelite labor, the same wealth that had funded the pursuit of the fugitives. The verse's implicit judgment is that the wealth was never truly theirs: it had been accumulated by injustice, and its disappearance in a morning demonstrated what the Qur'an states as a general law: *"And how many a city that exulted in its life We destroyed! So these are their dwellings, uninhabited after them, save a little — and We were the inheritors"* (28:58, and cf. 19:74; 22:45; 44:37). The gardens remained — the verse's point is that their *ownership* did not; what was built on oppression was repossessed by the Lord of the oppressed.

**The Mirror Held Up to the Makkans — and to Every Age of Accumulation**

The sūrah's placement of this inventory is deliberate: verses 25–28 stand in the middle of the Pharaoh narrative, but their audience is the Quraysh — and by extension every generation of the wealthy who hear the Qur'an. The classical commentators read the passage as an admonition that the Makkans' wealth and property could be taken from them at any moment for their opposition to the Prophet ﷺ, in the same way that the wealth of nations before them had been confiscated in a single stroke: *"Have they not seen how many a generation We destroyed before them, whom We had established upon the earth more firmly than We established you, and upon whom We rained abundant rains, and made rivers flow beneath them? Then We destroyed them for their sins"* (6:6). The verse's modern application writes itself: every economy that considers itself permanent — every empire of commerce, every real estate dynasty, every national prosperity built on injustice — is invited to read the inventory of Egypt and to ask the question the verse does not need to ask aloud: if Pharaoh's gardens could not protect Pharaoh, what protects yours?

**The Discipline of Leaving Well**

There is, finally, a personal discipline hidden in the verse for the believer who is not a tyrant at all — the ordinary owner of ordinary things. The verse's picture of the drowned host leaving everything behind is a memento mori of ownership: every human being will leave his gardens, in the sense that matters most, at a moment he does not choose. The Prophet ﷺ described the reality with a parable recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> "Two wolves let loose among sheep do not cause more harm than a man's greed for wealth and status cause to his religion." (al-Tirmidhī 2376, from Kaʿb ibn Mālik; cf. the similar wording transmitted by Aḥmad)

The believer's discipline, therefore, is to hold property the way the Qur'an commands it to be held — as a trust with an exit plan: the wealth that is spent in charity is the wealth that is sent ahead, the only portion of the garden that survives the crossing. The drowned host left their gardens behind because they had never converted any of them into the currency of the next world; the believer is invited to do the conversion while the sea is still at rest — to send on ahead, by giving, what death would otherwise confiscate: *"And whatever good you send forth for your souls, you will find it with God, better and greater in reward"* (73:20).

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:26

> **sown fields and a noble dwelling,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Catalogue Continues: Fields and Palaces**

The inventory of Egypt's abandoned wealth grows: *wa-zurūʿin wa-makāmin karīm* — "sown fields and a noble dwelling." The word *zurūʿ* (plural of *zarʿ*) denotes cultivated land — the sown fields of grain, the standing crops that were the staff of Egyptian life; *makām karīm* is a "noble station," the elevated, honorable place — the palaces and estates of the nobility, the seats of the powerful. The catalogue is arranged as a complete economic portrait: gardens and springs for pleasure (v. 25), fields for sustenance, and palaces for status (v. 26), then the general enjoyment of luxury (v. 27) — the full inventory of what a civilization considers its reasons for existence. The Qur'an's accounting is precise because the lesson depends on completeness: nothing that the Egyptians valued was missing from the list, and nothing on the list survived them. Every category of worldly ambition — beauty, food, power, comfort, status — was present in Egypt, and every category was left behind.

**The "Noble Dwelling" and Its Irony**

The adjective *karīm* — noble, generous, honored — carries a deliberate irony that the commentators do not miss. Egypt's palaces were called noble because they were the seats of honor and influence; the Qur'an applies the same word in this sūrah to the messenger sent to Pharaoh (v. 17: *rasūlun karīm*). The juxtaposition is the sūrah's quiet satire: the Egyptians invested nobility in stone and position — palaces on the Nile, titles in the court — and the nobility evaporated with the morning tide; the true nobility was the prophet standing in the court in his worn cloak, whose honor was in his message and whose palace was the divine presence. The two uses of *karīm* in the space of ten verses define two competing economies of honor: honor by location, which is confiscated with location, and honor by relation to God, which survives the confiscation of everything else. The Qur'an states the principle: *"Truly the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you"* (49:13) — nobility measured by nothing that can be left behind.

**The Fields That Feed vs. The Field That Feeds Forever**

There is a deeper current in the verse's pairing of *zurūʿ* with the previous verse's gardens. The ancient Egyptian economy — like every agrarian economy — was built on the annual miracle of the harvest: seed cast into the Nile mud, watered by the flood, reaped in its season. The Qur'an returns to the harvest throughout as the standing image of resurrection: *"Know that God gives life to the earth after its death. We have indeed made the signs clear unto you, that haply you may understand"* (57:17); *"And the earth — We spread it out and cast therein firm mountains, and caused to grow therein every thing weighed — ... as a provision for servants. And We revive thereby a dead land. Thus shall be the coming forth"* (50:7–11). The Egyptians, who watched the dead land revive every year with the flood, refused the revival of the dead that the same pattern announced; their sown fields taught the resurrection to their faces, and they denied it with their mouths. The verse's placement in a sūrah whose later movement defends the resurrection (vv. 34–39) is therefore not accidental: the fields left behind by the drowned host are evidence in the case — the One who raises the crops from the dead earth is the One who will raise the dead from their graves, and the fields themselves testify.

**The Dwelling That Was Never Home**

The verse's *makām karīm* also invites a reflection on the psychology of place and permanence. The Egyptians built for eternity — the pyramids, the tombs, the monuments that still stand — yet the Qur'an records that they were left behind in a moment, and their bodies' journey ended in the sea rather than in the tombs prepared for them. The deepest human anxiety is the one the verse exposes: the attempt to secure permanence through construction, to defeat death by leaving stone behind. Every age repeats the attempt — dynasties, monuments, endowments, reputations — and the Qur'an's inventory of Egypt is its verdict on the attempt: the builders did not dwell in their makām; they drowned in the sea, and the noble dwelling passed to strangers. The believer's answer to the anxiety of permanence is not construction but connection: the deed that outlasts the estate, the good deed that the Qur'an calls *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt*, "the enduring good deeds" (18:46; 19:76) — the only property whose title survives the transfer of every other property.

**Reading the Inventory as a Question**

The verse's function in the sūrah, and in the life of the reader, is interrogative: the inventory of Egypt is an invitation to inventory the self. What are my sown fields — the projects that consume my seasons? What is my noble dwelling — the position, the house, the reputation I have built and polished? The verse does not condemn fields or dwellings; Egypt's fields and palaces were not destroyed because they existed but because their owners had made them the whole of existence, refusing the messenger who reminded them of the Owner of both fields and palaces. The believer is invited to hold the inventory lightly: to work the fields as the Prophet ﷺ worked — with complete diligence and complete detachment — and to inhabit the dwelling as a guest who knows the date of departure. The drowned host left their gardens in a morning; the believer's task is to leave his in installments — giving, serving, loving — so that when the final leaving comes, the greater part has already been transferred to the account that no sea can reach.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:27

> **and prosperity in which they rejoiced?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Item on the List: Enjoyment Itself**

The inventory closes with its most subtle item: *wa-naʿmatin kānū fīhā fākihīn* — "and prosperity in which they rejoiced." The word *naʿmah* denotes the state of ease and delight — the pleasure that wealth purchases, the good life in its felt dimension; the commentators note that it can also be read *niʿmah*, meaning "blessing," in which case the verse counts among the abandoned things the very favor of God that had made Egypt's prosperity possible. The participle *fākihīn* — from *f-k-h*, to delight, to be glad — describes people actively enjoying themselves: not merely possessing wealth but tasting it, savoring it, living inside the enjoyment. The verse thus completes the catalogue with the one item that makes the catalogue painful: the gardens, fields, and palaces were not dead assets — they were the theater of daily pleasure. The Egyptians were not misers guarding gold; they were connoisseurs of life, drinking the wine of their own prosperity — and the verse's point is that enjoyment is the most fragile item on the list: pleasure is a derivative of life, and when the owners drowned, the enjoyment ended with them.

**The Suddenness: Pleasure's Built-In Expiration**

The sequence of the verses — gardens, fields, dwellings, delight — followed by verse 28's *kadhālika* ("thus it was") and the bequest to another people, is engineered to produce one effect: the total, instantaneous, irrevocable transfer of the good life. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same suddenness as the signature of divine confiscation: *"And how many a city that exulted in its life We destroyed! So these are their dwellings, uninhabited after them, save a little"* (28:58); *"Until, when the earth is adorned in its finery and its people suppose that they have power over it, Our command comes upon it by night or by day, and We make it a harvest mown down, as if it had not flourished the day before"* (10:24). The world's pleasure is described by the Qur'an as a crop — brilliant in its season, mown in an hour — and the verse's *fākihīn* (rejoicing) is set against the mowing: the rejoicing and the reaping are adjacent in the text, as they are adjacent in time. The reader is being taught the Qur'an's central economic law: pleasure has no tenure; only the enduring good deeds do.

**The Psychology of the Rejoicing: Why Enjoyment Blinds**

The verse's choice of *fākihīn* — those immersed in enjoyment — is also a precise psychological observation. The Qur'an repeatedly diagnoses the blindness of the prosperous to the warnings of the prophets as a function of their prosperity: *"And they said, 'We are greater in wealth and children, and we shall not be punished'"* (34:35); *"Nay, but man is insolent, for he sees himself self-sufficient"* (96:6–7). Enjoyment is not merely a reward that accompanies wealth; it is an anesthetic that deadens the perception of risk — the comfortable find it almost impossible to believe in the end of comfort, precisely because the present sensation of well-being is so complete. The Egyptians rejoiced in their prosperity *while Moses stood among them* warning of the sea; the rejoicing and the warning occupied the same decades, and the rejoicing won. The verse thus names the oldest failure of human foresight: the feeling of security produced by present pleasure overrides the knowledge of future account, and the Qur'an's cure is the regular practice of interrupting the enjoyment with remembrance — the daily prayers, the fasting, the charity, the recitation of verses like this one, all of which are devices for keeping the end of the rejoicing inside the frame of the rejoicing.

**The Two Readings of Naʿmah/Niʿmah and Their Lesson**

The classical readers' distinction between *naʿmah* (pleasure, luxury) and *niʿmah* (blessing, favor) — noted by al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī — opens a final door of meaning. If the word is read as pleasure, the verse mourns the loss of what the Egyptians enjoyed; if it is read as blessing, the verse mourns something far greater — the loss of the divine favor whose withdrawal is the true catastrophe. The two readings together articulate the Qur'an's deepest teaching about prosperity: every pleasure is a *niʿmah*, a blessing from God, and the blessing is only secured by gratitude; when gratitude is replaced by arrogance, the blessing is converted into its own punishment — the wealth remains, but the favor departs, and wealth without favor is a house without heat. The verse thus warns the prosperous believer in the same breath as the prosperous tyrant: enjoy the blessing — God does not forbid enjoyment, *"Say: 'Who has forbidden the adornment of God which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things of His provision?'"* (7:32) — but enjoy it as a blessing, with the gratitude that keeps the Giver in the gift; for the blessing that is enjoyed without the Giver is already in the process of being left behind.

**The Inventory as the Reader's Own**

Every reader of this verse in prosperity is invited to perform its arithmetic personally. The gardens, springs, fields, dwellings, and delights of Egypt have their equivalents in every reader's life — the home, the health, the income, the family, the pleasures — and the verse's catalogue is structured so that each reader can supply his own items and his own verb: *how many [blessings] did they leave behind, and in how many did they rejoice?* The question is not morbid; it is liberating, because it relocates the reader's center of gravity. The one who knows that the whole inventory is temporary is the one who can enjoy it without being owned by it, invest it without being trapped by it, and leave it without being broken by it — because he has already transferred his heart's property to the account of the enduring: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world; but the enduring good deeds are better with thy Lord in reward and better in hope"* (18:46).

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:28

> **Thus it was. And We bequeathed it unto another people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict in Two Words**

The inventory of verses 25–27 is sealed with the sūrah's most economical verdict: *kadhālika* — "thus it was." The single word is the closing of the file: the gardens, the springs, the fields, the palaces, the pleasures — *thus it was* with all of them; this is how the story of Egypt's glory ended. The commentators note that *kadhālika* functions here as an expression of the divine decree's completion — the matter was concluded exactly as the warning had said it would be — and al-Qurṭubī reads in it a note of wonder: such was the fate of the rejoicing, so unlike what the rejoicers had supposed. Then comes the transfer: *wa-awrathnāhā qawman ākharīn* — "And We bequeathed it unto another people." The verb *awrathnā* is from *w-r-th*, inheritance — the root of *irth* and *mīrāth* — and its subject is God: *We* made the inheritance. The wealth of Egypt did not dissolve into the sea; it changed hands — the vineyards were not uprooted, the fields were not salted; they were assigned by the divine will to a new owner, the "another people" who, in the most natural reading of the context and of the parallel verse at 26:59 — *"So it was, and We made the Children of Israel to inherit them"* — are the very people the Egyptians had enslaved.

**Inheritance as Divine Justice**

The verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement that the redistribution of wealth in history is a divine act with a moral content. The Egyptians had acquired their prosperity by oppression — the labor of the enslaved Israelites had built Egypt's store-cities and monuments, as the exodus narratives recall (see 28:4–6, where Pharaoh exalts himself in the land and oppresses the Children of Israel) — and the divine arithmetic of justice required that the wealth return to those who had produced it. *"Thus We caused the Children of Israel to inherit the land of the east and the west"* (7:137, in the fullest statement of the principle: *"And We made the people who had been oppressed inherit the easts of the earth and its wests, which We had blessed"* (7:137)). The bequest is not charity; it is restitution — the correction, at the level of nations, of the imbalance that oppression creates. The Qur'an generalizes the principle in a verse that the commentators cite in connection with this passage: *"And We desired to show favor unto those who were oppressed in the earth, and to make them imams, and to make them the heirs"* (28:5).

**The Warning Buried in the Bequest**

But the verse's second clause carries a warning as well as a promise, and the warning is directed at the heirs. The wealth that passed to the "another people" was not a certificate of their virtue; the same divine machinery that transfers wealth from oppressors can transfer it again — and history, as the Qur'an narrates it, shows the inheritance passing through many hands: the Israelites received Egypt's wealth and in time their own prosperity became their trial (the cow of Sūrah al-Baqarah is slaughtered among a people grown wealthy and evasive); the generations of ʿĀd and Thamūd received power and were destroyed for their arrogance; the Quraysh of Makkah, the immediate audience of this verse, held the wealth of the Hijaz and were warned that the same *kadhālika* could be written over their storehouses. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both note that the verse is aimed at the Makkans' sense of security: the property of Pharaoh — greater than yours — was bequeathed in a morning; what protects your property from the same decree? The inheritance of the earth is not a prize for permanence but a test of stewardship: *"He it is who made you vicegerents upon the earth"* (6:165) — and vicegerency is renewable or revocable, by the same hand that granted it.

**The Deeper Inheritance: What the Heirs Receive**

The verse's imagery of inheritance also carries a spiritual register that the commentators develop in their reflections on the exodus. The "another people" received not only Egypt's abandoned property but a standing invitation to something greater: the land of promise, the covenant, the revelation — the inheritance that no sea could confiscate. The Qur'an calls the reverent the true heirs of the highest inheritance: *"And those who are granted wisdom have been granted abundant good"* (2:269), and describes the Garden as the inheritance of the believers: *"That is the Garden which We cause the reverent among Our servants to inherit"* (19:63). The exodus thus prefigures the final transfer of the sūrah's closing movement: just as Egypt's gardens passed from the drowned to the delivered, so the gardens of the Hereafter — the *jannāt* of verse 52 — will pass from the world's temporary holders to the everlasting inheritors. The believer who reads verse 28 in the light of verse 52 sees the whole of history as a series of divine inheritances moving toward a single culmination: the world's wealth passes from hand to hand and is finally left behind entirely, and the only property that survives is the one bequeathed by mercy to the reverent.

**The Ethics of Inherited Wealth**

Finally, the verse carries a practical ethic for the believer who finds himself — as most prosperous believers do — on the receiving end of an inheritance. The Qur'an's teaching is that inherited wealth is a trust with obligations: the wealth that came from the labor of others (the Israelites' case) or from the efforts of ancestors (the ordinary case) is held subject to the claims of justice and mercy — the rights of the poor in it (51:19), the duty of restoring what was wrongfully taken, the prohibition of consolidating it while others starve. The verse's divine transfer is a model: when God bequeathed Egypt's wealth to the Israelites, He did so as part of a covenant that bound them to worship, justice, and the care of the orphan and the stranger — and when they failed the covenant, the inheritance was revoked. The modern heir of any prosperity — the citizen of a wealthy nation, the beneficiary of a family estate, the heir of a colonial or industrial past — is invited to read his own title in the light of this verse: the ownership is real but the tenure is moral; the wealth was someone's labor and may be someone's test; and the securest way to hold it is the way the Qur'an commands — acknowledging the true Inheritor of all things, *"And indeed it is We who give life and cause death, and We are the Inheritors"* (15:23), and spending the trust as the Owner directs.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:29

> **Neither Heaven nor earth wept for them, nor were they granted respite.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mourning That Did Not Happen**

The account of Egypt's fall closes with a verse of extraordinary pathos and terror: *fa-mā bakat ʿalayhimu al-samāʾu wa-al-arḍu* — "Neither Heaven nor earth wept for them." The verse draws on a cosmology in which the heavens and the earth are not dead matter but responsive presences that participate, in their own mode, in the moral history of creation — *"The seven heavens and the earth and all that is therein glorify Him"* (17:44). Within that cosmology, the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Māwardī, and al-Rāzī — transmit a tradition of extraordinary tenderness concerning the death of the believer: every human being, it is said, has a gate in heaven through which his deeds ascend and his provision descends, and a place on earth where he prays and remembers God; when the believer dies, the gate and the place weep for him — the gate weeps because the ascending deeds have stopped, the place weeps because the prostrations have ceased. The weeping of creation for the believer is the measure of his having been alive in the only sense that outlasts the body: he had filled a place in heaven with his worship and a place on earth with his remembrance. The people of Pharaoh had done neither — no good deed had ascended from them to claim a gate, no prayer had hallowed a patch of Egyptian earth — and so, when they died, nothing in creation missed them: *neither Heaven nor earth wept for them*.

**The Second Loss: Not Even a Respite**

The verse's second clause delivers its quieter terror: *wa-mā kānū munẓarīn* — "nor were they granted respite." The verb is from *n-ẓ-r*, to look, to wait — the same root as *inẓār*, warning: the people of Pharaoh were not given the looking-away, the delay, the extension that the Qur'an says is the standing mercy of God toward the guilty: *"And if God were to take mankind to task for their wrongdoing, He would not leave upon it a single creature, but He grants them respite (yuʾakhkhiruhum) till a term appointed"* (16:61). For other guilty nations, the Qur'an records respites: Noah's people were warned for centuries; the Makkans were granted the years between the revelation and Badr; even Pharaoh's own court was warned by Moses and Aaron over a prolonged confrontation of signs. But the final drowning admitted no extension: the sea closed, the case closed, and the sentence ran immediately — the punishment came upon them suddenly, with no interval in which to arrange a defense, no window in which a late faith could be offered. The verse's two halves together describe the completeness of the loss: nothing behind them mourned, and nothing ahead of them waited.

**The Absence of Mourning as a Moral Judgment**

The verse's theology of creation's mourning should not be pressed into a literal sentimentalism — the Qur'an does not teach that the sky weeps with visible tears for the righteous — but its moral content is precise and severe. The weeping of heaven and earth for a person is the creation's testimony that the person added something to creation: justice, mercy, remembrance, beauty. The people of Pharaoh subtracted; they were takers whose whole relation to the world was exploitative — consuming its gardens (v. 25), commanding its labor, corrupting its order — and the world's response to their passing was the response of a body to the removal of a parasite: relief rather than grief. The believer is invited to read the verse as the Qur'an's starkest statement of what a wasted life amounts to cosmically: not mourned, not missed, not extended — a name erased from the world's affection and the world's record. The Prophet ﷺ taught the same truth in positive form when he described what survives the believer: "When a man dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: a continuing charity, knowledge from which benefit is derived, and a righteous child who prays for him" (Muslim 1631) — the believer's death is mourned and his account continues because he planted continuities; the tyrant's death ends everything because he built nothing that outlives his appetite.

**Why the Tyrant's Death Is Not Mourned**

The verse also resolves, from the Qur'an's perspective, a puzzle that human beings have always found disturbing: why do the deaths of great tyrants so often seem to produce genuine grief among those who suffered them? The Qur'an's answer is that the grief of the oppressed for their oppressors is not the mourning of creation but the confusion of captive affection — the Stockholm dynamic of the enslaved, who sometimes love their jailers because the jail is all they know. The true moral order of the universe, the verse implies, is that the death of a tyrant is not a loss to the world but a liberation of it — which is why the Qur'an can record, without a note of tragedy, the drowning of Pharaoh's host, and why the angels in the Islamic tradition do not mourn the death of the oppressor. The reader who finds himself grieving the passing of an oppressor — a tyrannical parent, an abusive authority, an unjust regime — is invited to examine the grief: is it the creation's mourning, or the captive's conditioning? The verse's freedom lies in its permission to feel the truth: some deaths are not losses; some endings are the world's morning.

**The Death That Every Reader Is Preparing For**

Finally, the verse functions as a mirror held up to the reader's own life, and its question is quietly devastating: if you died tonight, would heaven or earth weep for you — that is, is there anything in your life that creation would miss? The believer's answer is not to be found in the number of his mourners but in the content of his days: the verse's tradition of the weeping gate and the weeping place locates the believer's cosmic significance in worship and remembrance — the prayer that ascends, the dhikr that hallows a spot of earth, the charity that continues, the knowledge that benefits, the righteous child. The people of Pharaoh had everything the world can give and nothing creation keeps; the believer is invited to invert the inventory — to hold the world's goods lightly and to accumulate the goods that heaven and earth register: *"And whatever good you send forth for your souls, you will find it with God, better and greater in reward"* (73:20). The one who lives thus has already secured the two things the verse denies to Pharaoh: the mourning of creation at his passing, and — more importantly — the respite of the grave from the terror of the account, for his deeds have gone ahead of him to speak in his favor.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:30

> **And We delivered the Children of Israel from a humiliating punishment—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Story**

The narrative now turns from the fate of the oppressor to the fate of the oppressed: *wa-laqad najjaynā banī Isrāʾīla mina al-ʿadhābi al-muhīn* — "And We delivered the Children of Israel from a humiliating punishment." The verb *najjaynā* — We saved, We delivered — is the Qur'an's verb of rescue, used repeatedly of the exodus (2:49; 7:141; 10:90; 20:80; 26:65) and, in this very sūrah, of the shielding of the reverent from the Fire (v. 56). The verse completes the diptych that the whole passage has been painting: the sea drowned the pursuers and saved the pursued — one host swallowed, one people delivered — by a single act of divine power. The Qur'an's account of history is never merely the record of punishment; every destruction in its pages is paired with a deliverance, and the deliverance is the point of the destruction: Egypt was drowned *for* the liberation of Israel, as the warning of the Qur'an to the Makkans is issued *for* the preservation of the believers.

**The Humiliating Punishment: What Exactly Was Delivered?**

The phrase *al-ʿadhāb al-muhīn* — "the humiliating punishment" — is the Qur'an's technical description of the Egyptian oppression, and its fullest inventory is given in the parallel passage: *"And remember when We delivered you from the House of Pharaoh, who inflicted upon you terrible punishment, slaying your sons and sparing your women. And in that was a great trial from your Lord"* (2:49, and cf. 7:141). The humiliation was systematic and gendered: the male infants murdered at birth to prevent the growth of Israelite power (2:49; 7:141; 28:4), the women spared to serve — the entire apparatus of an oppression designed not merely to exploit but to degrade: to break a people's continuity, dignity, and hope. The Qur'an calls this *muhīn*, humiliating, because its design was the production of humiliation — the reduction of a people to the status of a resource. The verse's deliverance is therefore more than political emancipation; it is the restoration of the divine image in a people that tyranny had worked to erase: the slaves of Pharaoh become, in the next verses, the servants of God, chosen and honored.

**The Divine Method of Deliverance**

The sequence of this sūrah's narrative — the plea of Moses (v. 22), the night departure (v. 23), the sea (v. 24), the drowned host (vv. 24–29), and now the delivered people (v. 30) — illustrates the Qur'an's understanding of how deliverance operates in history. It is not that God first delivered Israel and then punished Egypt; the two acts were one act: the deliverance of Israel *was* accomplished through the destruction of the power that held them. The commentators note the same structure in the Prophet's own biography: the victory of Badr, in which the Quraysh's military power was broken, was at the same moment the deliverance of the persecuted Muslims of Makkah from the threat of extinction — which is why this sūrah can move so directly from the story of Moses to the warnings of the Quraysh: the pattern is not merely similar; it is the same pattern, operated by the same Lord, in the same way. The believer who studies history through the Qur'an's lens learns to read every act of divine justice as an act of divine mercy for someone: the falling of one power is the rising of another people; the sea that closes over the tyrant opens the road for the freed.

**Deliverance as the Pattern of the Believer's Hope**

The verse's placement in the sūrah also carries a consolatory message aimed at the original audience of the revelation and at every oppressed community since. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, when these verses were being recited in Makkah, were themselves a persecuted minority — mocked, boycotted, threatened with extinction by a power that seemed as immovable as Egypt's. The recitation of the exodus narrative was their assurance that the pattern was fixed: *"God has promised those of you who believe and perform righteous deeds that He will surely make them vicegerents upon the earth, as He made those before them vicegerents, and that He will surely establish for them their religion"* (24:55). The deliverance of Israel is presented in the Qur'an as a precedent with the force of a promise: the oppressors of every age hold their power on lease from the same Lord who drowned Pharaoh, and the term of the lease is written. The believers' task during the lease is the task Moses modeled — the message delivered with courage, the patience maintained without despair, and the prayer offered without ceasing — and the divine response to that task is as certain as the sea.

**The Deliverance That Only God Could Give**

Finally, the verse's verb deserves a full stop: *najjaynā* — *We* delivered. The Qur'an is emphatic, throughout the exodus narratives, that the deliverance was not the achievement of Israelite strategy or Moses' generalship: the Israelites left Egypt in flight, unarmed, pursued by chariots, with the sea before them — every human avenue closed (26:61–62). The deliverance came from the only direction that remained: *"We revealed unto Moses: 'Strike the sea with thy staff,' and it parted"* (26:63), by the command of the One who said, *"Truly I am with you both, hearing and seeing"* (20:46). The verse teaches the believer the architecture of true rescue: human means are exhausted, divine means begin; the night of maximum despair is the eve of the maximum sign. This is why the Qur'an can promise the believers, in every generation, a deliverance proportioned to their sincerity — not always from the oppressor's hand in this world, but always from the deeper bondage that the exodus symbolized: the bondage to false gods, false fears, and false selves — *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of the darkness into the light"* (2:257). The Children of Israel were delivered from Pharaoh's humiliation that they might serve God freely; every believer's deliverance has the same double structure: freedom from what degrades, for the sake of the worship that dignifies.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:31

> **from Pharaoh. Truly he was foremost among the prodigal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naming the Oppressor**

Verse 31 narrows the instrument of the humiliation with a single name: *min Firʿawn* — "from Pharaoh." The Qur'an could have said "from the Egyptians" or "from the tyrant"; it names Pharaoh, because the Qur'an's history is not the record of impersonal forces but of persons and their choices — and because the name had become, by the time of the revelation, the archetype of the human being who sets himself against God: *"And We made them imams calling unto the Fire"* (28:41) is said of Pharaoh's house, and the Qur'an addresses the Prophet's contemporaries with the warning that Pharaoh's name is a category: every age produces its Pharaoh, and every Pharaoh meets the sea. Naming is also a form of justice: the verse fixes responsibility — the oppression of Israel was not a spontaneous social pathology but the policy of a named man and his court, and the record of history preserves the name so that the moral of the story is not lost in abstraction. The believer is taught by the naming that tyranny always has an address; oppression is never ownerless.

**Foremost Among the Prodigal: The Anatomy of Transgression**

The verse's characterization of Pharaoh is a single phrase of devastating precision: *innahu kāna mina al-musrifīn* — "truly he was foremost among the prodigal." The word *musrifūn* is from *s-r-f*, to exceed the limit, to waste, to extravagate — the root of *isrāf*, prodigality. In the Qur'an's moral lexicon, *isrāf* is the transgression of the just limit in any direction: the spendthrift exceeds the limit of wealth (25:67), the killer exceeds the limit of vengeance (17:33), and the tyrant exceeds the limit of power — claiming for himself what belongs to God, as Pharaoh claimed divinity (28:38; 79:24). The verse's *mina al-musrifīn* — "of the prodigal" — places Pharaoh in a class: he is not an isolated monster but the foremost member of a category that the Qur'an describes as the natural enemy of the prophets: *"Thus does God seal the heart of every tyrant, haughty one"* (40:35); *"And when We wish to destroy a town, We command its prodigal ones, and they act wickedly therein"* (17:16). The prodigal's defining mark is the refusal of limits — the conviction that his power, wealth, or desire is not subject to the boundary that God has set — and Pharaoh is its archetype because his prodigality extended to the ultimate limit: the claim to divinity itself.

**The Quiet Contrast: The Prodigal vs. The Prophet**

The verse's characterization of Pharaoh as foremost among the prodigal completes a pattern of contrasts that runs through the entire passage and gives it its moral geometry. Moses is described in this sūrah as *karīm*, noble (v. 17), and *amīn*, trustworthy (v. 18); Pharaoh is described as *musrif*, prodigal — the noble and the prodigal, the one who keeps limits and the one who breaks them, face each other across the passage like the two figures of a morality painting. The Qur'an develops the same contrast elsewhere at length: the prodigal is the one who spends his life force on the self — *"Eat and drink and be prodigal"* is the imagined divine address to the wasteful (cf. 7:31, where prodigality is prohibited even in permitted things); the noble is the one who spends his life force on God. The exodus narrative is thus a collision of two economies: Pharaoh's economy of unlimited self-expansion, which ended in the sea, and Moses' economy of limit and obedience, which ended in the deliverance of a nation.

**The Psychology of Prodigality: Why the Limitless Collapses**

The verse's placement of Pharaoh among the *musrifīn* also contains a psychological diagnosis of the tyrant's collapse that the commentators develop in their ethical reflections. Prodigality is not merely moral failure; it is a failure of reality-perception: the prodigal's core error is the belief that his resources — power, wealth, life itself — are without limits, when in fact everything he has is a loan with a fixed term. Pharaoh's prodigality expressed itself in ever-escalating claims — the monuments, the divinity, the pursuit of the fugitives to the floor of the sea — because the prodigal cannot stop: each exceeded limit demands the next, since the sense of limitlessness is addictive. The sea was not the punishment of Pharaoh's prodigality only; it was its logical terminus — the point where the claim of limitlessness met the actual limit of water. The Qur'an states the general law: *"And thus is the seizing of thy Lord when He seizes the towns that do wrong; truly His seizing is painful, severe"* (11:102). The prodigal's tragedy is that he mistakes the divine patience (16:61) for the divine approval — the delay of the limit for the absence of the limit — and builds his whole life on the mistake.

**The Modern Prodigals and the Believer's Alternative**

Every age produces its own *musrifīn*, and the verse invites the modern reader to recognize the category in its contemporary forms: the prodigality of consumption that treats the earth's resources as limitless; the prodigality of power that treats human beings as expendable; the prodigality of self-invention that treats the moral limits of human nature as obsolete; the prodigality of digital empires that treat attention and data as inexhaustible. The Qur'an's warning to each is the warning of this verse: the sea is not far from any Pharaoh. And the believer's alternative to the prodigal economy is sketched in the same verse by contrast: the *muḥsinūn*, the doers of good, the *muttaqūn*, the reverent — the people of the limit, who will appear at the sūrah's end in their secure station (vv. 51–57). Between the two — the prodigal dragged to the Zaqqūm (vv. 43–50) and the reverent established among the gardens (vv. 51–52) — the whole of human destiny is distributed. The reader is invited to audit his own economy: where do I exceed limits — in spending, in anger, in ambition, in the claims I make on others? And where do I practice the believer's discipline of the limit — the moderation that the Qur'an calls *qasd* (31:19), the balance that keeps the soul within the boundaries that keep the soul alive?

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:32

> **We knowingly chose them over the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Election of Israel: A Verse of Great Consequence**

The narrative now ascends from liberation to election: *wa-laqad ikhtarnāhum ʿalā ʿilmin ʿalā al-ʿālamīn* — "And We knowingly chose them over the worlds." The verb *ikhtarnā* is from *kh-y-r*, the root of choice and goodness — to select, to prefer; the clause *ʿalā ʿilmin* — "upon knowledge" — is the verse's hinge: God's choice of the Children of Israel was not arbitrary favoritism but an act grounded in His knowledge of what He was doing and whom He was choosing for what. The commentators — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī — are careful to delimit the verse's claim: the Children of Israel were chosen over *the worlds of their own time* — the nations contemporary with them — not over all peoples of all times; the choice was for the custody of revelation and the carrying of the prophetic line at that stage of sacred history. The Qur'an states the same election in its fullest form elsewhere: *"O Children of Israel! Remember My blessing which I bestowed upon you, and that I favored you above the worlds"* (2:47, and cf. 2:122; 45:16). The election was real, historical, and loaded with responsibility — and the Qur'an's account of Israel's subsequent history is the account of what a people did with an election it failed to sustain.

**The Knowledge Behind the Choice**

The phrase *ʿalā ʿilmin* rewards close reading, because it answers the oldest objection to divine election — the objection that a God who chooses some peoples over others is arbitrary or partial. The verse's answer is that the divine choice is an act of knowledge, not of whim: God chose Israel because His knowledge perceived in them — in Abraham's lineage, in the readiness developed through trial, in the role they were to play in the history of revelation — the suitability for the burden of the covenant. The Qur'an elsewhere ascribes the same knowledge-ground to the choice of prophets and of individuals: God says of Abraham, *"And We chose him in this world, and in the Hereafter he is among the righteous"* (2:130), and of the believers generally, *"Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those of Our servants whom We chose"* (35:32). Election in the Qur'an is never presented as a mystery of irrational preference; it is presented as the divine wisdom's appointment of instruments — the choice of the vessel that can carry the water at the stage of the journey in question. The knowledge clause thus preserves both truths the Qur'an always holds together: God's choice is absolutely free, and God's choice is never without wisdom.

**Chosen for What? The Content of the Election**

The sūrah's context defines the content of Israel's election with precision: the verses immediately following list what the choice entailed — *"And We gave them signs wherein was a manifest trial"* (v. 33) — and the wider Qur'an fills in the inventory: revelation (the Torah), prophets raised from among them, the deliverance from Pharaoh, the manna and quails, the cloud and the pillar, the land of promise. But the deepest content of the election is stated in the covenant verse of Sūrah al-Baqarah: *"And when We made a covenant with the Children of Israel: 'Worship none but God, and be good to parents and kin and orphans and the needy...'"* (2:83) — election to carry the knowledge of the One God and the law of justice into history. The commentators note the sobering corollary: election is not privilege without duty but duty with privilege — the chosen people's failures are judged by a stricter standard, which is why the Qur'an's address to the Children of Israel so often takes the form of *"O Children of Israel! Remember My blessing..."* — the reminder that the election was an act of divine generosity to be answered by gratitude and obedience, and that ingratitude converts the blessing into evidence.

**Israel's Election and the Final Community**

The verse's appearance in a Makkan sūrah addressed to the Quraysh raises the question of its contemporary application, and the commentators answer it in two directions. First, the election of Israel is adduced as evidence of the divine method: God chooses whom He will for the custody of revelation, and the choice is not determined by the world's standards of power or wealth — He chose the enslaved Israelites over the empire that held them, exactly as He chose the unlettered Prophet from the trading town of Makkah over the thrones of Persia and Rome. The verse thus prepares the Makkan audience for the startling claim of the revelation: that the final messenger and the final community are themselves an election — *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind"* (3:110) — chosen not for racial or national merit but for the carrying of the message. Second, the verse warns the Quraysh — and every subsequent community, including the Muslims — that election is a burden: the communities that were chosen before and failed their election (the Qur'an's account of Israel's history is largely this) were replaced, and the replacement is by the same principle: *"And if you turn away, He will substitute another people instead of you"* (47:38).

**The Election of the Reader**

The verse's final address is to the individual reader, whom the Qur'an invites to see himself inside the divine method of choice. The believer is, in the Qur'an's own words, chosen: *"Then We gave the Book as an inheritance to those of Our servants whom We chose"* (35:32) — and the choice of the individual, like the choice of Israel, is *ʿalā ʿilmin*, upon knowledge: God knows what He is choosing each soul for, and the soul's task is to discover the election by living it. The classical scholars expressed this as the doctrine of individual responsibility within the divine plan: the believer is not to be paralyzed by the question of whether he is among the chosen — the choice is actualized by response; the one who responds to the message is, by his response, the one chosen for it. Israel's election was made actual by the exodus — the deliverance that called forth the covenant at Sinai; the believer's election is made actual by the deliverance from false gods — the turning that the Qur'an calls *islām* — and the covenant that follows it: prayer, justice, mercy. The verse thus closes with a quiet question to every reader: the divine choice has been offered in the form of the message you now hold; are you making it actual — or are you, like the communities that were chosen and failed, returning the election unused?

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:33

> **And We gave them signs wherein was a manifest trial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gifts That Test**

The account of Israel's election closes with its instrument: *wa-ātaynāhum mina al-āyāti mā fīhi balāʾun mubīn* — "And We gave them signs wherein was a manifest trial." The signs (*āyāt*) in view are identified by the commentators along two lines, both recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Māwardī, and Ibn Kathīr. The first line reads the signs as the miracles of the exodus and the wilderness: the parting of the sea, the cloud that shaded them, the manna and the quails that fed them (2:57; 7:160; 20:80), the water struck from the rock (2:60; 7:160) — the continuous wonders that accompanied the journey from Egypt toward the promised land. The second line reads the signs as the miracles Moses performed before Pharaoh: the staff that became a serpent, the hand that shone white, the nine signs (17:101) — the public demonstrations of the manifest authority Moses had announced (v. 19 of this sūrah). The verse's genius is that it does not need to choose between the readings, because both sets of signs share one quality: each was *balāʾun mubīn* — a manifest trial — and the trial was the point of the giving.

**What Is a Manifest Trial? The Three Readings**

The phrase *balāʾ mubīn* is itself the verse's center of gravity, and the commentators unfold it in three directions that together map the Qur'an's doctrine of testing. First, the signs were a trial in the sense of a test of faith: the miracles divided the people into those who recognized the divine hand and those who attributed the wonders to sorcery or chance — the sea crossing separated the believing Israelites from the murmurers whose faith collapsed at the first hardship (2:61; 5:20–26). Second, the signs were a trial in the sense of a blessing that tests gratitude: the manna and quails, the shade and the water, were gifts so continuous that they became the occasion of ingratitude — the people complained of the single food (2:61) and in time treated the gift of manna as an entitlement, which is the quiet corrosion of every prolonged prosperity. Third, the signs were a trial in the sense of a punishment for those who failed them — the verse's own context makes this the primary register, for the account of the exodus in this sūrah has just narrated the drowning of those who rejected the signs. Al-Ṭabarī, drawing on the general principle of 21:35 — *"We try you with evil and with good, as a test"* — concludes that the manifest trial covers all three: the same signs were mercy for the believers who accepted them, a test for the grateful, and judgment for those who turned away.

**Why the Chosen Are Tested Most**

The verse's placement directly after the election (v. 32) is deliberate and contains a law of the divine method: the chosen are tested in proportion to their choosing. Israel was elected *ʿalā ʿilmin* — knowingly, for a purpose — and the purpose could only be accomplished through testing: a people entrusted with revelation had to be refined until it could carry the trust, and the refinement was the wilderness — forty years of miracles and trials, of which the Qur'an gives the summary law: *"And We tried them with the good things and the evil things, that haply they might return"* (7:168). The commentators note the same law operating in the life of every prophet and every believer: the greater the mission, the more searching the examination — the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, the most honored of creation, underwent the trials of Makkah, the loss of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib in a single year, the siege, the exile, and the slander of his own household, because the vessel of the final message had to be proven in every furnace. The verse thus reframes the believer's own trials: difficulty is not evidence of divine abandonment but often the signature of divine trust — the testing of the one chosen for something.

**The Manna and Quails: A Case Study in Blessed Trial**

The wilderness provision that the commentators cite as the paradigm of the sign-trial deserves a moment's attention, because its psychology is universal. The manna arrived each morning — sufficient for the day, impossible to store beyond it (the stored portion spoiled, according to the traditions of the commentators) — and the quails with it; the arrangement tested the people daily: would they trust the Provider for tomorrow's bread, or would their anxiety for the future drive them to hoard the present? The design of the daily provision was itself the curriculum: a people who had been slaves in Egypt, where provision was the master's gift and the future was the master's property, had to learn the grammar of trust — that the Lord who delivered them from Pharaoh would not starve them in the wilderness. The test was manifest because the choice was daily and visible: trust or anxiety, gratitude or complaint, and the record shows the Israelites oscillating between all of them for forty years. The modern believer who lives inside the same curriculum — the paycheck that arrives monthly, the provision that cannot be guaranteed by any calculation, the daily bread of the Lord's Prayer that Islam's daily prayers echo — is invited to recognize the manna: the divine method of teaching trust through the rhythm of provision has not been retired; it has merely been localized to each believer's own wilderness.

**The Trial That Every Sign Carries**

The verse's final word — *mubīn*, manifest — closes the circle of the sūrah's vocabulary: the clear Book (v. 2), the clear messenger (v. 13), the manifest smoke (v. 10), the manifest authority (v. 19), and now the manifest trial. The repetition is the Qur'an's insistence that the divine dealings are conducted in the open: the trial is manifest so that its results are manifest — no one can claim, at the reckoning, that he was tested in secret, tempted beyond his knowledge, or examined by hidden standards. The signs of God are public — in the history of Israel, in the cosmos (41:53), in the conscience, and in the scripture — and the trial they constitute is fair because it is visible: the tested one always knows, at the moment of testing, that he is being tested. The believer's discipline, then, is the cultivation of that awareness — the state the Qur'an calls *taqwā* in its root sense: the alertness of the one who knows he is observed, the careful walking of the one who knows the path is watched, the daily choice, made in the open, to respond to the sign with gratitude rather than complaint, with trust rather than hoarding, with obedience rather than murmuring — until the manifest trial of this world issues, for the one who passes it, in the secure station of the reverent that this sūrah will describe in its final movement.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:34

> **Truly these say,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return to the Makkans**

With verse 34 the sūrah's frame shifts back from the past to the present: *inna hāʾulāʾi la-yaqūlūn* — "Truly these say." The demonstrative *hāʾulāʾi*, "these," points at the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries — the Quraysh who are hearing the sūrah as it is recited — and the particle *inna* with its *lām* of emphasis marks what follows as the settled speech of a settled position. The sūrah has spent sixteen verses on Pharaoh — his arrogance, his pursuit, his drowning — and the return to the present audience is a verdict by parallel: *these* — the ones who heard the Qur'an's warning, who witnessed the Prophet's character, who saw the Muslims' steadfastness — *these say*... What they say, the next two verses will record, is the very denial that Pharaoh's own story has just refuted. The structure of the sūrah at this point is the structure of a closing argument: the evidence of history (vv. 17–33) has been presented, and now the prosecutor turns to the defendants in the dock: you have heard what happened to those who said what you are about to say.

**The Quraysh's Speech Acts: Denial as a Social Performance**

The Qur'an's use of *yaqūlūn* — "they say" — for the disbelievers' position is itself a subtle diagnosis. Throughout the Makkan sūrahs the deniers of the resurrection are described as *saying* rather than as *concluding* or *believing*: their materialism is not a philosophy they have reasoned to but a slogan they repeat — a social performance conducted in unison, whose function is mutual reinforcement rather than inquiry. The Qur'an captures the social psychology precisely: the denial of the afterlife in Makkah was a tribal consensus — the collective shrug of a society that found the doctrine inconvenient, repeated by each generation because the previous generation had repeated it: *"We found our fathers upon a community"* (43:22). The verse's *hāʾulāʾi* ("these") carries a note of distance and indictment — these who say — as if the speaker were holding them up for inspection: the people who will be identified in the next verses by the words that come out of their mouths.

**Why the Resurrection Was the Line in the Sand**

The sūrah's concentration on the denial of the resurrection at this point is no accident of theme; the resurrection was the specific point on which the Makkan opposition chose to make its stand. The Makkans could tolerate talk of one God — they already acknowledged a creator (v. 7 of this sūrah); they could even entertain the prophethood of a man from among themselves — the objection there was social rather than theoretical. What they could not accept was the resurrection: the claim that the dead would be raised, bodies reassembled, deeds weighed, and an eternal sentence passed — because that claim, if true, would overturn the entire moral economy of their world: the power of the strong, the privilege of the wealthy, the tribal honors and inherited statuses would all be temporary, judged, and reversible. The denial of the resurrection was thus the keystone of the Makkan system, and the Qur'an returns to it throughout the Makkan period with an insistence that measures its importance. The classical commentators note that the resurrection is, in the Qur'an's own accounting, the test case of faith in the unseen: the believer's entire orientation to this world — its pleasures, its powers, its pains — is governed by whether he believes that this world is followed by an accounting.

**The Speech That Identifies the Speaker**

The verse's placement also establishes a hermeneutic that the Qur'an employs throughout: people are known by their speech, and their speech is recorded — *"He utters not a word save that by him there is an observer ready"* (50:18). The "these say" of the sūrah is not merely reportage; it is the assembling of evidence for the trial that the sūrah is about to describe (vv. 40–42). The words of denial that the Quraysh are uttering now — in the streets of Makkah, in the assemblies of the tribe, in the hearing of the Prophet ﷺ — are being preserved verbatim in the revelation itself, so that on the Day of Division the defendants will be confronted with their own speech: *"This is that which you used to doubt"* (v. 50, of this sūrah). The believer who recites the sūrah is therefore taught a double lesson: first, that speech is serious — the words of denial and mockery uttered lightly in this world are entered into a record that this world does not control; and second, that the believer's own speech is his witness — the words of faith, recitation, and remembrance spoken now are equally recorded, and the believer who says what the believers say is assembling, word by word, the evidence of his own case.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:35

> **"There is naught but our first death, and we shall not be resurrected.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Materialist Creed in Two Clauses**

The words of "these" are now quoted in full, and they constitute the Qur'an's most compact statement of the materialist position: *in hiya illā mawtatunā al-ūlā wa-mā naḥnu bi-munsharīn* — "There is naught but our first death, and we shall not be resurrected." The first clause is a denial of everything beyond the present life: *in hiya illā* — "it is nothing but" — the formula of reduction; what "it" refers to is the whole of existence, and the reduction is complete: existence is our first death — the death we die at the end of this life, which the speakers presume to be the end of everything. The clause is grammatically striking: they speak of "our first death" as though it were also our only death, the "first" carrying the implicit taunt — you prophets speak of a second life after a first death; we say there is only the first death and nothing after it. The second clause completes the creed with its positive denial: *mā naḥnu bi-munsharīn* — "we shall not be resurrected" — from *n-sh-r*, to raise, to spread forth, to revive, the Qur'anic word for the resurrection's raising of the dead (cf. 35:9, where God revives the dead earth and is able to revive the dead). The denial is total, categorical, and stated without argument: the materialist creed presents itself as the self-evident default of sane people, against which the prophets' claim is the oddity requiring proof.

**The Oldest Argument and Its Refutation by the Cosmos**

The Qur'an's reply to this creed is not delivered in the abstract; it is delivered by the sūrah's own accumulated evidence. The denial of the resurrection is refuted by the two demonstrations the sūrah has already staged: the story of Pharaoh, whose "first death" in the sea was followed by a divine accounting that his power could not halt — the drowned host did not end at the water's edge, for the record of their deeds and the verdict on their lives survived the sea; and the signs given to Israel (v. 33), among which the most pointed was the manna and the quails — the provision that arrived each morning from a sky that the materialists had declared empty, the daily resurrection of food for a people who had not planted it. The Qur'an's broader argument for the resurrection rests on the two pillars that this sūrah has been setting in place since verse 7: the lordship of the One who gives life and causes death (v. 8), and the purposive character of creation (vv. 38–39, coming next). If the One who caused the first death is the One who gave the first life, then the power that originated existence is not exhausted by terminating it — the One who made the seed and the tree can make the tree and the seed again; and if creation has purpose, the purpose requires a completion that this world never provides.

**The Psychology of "The First Death": Why Reduction Comforts and Fails**

The clause *mawtatunā al-ūlā* also invites a psychological reading that the commentators gesture toward in their ethical reflections. The materialist creed of the Makkans — and of every age — performs a recognizable emotional function: the reduction of existence to the first death is attractive because it appears to cancel the fear of judgment. If there is nothing after death, then there is no reckoning; if there is no reckoning, then the powerful may enjoy their power without qualm and the guilty may enjoy their guilt without fear. The Qur'an names this dynamic in its diagnosis of the deniers: *"And they said, 'There is no life but our life of this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time'"* (45:24) — a creed whose function is precisely the licensing of the present. But the Qur'an's analysis is that the comfort is counterfeit: the denial of the reckoning does not remove the reckoning; it only removes the preparation for it — the man who denies the exam does not cancel the exam but guarantees his failure in it. The verse's terror is that it records the creed in the very scripture that will be the evidence: the denial itself becomes part of the record, and the speakers' own words at the judgment will be the exhibit — *"This is that which you used to doubt"* (v. 50).

**The Resurrection Denial and the Modern Parallel**

The modern reader recognizes in the Makkans' creed the familiar materialism of the contemporary age, and the sūrah's treatment of it retains its full force. The modern denial of the afterlife usually wears a scientific costume — the claim that the resurrection is "impossible" because the dead decay and the body's matter disperses — and the Qur'an's answer to that costume was delivered in advance: *"Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones? Yea, verily, We are able to restore his very fingers"* (75:3–4); *"And he strikes a likeness for Us and forgets his own creation, saying, 'Who will give life to these bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life who brought them into being the first time'"* (36:78–79). The argument is untouched by the centuries: the One who originated the assembly of matter into a living body — an event the materialist witnesses in every birth and cannot explain by any law — is not disabled from reassembling it. The verse's deeper challenge to the modern materialist is the same challenge it posed to the Makkan: not "can you prove the resurrection impossible?" but "why does your denial of it serve your moral comfort so conveniently?" — the question that exposes the creed's psychological function beneath its philosophical costume.

**The Believer's Answer to the First-Death Creed**

Finally, the verse defines the believer by contrast in a single stroke: the believer is precisely the one who does *not* reduce existence to the first death — who lives as one who will be *munshar*, raised, and whose present conduct is governed by that future raising. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to live inside this conviction: the grave is the first station of the next life; the deeds of this world are the provision for a journey whose first stage is death — *"The grave is either one of the meadows of the Garden or one of the pits of the Fire"* (al-Tirmidhī 2460, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī). The believer's discipline, then, is the constant rehearsal of the denial's opposite: the daily remembrance of death not as the end but as the door, the regular practice of the prayers that the resurrection makes meaningful, and the audit of the day's deeds in the light of a morning that is coming. The Makkans said: there is nothing but our first death. The believer answers, with the whole of the revelation: there is our first death — and then the rising, the division, the station, and the triumph; and the life that is lived for that sequence is the only life that is not lived for nothing.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:36

> **Bring us our fathers, if you are truthful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge That Answers Itself**

The deniers' speech ends with the taunt that the Qur'an records across three sūrahs of the Makkan period: *faʾtū bi-ābāʾinā in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "Bring us our fathers, if you are truthful." The challenge is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers: if the resurrection is real, then raise the dead of the past generation — produce our fathers, and we will believe. The demand is the materialist's last redoubt: not argument but demonstration — and not just any demonstration, but the spectacular one of raising the long-dead in the assembly of the living. The Qur'an's parallel accounts show that this taunt was a staple of the Makkan opposition: *"And they say, 'When we are bones and dust, shall we indeed be raised as a new creation?'"* (17:49); and in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt, *"And they say, 'What! When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be resurrected — and our fathers of old?'"* (37:16–17, and cf. 56:47–48).

**The Illogic Inside the Demand**

The verse's reply is carried by its own structure, and the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī — draw out the illogic. The demand "bring us our fathers" presupposes exactly what it denies: if the resurrection were performed on demand in this world, it would not be the resurrection — it would be a miracle like the other miracles, and the Qur'an's consistent teaching is that the disbelievers of every prophet demanded precisely such demonstrations and were never satisfied by them: *"Even if We sent down angels unto them, and the dead spoke unto them, and We gathered all things before them face to face, they would not believe, save if God willed"* (6:111). The demand is structurally unfalsifiable: each demonstration would breed the next demand, because the demand is not an inquiry but a stalling device — the request of people who have already decided, as the sūrah has recorded: they are *playing in doubt* (v. 9). The second illogic is temporal: the resurrection is not a conjuring trick to be performed at the audience's convenience but the appointed event of an appointed day — the Day of Division (v. 40), whose timing belongs to God: *"They ask thee about the Hour: when will it come to pass? Say: 'The knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none shall unveil it at its time but He'"* (7:187). The request to see the resurrection before its day is the request to see the harvest before the season — a demand that the divine calendar be rewritten to suit the spectator.

**Why God Does Not Perform the Resurrection on Demand**

There is a deeper wisdom in the divine refusal to answer the taunt with a demonstration, and the verse's context exposes it. The resurrection, in the Qur'an's teaching, is not merely an event; it is the event that ends the possibility of further testing — the moment when *"the excuse of every soul"* ends and the account begins. To produce it on demand, in the marketplace of Makkah, in response to a taunt, would be to collapse the entire structure of the test that this world constitutes: the world is the place where belief is chosen without sight — where the unseen is believed through the evidence of the seen, and where God's wisdom distinguishes the truthful from the false by their response to evidence rather than to spectacle. If the resurrection were exhibited as a spectacle, faith would no longer be faith but sight, and the test would be void. The Qur'an states the law: those who demand signs before believing are not seeking truth but seeking to be forced — and the God who created beings with wills does not force the choice that the will is for: *"And even if We opened unto them a gate of heaven and they continued to ascend through it, they would say, 'Our eyes are only dazzled; nay, we are a people bewitched'"* (15:14–15).

**The Fathers They Invoked: The Argument Turned Back**

The taunt contains one more turn of the screw that the commentators relish: the fathers the Makkans demand to see are the very authorities they had invoked against the Prophet — *"We found our fathers upon a community, and we are guided by their traces"* (43:22). The ancestors were, for the Makkans, the ground of truth: whatever the fathers believed was the standard. The challenge "bring us our fathers" is thus an appeal to the ancestral court — bring the fathers back so they can testify for us against you. But the Qur'an has already recorded what the fathers would testify if they were raised: every prophet's people had been warned, every generation of fathers had been summoned to the same truth, and the fathers of the Arabs themselves — the line of Abraham and Ishmael — had been worshippers of the One God before the idols entered: *"And when Abraham said to his father and his people, 'I am free of what you worship'"* (43:26). The fathers, if raised, would not support the idolaters; they would reproach them — the very point the resurrection will demonstrate, when the dead are raised not to arbitrate but to be judged, and the ancestors and descendants meet not as allies but as witnesses: *"On the Day when the wrongdoer bites his hands, saying, 'Would that I had taken a path with the Messenger...'"* (25:27–29).

**The Believer's Answer to the Demand for Proof**

The verse's final lesson is for the believer who encounters the same taunt in every age — the demand of the materialist for a sign: show me God, raise the dead, prove the unseen, and I will believe. The Qur'an's answer, modeled in this verse, is neither to produce the sign (which is not in the believer's power) nor to retreat from the claim, but to redirect the demand to its proper object: the signs have been shown — the creation, the conscience, the scripture, the prophets — and the resurrection has been promised on the authority of the One whose power has been demonstrated in every spring; the demand for a further sign is not a request for evidence but a refusal of the evidence already given. The believer's own certainty does not rest on the production of the dead but on the testimony of the Living — and the believer's task is not to satisfy the taunt but to be, himself, the sign that the taunt cannot explain: the changed life, the patient endurance, the incorruptible hope — the resurrection lived in advance, which is the only demonstration of the afterlife that this world can contain.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:37

> **Are they better, or the people of Tubbaʿ and those before them? We destroyed them. Truly they were guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from Comparative Power**

The sūrah now answers the taunt with the Qur'an's signature argument from history: *a-hum khayrun am qawmu Tubbaʿin wa-alladhīna min qablihim* — "Are they better, or the people of Tubbaʿ and those before them?" The rhetorical question invites comparison: the Makkans who demand that their fathers be raised — are they mightier, wealthier, better established than the people of Tubbaʿ, who made the same denial and met the same end? The answer is assumed: the people of Tubbaʿ — the kings of Ḥimyar in southern Arabia, whose dynasties had ruled Yemen for centuries with power and monuments the Quraysh could not approach — and the nations before them, ʿĀd and Thamūd and the generations between, were greater in every worldly measure, and it availed them nothing. The Qur'an's verdict is delivered in three strokes: *ahlaknāhum* — "We destroyed them"; *innahum kānū mujrimin* — "truly they were guilty." The verse is the pattern of the whole Makkan argument: the past is the proof of the future, and the destroyed nations are the standing demonstration that power does not purchase exemption from the divine accounting.

**Who Were the People of Tubbaʿ?**

The commentators identify Tubbaʿ as the title borne by a succession of Ḥimyarī kings who ruled over southern Arabia for centuries — a line of monarchs whose name had passed into the proverbial speech of the Arabs as the symbol of ancient majesty, so that "the people of Tubbaʿ" meant at once a historical nation and a byword for vanished glory. Ibn Kathīr notes that Tubbaʿ was the title the people of Sheba gave their kings — the rulers of the civilization whose destruction the Qur'an narrates in Sūrah Sabaʾ, where the bursting of the dam of Maʾrib turned their gardens into bitter fruit (34:15–19). The Qur'an mentions the people of Tubbaʿ in one other place, in the catalogue of the nations who denied the messengers: *"And the dwellers in the thicket and the people of Tubbaʿ — all denied the messengers, so the threat came true"* (50:14). Some of the commentators, following a tradition transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ, note the ambiguity of Tubbaʿ's own final state: it is reported that the Prophet ﷺ said, "I know not whether Tubbaʿ was a prophet," and a narration records him forbidding the reviling of Tubbaʿ because he had submitted — while the *people* of Tubbaʿ, the nation he ruled, persisted in denial and were destroyed for it. The verse's plural is thus precise: the king may have believed; the people did not — and the destruction fell on the people.

**The Geography of the Argument: Yemen at Makkah's Doorstep**

The choice of Tubbaʿ for the comparison is geographically pointed in a way the original audience would have felt immediately. Yemen was not a distant legend to the Quraysh; it was the southern terminus of the very trade route that sustained them — the caravans of winter and summer that the Qur'an itself acknowledges as the Quraysh's provision and security (106:1–2) — carrying leather and frankincense north from Ḥimyar through the Hijaz. The ruins of the Yemeni kingdoms — the great dams, the palaces, the irrigation works of a civilization that had turned the desert's edge into gardens — were visible along the route, and the Arabs told stories of their fall: the flood of ʿArim, the gardens of Maʾrib, the kingdoms that had vanished while their monuments stood. When the Qur'an asks, "Are they better, or the people of Tubbaʿ?" it is pointing the Makkans at ruins they had passed in their own caravans — the evidence of the argument written in stone along the road they traveled for profit. The traders who carried Yemen's goods north had also carried Yemen's lesson: the power that the Quraysh now wielded in Makkah was a fraction of the power that Ḥimyar had wielded in Yemen, and Ḥimyar's power had not saved it from the divine accounting.

**The Two Arguments in One Verse: Power and Resurrection**

The verse performs two arguments simultaneously. The first is the argument against the denial of the resurrection: the people of Tubbaʿ denied that the dead would be raised — and they are now themselves the dead, raised in the only sense the deniers can verify: their memory is invoked, their ruins are inspected, their fate is recited — and the Qur'an's recitation of their destruction is itself a partial resurrection: the dead of Ḥimyar have been brought back, in speech, to testify against the living of Makkah. The second argument is the argument against the taunt of verse 36: the Makkans demanded that their fathers be produced as a condition of belief; the Qur'an answers that the fathers of the nations — including the fathers the Makkans honored — have already produced themselves, in the form of their destruction, and the evidence of their fate is stronger than any conjured resurrection would be: the nations' end is the resurrection's preview, and the preview is available on the road to Yemen. The believers, for their part, read the same evidence forward: the God who destroyed Ḥimyar's gardens will raise Ḥimyar's dead; the power that ended the first life of the Tubbaʿs is the same power that the deniers deny can begin a second — and the denial is refuted by the very record of the destruction they cannot deny.

**The Guilt That Brings Destruction: A Warning to Every Superpower**

The verse's final clause — *innahum kānū mujrimin* — "truly they were guilty" — is the verdict that makes the destruction intelligible. The people of Tubbaʿ were not destroyed for their weakness or their poverty; they were destroyed for their guilt — the denial of the messengers, the oppression of the truth, the persistence in transgression that the Qur'an elsewhere details: *"All denied the messengers, so the threat came true"* (50:14). The verse's logic is the Qur'an's universal law of the rise and fall of nations, stated elsewhere in the same sūrah's family: *"And thy Lord would never destroy the towns unjustly, while their people were righteous"* (11:117), and towns are destroyed only after the warning has been delivered and refused (28:59). The modern superpowers — the empires and economic giants of the contemporary world — are addressed by this verse with the same question that was put to the Quraysh: are you better than the people of Tubbaʿ? Their dams are fallen, their palaces are dust, their gardens reverted to the wild — and their guilt was no greater than the guilt of a civilization that has the truth and refuses it. The believer's reading of history through this verse is the Qur'anic philosophy of history in miniature: nations rise by justice and fall by transgression, and the fall is not the cancellation of the rise but its verdict — the demonstration, written in the ruins, that the accounting the deniers denied is the most certain fact of history.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:38

> **And We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Ascends to Creation Itself**

The sūrah now turns from the evidence of history to the evidence of creation: *wa-mā khalaqnā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā lāʿibīn* — "And We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play." The verse is one of the Qur'an's central statements of the purposefulness of existence, and its placement is strategic: the denial of the resurrection (vv. 34–36) is now met with its deepest refutation — the denial of purpose is the only logical ground on which the denial of the resurrection can stand, and the denial of purpose is false. The word *lāʿibīn* — from *l-ʿ-b*, to play, to sport — describes the activity of the player: the one engaged in amusement without purpose, in movement without destination, in a game that produces nothing. The Qur'an's claim is categorical: the heavens — with their suns, moons, and ordered courses; the earth — with its seas, mountains, seasons, and harvests; and *mā baynahumā* — whatsoever is between them, the whole intermediate creation — were not brought into being as a game. The emphatic negative (*mā... lāʿibīn*, with the active participle carrying the sense "as players") leaves no room for the accidental universe: the creation's structure, order, and beauty are the signatures of intent, and intent implies a purpose — and purpose implies an end toward which the whole is moving.

**The Playful Universe Versus the Purposeful Universe**

The verse is the Qur'an's answer to two ancient and modern positions at once. The ancient position, present in the pre-Islamic worldview, was the cynicism that saw existence as a wheel of fortune — meaningless recurrence, fortune's sport, the universe as the playground of indifferent forces or gods; the pre-Islamic ode mourned the ruins of loved ones' dwellings because the world's way was to build and destroy without meaning. The modern position is the scientific materialism that sees the cosmos as the product of blind process — vast, ordered, beautiful, and purposeless: the universe without a why. The Qur'an's reply to both is the argument this verse conducts: a creation whose every level displays the signature of design — the heavens' order, the earth's provision, the human being's conscience — cannot be the product of play, and a creation that is not play must have an author whose act is purposeful. The verse is developed in the parallel passages of the Qur'an with the same insistence: *"And We did not create the heaven and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play. Had We wished to take a sport, We would have taken it from Our Presence — had We been doers of such"* (21:16–17), and *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and whatsoever is between them in vain; that is the conjecture of those who disbelieve — so woe to those who disbelieve, from the Fire"* (38:27).

**The Connection Between Purpose and Resurrection**

The verse's logic moves in a single chain, which the commentators — al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr among them — reconstruct: if creation has purpose, then human life has purpose; if human life has purpose, then the present world — where the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper, where justice is never fully done and virtue is never fully rewarded — cannot be the whole of the purpose; therefore there must be a completion beyond the present order, a day when the purposes are gathered and the accounts are settled. The resurrection is not an arbitrary add-on to the Qur'anic worldview; it is the logical requirement of creation's purposefulness. This is why the verse stands exactly where it stands — between the Makkans' denial of the resurrection (vv. 34–36) and the announcement of the Day of Division (v. 40): the denial of the resurrection entails the denial of purpose, and the denial of purpose is refuted by the existence of the ordered heavens and earth that the deniers themselves inhabit and enjoy. Al-Qurṭubī notes the verse's implied address: you who say there is no resurrection — you who live and die like the cattle of the field, without a why and without a whither — look at the sky you sleep under and the earth you farm: do players build such order?

**The Human Being: The Purpose Made Visible**

The verse's scope — the heavens, the earth, and all between — finds its center in the one creature among all creation who can ask the question of purpose: the human being. The Qur'an says of the human creature that he was not created in play either — *"Did you suppose that We created you frivolously, and that you would not be returned unto Us?"* (23:115, addressed to the denizens of Hell themselves) — and that his purpose is the worship that orients him to the One who made him: *"I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me"* (51:56). The human being is the point at which the universe's purpose becomes conscious: the heavens praise God without knowing it (17:44), the earth obeys God without willing it, and the human being alone stands at the junction — created with the capacity to know the purpose and the freedom to choose it. The verse's rebuke of the "play" cosmology is thus aimed at its most dangerous consequence: the human being who believes the universe is a game will live as a player — treating life as amusement, other people as pieces, and morality as rules made up for the sport. The believer who knows the creation was not made in play knows that his own existence is a commission, not a pastime — *"And He it is who made you vicegerents upon the earth"* (6:165) — and that the vicegerency has a term and a report.

**Living in a Non-Playful Universe**

The verse's practical translation is a discipline of seriousness that the modern believer must actively maintain against the ambient culture of play. The contemporary world — with its endless entertainment, its gamification of work and worship, its treatment of life as a sequence of experiences to be consumed — is the most sophisticated machinery of *laʿib* (play) that history has produced, and the Qur'an's diagnosis of the universe as non-play extends to the believer's own life as a summons to non-playfulness in the sense of seriousness: the life lived as preparation, work, worship, and love — with the destination kept always in view. The Prophet ﷺ modeled the seriousness without the grimness: he joked with his companions and played with children, but his laughter never obscured his mission, and his lightness never touched his obligations — the balance that the Qur'an itself strikes when it calls the present life *laʿib* and *lahw* (play and amusement, 57:20) *in relation to the Hereafter*, while insisting that the creation beneath and around the play is purposeful. The believer's task is to live inside the purpose — to treat the world as the Qur'an treats it: a serious garden planted for a serious harvest, whose Owner has promised to return for the reaping — the reaping that the next verse will name as the truth for which creation was made.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:39

> **We did not create them, save in truth. But most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Positive Half of the Argument**

Verse 39 completes the negative of verse 38 with its positive: *mā khalaqnāhumā illā bi-al-ḥaqq* — "We did not create them, save in truth." The phrase *bi-al-ḥaqq* — with truth, in truth, for the sake of truth — is the Qur'an's formula for the divine purposefulness of creation, recurring across the revelation with the same insistence: *"And We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, save in truth. And the Hour is certainly coming; so forgive with a beautiful forgiveness"* (15:85); *"We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them, save in truth and for a term appointed"* (46:3). The word *ḥaqq* itself carries the full weight of the Qur'anic worldview: it means truth, reality, right, justice, the due — and creation's being *bi-al-ḥaqq* means that it was brought forth in accordance with reality and justice: not arbitrarily, not as a jest, not for nothing, but as the expression of the divine wisdom, ordered toward the divine purpose. The pairing of the two verses is exact: verse 38 denies the negative (not in play), verse 39 affirms the positive (but in truth) — and the second is the ground of the first: creation is not play because it is truth; a thing that is real cannot be a game.

**The Two Clauses of the Truth of Creation**

The commentators parse *bi-al-ḥaqq* along two lines that together map the Qur'an's doctrine of creation. The first reading takes the phrase as describing the manner of creation: the heavens and the earth were created *with justice and wisdom* — proportioned, ordered, truthful to their own natures, each thing placed in its due station; the Qur'an develops this sense in its account of the cosmos: *"And the heaven — He raised it and set the balance"* (55:7), and the whole catalogue of the natural order's exactness that fills the Makkan sūrahs. The second reading takes the phrase as describing the purpose of creation: the heavens and the earth were created *for the truth* — that is, to manifest the divine names, to serve as the stage of the human trial, and to be the theater of the final judgment; creation's purpose is not exhausted by its own existence but looks toward the completion of the truth in the resurrection and the recompense. Al-Ṭabarī holds both senses together: creation is true in its making and true in its end — made by truth and moving toward truth — and the verse's placement in this sūrah, between the denial of the resurrection and the announcement of the Day of Division, fixes the second sense as the operative one: the creation that is true must end in the truth — the truth of the reckoning for which the human creature was made.

**"But Most of Them Know Not": The Blindness of the Majority**

The verse closes with the Qur'an's recurring verdict on the human condition: *wa-lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn* — "but most of them know not." The clause is not a statistical observation but a diagnosis: most people do not know the truth of creation — do not perceive that the world is purposeful, that their own existence is a commission, that the order of the heavens points to an order of the account. The Qur'an returns to this "most of them know not" refrain with a frequency that marks it as one of the scripture's central themes: *"And if thou obeyest most of those upon the earth, they will lead thee astray from the way of God"* (6:116) — and the refrain, in one form or another, is among the Qur'an's most repeated judgments on the human condition. The ignorance in view is not the absence of information — the heavens and the earth are available to everyone — but the failure of reflection: the majority look at the creation without reading it, as a man might stare at a written page without knowing letters. The Qur'an elsewhere names the failure precisely: *"And how many a sign there is in the heavens and the earth which they pass by, turning away from it!"* (12:105) — the signs are passed daily, and the passing is the ignorance.

**The Epistemology of the Verse: Knowledge as the Cure of Nihilism**

The verse's implicit epistemology deserves attention, because it reverses the modern assumption that ignorance is the default state of the uneducated. The Qur'an's diagnosis is that the ignorance of the majority is not cognitive but moral — the knowledge of purpose is available, and it is refused because it is inconvenient: to know that creation is true is to know that life has an accounting, and the accounting is unwelcome to those who wish to live as players. The verse thus explains the phenomenon of verse 34: *these say* there is no resurrection — because most of them do not know the truth of the creation that refutes the saying. The cure the Qur'an prescribes for the ignorance is the discipline of reflection (*tafakkur*) — the examination of the creation that the Makkan sūrahs command with a consistency unmatched by any other theme: *"Do they not reflect upon the kingdom of the heavens and the earth?"* (7:185); *"Say: 'Travel in the land and observe how He began creation'"* (29:20); *"Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for those of understanding"* (3:190). The believer's knowledge of purpose is not a dogma inherited but a perception trained: the daily prayer, the nightly recitation, the observation of the sky and the seasons and the conscience are the gymnasium in which the perception of the truth of creation is strengthened — until the believer reads the world the way the Qur'an reads it: as the sign of the truth that the majority does not know and the believer cannot deny.

**The Truth That the Believer Is Sent to Carry**

Finally, the verse's "most of them know not" is the premise of the mission that the rest of the sūrah presupposes: if the truth of creation were universally known, there would be no need of warners — but because most do not know, the Book was sent down (v. 3), the messengers were dispatched (v. 5), and the warning was issued (vv. 10–16). The believer who recites this verse stands inside that mission: he is one of the knowing — not by his own merit but by the mercy of the message — and the knowledge is a trust with a direction: the knowledge must be carried to the majority that does not know, by the only means that the prophets used — clarity, patience, and proof. The verse's final clause is thus not a counsel of despair — the majority does not know, therefore abandon them — but the specification of the field: the majority is the field, the ignorance is the soil, and the truth is the seed that the warners were sent to plant — *"And most of mankind, though thou strivest, will not believe"* (12:103) — the striving and the unbelief both being part of the divine design: the truth is sown in the world that was created in truth, and the harvest is the Day of Division that the next verse announces.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:40

> **Truly the Day of Division is their tryst, all together.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conclusion of the Argument**

The argument that began with the denial of the resurrection (vv. 34–36) and ascended through the ruins of Tubbaʿ (v. 37) to the purposefulness of creation (vv. 38–39) now arrives at its conclusion: *inna yawma al-faṣli mīqātuhum ajmaʿīn* — "Truly the Day of Division is their tryst, all together." The word *al-faṣl* — from *f-ṣ-l*, to separate, to decide, to distinguish — is the Qur'an's name for the Day of Judgment as the day of decisive separation: the day when everything that was mixed in this world is finally sorted — the truth from falsehood, the believer from the disbeliever, the reverent from the sinner, each claim from its evidence, each deed from its reward. The *yawm al-faṣl* is named repeatedly in the Qur'an: *"This is the Day of Division which you used to deny"* (37:21, addressed to the deniers); *"What is the Day of Division? And what will teach thee what the Day of Division is? A day when no soul will avail another soul in anything, and the command that day belongs to God"* (82:17–19); *"The Day of Division is the tryst of all of them"* (44:40, this verse; cf. 77:13–14, 38; 78:17). The verse's syntax gives the Day an appointment: *mīqāt* — from *w-q-t*, time — is the appointed time, the tryst, the rendezvous; and the appointment belongs to *them all together* — *ajmaʿīn* — the deniers and the believers, the Makkans who mocked and the prophets who warned, the people of Tubbaʿ and their destroyers, every generation of the human race.

**Why a "Day" Is Necessary: The Logic of the Tryst**

The verse's term *mīqāt* contains the Qur'an's answer to the question of why the reckoning must take the form of an appointed day rather than the form of immediate individual accounting at death. The appointment in time — a day toward which all of history moves — makes possible what the Qur'an calls the *faṣl*, the separation, in its full social dimension: the Day of Division is the day when the wronged are shown their vindication in the presence of their oppressors, when the prophets' patience is displayed before the nations that mocked them, when the believers' endurance and the tyrants' triumph are weighed in the same balance — *the day when every soul finds the good it did present, and the evil it did present* (3:30). A purely individual judgment at death would lack the public dimension that the Qur'an's picture of the Day emphasizes: the sorting is not only of souls but of histories — the histories that were entangled in this world are disentangled in the presence of their participants: *"That day no friend will avail a friend in anything, nor will they be helped"* (44:41, the verse that follows). The Day of Division is thus the completion of the creation that was made in truth (v. 39): a world created for truth must have a day when the truth of every life in it is fully manifest — and that day is the tryst.

**The Tryst of the Deniers: The Address Hidden in the Verse**

The verse's demonstrative — *their* tryst — points back to the deniers of verses 34–36, and the address carries the sūrah's full rhetorical force. The Makkans demanded the resurrection be brought to them (v. 36); the verse answers that it is not brought — it is *appointed*: the Day of Division is the tryst already fixed for them, toward which their whole lives are moving whether they acknowledge it or not. The word *mīqāt* is used in the Qur'an for the appointment of Moses with his Lord at Sinai — *"And when Moses came to Our appointed time (mīqāt), his Lord spoke to him"* (7:143) — and for the appointment of the guilty with their punishment: *"And truly Hell is the promised tryst of them all"* (15:43). The resonance is deliberate: just as Moses kept his appointment with God at the mountain — an appointment the Qur'an describes with terror and majesty — so every human being has an appointment with the Day of Division that he cannot decline, postpone, or delegate: the tryst is written, the time is fixed, and the parties are all — *ajmaʿīn*, all together, no exceptions, no absentees.

**The Day of Division and the Present Life**

The doctrine of the Day of Division carries an immediate practical consequence that the commentators draw out in their ethical works: if the present life is the period of mixture — faith and hypocrisy indistinguishable, justice and oppression entangled, truth and falsehood wearing similar clothes — then the believer's present task is to live in the direction of the *faṣl*: to practice, in advance and in miniature, the separation that the Day will perform in full. The classical scholars describe this as the life of *mīzān* — balance — the constant weighing of deeds, intentions, and words against the standard of revelation, so that the sorting of the Day finds nothing unexpected: the believer who has been sorting his own life all along enters the Day of Division as the student enters the final examination — the sorting is only the formal confirmation of the work of the years. The Prophet ﷺ taught his companions to live inside the tryst: "Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a wayfarer" (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar) — the stranger lives with his departure in view, and the believer's departure is the journey to the Day that is the tryst of all.

**The Verse That Joins the Sūrah's Two Halves**

Structurally, verse 40 is the pivot on which the entire second half of the sūrah turns. Everything that follows is a description of the Day's two outputs: the punishment of the guilty — the Zaqqūm, the molten lead, the boiling liquid, the seizure and the thrust into Hellfire (vv. 43–50) — and the reward of the reverent — the secure station, the gardens and springs, the silk and brocade, the wide-eyed maidens, the immunity from death (vv. 51–57). The Day of Division is the sūrah's center of gravity: the smoke warned of the lesser division in this world (vv. 10–16); the Day of Division is the greater one that the smoke prefigured — and between the two, the whole of the Qur'anic message moves: warn in this world so that the final Division may find you on the side that the sūrah's closing movement describes — the side of the reverent, whose station is secure and whose tryst is the Garden.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:41

> **The Day when no friend will avail a friend in any way, nor will they be helped,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Division of the Divided Day**

The Day of Division now displays its dividing power at the deepest level of human attachment: *yawma lā yughnī mawlan ʿan mawlan shayʾan* — "the Day when no friend will avail a friend in any way." The word *mawlā* is one of the richest in Arabic: it can mean patron and client, ally and friend, master and protégé — in the Qur'an's usage here it denotes the bond of loyalty and protection that human beings establish among themselves, the entire network of relationships by which people expect to be helped in their hour of need: the clan that defends its member, the friend who stands by his friend, the patron who shields his dependent, the alliance that comes to the aid of its ally. The verse announces the total failure of every such bond on the Day of Division: no *mawlā* will avail any *mawlā* in anything — *shayʾan*, even the smallest thing; the help that human beings have spent their lives arranging — the favors owed, the alliances cultivated, the friendships maintained partly as insurance against future need — will all come due on a day when none of them can be collected. The Qur'an states the same law in parallel passages with a thoroughness that leaves nothing to the imagination: *"And fear a Day when no soul will avail another soul in anything, and no intercession will be accepted from it, nor ransom taken, nor will they be helped"* (2:48); *"There shall be no kinship between them that Day, nor will they question one another"* (23:101); *"On the Day when a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his wife and his children — for every man that Day will be a matter sufficient unto him"* (80:34–37).

**The Social Bonds That Fail: An Inventory of Impotence**

The verse's inventory of failing bonds is worth examining item by item, because the Qur'an is precise about what will fail and why. The kinship bond — the clan, the tribe, the family — fails because its strength is collective: a clan can shield its member from other humans, but no clan can shield its member from the judgment of God; the strength that operates among creatures has no purchase on the decree of the Creator. The friendship bond fails because its currency is reciprocal service: the friend can repay what the friend owes — but the Day's debt is owed to God, and no friend holds the currency that can settle it. The patronage bond — the strongest in the Qur'an's own society, where the client's life was the patron's honor — fails because the patron is himself a client on the Day: *"Every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone"* (19:95) — the sheikh arrives without his tribe, the king without his army, the patron without his clients; the Qur'an's phrase is *fardan* — alone — the most terrifying word in the eschatological vocabulary, the singularity of the soul before its Maker with nothing and no one between them. The Prophet ﷺ described the Day in the same terms in the hadith of the believer's confession, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Ibn ʿUmar: "God will bring the believer near, and cover him, and make him confess his sins... and He will say, 'I concealed them for you in the world, and I forgive you for them this day'" — the privacy of the reckoning underlining the solitude of the soul.

**Why the Bonds Fail: The Structure of the Day**

The verse's logic is not merely bleak; it is theologically exact, and the commentators unfold it along two lines. First, the Day of Division is the day when the *faṣl* — the separation — is total: the very name of the Day announces that everything mixed will be sorted, and the deepest mixture of this world is the entanglement of souls with one another — the believer whose faith was borrowed from his father, the disbeliever whose denial was borrowed from his tribe, the righteous whose deeds were entangled with the expectations of patrons and the applause of friends. The Day of Division separates each soul from the human context that carried it, so that the soul's own faith or denial stands alone — which is why the Qur'an says the disbelievers will say on that Day, *"Had we but listened or reflected, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze"* (67:10): the herd of the world is broken on the Day, and each member of the herd stands as an individual. Second, the bonds fail because their object is misplaced: human beings arranged their mutual protections as though the ultimate danger were human — poverty, enemies, disgrace, death — and the Day reveals that the ultimate danger was always the divine accounting; the insurance policies of the world were written against the wrong perils.

**The One Avail That Remains: Mercy**

Yet the verse is not the Qur'an's final word on the Day's help, and its position between verse 40 and verse 42 makes the qualification immediate: the next verse will say, *"save him unto whom God has been merciful"* (44:42). The Qur'an's teaching is double: no *creature* will avail any creature, but the Creator's mercy remains the one avail that cannot fail — and the intercession that the Islamic tradition affirms (the intercession of the Prophet ﷺ on the Day of Resurrection, for which he says, "I am the master of the children of Adam on the Day of Resurrection" — al-Bukhārī and Muslim) is itself a mercy of God's granting, not a creature's independent power: the Prophet's intercession avails only those whom God permits it to avail, and it is exercised within the divine decree, not against it. The believer's preparation for the Day is therefore the reversal of the world's investment strategy: instead of accumulating the bonds that the Day will dissolve — the patrons, the alliances, the reciprocal favors — the believer is invited to invest in the only relationship that survives the Day: the bond with God, established in this world by faith, maintained by obedience, and sealed by mercy: *"The friends of God — no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve"* (10:62).

**The Social Ethics of the Verse: Bonds as Trusts, Not Saviors**

Finally, the verse carries a social ethic for the present life that is easily missed in its eschatological severity. The Qur'an does not teach that friendship, kinship, and alliance are worthless — the same revelation commands the maintenance of kinship ties (4:1), the solidarity of the believers (49:10), and the mutual help of the community (5:2); the Prophet ﷺ said, "The believer to the believer is like a building, each part of it strengthening the other" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī). What the verse dissolves is not the bonds but the *idolatry* of the bonds — the human tendency to treat social connection as ultimate security, to derive identity and salvation from the group, to do for the tribe what should be done for God and to fear for the tribe what should be feared from God. The believer is invited to hold his bonds as trusts: love the friend *for God*, serve the kin *as God's command*, build the community *as God's community* — so that when the Day of Division dissolves every bond that was made for the world, the bonds that were made for God are found transformed rather than destroyed: the friends of this world who loved one another in God meet in the Garden, and the *mawlā* who could not avail his *mawlā* on the Day finds that the love they practiced toward one another in the world has become, by God's mercy, the company of the blessed: *"Friends on that Day will be foes to one another — save the reverent"* (43:67).

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:42

> **save him unto whom God has been merciful. Truly He is the Mighty, the Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Single Exception That Changes Everything**

Verse 41's absolute statement — no friend will avail a friend — now receives its qualification: *illā man raḥima Allāh* — "save him unto whom God has been merciful." The exception clause is the hinge of the Qur'an's entire eschatology: the helplessness of the creatures on the Day of Division is total, but it is not the last word — because above the creatures stands the Creator, whose mercy is not a creature, does not fail, and cannot be exhausted. The verse teaches the precise structure of salvation in the Qur'anic scheme: no one is saved by his own merit alone — the merit is real but insufficient; no one is saved by another creature's help — the help is real but impotent on that Day; salvation is by the mercy of God, into which the believer's faith and deeds enter as the means appointed by the mercy itself. The classical theologians expressed the structure in the formula that runs through the tradition: *al-najāh bi-l-raḥmah* — deliverance is by mercy — and they grounded it in verses like this one and in the Prophet's ﷺ words: "None of you will enter the Garden by his deeds alone." They said, "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: "Not even me, unless God envelops me in His mercy — so be upright and draw near" (al-Bukhārī 5673 and Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah).

**The Two Names: Might and Mercy in One Breath**

The verse seals itself with the divine pair that has governed the whole sūrah: *innahu huwa al-ʿazīzu al-raḥīm* — "Truly He is the Mighty, the Merciful." The pairing is the Qur'an's most frequent conjunction of divine names, and its appearance here is exact: the Day of Division displays the divine might — the power that parts the sea, strikes with the terrible striking, and sorts the nations — and the divine mercy — the compassion that spares whom He wills; and the verse's claim is that both names belong to the same subject: the Might does not cancel the Mercy, and the Mercy does not weaken the Might. Ibn Kathīr and the commentators note the order: the Mighty is named first because the Day is the day of His power, and the Merciful immediately after because the only hope of the helpless on that day is that the Mighty One is also the Merciful One. The pairing answers the two distortions that human beings have always fallen into regarding God: the distortion that makes God all-merciful and therefore indifferent to justice — refuted by the Mighty who divides and punishes; and the distortion that makes God all-mighty and therefore indifferent to compassion — refuted by the Merciful who saves and spares. The God of the Qur'an is both, simultaneously and eternally, and the believer's relation to Him is calibrated by the same double register: fear that does not despair, and hope that does not presume.

**The Mercy That Precedes the Day**

The verse's phrase *man raḥima Allāh* — "him unto whom God has been merciful" — uses the past tense of the verb, and the commentators draw out the implication: the mercy that will save on the Day is not a new decision made at the judgment but the continuation of a mercy that began earlier — in this world, in the sending of the Book (v. 3 of this sūrah: the descent on the blessed night was itself an act of mercy), in the dispatch of the messengers (v. 5), in the guidance of the heart that responds — *"whomsoever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast to submission"* (6:125) — and in the repentance that the believer performs in the present life. The Day of Division merely ratifies the mercy that was accepted or refused in the world: the one who was merciful to himself by believing and obeying in the world finds the divine mercy awaiting him at the reckoning; the one who refused the mercy in the world — by refusing the message that carried it — finds on the Day that the exception clause was always addressed to him, and that he spent his life declining the only exception that could have saved him. The believer's present task is therefore the acceptance of the mercy where the mercy is offered: in the Book that is recited, in the repentance that is performed before the term expires, in the good deeds that are sent ahead — so that when the Day comes, the past tense of the verse (*has been merciful*) is true of him.

**The Intercession Within the Mercy**

The verse also provides the theological frame within which the Islamic doctrine of intercession (*shafāʿah*) is to be understood — a doctrine that the source's own context raises, since the Day when no friend avails a friend is the Day on which the Prophet's intercession is most needed and most celebrated in the tradition. The intercession of the Prophet ﷺ — for the hastening of the reckoning of his community, and for the admission of grave sinners among his community to the Garden — is affirmed by the authentic hadiths, most fully in the long hadith of intercession recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim; but the verse's exception clause fixes the boundaries of the doctrine: the intercession avails only *him unto whom God has been merciful* — the intercessor's plea is itself a gift of the divine mercy, granted to the Prophet ﷺ as an honor, and effective only within the divine will: *"And they intercede not, save for him with whom He is pleased"* (21:28). The believer's hope is therefore never placed in the intercession as an independent power that might override divine justice — that would be the polytheism of the mediators that the Qur'an refutes — but in the mercy of God, which appointed the intercession as one of its own instruments: the whole machinery of salvation — faith, deeds, repentance, intercession — is the machinery of *raḥmah*, and the verse reduces the believer's final state to its simplest formula: nothing avails but mercy, and mercy avails everything.

**Living Between the Mighty and the Merciful**

The verse's practical yield for the believer's daily life is the balance that the Qur'an calls *al-khawf wa-al-rajāʾ* — fear and hope — the two wings on which the believer flies between the two names of this verse. The Mighty keeps the believer from the presumption that sins are trivial — the Day of Division is real, the accounting is exact, and the might that parted the sea has not weakened. The Merciful keeps the believer from the despair that sins are unforgivable — the exception clause stands, the past tense of the mercy is still being written in the present, and the door of repentance remains open until the moment the term closes. The Prophet ﷺ described the balance when he said: "By Him in whose hand is my soul, if you did not sin, God would remove you and bring a people who sin and then seek forgiveness, and He would forgive them" (Muslim 2749, from Abū Hurayrah) — the sinner's return is not the exception to the divine order but one of its most beloved operations, the exercise of the mercy that this verse places at the center of the Day of Division. The believer who recites the verse is invited to examine his own heart for the balance: does the Mighty name me to carefulness? Does the Merciful name me to hope? For the Day will find each soul exactly where its balance has placed it — between presumption and despair, there is the path of the reverent, which the sūrah's closing movement now describes.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:43

> **Truly the tree of Zaqqūm**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns to the Two Ends**

With verse 43 the sūrah enters its final great movement, and the transition is abrupt by design: the mercy of verse 42 has named the exception — and now the two sides of the exception are displayed in full, beginning with the side of the excluded. *Inna shajarata al-zaqqūm* — "Truly the tree of Zaqqūm." The commentators note that the address of the verse is universal — there is no pronoun specifying "for the sinner" until the next verse — and the effect is suspense: the reciter hears the name of a tree, unfamiliar and foreboding, and must wait a breath to learn whose food it is. The name itself — *zaqqūm* — is one of the Qur'an's most striking inventions of language: the classical philologists note that it has the ring of an Arabic word without a clear root — an onomatopoeic strangeness suggesting something swallowed, gagged upon, choked on; the commentators add that it resembles the Arabic word for the bitterest of foods. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr record the tradition that Zaqqūm was the name of a vile and bitter tree of the Tihāmah region of Arabia — a tree so acrid that travelers avoided even its shade — and that the Qur'an took the known name of the most repulsive plant in the Arabs' experience and made it the name of the food of Hell: the familiar made terrifying, the earthly bitterness made eternal.

**The Food That Fits the Sinner**

The verse's placement introduces the Zaqqūm at exactly the point where the sūrah's argument requires it: the Day of Division has been announced (v. 40), the helplessness of all creatures on that Day has been stated (v. 41), and the single exception has been named (v. 42) — and now the division itself is displayed by its two menus. The Qur'anic picture of the Hereafter is consistently a picture of correspondence: as the people of Paradise receive fruits that befit their station, so the people of Hell receive food that befits theirs — and the Zaqqūm is the perfect anti-fruit: where the fruits of the Garden are sweet, nourishing, and freely given (v. 55 of this sūrah), the Zaqqūm is bitter, poisonous, and forced; where the Garden's fruit delights the palate, the Zaqqūm boils in the belly like molten lead (v. 45); where the believer eats in security and gratitude, the sinner eats in compulsion and despair. The symmetry is the sūrah's moral geometry made edible: the Hereafter does not invent new categories of experience; it eternalizes the categories that souls chose in this world — the soul that fed on forbidden pleasures in the world finds its food grown into the Zaqqūm; the soul that fed on obedience and gratitude finds its food grown into the fruit of the Garden.

**The Tree as the Heart's Harvest**

Some of the commentators — including al-Ālūsī in his reflection on this passage — read the Zaqqūm in a further register: as the image of what the soul itself has grown. Every human being plants a tree in his own heart during the life of this world — the tree of faith, watered by remembrance and obedience, or the tree of denial, watered by heedlessness and transgression — and the Hereafter, in this reading, does not impose a foreign punishment but serves each soul the fruit of its own planting: the Zaqqūm is the harvest of the heart that fed on doubts, resentments, and forbidden desires until they became its nature — the "tree" that grew inside the sinner all along, now made visible and edible for eternity. The Prophet ﷺ gestured at the same correspondence when he said, "The Fire is veiled by desires, and the Garden is veiled by hardships" (al-Bukhārī 6487 and Muslim 2823, from Abū Hurayrah): the choices that feel like pleasure in the world are the Fire's own fuel, and the choices that feel like hardship are the Garden's own planting — the Hereafter reveals which tree each soul was actually tending.

**The Tree That Was Planted in the World**

The verse's terror is therefore precisely calibrated to the believer's present life. The Zaqqūm is not a distant mythological plant; it is the name of a relationship — the relationship between the soul's worldly diet and its eternal food — and the verse's question to the reader is the question of diet: what am I feeding on? The Qur'an describes the worldly feeding of the heedless in terms that make the correspondence explicit: *"Those who consume usury shall not rise save as one rises whom Satan has struck with madness... God has permitted trade and forbidden usury"* (2:275) — the consumer of the forbidden rises in the resurrection as one convulsed, the body remembering the soul's diet; and the general law is the command: *"O mankind! Eat of that which is lawful and good upon the earth"* (2:168) — the lawfulness of the food being the first discipline of the body's training for the Hereafter. The believer who recites "the tree of Zaqqūm" is invited to audit his own hungers — the media consumed, the pleasures pursued, the gains acquired, the entertainments that fill the hours — and to ask which tree he is watering: for the sūrah's promise is that the tree of the Hereafter is not a new creation but a transplantation — the soul carries its garden with it, and the only question is which garden the soul has grown.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:44

> **is the food of the sinner,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Completed: The Sinner's Menu**

The suspended clause of verse 43 now lands: *ṭaʿāmu al-athīm* — "is the food of the sinner." The word *athīm* — from *-th-m*, to sin — is the Qur'an's term for the confirmed wrongdoer: not the one who errs and repents (that one is called *khaṭiʾ* or *mudhnib* and is promised mercy), but the one for whom sin has become a settled state, the persistent, deliberate transgressor whose wrongdoing is his identity rather than his lapse. The choice of *athīm* over the more common terms for disbeliever (*kāfir*, *mushrik*) is deliberate and instructive: the verse does not say the Zaqqūm is the food of the disbeliever alone, but of the *sinner* — broadening the category to include everyone whose life is defined by transgression, whether or not they have formally rejected the message: the one who knows the truth and disobeys it is as much an *athīm* as the one who denies it — and the food of the Zaqqūm is apportioned to the state, not merely to the label. The commentators note that the verse's pairing of the tree with the sinner completes the correspondence of the two menus: the *athīm* whose worldly delight was the violation of limits is served, in the Hereafter, a food whose only property is the violation of every limit of taste — the eternalization of transgression as consumption.

**The Grammar of Eternal Eating**

The verse's few words contain a precise eschatological picture that the following verses will develop: the people of Hell *eat* — the Zaqqūm is food (*ṭaʿām*), consumed, chewed, swallowed — and the eating never ends. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the futility of the Hell-dwellers' eating: *"Then for them shall be a boiling drink and a painful punishment, because they used to disbelieve"* (6:70); *"They will not taste therein any coolness nor any drink, save boiling liquid and pus — a fitting recompense"* (78:24–26); and of the Zaqqūm specifically: *"Is that better as a hospitality, or the tree of Zaqqūm? Truly We have made it a trial for the wrongdoers. It is a tree that comes forth from the root of Hellfire; its spathes are like the heads of satans; and truly they eat of it and fill their bellies with it; then upon it they shall have a drink of boiling liquid; then their return shall be unto Hellfire"* (37:62–68). The horror is not merely the bitterness of the food but the permanence of the hunger: the damned eat to fill bellies that never fill (the *fa-ʾimlāʾū minhā al-buṭūn* of 37:66 — they cram their bellies — an eating without satiation, a hunger that feeding only deepens), the eternal parody of the eternal satiety of the Garden.

**The Two Hunger Paradigms: Zaqqūm and the Garden's Fruit**

The sūrah's juxtaposition of the two foods — the Zaqqūm of the sinner (vv. 43–44) and the fruits that the reverent call for in security (v. 55) — encodes the Qur'an's deepest anthropology: human beings are defined by what they hunger for, and the Hereafter is the state in which every hunger is finally and eternally served its proper object. The sinner's hunger in this world was for the self — status, pleasure, possession, the endless consumption that the Qur'an calls the world's *matāʿ*, its passing goods — and the eternal form of self-hunger is the Zaqqūm: consumption without satisfaction, appetite without satiety, the self forever feeding on itself in the form of its own sins. The reverent hunger was for God — the remembrance, the obedience, the mercy — and the eternal form of that hunger is the Garden: the satisfaction that increases rather than ends, the fruit that is called for and given (v. 55), the peace of the soul that has found its object. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same anthropology in his prayer: "O God, give my soul its reverence (taqwā) and purify it; You are the best purifier of it; You are its Master and its Protector" (Muslim 2722, from Zayd ibn Arqam) — the soul's purity being the condition of its eternal satisfaction, and its impurity the condition of its eternal hunger.

**The Sinner's Identity in the Present Tense**

The verse's final service to the reader is diagnostic: it defines the *athīm* in a way that invites self-examination rather than self-congratulation. The classical commentators are careful to note that no one is born an *athīm* — the state is acquired by the accumulation of choices, and it is reversible until the moment the term closes: the Qur'an's own promise is that the door of return stands open through the whole of the present life — *"Say: 'O My servants who have transgressed against themselves! Despair not of the mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins'"* (39:53) — the address to the *musrifūn* (the prodigal, the same family of the *athīm*) assuring them that the mercy of verse 42 remains available while the world remains. The believer who recites this verse is therefore placed at a fork: the food of the sinner is named, and the name is the warning — the one who does not want the Zaqqūm must not become the *athīm*; and the one who recognizes in himself the beginnings of the *athīm* — the settled sins, the unrepented wrongs, the defiance grown familiar — is invited to perform the reversal while the reversal is possible: to return from the state of the sinner to the state of the servant, so that the menu that awaits him at the end of the sūrah's movement is not the tree of bitterness but the fruit of the Garden, called for in security, in the station of the reverent.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:45

> **like molten lead boiling in their bellies,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Food's Effect Described**

The verse now describes what the Zaqqūm does once swallowed: *ka-al-muhli yaghlī fī al-buṭūn* — "like molten lead boiling in their bellies." The word *muhl* is the Qur'an's term for the most terrible of liquids — the commentators gloss it as the dregs of oil, molten metal, the burning residue that forms when metal is smelted; al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr describe it as what remains when oil or metal is heated to its extreme — and the verse's simile is a physical impossibility made sensory: a food that enters the belly and there continues to boil, like molten lead, heating and seething within the body that must contain it. The Qur'an elsewhere uses the same image for the drink of the damned — *"And if they call for relief, they will be relieved with water like molten lead (al-muhl), which scalds their faces — evil the drink!"* (18:29) — and the present verse transfers the image from the throat to the belly: the Zaqqūm is not merely bitter to the taste but active in the gut, a burning mass that the sinner must swallow and carry, the food become furnace.

**The Qirāʾāt: Who Is Boiling?**

The verse's second word — *yaghlī*, "boiling" — carries a celebrated variant reading that the commentators — Ibn Juzayy and al-Zamakhsharī among them — preserve and expound. The standard reading makes the molten lead boil in the bellies: *yaghlī fī al-buṭūn*, the boiling of the food inside the body of the eater. A variant reading (*taghlī*) makes the tree of Zaqqūm the subject: the Zaqqūm itself boils in the bellies — *taghlī*, the tree's fruit boiling — an image in which the food's source shares in the food's torment. Either way the picture is of a digestion that is itself torture: ordinary food is transformed by the body into sustenance, the body assimilating what it eats; the Zaqqūm reverses the order — the body does not transform the food; the food attacks the body, the eater and the eaten locked in an eternal mutual destruction that neither can complete. The variant reading carries a further overtone noted by the commentators: the tree boils *in the bellies* because the sinner's own belly — the appetite that ruled his life — has become the furnace of his punishment; the organ of his worldly indulgence is the site of his eternal torment — the correspondence of the Hereafter operating with surgical precision.

**The Belly as the Seat of the Sinner's History**

The verse's choice of the belly as the site of the punishment invites a reflection that the Qur'an itself conducts elsewhere: the belly is, in the Qur'an's moral anatomy, the emblem of appetite — the seat of the consumption by which the heedless live: *"Those who disbelieve enjoy themselves and eat as the cattle eat, and the Fire will be their habitation"* (47:12). The sinner whose god was his belly (the Qur'an's phrase for such inversion is *"Have you seen him who takes his caprice as his god?"* — 25:43, and the Prophet's warning that the worst vessel the son of Adam fills is his belly — al-Tirmidhī 2380, from al-Miqdām ibn Maʿdīkarib) is punished in the very organ of his worship: the belly that demanded and consumed without limit in the world is filled, in the Hereafter, with the only food that can satisfy a limitless appetite — a food that burns instead of nourishing, that fills instead of satiating, that the eater must consume for eternity because his appetite has become his sentence. The Zaqqūm is thus the belly's own harvest: the appetite trained in the world to want everything and accept nothing is served, forever, a food that is everything it wanted — endless — and nothing it can use.

**The Trial of the Tree: Why the Zaqqūm Was Sent**

The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt adds a detail that illuminates the purpose of the Zaqqūm's description: *"Truly We have made it a trial (fitnah) for the wrongdoers"* (37:63). The tree is a trial — and the commentators explain that the trial operated already in this world: when the Qur'an first announced the Zaqqūm, the Quraysh mocked the very idea — Abū Jahl is reported to have said, in the accounts recorded by the commentators, "Muhammad promises you a tree in Hell from which fire eats — but fire consumes trees! How can a tree grow in the Fire?" — and the mockery was itself the trial's working: the derision of the Hereafter's realities exposed the mockers as people whose standard of possibility was the world's physics rather than the Creator's power — the same refusal of the unseen that the sūrah diagnosed in its opening movement (v. 9: they are playing in doubt). The Zaqqūm's description in this verse answers the mockery in advance: the tree that boils like molten lead in the bellies is not the tree of the world's gardens — it is a tree of the Fire, and its properties are the properties of the Fire's Lord, who is not bound by the botany of the world He created. The believer who recites the verse is reminded that the realities of the Hereafter exceed the categories of the world by definition — *"No soul knows what comfort is concealed from it"* (32:17) is said of the Garden's joys, and the same principle of unknowability governs the Fire's pains: both are described to the world in the world's images — fruit and fire, gardens and boiling — but both are vaster than the images, and the images are given only that the heart may choose while choice remains.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:46

> **like the boiling of boiling liquid.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Simile Completed: Boiling Upon Boiling**

The description of the Zaqqūm's effect closes with a second simile that intensifies rather than clarifies: *ka-ghalayi al-ḥamīm* — "like the boiling of boiling liquid." The word *ḥamīm* — from *ḥ-m-m*, the root of heat — is the Qur'an's term for the scalding liquid of Hell, the drink that has reached the maximum of heat; the commentators note that the root can also carry the sense of the most severe cold, so that *ḥamīm* denotes the extreme of temperature in either direction — the drink that is unbearable because it is at the utter limit of what a body can touch. The verse's construction is a simile of a simile: the molten lead boils in the bellies *like the boiling of the boiling liquid* — the Qur'an doubling its images because the reality exceeds each singly: the belly's torment is like lead boiling, and the boiling of that lead is like the boiling of the hottest thing conceivable — the simile reaching for a referent it cannot reach, because the damned experience what no image can carry. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note that the verse's piling of boiling upon boiling serves a precise function: to remove from the hearer the comfort of familiarity — the Zaqqūm is not "like" something one has felt (a bad meal, a stomach ache, a fever); it is like the boiling of the thing whose entire being is boiling — a torture whose essence is heat in motion, the eternal state of the liquid that never cools and the belly that never bursts.

**Ḥamīm Throughout the Qur'an: The Drink of the Damned**

The word *ḥamīm* recurs across the Qur'an's descriptions of Hell with a consistency that marks it as one of the central terms of the eschatological vocabulary: *"And those who disbelieve, for them shall be the fire of Hell; they shall not be done away with so as to die, nor shall its punishment be lightened for them. Thus do We recompense every ungrateful one. And therein they cry out, 'Our Lord! Bring us out and we shall do righteous deeds, not what we used to do!'"* (35:36–37, where the same passage continues with the warning that they were granted long life); *"He will be given to drink of a festering spring, which he will sip but will scarcely swallow, and death will come upon him from every side, yet he will not die — and beyond him is a harsh punishment"* (14:16–17); *"This — so let them taste it: boiling liquid and pus, and other torments of the like — a pairing"* (38:57–58); and the fullest catalogue of the damned's meal, in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah: *"Then eaters of the tree of Zaqqūm, filling their bellies therewith, then drinkers upon it of boiling liquid, drinking as the camel drinks"* (56:52–55). The Zaqqūm and the ḥamīm are the damned's complete diet: solid and liquid, the bitter tree and the boiling drink — and the present verse fuses them: the tree's fruit boils in the belly as the ḥamīm boils in the throat, the two torments interlocking so that the damned cannot eat without drinking and cannot drink without burning.

**The Body That Cannot Die: The Logic of Perpetual Torment**

The verse's insistence on boiling — an activity, a process, a continuous state — raises the question that every reader of the Qur'an's Hell-descriptions eventually asks: how can a body endure eternal boiling? The Qur'an's answer is given in its descriptions of the damned's indestructibility: *"They will cry out therein, 'Our Lord! Take us out! We shall do righteous deeds!'... It will be said: 'Did We not give you long life enough for him who reflected to reflect therein? And the warner came unto you? So taste! For the wrongdoers have no helper'"* (35:37); *"And those in the Fire will say to the keepers of Hell, 'Call upon your Lord to lighten for us a day of the punishment!'"* (40:49); and the Qur'an's categorical statement: *"Whenever their skins are consumed, We shall replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment"* (4:56). The bodies of the damned are sustained in a state that the world's physics cannot model — burned without being consumed, boiled without dissolving, alive to pain that would kill the living — because the punishment's purpose is not the destruction of the person (annihilation would be an end, and the Qur'an is explicit that for the damned there is no end) but the eternal experience of the separation from God that the sinner chose: *"They will not be done away with so as to die, nor will its punishment be lightened for them"* (35:36). The boiling that never stops is the outward form of the inner state that never ended: the soul that refused God in the world is the soul that can no longer be stilled — the ḥamīm's perpetual motion is the restlessness of the soul that has lost its rest.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Warning**

Yet even here, at the furthest reach of the sūrah's terror, the Qur'an's pedagogy remains what it has been from verse 3: the warning is mercy. The description of the ḥamīm is given in the world — where it can still function as a warning — and its function is precisely to make the choice real: the believer who recoils from the image is being given the recoil as a gift, the aversion that drives him to the refuge that the sūrah has already named: the mercy of God (v. 42), the station of the reverent (v. 51). The Prophet ﷺ taught that the description of the Fire in the Qur'an is itself a mercy when he told of the Fire's complaint, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah: the Fire said to its Lord, "Lord, parts of me consume other parts," so God gave it two breaths — one in winter and one in summer — "and this is the severity of the cold you feel and the severity of the heat you feel" — the world's extremes of temperature being the merciful samples of the reality the body cannot yet know, given so that the soul may choose the Garden while the choosing is free.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:47

> **"Seize him and thrust him into the midst of Hellfire!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Becomes an Order**

The sūrah's scene shifts with a jolt from the description of the food to the drama of the serving: *khudhūhu fa-ʿtillūhu ilā sawāʾi al-jaḥīm* — "Seize him and thrust him into the midst of Hellfire!" The speakers are not identified in the verse, and the commentators — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Juzayy — explain that the command is God's, addressed to the angels who guard Hell, whom the Qur'an elsewhere calls *al-zabāniyah*, the fierce keepers (96:18): *"We shall call the zabāniyah"* is the Qur'an's threat to the man who forbade the Prophet's prayer, and the keepers of the Fire are described as *"harsh, severe angels who do not disobey God in what He commands them"* (66:6). The command's terseness is part of its terror: there is no preamble, no charge read aloud, no deliberation — the record was completed in the world, the sentence was fixed at the Division, and the execution order is one imperative after another: *seize* — the arrest of the soul that evaded every arrest in the world, the summons that no power can decline; *thrust* — the verb *ʿatala* used in Arabic for the violent dragging of a captive, the rough handling of one who will not walk to his own punishment; *into the midst* — not the edge of the Fire, where the heat might be bearable, but *sawāʾ*, the level middle, the deepest heart of the blaze.

**The Sinner in the Dock: Who Is "Him"?**

The verse's pronoun — *khudhūhu*, "seize *him*" — points back through the whole of the sūrah's indictment to its subject: the *athīm*, the sinner of verse 44, whose food is the Zaqqūm — and through him, every figure the sūrah has drawn: the players in doubt (v. 9), the deniers of the resurrection (vv. 34–35), the taunters who demanded their fathers (v. 36), the people of Tubbaʿ who were destroyed for their guilt (v. 37). The singular pronoun with the universal application is the Qur'an's way of making the scene both specific and general: the sinner who is seized is every sinner, and the reader is invited — as the Qur'an always invites — to see himself in the dock: the command is issued to the angels, but the identity of the one seized is determined by the life lived in the world. The classical commentators note the rhetorical genius of the passage: the Qur'an does not name the seized one with a proper name (though some accounts relate that the scene was understood by some of the early Muslims to describe Abū Jahl, the arch-opponent of the Prophet who was killed at Badr, as the archetype of the seized sinner) — the anonymity makes every hearer ask the question the scene is designed to produce: if not he, then who? If he, then why not I?

**The Angels of the Execution and the Sovereignty of the Command**

The verse's implicit picture of the angels executing the divine command carries a theological lesson that the Qur'an develops across its descriptions of the Hereafter: the punishment of Hell is not the work of a God who personally tortures but the administration of a sovereign whose justice has appointed executors — the angels of punishment act *by the command of their Lord*, and their severity is the severity of obedience, not of malice: they are described in the Qur'an as those who "do not disobey God in what He commands them and do what they are commanded" (66:6). The scene thus preserves the Qur'an's careful balance: God's mercy is direct and personal — the exception clause of verse 42 is His own act; God's judgment is mediated through His appointed order — the seizure and the thrust are performed by the keepers whom He commands. The same structure appears in the parallel scene of the Day: *"And We shall drive the guilty unto Hell, thirsty"* (19:86); *"On the day when they are thrust into the Fire of Hell with a thrust"* (52:13). The believer's comfort in the scene's terror is the precision of the divine justice: the one seized is the one whose record was completed and whose sentence was fixed by the same mercy that fixed the exception — no soul is seized arbitrarily, and no soul that the mercy has marked is seized at all.

**The Midst of the Fire: The Geography of Despair**

The command's destination — *sawāʾ al-jaḥīm*, the midst of the Hellfire — is specified with a precision that the commentators unpack: the middle of the Fire is its most intense region, the place of maximum heat and minimum hope, where the damned are furthest from the possibility of relief. The Qur'an elsewhere records the damned's own request to be moved from the midst: *"Those in the Fire will say to the keepers of Hell: 'Call upon your Lord to lighten for us a day of the punishment!'"* (40:49) — the request for even the smallest lightening, a single day's reprieve, addressed to the keepers who cannot grant it because the command is the Lord's; and the Qur'an describes the damned's different depths: *"The hypocrites will be in the lowest depth of the Fire"* (4:145), the worst offenders in the worst regions, the geography of Hell corresponding to the geography of sin — the midst of the Fire for the athīm whose sin was the settled state of his life. The believer who recites the verse is invited to understand the correspondence as the Hereafter's justice: the soul that lived at the center of its own self — the self at the midst of every calculation, every pleasure, every plan — is placed, for eternity, at the center of the Fire: the self's centrality becomes its torment, and the midst that the sinner made his own in the world is the midst that he cannot escape in the Hereafter.

**The Seizure That the Believer Practices Now**

The verse's final lesson is the inversion that the Qur'an perpetually teaches: the seizure that the sinner cannot escape on the Day of Division is escaped by a voluntary seizure in the present life — the seizure of the self that the believer performs daily in repentance and prayer: the soul that seizes its own appetites in the world (the fasting that seizes the belly, the lowering of the gaze that seizes the eye, the restraint that seizes the tongue) is not seized by the keepers in the Hereafter; the soul that was never seized in the world — that let every appetite run unarrested — is the soul that the command of verse 47 arrests at last: *"Seize him."* The Prophet ﷺ described the voluntary seizure as the essence of the spiritual life: "Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed" (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws — the hadith of self-accounting is recorded by al-Tirmidhī with a weak chain but is cited continuously by the scholars of the heart) — the self-accounting being the believer's rehearsal of the Division: the audit performed in the world, in freedom and mercy, so that the audit of the Day finds the books already balanced, and the command that the sinner hears — seize him — is heard by the believer as the command that was never needed: the soul that seized itself in the world is the soul that the keepers pass by, on the way to the station of the reverent that the sūrah now prepares to describe.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:48

> **Then pour the punishment of boiling liquid upon his head."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Command: The Pouring**

The execution order continues with its second imperative: *thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihi min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīm* — "Then pour the punishment of boiling liquid upon his head." The verb *ṣubbū* — from *ṣ-b-b*, to pour — describes the deliberate emptying of a vessel: the boiling liquid is not administered by the cup but poured from above, and the target is specified with anatomical precision: *fawqa raʾsihi*, upon his head — the crown, the topmost point of the body, the seat of the face that the sinner raised in arrogance against God and His messenger. The Qur'an's descriptions of the damned's punishment are consistently anatomical in this way — the punishment meets the body at the precise sites of its sins: the eyes that looked with lust are confronted with the Fire's sights; the mouths that mocked are fed the Zaqqūm; the heads that refused to bow in prostration receive the pouring of the boiling liquid. The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Ḥajj makes the correspondence explicit: *"These two adversaries dispute concerning their Lord. As for those who disbelieve, garments of fire will be cut out for them, and boiling liquid will be poured over their heads, whereby that which is in their bellies is melted, and their skins as well"* (22:19–20) — the pour from the head melts the interior, the punishment entering through the very gate by which the sinner's thoughts and plans had entered: the head that devised the schemes against the truth is the head through which the truth's vengeance enters.

**The Head That Would Not Bow**

The verse's choice of the head as the site of the pouring carries a specific moral resonance that the commentators draw out in connection with the rest of the sūrah. The head is the seat of arrogance in the Qur'an's moral anatomy — the part of the body that rises highest, the part that the arrogant refuse to lower: the Qur'an's command to the believers is to prostrate — *sujūd*, the placing of the forehead on the ground — and the refusal of the prostration is the signature of the arrogant: the Qur'an says of the disbelievers in the Hereafter, *"On a day when shins shall be bared and they are called to prostrate, they will not be able — their eyes humbled, abasement covering them — for they were called to prostrate while they were sound"* (68:42–43). The head that was never lowered in worship is the head that receives the pouring: the boiling liquid descends upon the crown that would not descend in prostration — the inversion of the sujūd, the eternal posture of the head that refused its one legitimate lowering. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr connect the scene with the sūrah's own mention of the arrogant who "rose against God" (v. 19 of this sūrah — *lā taʿlū ʿalā Allāh*): the rising against God in the world is answered by the pouring from above God's command in the Hereafter; the height that the sinner claimed is given to him — the height of the target, the eminence of the one singled out for the pour — and the claim of height becomes the condition of the punishment's precision.

**The Drink That Becomes a Pour: The Two Forms of the Ḥamīm**

The verse's *min ʿadhābi al-ḥamīm* — "of the punishment of boiling liquid" — uses the word *ʿadhāb*, punishment, for the liquid itself, and the commentators note that only this verse and its parallel in Sūrah al-Ḥajj describe the ḥamīm as poured upon the head, while other verses describe it as drunk — *"drinkers upon it of boiling liquid"* (56:54); *"and given to drink of boiling liquid that severs their bowels"* (47:15); *"and if they call for relief, they will be relieved with water like molten lead that scalds their faces"* (18:29). The two forms are not contradictory but cumulative — the Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire are additive, each verse supplying a new angle on a reality too vast for a single image: the damned both drink the ḥamīm and are drenched by it; it enters through the mouth and through the crown; it sears the face and melts the belly — the totality of the body's surfaces and passages are covered by the punishment, so that no part of the sinner remains untouched by the element he served. Al-Ālūsī notes in this connection that the verse's specification — the *punishment* of the boiling liquid — implies that this is only one punishment among many: the pour upon the head is a single item in an inventory of torments that the sūrah elsewhere leaves uncounted — the point being that the Fire's reality exceeds even the Qur'an's own exhaustive descriptions: *"And for them besides this are torments of the like — a pairing"* (38:57–58) — the "besides" indicating that the catalogue is never complete.

**The Pour That the Believer Invites in the World**

The verse's final meaning for the believer is the inversion that the whole sūrah has been conducting: the pour of the boiling liquid upon the head in the Hereafter is escaped by the voluntary pouring in the world — the water of ablution that the believer pours upon his head before every prayer, the tears of repentance that the sinner pours from his eyes, the coolness of the prostration's forehead on the earth: the believer's daily practice rehearses the inverse of the verse — the head that is washed in the world's water in obedience is not the head that is scalded in the Hereafter's ḥamīm. The Prophet ﷺ described the ablution's eschatological effect in the hadith recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah: "When the Muslim performs the ablution and washes his face, the sins of his face depart with the water — to the last drop" — the washing of the limbs in the world being the pre-emptive reversal of the burning of the limbs in the Hereafter. The believer who recites the verse is thus invited to a precise act of imagination: to see, in the water that runs over his head in every wuḍūʾ, the mercy that extinguishes the pour — and to understand that the difference between the two pours, the world's water and the Hereafter's ḥamīm, is written entirely by the choices made between them: the obedience that washes, or the arrogance that waits for the pouring.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:49

> **"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mockery of the Mighty**

The scene reaches its climax in a sentence that the commentators describe as the most searing rebuke in the Qur'an: *dhuq innaka anta al-ʿazīzu al-karīm* — "Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble." The words are spoken — the commentators differ as to whether by God directly or by the keepers of Hell executing His command, with Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī recording both possibilities — and their content is the sinner's own self-image thrown back at him: the *ʿazīz*, the mighty one, whose power in the world had seemed absolute; the *karīm*, the noble one, whose honor and lineage had seemed unchallengeable. The imperative *dhuq* — "taste" — is the Qur'an's verb of punitive experience, used throughout the scripture's descriptions of the damned: *"Taste the touch of the Fire"* (3:181; and cf. 8:35; 22:22; 32:20; 34:42; 46:34) — and the "taste" is always the tasting of the thing whose reality the world had concealed: the might that could not prevent the seizure, the nobility that could not purchase a drop of coolness.

**The Irony That Cuts Both Ways**

The verse's irony is layered, and the commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī — unfold it with relish. The sinner had been called mighty and noble by his society — the titles of honor that the world confers on power and lineage; the verse addresses him by those very titles at the moment when they are shown to be empty: *you are* the mighty — mighty enough to be seized; *you are* the noble — noble enough to be poured upon. The titles that were his pride become his address of humiliation: the Fire does not strip him of his titles; it makes him taste their nullity. The early Muslims understood the verse to have been exemplified in Abū Jahl — ʿAmr ibn Hishām, the leader of the Quraysh's opposition, whose very name means "father of ignorance" and who was known among his people for his wealth and status; the accounts record that he once said, according to the traditions preserved by the commentators on this verse, that between the two mountains of Makkah there was none mightier or more protected than he — and the Qur'an's *"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble"* was understood by Ibn ʿAbbās and others to be precisely the divine answer to that boast, pronounced over his corpse at Badr, where the mightiest man of Makkah lay among the slain: the might that could not save its owner from the sword of the believers in this world was summoned, in the Hereafter, to taste its own nullity for eternity.

**The Exalted Image and the Exalted One**

The verse's rebuke carries a precise theological content about the nature of the sinner's sin. The sinner's fault was not merely the commission of acts but the maintenance of an image — the self-exaltation that the Qur'an diagnoses as the root of the denial of the truth: *"Thus does God seal the heart of every tyrant, haughty one"* (40:35); *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of wrongdoing and exaltation"* (27:14, of Pharaoh and his people denying the signs). The verse's address — *anta al-ʿazīz al-karīm* — is the mirror of the sinner's own self-estimate: the one who made himself the mighty and the noble in his own eyes is addressed as the mighty and the noble at the moment of his abasement — the eternalization of the image he chose: the soul that worshipped its own exaltation is given, forever, the exaltation as its punishment — the title that it can never live up to, the eminence that is only the height from which it falls and the target on which the ḥamīm is poured. The believer's discipline against the disease is the daily practice of the divine exaltation instead of the self's: the *takbīr* that opens every prayer — *Allāhu akbar*, God is greater — is the ritual demolition of the self's claim to the titles: each prayer rehearses the transfer of might and nobility from the creature to the Creator, so that the soul arrives at the Hereafter having already surrendered the titles that the Fire mocks — the soul that never claimed to be mighty and noble is not addressed by the verse's mockery; it is addressed by the verse that follows the scene: the address of mercy to the reverent.

**The Bookkeeping of Boasts**

There is a final register in the verse that the commentators note in their discussions of divine justice: the Qur'an's Hereafter conducts a precise bookkeeping of the world's speech — the boasts, the self-praises, the claims of might and nobility uttered in the world are preserved and returned to their speakers in the Hereafter with exact interest: *"On the day when their tongues and their hands and their feet bear witness against them as to what they used to do"* (24:24); *"And they will say, 'Had we but listened or reflected, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze'"* (67:10). The verse's mockery is the speech of the sinner returned: the world heard him say "I am mighty, I am noble" — the Hereafter says it back to him, in the only tone that the claim can bear once the might is broken and the nobility is ash: the tone of the verdict. The believer who recites the verse is invited to audit his own speech for the boasts — the claims of self-sufficiency, the invocations of his own power and honor, the quiet conviction that he is the exception — and to practice, against each boast, the Qur'an's counter-speech: the praise of the only Mighty, the only Noble — *"So glory be to Him in whose hand is the dominion of all things, and unto Him you shall be returned"* (36:83) — so that the titles that the sinner hears in mockery are heard by the believer, on that Day, as the titles of the One he praised, and the mockery that addresses the mighty and the noble passes over the head of the servant who never claimed to be either.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:50

> **This is that which you used to doubt."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict's Final Word**

The scene of the sinner's punishment closes with the sentence that converts the whole of the Fire's reality into evidence: *inna hādhā mā kuntum bihi tamtarūn* — "This is that which you used to doubt." The verse is spoken — the commentators again note the alternatives: the words of God or of the keepers, or of the believers as they watch from the Garden's edge — and its content is the most devastating possible: the Fire does not merely punish; it *demonstrates*. Every element of the scene — the seizure, the thrust, the pour, the taste — is the materialization of a proposition that the sinner had doubted in the world: the resurrection, the reckoning, the recompense, the reality of the warners' message. The verb *tamtarūn* — from *m-r-y*, to doubt, to be in uncertainty — is the same family as the *shakk* (doubt) of verse 9: the people who played in doubt are now shown the object of their doubt made flesh, fire, and boiling liquid — and the showing is the last word of the sentence: the doubt is not refuted by argument; it is refuted by experience, in the only forum in which refutation is final. The verse's demonstrative — *hādhā*, "this" — points at the totality of the scene: this Fire, this Zaqqūm, this boiling, this abasement — *this* is what you doubted; the doubt is over; the object of the doubt is now the environment of the doubter.

**The Doubt That Was Never Honest**

The verse's indictment cuts to the heart of the psychology of denial that the sūrah has been dissecting since verse 9: the doubt of the damned was never the honest uncertainty of the seeker — it was the convenient skepticism of the one who did not want the message to be true. The Qur'an elsewhere exposes the same structure: *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of wrongdoing and exaltation"* (27:14); *"Rather, they deny the truth when it comes to them, so they are in a confused affair"* (50:5). The doubters of the resurrection doubted with their tongues what their consciences suspected with their hearts — the suspicion that the prophets were right, that the accounting was real, that the life of heedlessness was a loan against an unknown future; and the verse's *tamtarūn* — you used to doubt — preserves the past tense with precision: the doubt was an activity of the world, a posture maintained by effort, a choice repeated against the evidence of conscience and creation — and the Hereafter presents the bill for the choice: the doubt that was chosen as a stance in the world becomes, in the Fire, the truth that the doubter can no longer doubt and can never escape.

**The Trial Completed: Evidence, Verdict, Sentence**

The verse completes the juridical structure that the whole sūrah has been constructing, and the commentators organize the scene's elements accordingly: the evidence was presented in the world — the clear Book (v. 2), the clear messenger (v. 13), the manifest smoke (v. 10), the signs given to Israel (v. 33), the destroyed nations (v. 37), the purposefulness of creation (vv. 38–39); the verdict was announced at the Division (v. 40) — the separation of the doubter from every bond (v. 41) and from the mercy that alone saves (v. 42); and the sentence is now executed — the seizure (v. 47), the pour (v. 48), the taste (v. 49) — and sealed with the identification of the sentence with its ground: *this is what you used to doubt*. The verse thus closes the loop of the sūrah's argument: the deniers demanded that the resurrection be brought to them (v. 36: "Bring us our fathers") — and here, at last, it has been brought: the resurrection, the reckoning, and the recompense stand before them in the form of the Fire — the demand of the taunt answered by the reality of the sentence: you asked to see it; now see it; the seeing is the punishment.

**The Believer's Reading of the Verse**

For the believer, the verse functions as the sūrah's final warning and its final consolation in one. The warning: the doubt that is played with in the world (v. 9) has an appointment with its own object — the soul that refuses to settle the question of the Hereafter will have the Hereafter settle it for the soul, in the only mode that leaves no remainder. The consolation: the believer who settles the question in the world — who chooses certainty over doubt, response over play — will never hear the verse's address; the *"This is that which you used to doubt"* is spoken only to those who doubted, and the believer's whole life is the practice of not doubting: the daily prayers that rehearse the meeting, the charity that sends ahead the provision, the repentance that clears the account before the audit — the believer lives so that the demonstrative of the verse can never point at him. And there is a further consolation hidden in the verse's structure that the commentators al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr draw out: the verse proves that the punishments of the Hereafter are *known* — the damned recognize the Zaqqūm, the ḥamīm, and the sentence as the things they doubted — and recognition implies the preservation of identity: the soul that is punished is the same soul that doubted, remembered and aware, its memory intact — the memory that will be its torment and its witness, as the believer's deeds will be his witness and his light: the two ends of the sūrah's division, the Fire of the doubters and the Garden of the reverent, both entered with the full selfhood that the world was given to form — *"This is that which you used to doubt"* for the one; and for the other, the address that the sūrah now turns to speak: the station of the reverent, the gardens and the springs, where doubt is ended by mercy rather than by fire.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:51

> **Truly the reverent are in a station secure,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Turn of the Sūrah**

With verse 51 the sūrah executes its final and most decisive turn: *inna al-muttaqīna fī maqāmin amīn* — "Truly the reverent are in a station secure." The particle *inna* with its emphasis matches the *inna* of verse 43 (the Zaqqūm) and verse 40 (the Day of Division): the sūrah's three great assertions are locked together — the Day of Division is their tryst; the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinner; the reverent are in a station secure. The word *muttaqūn* — the reverent — is the Qur'an's master-term for the believers who have made *taqwā* their character: the consciousness of God that governs conduct, the alertness of the soul that walks carefully through a dangerous world because it knows it is observed and accountable; the term's root *w-q-y* means to protect oneself — the muttaqī is the one who has protected himself from the Fire by obedience and from the loss of God by remembrance. The commentators from Ibn ʿAbbās onward defined *taqwā* in the practical terms the Prophet's ﷺ companions used: Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said that taqwā is that God be obeyed and not disobeyed, remembered and not forgotten, thanked and not denied — the whole of the reverent life compressed into the three relations of the servant to his Lord.

**The Station Secure: What the Reverent Have Been Given**

The verse's gift to the reverent is *maqām amīn* — a station secure. The commentators — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī — read the phrase in two grammatical registers: *maqām* (with the first vowel open, the place where one stands) or *muqām* (with the vowel folded, the place where one abides); the rendering "station secure" covers both — the place of standing that is also the place of staying, the position that the reverent occupy at the reckoning and the residence that they never leave: a *muqām* is a dwelling, and the reverent's dwelling is *amīn* — secure. The commentators enumerate the contents of the security: security from death, for the reverent taste no death after the first (v. 56 of this sūrah); security from Satan, whose whispers can no longer reach the soul that has passed beyond his jurisdiction; security from fear and grief, the two afflictions that the Qur'an promises are lifted from the believers: *"Truly the friends of God — no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve"* (10:62); security from the terror of the reckoning, for their book is given in the right hand (69:19); security from the envy of the wicked and the harm of the envious; and security from the very possibility of loss — the station from which nothing can be taken, the dwelling that no decree can revoke. Ibn Kathīr summarizes: the reverent are secure from all that the sinners suffer — the terror, the seizure, the thirst, the boiling — and from the fear of leaving their station, which is the one insecurity that even earthly happiness cannot escape: the earthly secure are never secure from the end of their security, and the Hereafter's secure are secure from ending altogether.

**The Contrast That Defines Both Parties**

The verse's placement makes it the positive pole of the sūrah's entire system of contrasts, and the reader who has followed the sūrah from its beginning feels the symmetry resolving: the gardens and springs that the Egyptians left behind (vv. 25–27) are answered by the gardens and springs of the reverent from which they will never be expelled (v. 52); the food of the sinner, the Zaqqūm boiling in the belly (vv. 43–46), is answered by the fruits that the reverent call for in security (v. 55); the garments of humiliation that the seized sinner wears into the Fire are answered by the silk and brocade of the reverent (v. 53); the first death that the deniers claimed was their only end (v. 35) is answered by the reverent's immunity from death after the first (v. 56); the doubt that was the sinners' element (vv. 9, 50) is answered by the certainty that is the reverent's station; and the *ʿadhāb* — punishment — that the sinners taste is answered by the *faḍl* — bounty — that the reverent receive (v. 57). The sūrah's two movements are two portraits of one division: the sinner's eternal experience is the eternalization of his worldly choices, and the reverent's eternal experience is the eternalization of theirs — the *maqām amīn* being the place that the reverent's own reverence prepared for them: *"And the Garden will be brought near to the reverent, not far away: this is what you were promised — for every penitent, watchful one"* (50:31–32).

**The Reverence That Buys the Station: Taqwā in the Present**

The verse's present tense — the reverent *are* in a station secure — carries a quiet eschatological claim that the commentators articulate: the station is, in a real sense, already occupied. The reverent's security begins in this world — the Qur'an's promise to the believers is that taqwā secures them here as well: *"And whosoever reveres God, He will make a way out for him and provide for him from where he does not reckon"* (65:2–3); *"Whosoever reveres God, He will make his affair ease"* (65:4) — the *maqām amīn* of the Hereafter is the completion of the security that taqwā established in the world: the believer who walks carefully with God in the world finds that the carefulness has built him a dwelling; the reverence is not the price of the station but its foundation — the station is where the reverence has been leading all along, and the reverent have, in a sense, been standing in it since the day they first turned: the prayers they performed in the world were rehearsals of the station, and the station itself is the prayer made eternal: the standing (*maqām*) of the servant before the Lord, freed forever from the interruption of the *salām* that closes every prayer — the peace that ends the worship of the world becoming the peace that never ends.

**The Practical Geography of Security**

The verse's final lesson is the inversion of the world's geography of security that the sūrah has been conducting throughout: the world's secure are the mighty and the noble — the people of Pharaoh in their palaces (vv. 25–28), the people of Tubbaʿ in their kingdoms (v. 37), the mockers in their assemblies — and the world's insecure are the reverent: the believers of Makkah were a persecuted minority when this sūrah was revealed, without power, without status, threatened by the very people the sūrah describes as secure. The verse reverses the map: the true security is the station of the reverent, and the palaces of the world are the anterooms of the Fire. The modern believer who feels the insecurity of faith — the marginalization, the mockery, the worldly disadvantage of obedience — is invited by this verse to read his own position by the sūrah's geography: the station that the world cannot see is the station that the world cannot touch; the security that the world confers is the security that the Day of Division dissolves; and the believer's task is not the conquest of the world's security but the maintenance of the reverence that builds the secure station — the daily walk of taqwā that the sūrah has been teaching since its first movement, now revealed in its final movement as the walk that ends in the dwelling: *maqām amīn* — the station secure, from which nothing can remove the one who has arrived.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:52

> **amid gardens and springs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory of the Station**

The secure station is now furnished: *fī jannātin wa-ʿuyūn* — "amid gardens and springs." The two words are the sūrah's most deliberate echo: the gardens and springs of verse 25 were the wealth of Pharaoh's people, left behind in a single morning; the gardens and springs of this verse are the wealth of the reverent, entered and never left. The Arabic of the two verses is nearly identical — *jannātin wa-ʿuyūn* in both — and the repetition is the sūrah's way of forcing the comparison: the same words, two owners, two tenures. The commentators note that the Qur'an's description of the Garden is always in the language of the world's best things — gardens, rivers, fruits, couches, companions — because the human imagination can only be addressed in its own vocabulary: the reality of the Garden exceeds the images (32:17: *"No soul knows what comfort is concealed for it"*), and the images are given as approximations, the way a parent describes a journey to a child in terms of the child's own experience — the description is true as far as it goes, and the going further is the surprise of the arrival.

**The Garden in the Qur'an's Own Description**

The two words of this verse open onto the Qur'an's fullest portraits of Paradise, and the commentators — al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr especially — gather the descriptions that the believer should hold together when reciting the verse. The *jannāt* — gardens — of the Hereafter are the gardens that the Qur'an elsewhere describes as having rivers running beneath them: *"And give glad tidings to those who believe and perform righteous deeds that for them are Gardens with rivers running beneath them"* (2:25), where the same verse specifies the Garden's permanent freshness: *"whenever they are provided with fruits therefrom as provision, they will say, 'This is what we were provided before,' and they will be given things in likeness"* — the fruits of the Garden resembling the fruits of the world but exceeding them, so that the believer's worldly experience of goodness is always an echo of the original that awaits him. The *ʿuyūn* — springs — are the water sources of the Garden, which the Qur'an describes as flowing at the command of its inhabitants: the Garden's water is not the standing water of cisterns but the running water of springs — *"therein is a running spring"* (88:12) — and the righteous are promised drink from its sealed springs: *"Truly the pious will drink of a cup whose mixture is of camphor — a spring from which the servants of God drink, making it flow abundantly"* (76:5–6); *"and a spring therein called Salsabīl"* (76:18). The gardens and springs of the verse are thus the complete environment of delight: the shade and fruit of the garden, the sound and coolness of the water — the two elements that the desert-dwellers of the Qur'an's first audience prized above all wealth, and that the Qur'an universalizes as the language of every human longing for rest.

**The Garden as the Reversal of the Egyptians' Loss**

The verse's echo of verse 25 is the sūrah's final verdict on the world's economy, and the commentators draw the moral with complete clarity: the gardens and springs that the world's mighty accumulate are held by a tenure that the world cannot secure — the tenure of a morning; the gardens and springs of the reverent are held by a tenure that nothing can revoke — the tenure of the divine promise. The Egyptians' gardens were left behind because their owners had made them their gods: the gardens were the point of their existence, and the point of their existence died with them; the reverent's gardens are entered because their owners never made them the point — the reverent's point was God, and the gardens are the gift of the One who was their point: the difference between idolatry and worship is the difference between the two tenures. The sūrah's placement of the echo — the gardens of the drowned at verses 25–27, the gardens of the reverent at verses 51–52 — frames the entire Pharaoh narrative as a lesson in the ownership of beauty: the beauty that is owned for itself is lost with the owner; the beauty that is received as a gift from God is kept by the Giver for the receiver: *"And whatever is with you passes away, and whatever is with God endures"* (16:96).

**The Springs of the Heart: The Garden's Inward Beginning**

The classical commentators and the spiritual writers of the tradition found in the verse's pairing of gardens and springs an image of the believer's own interior: the Garden's springs correspond, in the soul, to the springs of the heart — the sources of life that flow within the believer: the spring of remembrance (*dhikr*) that waters the heart's garden; the spring of tears that softens the soil of repentance; the spring of gratitude that keeps the garden green through the droughts of trial. The Qur'an describes the world's life in the same imagery: the heart is watered by the remembrance of God — *"Is he whose breast God has expanded to submission, so that he is upon a light from his Lord, [like the hard-hearted]?"* (39:22) — and the believer's present life is the cultivation of the garden that the Hereafter will transplant: the prayers, the fasting, the charity, the patience are the irrigation of the soul's garden, and the verse's promise is that the garden cultivated in the world — in secret, in the small hours, in the unnoticed acts of obedience — is the garden that will be revealed, transplanted and perfected, in the station secure: *"The parable of the Garden that the reverent have been promised: rivers flow beneath it, its food is everlasting, and its shade"* (13:35). The believer who recites the verse is invited to examine his own inner garden: what am I watering — the springs of remembrance or the cisterns of heedlessness? For the verse's promise is not that the negligent will be given the gardens of the reverent, but that the reverent — those whose hearts were gardens in the world — will find their gardens again, made eternal, in the station that this verse describes.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:53

> **wearing fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Garments of the Station**

The description of the reverent's state now turns to their dress: *yallabasūna min sundusin wa-istabraq* — "wearing fine silk and rich brocade." The word *sundus* is the Qur'an's term for the finest silk — the thin, smooth silk of the highest quality; *istabraq* — from the same root as the English "brocade" via the Greek — is the thick silk brocaded with gold, the heaviest and most luxurious of the weaver's arts; the two together span the whole register of silk, from the delicate to the sumptuous, so that no grade of the world's most precious fabric is missing from the reverent's wardrobe. The Qur'an uses the pair elsewhere in its descriptions of the Garden's inhabitants: *"They will be adorned with bracelets of gold and will wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining therein upon couches"* (18:31); *"Upon them will be garments of green silk and rich brocade, and they will be adorned with bracelets of silver"* (76:21). The garments of the Hereafter are, in the Qur'an's consistent picture, the outer expression of the soul's inner state: the believer whose life in the world was clothed in reverence is clothed, in the Hereafter, in the fabric that reverence weaves — the garment that the Qur'an calls the *libās al-taqwā*, the raiment of reverence: *"O Children of Adam! We have sent down unto you a garment to cover your nakedness, and feathers — and the garment of reverence — that is best"* (7:26). The silks of the Garden are the taqwā of the world made visible: the unseen clothing of the soul's modesty and obedience, now seen by all.

**The Modesty That Becomes Silk**

The verse's echo of the garment imagery of 7:26 carries a precise moral content that the commentators develop: the reverent are those who clothed themselves in the world — who covered what God commanded to be covered, who adorned themselves with the modesty that the Qur'an makes the defining dress of the believer — and the Hereafter rewards the covering with covering: the souls that guarded their nakedness in the world are dressed in the finest fabrics of eternity, while the exposure that the sinners practiced — the flaunting that the Qur'an describes as the dress of those who do not believe: *"O Prophet! Say to thy wives and thy daughters and the women of the believers to draw their wraps over themselves"* (33:59) is the command whose observance is the world's silk — is answered by its opposite: the sinners' exposure is eternal (the Fire strips and burns), and the reverent's covering is eternal (the silk that never wears, the brocade that never fades). The Prophet ﷺ connected the world's modesty with the Hereafter's reward in his saying recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn: "Modesty brings nothing but good" (*al-ḥayāʾ lā yaʾtī illā bi-khayr*) — the believer's shyness of disobedience in the world being the seed of the silk of the Garden.

**Facing One Another: The Society of the Station**

The verse's final clause — *mutaqābilīn* — "facing one another" — describes the arrangement of the reverent in the Garden, and the commentators unfold its social meaning: the inhabitants of the Garden sit facing one another in mutual presence — no one behind another, no one excluded from the company, no averted faces: the posture of perfect equality and perfect intimacy. The Qur'an repeats the description in its portraits of the blessed: *"We shall strip away whatever rancor is in their breasts — as brothers, upon couches, facing one another"* (15:47); *"upon couches, facing one another"* (56:16); and the deeper meaning is spelled out in the stripping away of rancor: the society of the Garden is possible because the world's resentments have been removed — the envy, the grudges, the rivalries that made human beings unable to face one another in the world are dissolved by the divine mercy, and the facing of the Garden is the facing of souls from whom all cause of aversion has been taken: *"And We shall strip away whatever rancor is in their breasts"* (7:43; 15:47). The verse thus paints the social perfection that the world's societies approximate and never reach: the community of the Garden is the community of the healed — every soul able to meet every other soul without flinching, because nothing hidden remains between them.

**The Two Silks: The World's and the Hereafter's**

The verse's garments invite the final comparison that the sūrah has been preparing since its inventory of Egypt's wealth: the Egyptians wore the fine fabrics of their age (the commentators note that Egypt was famed for its linen and its luxury textiles) and left them behind in a morning (vv. 25–28); the reverent wear the silk and brocade that no morning can confiscate. The world's silk is the emblem of the status system — the garments by which human beings rank one another, the labels and the brands by which wealth announces itself; the Hereafter's silk is the emblem of the status that only God confers — the rank of reverence, which the world cannot see and the Day reveals: *"The most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you"* (49:13). The believer who wears the world's fabrics is invited by the verse to wear them as the Qur'an wears them — as neutral covering, neither despised nor worshipped, the body's clothing held lightly while the soul's clothing is tended: the taqwā that is the only garment that the grave does not strip, the only fabric that the Fire cannot burn, and the only dress that the Garden's wardrobe perfects rather than replaces. The verse's promise is that the believer who clothes his soul in reverence in the world — in the modesty, the honesty, the hidden good deeds that are the soul's raiment — will find himself, in the station secure, wearing the silk that the sūrah describes: the outward and visible sign of the inward and invisible grace that was his all along.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:54

> **Thus it is. And We shall wed them to wide-eyed maidens.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seal of the Description**

The portrait of the reverent reaches its summit: *kadhālika wa-zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin ʿīn* — "Thus it is. And We shall wed them to wide-eyed maidens." The *kadhālika* — "thus it is" — is the same word that sealed the Egyptians' loss in verse 28 (*kadhālika*, "thus it was"): there, the word closed the inventory of confiscated wealth; here, it opens the inventory of conferred bliss — as if to say: as for the gardens and the springs and the silk, *thus it is* — this is the state of the reverent, and beyond this state there is more. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the *kadhālika* here functions as an expression of wonder and of the incomparability of what follows — the like of this reward is beyond description — while al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī read it as "likewise": just as We admitted them to the Garden and dressed them in its silks, likewise We have honored them with the companions of the Garden. The verb *zawwajnā* — from *z-w-j*, the root of pairing and marriage — is the Qur'an's own word for the institution that joins two into one: the Garden's bliss includes the restoration and perfection of the deepest human bond — the union of the spouses — and the verb's subject is God: *We* shall wed them: the marriages of the Hereafter are contracted by the divine hand, the weddings of the Garden performed by the Lord of the worlds.

**The Wide-Eyed Maidens: What the Phrase Means**

The phrase *ḥūr ʿīn* — rendered "wide-eyed maidens" — is one of the Qur'an's most discussed descriptions, and the commentators are careful to explain it in its own terms. The word *ḥūr* (plural of *ḥawrāʾ*) describes, in the classical Arabic of beauty, the eye of perfect contrast — the intense black of the pupil against the brilliant white of the sclera — which the Arabs considered the height of the eye's beauty; *ʿīn* (plural of *ʿaynāʾ*) intensifies the same image — the wide, full, lustrous eye. The phrase thus paints, in the physical vocabulary of its first hearers, a beauty whose center is the eye — and al-Rāzī observes, in his commentary on the parallel verse of Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah, that the choice of the eye as the seat of the description is not accidental: the eye is the mirror of the soul, and the *ḥūr ʿīn* are described by their eyes because their beauty is a beauty of the soul made visible. The commentators record the traditions that describe their creation — reports transmitted in the collections that they are created of musk, camphor, and saffron, and that their creation is from the glorifications of the angels — while the majority of the commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, affirm that the *ḥūr ʿīn* are a distinct creation of the Garden prepared for the blessed, and that the righteous women of this world are of a higher station than they, as the reports indicate and as the commentators al-Ālūsī and others state. A minority reading, reported from Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and preserved by al-Ṭabarī, understands the *ḥūr ʿīn* as the righteous women of this world themselves — the believers' own wives, transformed and perfected — a reading that reminds the reader that the Qur'an's descriptions are signs whose full reality is known only to God: *"No soul knows what comfort is concealed for it"* (32:17).

**The Marriage of the Hereafter: The Bond Perfected**

The verse's placement of the marriage at the summit of the Garden's blessings is itself a statement about the human person that the commentators unfold: the Garden is not a solitary paradise — the bliss of the reverent includes the presence of companions, and the deepest form of companionship that the Qur'an can name is the marriage bond. The Qur'an describes the Garden's inhabitants as accompanied: *"Enter the Garden, you and your spouses, made happy"* (43:70); *"Gardens of Eden, which they enter with the righteous among their fathers, their spouses, and their offspring"* (13:23); and the believers' prayer in the world is precisely for the perfection of the family in the Hereafter: *"Our Lord! Grant us from our spouses and our offspring the comfort of eyes, and make us imams of the reverent"* (25:74). The marriage of the Garden heals the wounds of the world's marriages — the separations of death, the failures of love, the disappointments of intimacy — by reuniting the believing spouses in a state from which every cause of grief has been removed: *"We shall strip away whatever rancor is in their breasts, as brothers upon couches, facing one another"* (15:47) — the marriages of the world that were built on faith are not dissolved by death but perfected by it: the love that was practiced for God in the world is the love that God honors with the wedding of the Garden.

**The Eye of the Soul: What the Believer Is Taught to Desire**

The verse's concentration on the eye carries the sūrah's final moral lesson, and it is the lesson of the whole of the Qur'an's ethics of the gaze. The world's eye is trained by the culture of display — the looking that the Qur'an commands the believers to lower (24:30–31) because the undisciplined gaze consumes the soul's purity and feeds the Fire: the Prophet ﷺ said, "The eye commits fornication, and its fornication is the look" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah). The believer's discipline of the gaze in the world — the lowering of the eye from what God has forbidden — is precisely the training that makes the eye of the Hereafter capable of its reward: the soul that did not feast its eyes on the forbidden in the world is given, in the Garden, the vision that the verse describes — the beauty that is lawful, the looking that is pure, the eye finally and forever satisfied: the *ḥūr ʿīn* of the verse being, in the deepest reading, the answer to every look that the believer lowered in obedience: the gaze that was withheld from the world's display for God's sake is the gaze that God rewards with the Garden's vision. And beyond all the descriptions, the believer's deepest longing remains the vision that the Qur'an itself places beyond the Garden's imagery — the vision of the Lord that the believers are promised as the greatest of the Garden's joys (75:22–23: *"Faces that day will be radiant, looking upon their Lord"*), the seeing after which every other seeing is preface — the *ḥūr ʿīn* of the verse and all the Garden's beauty being, for the soul that knows its Lord, the antechamber of the Face.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:55

> **Therein they call for every fruit, secure.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Service of the Garden**

The portrait of the reverent continues with the Garden's provision: *yadʿūna fīhā bi-kulli fākihatin āminīn* — "Therein they call for every fruit, secure." The verb *yadʿūna* — they call — is the key of the verse: the inhabitants of the Garden do not wait for the fruit to be brought; they call for it, and the calling is the receiving: the fruit arrives at the call, the service of the Garden responding to the desire of its inhabitants with an immediacy that the world's service never achieves. The Qur'an describes the same economy of desire throughout its portraits of Paradise: *"They will have therein whatsoever they call for"* (36:57); *"Therein you will have whatsoever your souls desire, and therein you will have whatsoever you call for — as a hospitality from the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (41:31–32); *"They will have therein whatsoever they will, abiding therein"* (25:16); *"For them therein is whatsoever they will, and with Us there is yet more"* (50:35). The world's economy is the economy of scarcity and delay — desire outruns provision, the hand is empty when the heart is fullest; the Garden's economy is the economy of perfect correspondence — desire and provision meet in the instant, the soul's call and the Garden's answer occurring as one act: the state that the theologians describe as the creature's final harmony — the will that wants only what is given, and the giving that answers every wanting.

**Every Fruit: The Totality of the Good**

The verse's *kulli fākihatin* — every fruit — is a claim of totality that the commentators unfold in two directions. Horizontally, the Garden contains every fruit that the world's orchards ever bore and every fruit that no world ever imagined: the Qur'an's lists — the dates and pomegranates of the world's best gardens (55:68), the fruits that resemble the world's fruits but exceed them (2:25: *"whenever they are provided with fruits therefrom, they say, 'This is what we were provided before' — and they will be given things in likeness"*) — are samples of the Garden's infinite variety: *"therein are fruits, and date palms and pomegranates"* (55:68), *"and fruit such as they choose, and flesh of fowl such as they desire"* (56:20–21). Vertically, the fruit of the Garden is the fruit of the soul's own planting — the good deeds of the world returning as the harvest of the Hereafter: *"Whosoever does a mote's weight of good will see it"* (99:7–8); and the fruit that the soul calls for in the Garden is, in this register, the fruit of the very acts of worship, charity, and patience that the soul performed in the world — the deeds returning to their doer as fruit, ripe and perfected. The security is the knowledge that the fruit will never be cut off: unlike the world's fruit, which ends with the season, the Garden's fruit is *"everlasting, and its shade"* (13:35): the call is always answered, the season never turns.

**Secure: The Word That Governs the Whole Verse**

The verse's final word — *āminīn*, secure — is the clause that distinguishes the Garden's provision from every earthly abundance, and the commentators make it the pivot of the verse: the inhabitants call for the fruit *secure* — secure that the call will be answered, secure that the answering will not end, secure from the very anxieties that attend earthly eating: the fear of shortage, the fear of loss, the fear of the fruit's end, the fear that the enjoyment will be interrupted by death, disease, or the envy of others. The Qur'an elsewhere contrasts the Garden's security with the world's insecurity in the very terms of this sūrah: the sinners' desire is frustrated — *"a barrier is set between them and that which they desire"* (34:54) — while the reverent's desire is answered and secured: *"Therein they call for every fruit, secure."* The world's eating is never fully secure — the eater knows, even in the best meal, that the meal ends, the body declines, and the provider may fail; the Garden's eating is the first eating in the history of the human creature that carries no remainder of anxiety — the first meal since the Garden of Adam that is not shadowed by the tree's prohibition and the expulsion: the *āminīn* of the verse is the restoration of the security that humanity lost at the beginning: *"We said, 'O Adam, dwell in the Garden, you and your spouse...' Then Satan caused them to slip"* (2:35–36) — and the reverent's secure calling is the final reversal of that slip: the fruit that Adam reached for in doubt and was expelled for, the reverent call for in certainty and are kept for.

**The Hunger of the World and the Fruit of the Hereafter**

The verse's final lesson for the believer's present life is the discipline of desire that the Qur'an calls the training of the *nafs*: the world trains the soul to want without calling — to grasp, to hoard, to consume in anxiety — and the believer is invited to practice, in the world, the posture of the Garden: the turning of desire into supplication, the replacement of grasping with calling: the believer who wants is taught to call — to pray — and the Qur'an's promise is that the calling of the world is the rehearsal of the calling of the Garden: *"Call upon Me; I will answer you"* (40:60). The prayers of the world — the outstretched hands, the requests made in the night — are the soul's apprenticeship in the economy of the Garden: the soul that learns in the world that its provision comes by calling upon God, not by grasping at the world, is the soul prepared for the Garden where the calling is answered instantly and forever: the hunger that drove the believer to prayer in the world becomes, in the Garden, the hunger that is the very mechanism of bliss — the desire that is always answered and never exhausted, the calling that is always heard and never refused: *"Therein they call for every fruit, secure"* — the verse that the believer is invited to make true in advance, by the life that turns every worldly want into a prayer, and every prayer into a foretaste of the Garden's answering.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:56

> **They taste not death therein, save the first death. And He will shield them from the punishment of Hellfire—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Death That Is Cancelled**

The portrait of the reverent now reaches the deepest of its gifts: *lā yadhuqūna fīhā al-mawta illā al-mawtata al-ūlā* — "They taste not death therein, save the first death." The verse is the Qur'an's most precise statement of the believer's relation to death: the death that every soul tastes in this world — the *mawtat al-ūlā*, the first death, the one the deniers of verse 35 claimed was their only end — is, for the reverent, the last death: they taste no death in the Garden, because death has been tasted already, once, in the world, and the second death that the deniers feared (or denied) never comes to them. The commentators — al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Zamakhsharī — explain the grammar of the verse with care: the exception (*illā al-mawtata al-ūlā*) means that the only death they experience is the one they have already died — the death of this world, which was the door — and that the Garden contains no death at all: not the death of the body (their bodies are made eternal), not the death of joy (their bliss is everlasting), not the death of love (their company is permanent), not even the death of the state itself: the *mawt* that the world's inhabitants die in every goodbye — the ending of every beautiful thing — has no place in the Garden: *"Therein they will not hear any idle talk, nor any call to sin — only the saying, 'Peace, peace'"* (56:25–26): the Garden's eternity is the eternity of the *salām* — the peace that does not end because nothing in it dies.

**The Claim of the Deniers Answered**

The verse's placement makes it the direct answer to the denial of verse 35: the deniers said, *"There is naught but our first death, and we shall not be resurrected"* — they reduced existence to the first death, denying the resurrection that follows it; the verse answers that for the reverent the first death is followed by a life in which death is never tasted again — the first death is not the last word but the first word: the door that opens onto the deathless state. The two verses frame the sūrah's entire eschatology: the deniers' creed — one death and nothing after — is the creed of despair, the reduction of existence to its narrowest possibility; the believers' creed — one death and then the deathless Garden — is the creed of hope, the expansion of existence to its fullest possibility: the same first death, two meanings: for the denier, the end of everything; for the believer, the beginning of the endless. The commentators note the subtlety of the verse's exception: the reverent do not escape the first death — no one does: *"Every soul shall taste death"* (3:185) — but they pass through it as through a gate, and the gate closes behind them forever: death is behind them, not before them: the terror of the world's relation to death — the fear of the future ending — is reversed: the believer's death is his past, his future is the deathless state: the verse's *illā al-mawtata al-ūlā* — save the first death — is the believer's certificate of mortality discharged, the death already paid, the remainder eternal.

**The Shield From the Fire: The Negative Gift**

The verse's second clause completes the believer's security with its negative side: *wa-yaqīhim ʿadhāba al-jaḥīm* — "And He will shield them from the punishment of Hellfire." The verb *yaqīhim* — from *w-q-y*, the root of *taqwā* itself — is the divine act corresponding to the human act: the reverent (*muttaqūn*) were those who shielded themselves (*ittaqaw*) from the Fire in the world by obedience; now God Himself shields them (*yaqīhim*) from the Fire in the Hereafter by mercy: the human protection and the divine protection meeting in the single root: the believer's taqwā is answered by the divine *wiqāyah* — the shield that the believer built by his reverence in the world is completed by the shield that God places over him in the Hereafter: the *jaḥīm* — the Hellfire whose midst the sinner was commanded to be thrust into (v. 47) — is kept from the reverent entirely: they do not approach it, they do not see its flame, they are not singed by its edge: *"And God will deliver those who reverenced Him to their place of triumph; no evil will touch them, nor will they grieve"* (39:61); the distance between the reverent and the Fire is the distance of the divine protection: absolute, eternal, and founded on the mercy that the sūrah named as the only avail of the Day (v. 42).

**The Mercy That Exceeds the Mother's**

The verse's double gift — no death, no Fire — is the Qur'an's fullest statement of the divine mercy toward the reverent, and the commentators connect it with the Prophet's ﷺ most tender teaching about the divine compassion, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb: the Prophet ﷺ saw a woman among the captives searching frantically for her child, and when she found it she clasped it to her and nursed it; the Prophet ﷺ asked his Companions: "Do you think this woman would cast her child into the Fire?" They said, "No, by God, not while she is able to prevent it." The Prophet ﷺ said: "God is more merciful to His servants than this woman is to her child" (al-Bukhārī 5999 and Muslim 2754, from ʿUmar). The hadith is the tradition's commentary on the verse: the mercy that shields the believer from the Fire is not the reluctant mercy of a judge overruled by sentiment but the active mercy of a mother's nature — the compassion that is, in the Prophet's ﷺ words, greater than the strongest natural love the world can show: the *yaqīhim* of the verse is the act of the God whose mercy precedes His wrath (al-Bukhārī and Muslim: *"Truly My mercy prevails over My wrath"*): the shielding of the reverent from the Fire is not the exception to the divine nature but its most consistent expression — the mercy that created the Garden for the reverent is the same mercy that shields them from the Fire: the two acts are one act, the positive and negative faces of the single divine compassion: *"Your Lord has prescribed mercy for Himself"* (6:54).

**The Deathless Life as the Present Discipline**

The verse's final lesson for the believer is the inversion of the fear of death that the whole sūrah has been conducting: the deniers made death their argument against the resurrection (v. 35: there is naught but our first death); the believer makes death his argument for it — the first death that the verse acknowledges is the proof that the second life is real: the One who caused the first death is the One who raises (v. 8 of this sūrah: He gives life and causes death), and the raising that follows the first death is the life in which death is never tasted again. The believer's present discipline is therefore the practice of the deathless perspective: to live each day as one who has already died once — the death of the self's pretensions in repentance, the death of the world's claims in detachment — so that the first death, when it comes, finds nothing left to take: the soul that has died to the world in the world is the soul for whom the first death is only the formality of the transfer — the door that the verse describes as the last death, the end of endings: the believer who lives the deathless life in advance — whose joy, love, and hope do not depend on the things that die — is already tasting the Garden's immunity: the peace of the soul that has nothing left to lose because it has already given everything to the One who dies not: *"And put thy trust in the Living who dies not"* (25:58).

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:57

> **a bounty from thy Lord; that is the great triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Source of the Whole: Bounty**

The portrait of the reverent's reward now receives its final clause, and the clause names the reward's source: *faḍlan min rabbika* — "a bounty from thy Lord." The word *faḍl* — from *f-ḍ-l*, to exceed, to be surplus — is the Qur'an's term for the divine generosity that exceeds all deserving: the bounty that is not earned but given, the surplus of the divine goodness over every claim of the creature. The verse's claim is categorical and the commentators — al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr especially — unfold it with theological care: everything that the reverent have received in the preceding verses — the secure station, the gardens and springs, the silk and brocade, the wide-eyed maidens, the fruit called for in security, the immunity from death, the shielding from the Fire — all of it is *faḍl*, bounty: not the wages of their deeds but the gift of their Lord. The deeds were real — the reverence, the obedience, the patience — but the reward exceeds the deeds as the ocean exceeds the cup that draws from it: the believer's good deeds are the occasion of the reward, not its price: the price of the Garden would exceed any human capital, and the Qur'an's consistency on the point is absolute: the reward is *min rabbika* — from thy Lord — the bounty of the Giver, not the balance of the account: *"That is the bounty of God; He gives it to whom He wills, and God is the Possessor of tremendous bounty"* (57:21; and cf. 62:4).

**The Logic of Grace: Why the Reward Must Be Bounty**

The verse's theology of bounty rests on a precise analysis of the human condition that the classical theologians developed in their discussions of merit and grace: if the Garden were the wages of the believer's deeds, then the believer's deeds would have to be adequate to the Garden — and no finite deed is adequate to an infinite reward: the deeds of the creature are finite, the Garden is everlasting, and the disproportion between the two can only be bridged by the *faḍl* that exceeds the finite: the believer's obedience is real but small; the divine generosity is what magnifies it beyond its measure. The Qur'an states the disproportion in the believer's own mouth in the Hereafter: the inhabitants of the Garden, seeing what they have received, acknowledge that their deeds did not purchase it: *"And they will say, 'Praise be to God, who has fulfilled His promise to us and made us inherit the earth, dwelling in the Garden wherever we will. How excellent is the reward of the workers!'"* (39:74) — the praise of the Giver attending the receipt of the gift. And the Prophet ﷺ stated the same logic in the hadith already cited in this sūrah's commentary: "None of you will enter the Garden by his deeds alone" — and when the Companions asked, "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" he answered, "Not even me, unless God envelops me with His mercy" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah): the mercy that the sūrah named as the single exception of the Day of Division (v. 42) is here named again as the single source of the Garden: the bounty from thy Lord.

**The Great Triumph: The Scale of the Gift**

The verse closes with the Qur'an's highest term for the believer's attainment: *dhālika huwa al-fawzu al-ʿaẓīm* — "that is the great triumph." The word *fawz* — from *f-w-z*, to succeed, to be delivered, to win — is the Qur'an's word for the success that is ultimate: the deliverance from every evil and the attainment of every good; and the adjective *ʿaẓīm*, great, marks this success as the greatest of all successes — the triumph beside which every other triumph is failure. The Qur'an uses the phrase as the refrain of the believers' attainment: *"And whoever is delivered from the stinginess of his soul — it is they who are the successful"* (64:16); *"And whoever obeys God and His Messenger has achieved a great triumph"* (33:71); *"So God shielded them from the evil of that Day, and gave them radiance and joy, and rewarded them for their patience with a Garden and silk"* (76:11–12); and the fullest statement of the scale: *"Truly the companions of the Garden are that day in joyous occupation — they and their spouses, in shade, reclining upon couches. Therein they have fruits, and whatsoever they call for. 'Peace' — a word from a Merciful Lord"* (36:55–58). The verse's *that is the great triumph* is the sūrah's closing judgment on the whole of its argument: the Day of Division divided humanity into two companies — those who seized and were seized, and those who were shielded and secured; and the verse's demonstrative — *dhālika*, that — points to the entire inventory of the reverent's reward and declares it the triumph: not the triumph of power, which the sinners pursued and lost (vv. 47–50), but the triumph of the servants, which the world could not see and the Day revealed: the triumph that the world's mighty and noble never achieved because they sought it in the world's currency.

**The Bounty in the Believer's Present Life**

The verse's final lesson is the discipline of gratitude that the whole of the Islamic life is designed to train. The believer who knows that his final reward is *faḍl* — bounty — lives in a state that the Qur'an calls *shukr*: the gratitude that acknowledges the gift and the Giver in every moment: the believer's daily bread is bounty, his health is bounty, his faith itself is the greatest bounty — *"And God has endeared faith to you and adorned it in your hearts"* (49:7) — and the believer's practice is the acknowledgment: the *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh* that follows every meal, every recovery, every answered prayer — the gratitude that is itself the beginning of the Garden's praise: the inhabitants of the Garden praise God (39:74), and the believer's praise in the world is the rehearsal of the Garden's liturgy: the soul that thanks God for the small bounties of the day is the soul being trained for the great bounty of the station. The verse's *from thy Lord* — the address turning, at the sūrah's climax, from the third person (the reverent) to the second (the Prophet and, through him, the reader) — is the sūrah's final intimacy: the bounty is from *thy* Lord — the Lord who sent down the Book on the blessed night (v. 3), the Lord of the mercy (v. 6), the Lord who is the Hearing, the Knowing — and the verse's promise is that the great triumph is not a distant abstraction but a personal address: the bounty is prepared, the triumph is appointed, and the only question that remains is the question with which the sūrah will close: whether the reader will be of those who are shielded and secured, or of those who taste and doubt.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:58

> **We have only made this easy upon thy tongue, that haply they may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return to the Book**

With verse 58 the sūrah completes its great arc and returns to its beginning: *fa-innamā yassarnāhu bi-lisānika* — "We have only made this easy upon thy tongue." The pronoun *hu* refers back to the Qur'an itself — the Book sworn by in verse 2, sent down on the blessed night in verse 3 — and the verse closes the frame that the sūrah opened: the sūrah began with the Book's descent and now ends with the Book's accessibility: the Book that came down from heaven on a blessed night has been made easy upon the tongue of the Prophet ﷺ — the tongue that recites it, the language in which it was revealed, the mouth by which it is conveyed. The verb *yassarnā* — from *y-s-r*, the root of ease, the opposite of *ʿusr*, hardship — is the same verb that Sūrah al-Qamar repeats as its refrain: *"And indeed We have made the Qur'an easy to remember — is there then any who remembers?"* (54:17, 22, 32, 40) — and its use here carries the full weight of the divine pedagogy: the Qur'an's ease is not an accident of its composition but an act of its Author: the revelation was made easy — easy to recite, easy to memorize, easy to understand, easy to convey — by the same power that sent it down: the miracle of the Book includes its accessibility: the most profound book in the history of the world is also the most recited, the most memorized, the most available: the Book that the unlettered Prophet ﷺ received is the Book that unlettered believers have carried in their hearts for fourteen centuries.

**Made Easy Upon Thy Tongue: The Language of the Miracle**

The verse's phrase *bi-lisānika* — upon thy tongue — points to the linguistic miracle that the whole of the Qur'an's eloquence presupposes and that the sūrah's own audience could verify daily: the Qur'an was revealed in the tongue of the Arabs — the very language that the Prophet's ﷺ contemporaries spoke, the language whose finest poetry they knew by heart — and its ease was not the ease of simplicity but the ease of mastery: the speech that flows without effort because it is perfectly formed: the reciter finds the words fitting his breath, the memorizer finds the phrases fitting his memory, the hearer finds the meanings fitting his heart. The commentators — al-Rāzī in particular — note that the verse's claim of ease is also the closing argument of the sūrah's challenge: the Makkans, who had heard the Qur'an and called it the speech of a man taught by others (v. 14), are reminded that the Book's ease upon the Prophet's tongue is itself the sign: an unlettered man, untrained in the poets' craft, produced upon his tongue — without effort, without revision, over twenty-three years — a speech that the most eloquent of his age could not match: the ease is the evidence: *"And it is not the speech of a poet — little do you believe"* (69:41); *"And We have made this easy upon thy tongue that thou mayest give glad tidings thereby to the reverent, and warn thereby a contentious people"* (19:97): the same formula, the same purpose — the ease is for the conveying.

**The Purpose Clause: That Haply They May Remember**

The verse's final clause states the purpose of the ease: *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* — "that haply they may remember." The word *tadhakkur* — remembrance, taking heed — is the Qur'an's term for the response that the revelation is designed to produce: not mere hearing, not mere admiration, but the remembrance that changes conduct: the mind that turns back to God, the heart that recollects the warning, the will that acts on the reminder. The verse's *laʿalla* — haply, perhaps — is the Qur'an's particle of hope and possibility, used throughout the revelation to express the divine purpose's openness: the purpose of the ease is the remembrance, and the remembrance is possible — the ease makes the remembrance accessible; the remembrance itself remains the hearer's act: the Qur'an is made easy that they may remember — and the tragedy of the deniers (vv. 9–16) was precisely the refusal of the remembrance that the ease made possible: they heard the easiest of books with the hardest of hearts. The commentators note the verse's implicit address to the Prophet's ﷺ grief: the Prophet, whose people had turned away and said "taught, possessed" (v. 14), is told that the Book's ease has its purpose and its limit: the ease is guaranteed, the remembrance is not: the messenger's task is the conveyance of the easy Book; the remembrance is between the hearer and the Lord: *"Upon the Messenger is only the conveyance"* (5:99, and cf. 3:20; 13:40; 16:82 — *mā ʿalā al-rasūli illā al-balāgh*).

**The Ease of the Book and the Discipline of the Believer**

The verse's practical lesson for the believer is double. First, the ease of the Qur'an is a mercy to be used: the Book was made easy that it might be recited, memorized, and reflected upon — and the believer who neglects the easy Book has neglected the very mercy that was tailored to his capacity: the Qur'an's memorization, which the world considers a prodigy, has been accomplished by millions of ordinary believers — children, elders, the illiterate and the learned — because the ease is in the Book itself: the believer's practice is the daily recitation that keeps the easy words on the tongue: the Prophet ﷺ urged the constant recitation — "Read the Qur'an, for it will come on the Day of Resurrection as an intercessor for its companions" (Muslim 804, from Abū Umāmah) — and the sūrah's own virtue-reports, though weak in their chains, preserve the community's sense that al-Dukhān's recitation — the sūrah that announces the Book's ease — participates in the general grace of the easy Book. Second, the verse's purpose clause is the believer's mission statement: the Book was made easy upon the Prophet's tongue that people may remember — and the believer who has received the easy Book inherits the purpose: the conveying of the reminder to those who have not yet remembered: the believer's tongue, like the Prophet's, is the vehicle of the ease: the words that the believer speaks of the Qur'an, the verses that the believer shares, the life that the believer lives as the Qur'an's commentary — these are the continuations of the *taysīr*: the ease that was made for the conveyance is conveyed by those who have received it: *"And who is better in speech than one who calls unto God and works righteousness and says, 'Truly I am among the submitters'?"* (41:33).

**The Sūrah's Frame Completed**

The verse's return to the Book completes the sūrah's architecture with an exactness that the commentators admire: the sūrah opened with an oath upon the clear Book (v. 2) and the account of its descent on the blessed night (v. 3); it closes with the Book's ease upon the Prophet's tongue (v. 58) — the descent from above answered by the conveyance below, the blessed night answered by the remembering day: the whole of the sūrah's content — the smoke, the striking, the story of Pharaoh, the gardens of Egypt, the denial of the resurrection, the Day of Division, the Zaqqūm and the Garden — is the content of the easy Book: the warning and the promise that were sent down on the blessed night that they might be remembered in every night: and the believer who recites the sūrah to its end has performed the remembrance that the verse intends: he has heard the easy words and remembered — the remembering that the sūrah's final verse will now define as the posture of the watcher: the one who has remembered the warning and waits, in the ease of the Book, for the fulfillment that the Book promised.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:59

> **So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Command: Watch**

The sūrah closes as it opened — with a command to watch: *fa-rtaqib innahum murtaqibūn* — "So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch." The verb *irtaqib* — from *r-q-b* — is the same verb that commanded the Prophet in verse 10 to keep watch for the day of the smoke; the sūrah's final word returns to the watch and completes it: verse 10 commanded the watch for the first sign; verse 59 commands the watch that encompasses all the signs — the watch of the whole life until the Day of Division: the believer's existence between the two verses is the waiting period, and the final verse defines the waiting as the confrontation of two watches: *you* watch — and *they* watch: the believers and the disbelievers are both waiting, but their waits have opposite objects: the believers wait for the fulfillment of the divine promise — the victory, the deliverance, the reckoning; the disbelievers wait for the failure of the promise — the death of the Prophet, the collapse of the mission, the end of the warning: al-Qurṭubī and the Jalālayn record the classical readings of the verse: "Await the destruction that I have promised you against them, as they await the death that they hope will end your mission" — and the verse's verdict is the Qur'an's settled confidence: the two waits are not equal: one wait is the wait of certainty, the other the wait of illusion: the believer waits for what God has promised; the disbeliever waits for what his wish has invented: *"Say: 'Are you waiting for anything for us but one of the two best things [victory or martyrdom]? And we are waiting for God to visit you with punishment from Him or at our hands — so wait; we are waiting with you'"* (9:52); *"Say: 'Wait — for I, too, with you, am of those who wait'"* (52:31).

**The Parallel Watches of the Sūrah's History**

The verse's double watch gathers the whole of the sūrah's history into a single posture. Moses watched — after his call to his Lord (v. 22), he was commanded to depart by night and to leave the sea at rest (vv. 23–24), and the watch was answered: the sea closed, the host drowned, the gardens were left behind (vv. 25–29): the watcher's patience was vindicated by the divine action. The people of Pharaoh watched — they watched for the failure of Moses' mission, for the return of the fugitives, for the collapse of the strange God's power — and their watch was answered by the sea. The Quraysh of Makkah watched — they watched for the death of the Prophet, the end of the Muslims, the passing of the threat — and the sūrah's warning is that their watch would be answered as Pharaoh's was: by the smoke, the striking, the division. And the Prophet ﷺ watched — the watch commanded in this verse — and the history that followed the sūrah's revelation recorded the answer: the Badr that the commentators saw in the terrible striking (v. 16), the conquest of Makkah, the establishment of the religion — the watch of the believers ending in the vindication that the Qur'an promised: *"God has promised those of you who believe and perform righteous deeds that He will surely make them vicegerents upon the earth"* (24:55). The verse thus closes the sūrah with the whole of its argument resolved into action: the believer's response to the Book, the smoke, the histories, and the division is the watch — the patient, alert, active waiting of the one who knows the promise is true.

**The Psychology of the Two Watches**

The verse's pairing of the two watches is a precise study in the psychology of hope and its counterfeit. The believer's watch is the watch of *rajāʾ* — the hope that the Qur'an praises: the expectation founded on the divine promise, the waiting of the farmer who has sown and trusts the season: the watch that does not make the believer idle but makes him active — the watcher keeps his vigil precisely because he expects the arrival: the believer's watch is the night prayer, the daily obedience, the readiness of the one who expects the Guest: *"So be patient with what they say, and glorify thy Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before its setting"* (50:39) — the watch expressed as worship. The disbeliever's watch is the watch of *umniyyah* — the wish that the Qur'an condemns: the expectation founded on nothing but desire: the waiting of the one who hopes the truth will die because he has refused to live by it: the watch that also makes its keeper active — the disbelievers' watch was the persecution, the boycott, the plots against the Prophet — but its activity was the activity of illusion: they watched for a death that would not come, a failure that was not appointed, a morning that belonged to the other watcher: the Qur'an describes the end of the false watch: *"Or do they say, 'He is a poet for whom we await the vicissitudes of fate'? Say: 'Wait, then, for I, too, with you, am of those who wait'"* (52:30–31) — and the vicissitudes that the disbelievers awaited overtook the disbelievers themselves: the fate they assigned to the Prophet was the fate that history assigned to them: the two watches met in the event, and the event belonged to the watcher who had watched with God.

**The Watch as the Believer's Permanent State**

The verse's command is the sūrah's final gift to the believer, and its permanence is its point: the watch is not the posture of a season but the state of a lifetime — the believer lives between the two verses of the watch (vv. 10, 59) as the sentry lives between the alerts: always positioned, always alert, never asleep at the post. The Prophet ﷺ described the world itself as the watch's station: "Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a wayfarer" (al-Bukhārī, from Ibn ʿUmar) — the stranger watches because he expects to move on; the wayfarer watches because he expects to arrive — and the believer's watch is the combination: the alertness of the stranger to the world's deceptions and the expectancy of the wayfarer for the journey's end: the verse's final word — *murtaqibūn* — leaves the believer standing at his post, the sūrah ending as the watch begins: the reciter who closes the sūrah with *fa-rtaqib* is not closing a book but accepting a commission: the commission of the watcher who has heard the warning of the smoke, the story of Pharaoh, the promise of the division, and the description of the two ends — and who now waits, with the certain wait of the believer, for the fulfillment that the Lord of the blessed night has promised: the wait that the Prophet ﷺ himself practiced until his death, and that the believers after him have practiced through every century of the watch: the wait that is itself a worship, the patience that is itself a proof, and the hope that is itself the beginning of the great triumph: *"So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch"* — and the watchers of God have never watched in vain.
