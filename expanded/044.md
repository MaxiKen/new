# Sūrah al-Dukhān (Chapter 44) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smoke: A Name Taken from the Sky's Warning**

Sūrah al-Dukhān — "The Smoke" — the forty-fourth sūrah of the Qur'an, takes its name from the startling image of verses 10–11: a day when the sky itself produces a *dukhān mubīn*, a manifest smoke, that envelops the people. Of the fifty-nine verses of this Makkan sūrah — with verse 15 considered by some early authorities to be Madinan — none gave the chapter its title but this one, because none fixed itself in the memory of the first listeners like it. Smoke is, of all natural images, the most perfectly ambiguous: it is what rises when something is being consumed; it obscures the air everyone must breathe; it signals fire without showing the fire. As a title for a sūrah whose subject is certainty, it is deliberately unsettling. The sūrah opens with an oath upon the clear Book and closes with two parties watching one another — and between those two points it runs the whole argument of revelation: what was sent down, why it was sent down, what happens to those who play with it in doubt, and what awaits those who received it with reverence.

**Its Place Between Two Neighbors**

Al-Dukhān belongs to the celebrated group of seven consecutive sūrahs that open with the letters *Ḥā Mīm* (chapters 40–46), known collectively as the *Ḥawāmīm*, and it is the fifth of them. The classical commentators read it as standing in deliberate continuity with the sūrah before it, al-Zukhruf. Al-Zukhruf ended with the Prophet's sorrowful complaint about his people — *"O my Lord, truly these are a people who believe not"* (43:88) — and with the command, *"Farewell! Soon they will know"* (43:89). Al-Dukhān picks up precisely that thread: it opens with the oath, *"By the clear Book,"* as if answering, "you will soon know what? — you will know the reality of this clear Book," and it later returns Moses' own verdict upon a defiant tyrant and nation, *"These are a guilty people"* (44:22), as an echo of the Prophet's own lament. The sūrah that follows, al-Jāthiyah ("The Kneeling"), then carries the same eschatological argument forward to the image of every nation kneeling at the judgment. Read in sequence, the three form a single escalating address: the Book sworn to (44), the evidences in creation and history laid out (45), the courtroom assembled (45:28).

**The Architecture of Fifty-Nine Verses**

The sūrah's structure is tight and cinematic, moving through five scenes. The first scene (vv. 1–8) is heavenly: an oath upon the Book, the blessed night of its descent, the annual distinguishing of every wise affair, and the declaration that all of this is mercy from the Lord of the heavens and the earth — closing with a rebuke: *"would that you were certain."* The second scene (vv. 9–16) descends to earth: the doubters who treat revelation as play, and the smoke, the plea, the closed door of the reminder, and the coming terrible striking. The third scene (vv. 17–33) is the longest and is historical: the trial of Pharaoh's people, Moses' four brief speeches, the night exodus, the parted sea left at rest, the drowned host, the abandoned gardens and springs, heaven and earth not weeping for the tyrants, and the delivered, chosen, sign-bearing Children of Israel. The fourth scene (vv. 34–42) swings back to the Makkan present and beyond it: the materialists who admit only one death, the rhetorical question about the people of Tubbaʿ, the declaration that creation was not made in play but in truth, and the Day of Division when no friend will avail a friend. The fifth scene (vv. 43–59) presents the two hospitality halls of the Hereafter side by side — the tree of Zaqqūm, the molten lead, and the mocking *"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble"* set against the secure station, the gardens, the silk, the marriage to the wide-eyed maidens, and the bounty that is the great triumph — before closing as it began: *"So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch."*

**The Virtue of Its Recitation**

Al-Dukhān is among the sūrahs singled out in the tradition for nocturnal recitation. The introduction of the classical commentaries preserves the report:

> *"Whosoever recites al-Dukhān during the night will greet the morning with seventy thousand angels asking forgiveness for him."* (reported in the tafsīr and faḍāʾil literatures — al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, and others transmit variants of it; its chains have been discussed and graded variously by the ḥadīth critics)

Al-Tirmidhī records in his Sunan a related report concerning the eve of Friday:

> *"Whoever recites Sūrat Ḥā Mīm al-Dukhān on a Friday night, his sins will be forgiven."* (al-Tirmidhī 2888; he graded it ḥasan gharīb)

Whatever the technical grading of individual chains, the practice of the early community confirms that this sūrah held a fixed place in devotional life — and with reason: few sūrahs of comparable length compress so completely the Qur'an's three great subjects, the majesty of revelation, the fate of denial in history, and the architecture of the Hereafter.

**The Night and the Decree: The Sūrah's Central Doctrine**

At the heart of the sūrah stands one of the Qur'an's most concentrated statements about how revelation enters the world: *"truly We sent it down on a blessed night"* (44:3). The commentators relate this night to the Night of Power of Sūrah 97, and to the tradition of the yearly distinguishing of affairs — the *"every wise command made distinct"* of verse 4 — connecting the descent of the Book to the descent of the year's decrees, the transfers written upon the Preserved Tablet, and the traffic of angels between the heavens and the earth. A sūrah revealed to a persecuted community in Makkah thus opens by teaching them that the address which reached them in a cave outside their city originated in a transaction far older and vaster than their city — in a night in which every wise affair of the coming year was being separated and dispatched by command from God's own Presence. The pastoral effect of this opening on the original hearers deserves emphasis: it tells the weak believer that the word sustaining him was issued from the same chancery that issues the decrees of the universe.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the fifty-nine verses of Sūrah al-Dukhān as an independent, complete discourse, in strict canonical order, exactly as the verse-by-verse method requires. Where the source commentary groups verses together (as it does with 44:3–5, 44:10–11, 44:13–14, 44:23–24, 44:25–28, 44:30–31, 44:34–36, 44:38–39, 44:41–42, 44:43–44, 44:45–46, 44:49–50, 44:51–52, and others), those verses are here separated and each receives its own full treatment. Every verse is expanded through its Arabic language and roots, its cross-references with the actual Qur'anic text, the authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the classical exegetes — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Taymiyyah, and others — the occasions and historical setting, and the reasoning that connects its teaching to modern human experience. Nothing has been compressed because the sūrah is long in its meanings, nor will any verse be merged into another: the smoke clears verse by verse, from *Ḥā Mīm* to *"So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch."*

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:1

> **Ḥā. Mīm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth Gate of the Ḥawāmīm**

The forty-fourth sūrah of the Qur'an opens with two letters of the Arabic alphabet pronounced as names — *Ḥā, Mīm* — and it does so as the fifth in a sequence of seven consecutive sūrahs (chapters 40 through 46) that all begin with this same pair, the sequence the Companions and the early scholars called *al-Ḥawāmīm*. Al-Qurṭubī records the celebrated saying attributed to ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd (d. 32/652):

> *"Al-Ḥawāmīm are the lush meadows (rawḍ) of the Qur'an; when I arrive at one of the Ḥawāmīm, I have arrived at its meadow."*

The image is precise: a meadow is where one grazes slowly, not gallops through. And these seven sūrahs justify the metaphor, for between them they carry some of the Qur'an's grandest openings — the descent of the Book (40:1–3), the oath upon the clear Book (43:1–4, 44:1–3), and the great invocation of the Throne and the angels of 40:7. To open al-Dukhān with *Ḥā Mīm* is to tell the reciter that he is entering cultivated, ornamented ground.

**The Disconnected Letters and Their Mystery**

*Ḥā* and *Mīm* belong to the *al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the separated or disconnected letters, which stand at the heads of twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an, in combinations of one, two, three, four, and five letters. Fourteen of the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet are ever used in them, and they appear in fourteen distinct combinations. The overwhelming majority of the early community, including the practice transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and the caution recorded from Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq (d. 13/634) —

> *"Every Book has a mystery (sirr), and the mystery of the Qur'an lies in the openings of the sūrahs."*

— treated them as divine knowledge whose full meaning God kept to Himself. Al-Ṭabarī assembles the reports and concludes that the safest position is recitation with submission: they are part of the revealed text, they are recited as letters, and their interpretation belongs to God unless the Prophet ﷺ explained them.

**The Letters as a Standing Challenge**

Alongside the position of mystery runs the position of argument, developed powerfully by al-Zamakhsharī (d. 538/1144) and endorsed in substance by Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373): the letters are a proclamation of the Qur'an's miraculousness (*iʿjāz*). The reasoning is elegant and unanswerable. These are not exotic symbols; they are the listener's own alphabet — the very inventory from which every poet of Arabia composed his odes. By opening sūrah after sūrah with the raw materials of the Arabs' language, revelation effectively announces: here is everything you possess; produce from it the like of this. The challenge is then stated explicitly in the adjacent sūrahs: *"Or do they say, 'He has fabricated it'? Say: then bring ten sūrahs like it, fabricated, and call whomever you can besides God, if you are truthful"* (11:13). Notice also the structural habit of the Qur'an: after these letters, the text almost invariably proceeds to speak of the Book itself — *"Ḥā Mīm. The revelation of the Book is from God, the Mighty, the Knowing"* (40:1–2); *"Ḥā Mīm. By the clear Book"* (43:1–2; 44:1–2). The letters thus function as a herald's flourish before the proclamation: attention, what follows is of another order.

**Why This Sūrah Begins in Secret and Ends in Watching**

There is a final symmetry worth registering before the commentary moves on. The sūrah begins with letters whose meaning is withheld, and it ends with an order to watch — *fartaqib innahum murtaqibūn* (44:59). Between concealment at the opening and vigilance at the close, the whole sūrah trains one disposition: the reverence of standing before meanings one has not yet exhausted. The one who treats *Ḥā Mīm* with the adab of not knowing, yet still recites, has already rehearsed the posture the sūrah will demand of its hearers at the smoke, at the sea left at rest, and at the Day of Division.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:2

> **By the clear Book,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Sworn by the Book Itself**

After the letters comes the oath: *wa'l-kitābi'l-mubīn* — "By the clear Book." The Qur'an swears by the Qur'an. This is not circularity but testimony of rank: in the grammar of oaths, one swears only by what is majestic, and when God swears by His own speech He is announcing that this speech is among the greatest of created things — for the Book, in the orthodox doctrine recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, is God's speech, and the recited, written, memorized Book in our hands is that speech conveyed. The same oath opens the immediately preceding sūrah (*"Ḥā Mīm. By the clear Book"* — 43:1–2), recurs in Yā Sīn (*"Yā Sīn. By the wise Qur'an"* — 36:1–2), and appears with variations throughout: *"By the heaven, holder of the constellations... Nay, but this is a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet"* (85:1, 21–22). When the Qur'an swears by itself, it is placing its own existence among the witnessed wonders of the universe, like the heaven and the constellations.

**The Weight of Mubīn: Clear in Itself, Clarifying for Others**

The adjective is *mubīn*, from the root *b-y-n*, separation and distinction — the same family as *bayān*, lucid exposition. Grammatically the form *mubīn* carries a double sense, and the exegetes kept both alive. First: the Book is *clear in itself* — its language is perspicuous Arabic, its guidance unmistakable in what is required of a human being: *"This is the Book in which there is no doubt"* (2:2); *"We have sent down upon you the Book as a clarification of all things"* (16:89). Second: the Book is *clarifying for others* — it makes plain what had been concealed of the earlier revelations, adjudicating what the Children of Israel disputed among themselves (43:63–64), and making visible the distinction between guidance and error, truth and falsehood, the reverent and the denier. This is why Sūrah 5, which legislates the final covenant, names it *"a guardian over the Book before it"* (5:48): clarity is not merely a literary quality here; it is a juridical one — the clear Book is the Book that can judge, because a judge's ruling must be legible.

**The Oath's Unspoken Conclusion**

A feature of Qur'anic oaths deserves attention: the thing sworn for — the *jawāb al-qasam*, the answer of the oath — is often left unstated, for the context supplies it. Here, after swearing by the clear Book, the sūrah proceeds directly into *"truly We sent it down on a blessed night"* (44:3). Scholars such as al-Zamakhsharī observe that the suppressed conclusion is something like: "By the clear Book — this Qur'an that you are mocking is truly from Us; its descent is real; its warning is real." The oath thus functions as a slammed hand upon the table before the argument begins. Consider the psychology of the original audience: a merchant people for whom an oath was a legal instrument, binding and feared, heard the Most High binding His own Name to the descent of this text. The sūrah's later complaint — *"Nay, but they are playing in doubt"* (44:9) — is set deliberately against this opening: an oath of this weight was met with play.

**What Clarity Demands of the Reader**

The verse also quietly assigns responsibility. A book that is *mubīn* removes the excuse of incomprehension. The Qur'an repeatedly refuses the defense of obscurity: *"This is a proclamation for the people, that they may be warned thereby"* (14:52); *"And indeed We have made the Qur'an easy to remember — so is there any who will remember?"* (54:17). If the Book is clear, then the residue of resistance is not in the text but in the reader — in appetite, in inherited custom, in the doubt that prefers play to inquiry. The whole remaining argument of al-Dukhān is, in one sense, the unpacking of that single word: since the Book is clear, everything that follows — the smoke, the drowning, the Day of Division — is not ambiguity but consequence.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:3

> **truly We sent it down on a blessed night—truly We are ever warning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night That Carried the Book**

This is one of the three great verses of the Qur'an about the descent of the Qur'an, alongside *"The month of Ramadan, in which the Qur'an was sent down as guidance for mankind"* (2:185) and *"Indeed, We sent it down upon the Night of Power"* (97:1). The verb is *anzalnāhu* — "We sent it *down*" — the vocabulary of vertical descent from a height, and the pronoun in "it" refers to the whole Book: the entire Qur'an, not a fragment of it. The classical exegetes, reporting from Ibn ʿAbbās (d. 68/687) and transmitted through al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr, describe a two-stage descent. First, the Qur'an was sent down all at once from the Preserved Tablet (*al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*, cf. 85:21–22) to the House of Glory (*bayt al-ʿizzah*) in the lowest heaven, on the blessed night. Second, from the lowest heaven it was revealed upon the Prophet ﷺ piecemeal across the twenty-three years of his mission, in response to events, questions, and needs — the gradual descent the Qur'an itself explains: *"And the Qur'an We have divided, that you may recite it to the people at intervals, and We sent it down by successive sendings"* (17:106); *"Thus — that We may strengthen thereby your heart"* (25:32).

**Which Night: Power, or the Middle of Shaʿbān?**

The majority of exegetes identify the blessed night as *Laylat al-Qadr*, the Night of Power, in Ramadan — the identification the verse's companions enforce: 2:185 fixes the month of the descent as Ramadan, and 97:1 names its night. The verse is therefore describing the single most consequential night in the history of speech: the night the final address left the register of the Preserved Tablet for the lowest heaven. A second report, cited by al-Qurṭubī and others, relates the blessed night to the middle night of Shaʿbān — the eve of the fifteenth of the month preceding Ramadan — on which, in that tradition, the yearly decrees are distinguished (as verse 4 will discuss), the transfer of the Book to the agents of descent being completed upon the Night of Power. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have harmonized the two traditions thus:

> *"God decrees all the affairs on the middle night of Shaʿbān; then He delivers them to their executors on the Night of Power."*

The devotional literature around the middle of Shaʿbān — including the report of the Prophet ﷺ standing in prayer during its night and fasting its day (Ibn Mājah 1388 and others, with chains that the critics have discussed) — belongs to this tradition. Whichever identification is preferred, the sūrah's doctrine stands: revelation entered time at a single, named, blessed point — it did not leak in gradually from the Prophet's own mind.

**The Meaning of "Blessed"**

The night is *mubārakah* — from *b-r-k*, the root of the camel kneeling firm upon its ground: blessing is fixed, abounding, durable good. One night carrying the Book outweighs a thousand months (97:3) precisely because blessings of this order do not obey arithmetic. The Prophet ﷺ taught the community how to meet such a night:

> *"Whoever stands in prayer on the Night of Power, out of faith and hope of reward, his previous sins will be forgiven him."* (al-Bukhārī 1901; Muslim 760)

The blessing of the night is thus not merely historical but renewable: every Ramadan it returns, and the sūrah's original words — *truly We sent it down on a blessed night* — are recited as a standing invitation to be present where blessing descends.

**"Truly We Are Ever Warning": The Purpose Clause of the Descent**

The verse closes with *innā kunnā munẓirīn* — "truly We are ever warning." The descent had an office: *indhār*, warning — the naming of danger before it arrives. The Qur'an assigns the Prophet ﷺ the same office (*"We have only sent you as a bearer of good tidings and a warner"* — 34:28), and describes the Book itself in the same breath as Sūrah 97's night: peace until the rising of the dawn. Two observations follow. First, warning is a form of mercy: only a friend warns; an enemy lets you walk on. The verse therefore pairs the descent of scripture with the continuity of divine warning throughout history — every messenger was a warner (35:24), so the Qur'an's warnings are not a new severity but the continuation of an eternal courtesy. Second, the phrase *ever warning* — with the imperfect of continuity — tells the believer that the warning function of this Book never expires: as long as it is recited, the courtesy continues; the smoke of verses 10–11 is still being announced, not yet merely remembered.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:4

> **Therein every wise command is made distinct,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chancery of the Year**

*Fīhā yufraqu kullu amrin ḥakīm* — "Therein is made distinct every wise affair." The verb *yufraqu* is from *f-r-q*, separation and division — the same root as *al-Furqān*, the Criterion, one of the Qur'an's own names (25:1), and of *Farʿūn's* undoing at the sea, *yawm al-furqān*, the Day of Distinction (8:41). On the blessed night, the undifferentiated mass of what is to come is separated out affair by affair, destiny by destiny. *Amr ḥakīm* — "a wise affair/command" — carries, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī note, two senses: every affair that is itself wise (full of wisdom, rightly placed), and every affair that is decisive and definitive (*muḥkam*, admitting no alteration once dispatched). Both readings agree on the substance: the world's coming year — its provisions, its deaths, its births, its victories, its famines — is written out in detail and assigned to its executors. Ibn ʿAbbās's report, preserved in the tafsīr tradition, is explicit:

> *"It is on the middle night of Shaʿbān that God distinguishes every wise affair: the provisions of the year, the births, the deaths — even the pilgrim is written, 'so-and-so will perform the pilgrimage, and so-and-so will not.'"*

**How This Squares with the Preserved Tablet**

The theology here is delicate, and the exegetes handled it with care. God's eternal knowledge and its primordial record — the Preserved Tablet, *"in which is no alteration"* — stand outside time. What is distinguished on the blessed night is the *yearly transcription*: the extract delivered to the angels charged with each domain of creation — provision, rain, life, death — which Sūrah 97 describes descending with the Spirit: *"The angels and the Spirit descend therein by the permission of their Lord, for every affair"* (97:4). It is at this level that the Qur'an speaks of divine effacement and confirmation: *"God effaces what He wills and establishes, and with Him is the Mother of the Book"* (13:39). The Mother — the original register — is untouched; the working copies are where erasure and fixing occur, and the distinction made on the blessed night belongs to that working level. Ibn ʿAbbās's harmonization (cited at verse 3) pictures the process beginning on the middle night of Shaʿbān and being handed to its executors on the Night of Power — the chancery drafting, then dispatching.

**A Universe Under Administration**

The pastoral doctrine of the verse is the majesty of divine administration. Nothing in the coming year is random: every affair is *ḥakīm* — wise, even when its recipient cannot read the wisdom. Famine and plenty, the smoke and the rain, are each dispatched as *amr ḥakīm*, a wise command from a wise Commander. This is why the sūrah can speak of the famine that crushed Quraysh (vv. 10–16) without tragedy: it too was affair, distinction, dispatch — a mercy in the form of pressure, offered while the recipients still had time to say *"Our Lord, remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers"* (44:12). Compare the great statement of the same doctrine in Sūrah al-Qadr — *"Peace is it, until the rising of the dawn"* (97:5) — for the descending decrees of that night include peace alongside trial. The believer who internalizes verse 4 meets his year differently: the calendar becomes correspondence from the chancery of God, each day an affair already distinguished, already wise, already accompanied by its appointed angels. Anxiety, which is the imagination of an unadministered future, loses its object.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:5

> **as a command from Us—truly We are ever sending,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Origin of the Dispatch: "From Our Presence"**

*Amran min ʿindinā* — "a command from Us," or more precisely, "from Our presence." The preposition *ʿind* denotes proximity and source: these distinguished affairs do not filter down through indifferent intermediaries; they originate at the Presence itself. Ibn Kathīr comments that everything that occurs, occurs by a command proceeding from God's presence — nothing is delegated away from His authority, and revelation itself belongs to this same stream of descent: the Book came as the distinguished affairs come, *from ʿind Allāh*. This is the Qur'an's consistent idiom for revelation's source: *"A revelation from the All-Merciful"* (41:2); *"Say: the Spirit of Holiness has brought it down from your Lord in truth"* (16:102); *"And it is not for any human that God should speak to him except by revelation, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger"* (42:51). Al-Rāzī draws out the argumentative function: the verse answers the implicit objection of the Makkan doubters — how can an affair concerning the Year, the Book, and the Decree descend to an orphan of Makkah? — by declaring that the entire universe runs on exactly this principle: command descending from the Presence into the world. The Qur'an is not an exception to cosmic procedure; it is cosmic procedure at its most articulate.

**"Truly We Are Ever Sending": The Open Channel**

*Innā kunnā mursilīn* — "truly We are ever sending." The imperfect of continuity again. The exegetes divide the reference of this sending between two grand streams, and both are sound. First, the sending of revelation and mercy: the Qur'an is a mercy sent down (and verse 6 will say so explicitly), and through it blessings descend upon every believer — al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record this reading. Second, the sending of the messengers across history: God has been, and is, the One who sends — to Noah, to Abraham, to Moses, to every nation its warner (35:24) — until the seal of the messengers ﷺ, after whom revelation is closed but whose mission continues through what he left: *"I have left among you that which if you hold fast to it you will never go astray: the Book of God and the Sunnah of His Prophet"* (Muslim 1218, from Abū Hurayrah, in the longer form recorded by al-Ḥākim and others). The channel from Presence to world is therefore never shut; what changes across the ages is its content — from new scripture to the preservation and enactment of the final one.

**The Logic of Continuous Sending**

Why does God send at all, and continually? The sūrah's own answer follows in verse 6 — mercy — but the logic can be stated plainly, for it is one of the Qur'an's great rational principles. Human beings are born needing what they cannot generate: guidance for the soul is as unproducible by the soul as light is by the eye. The eye was made for a sun it cannot make; the heart was made for a guidance it cannot compose. A God who creates the need and withholds the supply would be creating frustration, and frustration of this order, sustained across every generation, would be unworthy of wisdom. Hence *We are ever sending* is not sentiment but theodicy: the continuous sending is the proof that creation was made in earnest and in mercy. This is why the sūrah will shortly rebuke those who play in doubt (44:9), and why it will later declare that the heavens and the earth were not created in play (44:38): the same divine character — sending, warning, providing — runs through revelation and creation alike, and the doubter is doubting both at once.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:6

> **as a mercy from thy Lord. Truly He is the Hearing, the Knowing,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy as the Reason for the Whole Operation**

*Raḥmatan min rabbika* — "as a mercy from your Lord." The word *raḥmah* is placed as the cause of everything the preceding verses described: the blessed night, the distinguishing of the affairs, the command from the Presence, the continual sending. All of it — mercy. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the grammatical force: *raḥmatan* is a purpose-statement — the sending happened *for the purpose of mercy*. And the exegetes, including al-Qurṭubī, observe that the mercy here can equally be read as continuing the previous sentence — "We are sending a mercy from your Lord" — in which case the mercy sent is identified with what is sent, and several early authorities identify it with the Prophet ﷺ himself, of whom the Qur'an says in the most explicit terms:

> *"And We sent you not save as a mercy unto the worlds."* (Qur'an 21:107)

Book, messenger, and night are thus a single merciful event in three aspects. This doctrine reshapes the believer's reading of revelation's severities: even the warnings — even the smoke — are instruments of the mercy, as a surgeon's knife is. The ḥadīth qudsī establishes the metaphysical priority of mercy over wrath:

> *"Indeed, My mercy overcomes My wrath."* (al-Bukhārī 7404; Muslim 2751)

and the Prophet ﷺ gave it a picture the desert mind could never forget, when a captive mother was seen gathering her child to her breast, and he asked his Companions whether she could cast it into the fire, and then said:

> *"God is more tender toward His servants than this woman toward her child."* (al-Bukhārī 5999; Muslim 2754)

**"From Your Lord": The Intimacy in the Address**

The shift to the second person — *rabbika*, "your Lord" — is directed at the Prophet ﷺ, and it is a deliberate tenderness at the point of maximum abstraction. The verses have been describing cosmic chancery: tablets, nights, distinguished affairs, angels descending. Then the address bends toward one man in Makkah: *from your Lord*. The message is that the machinery of the heavens is not indifferent administration; it is personal provision — the Lord who managed the descent manages the one to whom it was descended. This is the same consolatory second person that opens Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā (*"Your Lord has not forsaken you, nor does He hate you"* — 93:3) and closes the greatest verse of the Qur'an's middle (*"God is the Guardian of those who believe"* — 2:257). The pattern is a pastoral law of the revelation: the larger the doctrine, the nearer the pronoun.

**The Two Names That Seal the Verse**

The verse ends with *al-Samīʿ al-ʿAlīm* — the Hearing, the Knowing. Why these two names here? Because they answer the two silent questions of every recipient of mercy. *The Hearing*: God hears the petition, the whispered prayer, the complaint that never became words — He who heard the famine-broken Quraysh cry *"Our Lord, remove from us the punishment"* (44:12), and He who hears every servant who asks. *The Knowing*: God knows what the servant actually needs, as distinct from what he asks; a mercy administered by mere hearing could be deceived by eloquent request, and a mercy administered by mere knowledge could remain deaf to the cry; the mercy of the Qur'an is administered by the One who unites both. Al-Qurṭubī draws the practical lesson: when you ask, ask as one heard; and when you are refused, receive it as from one who knows. The opening doctrinal section of the sūrah closes here, with revelation established as descended, distinguished, dispatched, and merciful — and with the names of God standing guard over the whole, before the camera of the sūrah turns, in verse 7, to the heavens and the earth themselves.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:7

> **Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them; would that you were certain.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Signature on the Chancery's Dispatches**

The first doctrinal section of the sūrah now names the Author of everything it has described. *Rabbi's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them." The title *Rabb* — Lord, Sustainer, the One who brings a thing from inception to completion by successive care — has been quietly present through the whole opening (*"a mercy from your Lord,"* 44:6); now its jurisdiction is stated in full: not one province but the whole estate, the heavens, the earth, and the entire inhabited space between them. This is the logic the verse constructs against the doubters of Makkah. You already confess — as the Qur'an repeatedly records — that this universal Lordship belongs to God alone:

> *"And if you ask them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they will surely say, 'God.'"* (Qur'an 31:25; cf. 29:61, 39:38)

The idolaters of Makkah were not atheists; they affirmed the Creator while assigning partners in worship. The sūrah's argument therefore runs as a syllogism the listeners themselves supplied the premises for: the One who is Lord of the heavens and the earth and all between them is the only One who can send a Book, distinguish the year's affairs, and dispatch mercy from His presence; you confess He is that One; therefore the Book, the affairs, and the mercy come from Him — and associating partners with Him is incoherence. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī bring out this reasoning explicitly: had their confession of Lordship been knowledge and not mere inherited phrase, it would have compelled them to accept the revelation, for revelation is simply Lordship exercising its office of guidance.

**"Would That You Were Certain": The Missing Ingredient**

The verse's final clause is its sting: *in kuntum mūqinīn* — "if you were certain," or "would that you were certain." *Yaqīn*, certainty, is the Qur'an's highest epistemic rank, above knowledge (*ʿilm*), and it appears throughout this sūrah's neighborhood as the substance the deniers lack. What is the verse saying? Two readings, both recorded by al-Ṭabarī, and both true. First: "If you possessed certainty, you would know that the Lord of the heavens and the earth is the One who sends revelation" — meaning their acknowledgment of God was not certainty at all, but habit, imitation, and guesswork; a confession that coexists with doubt about God's speech is not *yaqīn*. Second, the more piercing reading: "Would that you were certain!" — an exclamation of regretful instruction, as if to say: if only you realized, with certainty, what awaits you. In the very next scene (vv. 9–16) the sūrah will show the doubters under the smoke, reduced to begging *"Our Lord, remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers"* (44:12) — a belief born of pressure, not of certainty. The verse thus diagnoses in advance the difference between the two kinds of faith it will dramatize: certainty that precedes the trial, and coerced confession during it. Only the first saves.

**Certainty as a Craft, Not an Accident**

The modern reader should not glide past *mūqinīn*. The Qur'an treats certainty as something built — from repeated looking at the signs in creation (45:3–6, the sūrah immediately following this one, is an entire catalogue of them), from honest weighing of the messenger's claim, from acting on what one already knows. Imitation (*taqlīd*) of one's fathers, which the sūrah will shortly confront (44:8), produces confession without certainty; and confession without certainty collapses the moment social cost or physical suffering arrives — precisely the collapse of Pharaoh's courtiers and of the Quraysh under the famine. The verse, read against its whole context, is therefore an invitation to convert inherited theism into examined certainty before the smoke arrives; after it arrives, certainty is no longer available at all.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:8

> **There is no god but He; He gives life and causes death, your Lord and the Lord of your fathers of old.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Declaration, Then Its Two Proofs**

*Lā ilāha illā huwa* — "There is no god but He." After the jurisdiction (Lord of the heavens and the earth, 44:7) comes the exclusive claim: divinity — the right to be worshiped, obeyed ultimately, and appealed to absolutely — belongs to that Lord alone. The Qur'an's standard method then supplies the proofs, and here they are two. The first is the monopoly over life and death: *yuḥyī wa yumīt* — "He gives life and causes death." This formula recurs across the Book (2:258; 3:156; 7:158; 10:56; 15:23; 23:80; 40:68; 53:44; 57:2) because it names the two boundaries no creature can cross: no idol brings a single thing into being, and no idol retrieves a single thing from death. Whatever sits between the two boundaries — the whole of ordinary life — is likewise administered, for the sūrah has just said it: the year's affairs, every one, are distinguished by command from His presence (44:4). Life and death are the two doors of existence; He who owns both doors owns the house, and nothing between the doors deserves worship. This is also the premise of the Resurrection argument the sūrah will develop at 44:34–39: the One who gave life the first time gives it again; denial of the second giving is denial of the power of the first.

**The Second Proof: Your Lord, and the Lord of Your Fathers**

*rabbukum wa rabbu ābāʾikumu'l-awwalīn* — "your Lord and the Lord of your fathers of old." This clause is the verse's masterpiece of argument, and al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr linger on it. The Quraysh justified their idolatry principally by ancestral authority: they were walking in the religion of their fathers. The verse meets that plea on its own ground and overturns it. You appeal to your fathers — but who was the Lord *of* your fathers? Not Hubal and not al-ʿUzzā; the same One who is your Lord now. The truest way to honor the earliest fathers — and the Arab genealogists themselves traced the line to Ismāʿīl and Ibrāhīm, the Hanīf who broke the idols — is to worship what they worshiped: *"Say: God has spoken the truth; so follow the creed of Abraham, the upright, who was not of the idolaters"* (3:95). Ancestor-worship, pressed honestly, cancels idolatry. Ibn Kathīr draws the consequence bluntly: the verse strips the idolaters of their one respectable excuse, because the God they are being summoned to is not a novelty — He is the oldest family tradition they have.

**The Rhetoric of Personal Ownership**

Notice finally the shift from third person to second: "your Lord and the Lord of your fathers" — the address turns to face the listeners directly, just as *"from your Lord"* (44:6) turned to face the Prophet ﷺ. The sūrah's method throughout is to make doctrine personal: the Lord of the cosmos is *your* Lord; the covenant of creation runs through *your* family line; the Book descended on the blessed night descended to a man of *your* city and *your* tribe. The closing section of these opening eight verses thus leaves the doubters with no comfortable distance: the argument is not about theology in general but about their own ancestor's God, their own breath, their own deaths. The stage is set for verse 9's devastating diagnosis: after all this — "Nay, but they are playing in doubt."

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:9

> **Nay, but they are playing in doubt.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis in Three Words**

*Bal hum fī shakkin yalʿabūn* — "Nay, but they are in doubt, playing." The particle *bal* — "nay, rather" — strikes down the posture the previous verses dismantled: the confession of God without certainty, the appeal to fathers. What remains is named with surgical economy. The condition is first identified — *shakk*, doubt — and then its posture is identified — *laʿib*, play. This order matters. The Qur'an does not here accuse them of having investigated the messenger's claim and refuted it; it accuses them of never having investigated at all. They are *in* doubt as one is in water — surrounded, immersed, and not swimming. And their occupation within that doubt is play: mockery, jest, diversion, the games of social life carried on around the unanswered question as if it were not a question. Ibn Kathīr explains: they turned away from serious consideration and occupied themselves with ridicule; al-Qurṭubī adds that their doubt was itself not even honest inquiry but inherited bewilderment they preferred to keep comfortable.

**Why "Play" Is the Qur'an's Chosen Word**

The root *l-ʿ-b* denotes activity with no serious object — the child's game, the spectator's amusement. The Qur'an repeatedly pairs it with *lahw*, diversion, as the twin description of worldly life itself: *"The life of this world is naught but play and diversion, and the abode of the Hereafter is better for the reverent"* (6:32; cf. 57:20, 29:64, 47:36). To say the doubters are "playing in doubt" is to diagnose the deepest mechanism of denial the Qur'an anywhere describes: the refusal to treat the ultimate question with ultimate seriousness. It is cheaper than disbelief and easier than faith. Formal disbelief requires a position to defend; earnest inquiry requires labor; but play requires nothing — laughter at the reciter, a witticism about the warning, a shrug at the smoke that is promised. This is why the Qur'an's rebuttals so often take the form of *interrupting the game*: *"Then leave them to plunge into vain talk and play until they meet their Day"* (70:42); *"And on the Day when the Hour arrives, the falsifiers will be ruined"* (30:55–56). The game has a scheduled interruption.

**The Modern Form of the Same Game**

The verse reads with unsettling accuracy against contemporary culture. The dominant posture of the modern age toward the ultimate questions is neither studied atheism nor committed faith but amusement: the infinite scroll, the permanent entertainment, the meme-ification of every serious thing. Doubt has become an environment one plays in rather than a problem one solves. The Qur'an's diagnosis is that this is not intellectual freedom but evasion with a lower cost of admission — and that it is fatal, because it prevents the doubt from ever maturing into either certainty or honest denial. The cure the sūrah proposes is implicit in its structure: seriousness. In the very next verses the smoke arrives — the interruption of the game — and the players are suddenly heard praying (44:12). Everything between verse 9 and verse 12 is the Qur'an's argument that play has an expiry date; the mercy of the warning is that it is announced before the date, not after.

**A Psychological Law**

One further observation belongs here, for the classical commentators sensed it: mockery is not strength but defense. A confident conviction mocks nothing; only a secretly unsettled conscience needs to laugh at the warning in order to keep it at arm's length. Ibn Taymiyyah's school frequently noted that ridicule is the weapon of those who cannot answer — an observation the sūrah itself will confirm when the same people who mocked later beg. The verse is therefore both diagnosis and prediction: because they are playing in doubt, they will not be able to stand when the plaything is removed. The smoke follows as surely as the sentence follows the subject.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:10

> **So keep watch for a day when the sky brings forth manifest smoke,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Watch**

*Fartaqib yawma taʾtī's-samāʾu bi-dukhānin mubīn* — "So watch for the day when the sky comes with a manifest smoke." The verb *irtaqib* — from *r-q-b*, the watchman's vigil, the sentinel's fixed gaze — will return as the sūrah's very last word (44:59, *fartaqib innahum murtaqibūn*), enclosing the chapter in a frame of vigilance. The Prophet ﷺ is ordered to stand at his post and look toward the horizon; the doubters, the final verse will reveal, are standing at their own post looking back. Between the two posts runs the entire history of denial. The smoke announced here became one of the most discussed signs in the Qur'an's eschatology and its history alike, because the sūrah itself — as the exegetes transmit — was heard by the first community as pointing to two fulfillments at once.

**The Two Readings: The Famine, and the Final Sign**

The first reading is historical, and it is carried by the most authentic chain of testimony. Al-Bukhārī records in his Ṣaḥīḥ, in his commentary on precisely this sūrah, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd (d. 32/652):

> *"The Prophet ﷺ, when he saw that the people had turned away from him, said: 'O God, (punish them) with seven (years of famine) like the seven years of Joseph.' So they were stricken with a famine that encompassed everything, until the people ate bones and carrion from hunger, until one of them would see between himself and the sky something like smoke from the effort of hunger. God revealed: 'So keep watch for a day when the sky brings forth manifest smoke, covering the people; this is a painful punishment' (44:10–11). They came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked him to pray for rain, and he did, and they were given rain — and then they returned (to disbelief). The meaning of 'Surely you will revert' (44:15) is their reversion; and 'the terrible striking' (44:16) was the Day of Badr."* (al-Bukhārī 4820–4822; cf. Muslim 2798 for the companion account)

On this reading the smoke is the visual distortion produced by extreme starvation — the famine's own hallucination hanging between the starving and the sky — and the verses were fulfilled within a decade of their revelation, at the famine and at Badr. The second reading is eschatological: the smoke is a literal sign before the Hour, one of the great portents. Muslim records the ḥadīth of Ḥudhayfah ibn Asīd al-Ghifārī:

> *"The Prophet ﷺ looked out at us while we were discussing and said: 'What are you discussing?' They said: 'The Hour.' He said: 'It will not come until you see before it ten signs.' He mentioned: the smoke, the False Messiah, the Beast, the rising of the sun from its place of setting, the descent of Jesus son of Mary, Gog and Magog, three sinkholes — a sinkhole in the east, a sinkhole in the west, and a sinkhole in the Arabian peninsula — and the last of them is a fire that emerges from Yemen, driving the people to their place of assembly."* (Muslim 2901)

According to this reading, transmitted from Ḥudhayfah and ʿAlī and a group of the Companions, the smoke is a future enveloping smoke, which — as the reports collected by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī describe — takes the believer like a passing cold (*zukkām*) while it enters upon the disbeliever with violent affliction.

**Which Reading Governs?**

The exegetes preserved both without forcing a choice, and their instinct is sound: the Qur'an frequently layers an immediate fulfillment and a final one upon a single promise, the first being a pledge of the second. Ibn Kathīr, while personally preferring the eschatological reading for the literal sense, records Ibn Masʿūd's position in full and treats the famine-fulfillment as established history. The practical lesson survives either way, for in both readings the smoke is *mubīn* — manifest — removing the excuse of ambiguity when it comes, and in both readings it arrives precisely where play was taking place. A few commentators, al-Qurṭubī notes, proposed a third reference — the dust raised by the Muslim army at the conquest of Makkah — but this reading remained marginal beside the two that the authentic reports carry.

**The Vigilance the Verse Installs**

Whatever the day's final calendar, the verse's instruction is present tense: *fartaqib* — keep watch. The Qur'an's eschatology is not information for the curious but vigilance for the addressed. Every listener is placed, by this single imperative, at a watch-post: the smoke has been announced; its description has been delivered; what remains is the watching. And watching, in the Qur'an's usage, is not passive waiting but active preparation — the sentry who watches the road is the one who has already decided what he will do when the dust appears on it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:11

> **covering the people. This is a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Smoke That Envelops**

*Yaghshā'n-nās* — "enveloping the people." The verb *ghashiya* denotes an overwhelming that settles upon and covers, as night covers (92:1, *wa'l-layli idhā yaghshā*) and as the calamity of the overwhelming event covers (79:34, *idhā jāʾati'ṭ-ṭāmmah*). The smoke is not a spectacle observed at a distance; it is an environment entered. On the historical reading of Ibn Masʿūd, the enveloping was the famine's totality: it covered the people in the literal sense that nothing of their ordinary life remained above its surface — trade stopped, food stopped, sight itself was covered, since the starving saw the very air as smoke. On the eschatological reading, the enveloping is atmospheric: the sign will not be confined to a region or a class but will cover the people, the believers and the deniers alike, with its differing effects upon each — upon the believer as a cold passing through, upon the denier as a penetrating affliction, as the reports transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī describe. In both readings the verse teaches the same grammar of divine signs: they do not negotiate with human arrangements; they envelop them.

**"This Is a Painful Punishment": Who Speaks?**

The verse's final sentence — *hādhā ʿadhābun alīm* — is short and its speaker disputed, and the dispute itself is instructive. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr record three possibilities. First: it is God's own announcement of what the smoke is — "this is a painful punishment" — the declaration of the Judge naming the sentence. Second: it is the people's own cry under it — they are quoting their suffering, as if the verse were heard as: "(they say:) this is a painful punishment" — the parallel being the cry of the next verse, *"Our Lord, remove from us the punishment."* Third: it is said *to* them by way of rebuke — "this is the painful punishment (you were promised)" — the pattern of 52:13–14: *"The Day when they are thrust into the Fire of Hell with a thrusting: 'This is the Fire which you used to deny.'"* Each reading places the sentence at a different moment — before the envelopment, during it, and as verdict after it — and together they form the complete circuit: announced, experienced, confirmed.

**The Doctrine of the Enveloping**

The verse deserves one further reflection, for it encodes a Qur'anic law of consequences. When the Qur'an describes collective punishment, it consistently describes it as covering: the flood covered the people of Noah (7:64, 11:44), the stones covered the cities of Lot (11:82, 15:74), the famine covered Makkah. Individual sin is answered individually (6:164), but socialized sin — denial made into public culture, mockery made into consensus, play made into an institution — is answered with enveloping consequences that spare no participant, because the culture itself was an envelopment of falsehood over the truth. The smoke is thus described with the same verb as the culture it ends: the people covered themselves with doubt and play, and were covered in return by a manifest smoke. The justice is exact, and the mercy preceding it was equally exact — for the smoke was announced before it came, and between the announcement and the arrival stood an entire lifetime of opportunity, which is the interval in which every reader of this verse is still standing.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:12

> **"Our Lord! Remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Prayer of the Players**

*Rabbanā'kshif ʿannā'l-ʿadhāb innā muʾminūn* — "Our Lord, remove from us the punishment; truly we are believers." The game of verse 9 has ended; the players are on their knees. The verse is reported, on Ibn Masʿūd's account in al-Bukhārī (4821–4822), as fulfilled when the famine-struck Quraysh came to the Prophet ﷺ asking him to pray for rain, Abū Sufyān among their spokesmen, promising belief if relief came. It is also the standing picture of every coerced confession under divine pressure, and the Qur'an has already hung the same portrait in its gallery of Pharaoh: *"until, when he was overtaken by drowning, he said: 'I believe that there is no god but the One in whom the Children of Israel believe'"* (10:90) — a belief pronounced from inside the punishment, and rejected (10:91). Al-Qurṭubī reads the verse grammatically as containing a suppressed condition: "(were You to) remove from us the punishment, (then) we are believers" — and the suppressed condition is the whole tragedy: the faith is conditional on relief, which is to say it is not faith but negotiation.

**Anatomy of Crisis-Belief**

The psychology of the verse is precise and universal, and the exegetes dissected it. Three features mark the prayer. First, its object is correct — they address *our Lord*, the very Lordship verses 7–8 argued for; suffering taught them in an hour what argument taught them in years, and taught it incompletely, since they had already been told. Second, its content is minimal — not repentance, not restitution, not naming the wrong, but a single request: remove it. Third, its claimed credential is asserted, not demonstrated: *truly we are believers* — an oath of belief produced at the moment belief costs nothing and demanded at the moment relief is everything. The Qur'an's judgment on this structure is given in a sister passage of terrible clarity:

> *"But when We remove affliction from them a little, behold they revert; and when great affliction touches them, they despair."* — cf. the pattern of *"Then when We made them taste mercy, they rejoiced; but if evil afflicts them for what their hands have put forth, at once they despair"* (30:36); *"And when man is touched by evil he cries out to Us; then when We bestow on him a favor from Us, he says, 'I was only given it because of knowledge'"* (39:49).

Crisis-belief is real as experience but invalid as contract, because a contract signed under the knife of one's own consequences, with the sole intent of stopping the consequences, binds nothing in the signer; the moment the knife lifts, he reverts — which is exactly what the next verse group will announce (*"Surely you will revert,"* 44:15).

**Why Such a Prayer Is Rejected**

The rejection is not cruelty but logic, and the sūrah's next verses will supply the logic (44:13–15): a reminder had already come in comfort, in clarity, with a trustworthy messenger — and was met with "taught, possessed." God does not honor the prayer of the smoke because honoring it would make the smoke profitable: it would teach every generation that one may mock in prosperity and purchase exemption with a sentence in adversity, after which the entire economy of trial collapses. Al-Ṭabarī records the parallel petitions of the Hereafter — *"Our Lord, we have seen and heard; return us, we will do right"* (32:12); *"My Lord, send me back, that I may do right in what I have left behind"* (23:99–100) — and notes that every one of them is the same sentence in different tense: relief first, righteousness later. The answer is always the same, because the offer was always made earlier, in the other direction: righteousness first, relief after. The believer's life is that reversed order freely chosen; the denier's prayer is that order refused until it can no longer be chosen. Verse 12 is therefore among the Qur'an's great standing warnings — not against praying, for prayer is commanded, but against the plan of postponing sincerity until sincerity is worthless.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:13

> **How can there be a reminder for them, when a clear messenger had come to them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Lock Closing on the Door**

*Annā lahumu'dh-dhikrā wa qad jāʾahum rasūlun mubīn* — "How could the reminder be theirs, when a clear messenger had already come to them?" The word *annā* — "how, from what direction?" — is the Qur'an's instrument of rhetorical impossibility: it does not ask whether, but shows that the direction no longer exists. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Zamakhsharī agree on the verse's logic: the people just heard praying *"truly we are believers"* (44:12) are praying toward a door that their own history has shut. A reminder (*dhikrā*) is by definition something that comes *before* the consequence it warns against — its entire function is advance notice. These people had received the advance notice in its most complete possible form: not a vague portent but *a clear messenger*, face and voice and character, among them for years. To ask now for the removal of punishment on the strength of a sudden belief is to ask that the reminder be delivered twice — and the second delivery would be useless by definition, since it is being demanded only because the first was disobeyed.

**"A Clear Messenger": The Weight of Mubīn**

The messenger is described with the same adjective the Book received in verse 2 — *mubīn*, clear, making plain. On the historical reading the reference is the Prophet ﷺ himself: his clarity was his biography. Forty years before the first revelation he was known to the same men now begging as *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy; he had never told a single lie in commerce, let alone about the heavens. The Qur'an presses this fact upon the doubters elsewhere with devastating simplicity: *"Say: had God willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor made it known to you; indeed I remained among you a lifetime before it — will you not reason?"* (10:16). Ibn Kathīr gathers the family of verses in which the messenger's own life is offered as the authentication of his message: *"Or have they not reflected? Their companion is not possessed"* (7:184); *"You are not, by the blessing of your Lord, a soothsayer nor possessed"* (52:29). A clear messenger is one whose message is legible and whose person is evidence — and both had been placed before Makkah in daylight. The exegetes also note, on the famine reading, that the verse can simultaneously carry the memory of Moses before Pharaoh, which the sūrah will narrate four verses later: the same structure — clear messenger, rejection, prayer too late — ran its full course once already, in the greatest empire on earth.

**The Principle of the Closed Window**

Behind the verse stands a doctrine of divine procedure that the Qur'an repeats at every scale: opportunity is real, bounded, and non-renewable once its term arrives. *"On the day when some of the signs of your Lord arrive, no soul will benefit by its faith if it did not believe before, or earn good by its faith"* (6:158); *"And when they turned away, God turned their hearts"* (61:5); *"How then, when the angels take them, striking their faces and their backs?"* (47:27). The scholars of creed drew from these texts the rule that faith is credited as the free act of an uncoerced heart in the time of capacity; coerced confession in the time of compulsion is testimony under torture, and torture's testimony convicts the one who administers it rather than the one who gives it. Verse 13 is the doctrine stated as rhetoric: the reminder cannot be theirs now, because it was theirs then, and they spent it. The sūrah will sharpen this in the next verse by quoting exactly how they spent it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:14

> **Then they turned away from him and said, "He is taught, possessed."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Slanders and Their Exhaustive Function**

*Fa tawallaw ʿanhu wa qālū muʿallamun majnūn* — "then they turned away from him and said: taught, possessed." Two words, two archetypes. The exegetes observe that these two slanders divide between them the entire space of possible attacks upon a messenger's authority, which is why the Qur'an returns to refuting them across many sūrahs. The first — *muʿallam*, "one who has been taught" — attacks the *source* of the revelation: he learned this from a human teacher; it is borrowed, plagiarized, imported. The second — *majnūn*, "possessed, mad" — attacks the *faculty* of the messenger: his mind is disordered; a jinn speaks through him. Between them the slanderers attempted to close every road by which the message could be true: if it came from outside him, it is plagiarism; if it rose from inside him, it is madness. The Qur'an answers both charges, separately and completely, and the answers are among its most argued passages.

**Against "Taught": The Language Argument**

The slander of instruction is quoted verbatim elsewhere: *"Indeed, We know that they say, 'He has merely been taught by a human being'"* — and the reply is immediate and philological: *"The tongue of the one they attribute it to is foreign, and this is a clear Arabic tongue"* (16:103). The argument is elementary and therefore unanswerable: the rumored instructor was a non-Arab speaker; the alleged product is the summit of Arabic eloquence; no teacher gives what he does not possess. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī press the argument further into the biography: the one accused of secret schooling was the unlettered one (*al-nabī al-ummī*, 7:157–158), known to his people to have read no book and attended no school — *"You never recited any book before this, nor did you write one with your right hand; else the falsifiers might have doubted"* (29:48). Had there been a teacher, Makkah — a small, gossip-saturated town — would have produced him by name; the slander itself never managed to, which is why the Qur'an can quote it in the indefinite: "a human being," no name, because no name existed.

**Against "Possessed": The Order Argument**

The slander of possession is answered across a family of verses: *"Your companion is not possessed"* (81:22, spoken of the Prophet ﷺ in the same passage that swears by the horizon); *"By the grace of your Lord, you are not mad"* (68:2); *"Or have they not reflected? Their companion is not possessed; he is only a plain warner"* (7:184). The argument is empirical: possession produces incoherence — the madman's speech is fragments, breaks, and terrors; the Qur'an is the most internally consistent, structurally sustained, and rhetorically ordered speech in the language, its legal precision its poetry and its poetry its law. Ibn Taymiyyah's school frequently argued the point from the known effects of the jinn upon the afflicted — convulsion, aversion to the divine names, inability to recite — against what was publicly observed of the Prophet ﷺ, whose recitation was the community's daily bread. Add to this the witness of character: madness is not fertile in forty years of unbroken honesty, of returned trusts, of treaties kept against advantage. The two slanders were thus refuted in their own century, by the town that invented them — which is precisely why verse 13 judged the later prayer untimely: the people who invented two mutually exclusive lies against the messenger (taught *and* possessed — a man cannot be simultaneously plagiarist and lunatic in the same breath) had already demonstrated that their problem was not evidence but will.

**The Verdict Stands**

Al-Qurṭubī adds a pastoral note on *fa tawallaw ʿanhu* — "then they turned away from him." The turning away precedes the slander: the diagnosis is not that they investigated and concluded "taught, possessed," but that they had already decided to turn, and manufactured the verdict afterward. This is the Qur'an's constant autopsy of denial: the conclusion is chosen first, and the reasons are recruited afterward. Verse 14 preserves the recruited reasons in the Book forever, so that every later generation can see how small the evidence of denial has always been — two words, and both of them false.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:15

> **We shall indeed remove the punishment a little. Surely you will revert.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Respite and Its Certainty**

*Innā kāshifū'l-ʿadhābi qalīlan innakum ʿāʾidūn* — "Indeed, We will remove the punishment a little; indeed, you will revert." Al-Bukhārī's narration of Ibn Masʿūd (4821–4822) gives the verse its first fulfillment: the Prophet ﷺ prayed, the rain came, the famine broke — and Quraysh reverted to its hostility, as the verse predicted, within the respite it had begged for. The grammar deserves attention. Two *inna* clauses stand back to back, each carrying the same particle of emphasis and certainty: *We will remove* and *you will revert*. God's mercy is announced as certain — and so is their reversion. The verse thereby performs the exact autopsy of crisis-belief that verse 12 displayed: it credits the sincerity of the prayer (the relief really comes), and it records in advance, before the relief has even arrived, the inevitable outcome of the relieved heart. Ibn Masʿūd, al-Ṭabarī reports, heard in *ʿāʾidūn* ("reverters") the return to disbelief, and in the next verse the striking of Badr that followed the reversion.

**Respite as Test, Not Reward**

The doctrine encoded here is one of the Qur'an's most repeated laws of history, and al-Dukhān states it in miniature: the lifting of a punishment is not its vindication of the punished but a second, subtler trial. *"And when We let man taste mercy from Us, he rejoices in it; but if evil afflicts them for what their hands have put forth, then indeed man is ungrateful"* (42:48); *"And when We remove affliction from them a little, behold they revert"* (cf. 23:76's pattern: *"And even when We opened upon them a gate of severe punishment, behold, they were therein in despair"* — while 6:44 describes the fatal variant: *"Then when they forgot that by which they had been warned, We opened upon them the gates of every good thing; until, when they rejoiced in what they were given, We seized them suddenly"*). The exegetes extracted from these texts a practical rule: relief after divine pressure is a summons to gratitude and amendment; relief treated as acquittal becomes the preparation for a heavier account. Pharaoh's courtiers walked the identical road — plague after plague lifted upon a promise of belief, each lifting followed by reversion (7:133–135) — until the sea, which lifts for no one, took them.

**The Alternative Reading: Returning to the Fire**

Al-Qurṭubī records a second reading of *ʿāʾidūn*: "you will return" — to the punishment of the Hereafter, returning upon it again and again, as in *"Whenever they wish, in their grief, to leave it, they are returned unto it"* (22:22). On this reading the verse tightens further: the worldly respite is brief (*qalīlan*), and the return it purchases is to a place from which there is no removal. And a third shading, also recorded: "you will return to God" — the universal return, believer and denier alike, which no respite postpones. All three readings stand together in the classical commentaries, and they give the verse its full terror and mercy at once: the respite is real, brief, and already surrounded by its consequences — exactly like every respite in every life, including the reader's.

**The Modern Lesson of the Second Inna**

Nothing in this verse is confined to seventh-century Makkah. Individuals and nations alike receive their famines — crises, illnesses, crashes, wars — that drive them to prayer, and their rains that dissolve the prayer. The verse's contribution to the philosophy of these cycles is its refusal of surprise: *innakum ʿāʾidūn* is announced before the rain falls. The merciful use of the respite, the sūrah implies, is to treat the second *inna* as if it had been spoken about oneself: assume the reversion is possible in you, since it is certain in the species, and bind the heart to its resolution while the pressure still makes honesty easy — before the relief makes it hard.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:16

> **On the Day when We strike with a terrible striking—surely We are vengeful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Striking and Its Date**

*Yawma nabṭishu'l-baṭshatā'l-kubrā innā muntaqimūn* — "the Day when We strike with the great striking; indeed, We are vengeful." The vocabulary is of a single blow — *baṭshah* — and of its greatness. The verse stands as the sūrah's announced terminus for the cycle it has just traced: the smoke, the prayer, the respite, the reversion — and then the striking. The exegetes record two primary referents. On the historical reading carried by Ibn Masʿūd and recorded in al-Bukhārī (4822), the great striking is the Day of Badr (2/624): the day when the persecuted community of Madinah met the army of Makkah — the same men who had starved, mocked, and tortured the believers — and the Quraysh leadership was broken in a single morning. Ibn Masʿūd swore to it in the ḥadīth: *"As for the striking, it was the Day of Badr."* On the eschatological reading, preferred by a group of exegetes including Ibn Kathīr for the verse's final sense, the great striking is the punishment of the Hereafter or of the Hour itself — the striking of which every earthly striking is the rehearsal. The two readings do not contradict; the Qur'an's method throughout is to let the historical fulfillment stand as the certified pledge of the final one, Badr being to the Hour what the famine's smoke was to the last smoke.

**"Indeed We Are Vengeful": The Ethics of Divine Vengeance**

The closing clause — *innā muntaqimūn* — requires careful reading, for *al-muntaqim* (the Avenger) is among the divine names authenticated by the name-lists transmitted in al-Tirmidhī and others, and vengeance, attributed to God, is not passion but justice executing its sentence after mercy has exhausted its offers. Al-Qurṭubī's treatment is representative: God's *intiqaam* is His requital of the one who persists in wrongdoing after the completion of the proof and the passing of the respite — and it is named vengeance rather than mere punishment because it answers an injury: the injury done to His signs, to His messengers, and to His servants whom Pharaoh's people tortured and Quraysh's chiefs starved. The Qur'an is explicit that the door to this name can be closed from the human side at any time before the term: *"Say to those who believe, let them forgive those who do not hope for the days of God"* (45:14) — and then balances it with *"Whoever avenges himself after having been wronged — upon such there is no way of blame"* (42:41) and its crown, *"but whoever pardons and reconciles, his reward is with God"* (42:40). Mercy is preferred and opened; vengeance is reserved and certain. The sūrah has spent its first sixteen verses showing that every mercy-door was opened to these doubters — the Book, the night, the clear messenger, the famine-warned respite — which is why the verse can announce the striking without apology: *innā muntaqimūn* is pronounced over a file in which every offer is documented as received.

**Badr: The Striking in Historical Detail**

The historical referent deserves its moment, for Badr is the hinge on which the sūrah's argument was visibly performed. The Makkan notables who had pronounced *"taught, possessed"* (44:14) — Abū Jahl foremost among them — were struck down within seven years of the revelation of these verses, in a single valley, by the very community they had driven into exile. The Qur'an elsewhere narrates the day's inner meaning: *"You did not throw when you threw, but God threw"* (8:17); *"When you sought help of your Lord and He answered you"* (8:9) — the Prophet ﷺ's famous night-long supplication in the ʿarīsh before the battle, recorded in Muslim (1763), in which he pleaded the promise until his cloak fell from his shoulders: *"O God, fulfill for me what You have promised me; O God, if this band of Islam is destroyed today, You will not be worshiped on earth"* — and Abū Bakr's quiet word: *"Enough, Messenger of God — He will fulfill for you what He promised you."* Badr was the great striking in miniature: the respite expired, the reversion was recorded, and the blow fell with a precision that the survivors spent the rest of their lives describing.

**The Verse as Vigilance**

The striking is announced, like the smoke, before it arrives — and the announcement is itself a mercy, for a striking announced is a striking still avoidable by anyone within its radius who turns. The sūrah will now widen the lens from Makkah to the ancient world, and show the identical sentence executed in full upon the largest power that ever played the doubters' game: *And indeed We tried before them the people of Pharaoh* (44:17).

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:17

> **And indeed We tried the people of Pharaoh before them, and a noble messenger came unto them:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**History Called as Witness**

*Wa laqad fatannā qablahum qawma Firʿawn* — "And We had already tried, before them, the people of Pharaoh." The verb is *fatannā* — from *f-t-n*, the assayer's fire in which gold is tested and ore is smelted; from it comes *fitnah*, trial, sedition, persecution. The transition is one of the Qur'an's characteristic moves: from the Makkan present to the Egyptian archive, summoned not as story but as precedent. The trial of Pharaoh's people consisted precisely in what Makkah was now undergoing in the mirror: a messenger sent, a choice offered, a decision demanded of power. Al-Rāzī draws out the word *fatannā*: nations are not tried primarily by famine or flood but by prophets — the arrival of a messenger is the supreme *fitnah* of a civilization, because it forces every rank of it to declare what it is. The Qur'an states the law explicitly: *"And every nation, when its messenger comes, is judged between them with justice"* (10:47); *"Did We not take from you an oath... and there came to you a warner?"* (35:37). Pharaoh's Egypt — the greatest superstructure of the ancient world — was tried, and the record of the trial was preserved in the scripture of three religions; the sūrah now reopens the file for Makkah, whose power beside Egypt's was, as verse 37 will say, a question and not an answer.

**"A Noble Messenger": The Rank of Moses**

The messenger is described, before he is named, as *karīm* — noble, honored, generous. Moses upon him peace carries this title because he concentrated in one person the honors the sūrah's audience could measure: chosen by God for Himself — *"And I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed"* (20:13); addressed directly — *"God spoke to Moses with speech"* (4:164); the one whose hand shone and whose staff became a serpent; the deliverer of a nation. The adjective also prepares the contrast the narrative is built on: a *noble* messenger against an ignoble power. Pharaoh, who claimed *"I am your lord, the most high"* (79:24), is stripped by the sūrah of every adjective of honor — he will appear as *"foremost among the prodigal"* (44:31) — while the refugee who stood before him carries *karīm* into the record forever. Ibn Kathīr notes the further resonance: nobility in the Qur'an is not lineage but rank with God — *"Indeed, the most noble of you in God's sight is the most reverent of you"* (49:13) — and Moses' nobility was the nobility of the mission he carried, which the next verse will state in three clauses.

**Why This Story, Here**

The placement of the Moses cycle in al-Dukhān answers the sūrah's own opening argument. The first scene established that the Book descends as mercy and warning (vv. 2–6); the second scene showed Makkah playing in doubt and being answered with smoke, respite, and promised striking (vv. 9–16). The third scene now supplies the certified laboratory record of how that sequence terminates when a nation runs it to the end. Everything Makkah was doing — mocking the messenger, demanding he be pronounced taught or possessed, praying only under pressure, reverting at relief — Pharaoh's people had done in its complete form, with all the resources of an empire behind the refusal. The sūrah is therefore not interrupting its argument with history; it is completing the argument with the only evidence stronger than prophecy: fulfilled prophecy about nations already gone. The camera of revelation now moves with Moses through the court, the confrontation, the night march, and the sea — verse by verse, each a station of the trial.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:18

> **"Deliver God's servants unto me! Truly I am a trustworthy messenger unto you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand That Ends Tyranny**

*An addū ilayya ʿibāda Allāh* — "Deliver to me the servants of God." The demand is the opening move of the confrontation, and its wording is the confrontation's entire theology compressed into one sentence. Moses does not first say "let my people go" — the framing is not ethnic but theological. The Israelites are not claimed in their own name but in God's: *ʿibāda Allāh*, the servants of God. Against Pharaoh's claim of ownership — recorded earlier in the sūrah's neighbor, where Pharaoh describes the Israelites as *"a faction of ours"* (26:54) and his own regime's property — Moses asserts a prior title: they are servants of God before they are workers of Pharaoh, and no state's claim supersedes the Creator's. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of freedom in a single clause: human liberation is never framed as the transfer of a person into his own absolute ownership, but as his recovery by his true Owner, against the usurper. The sister passages carry the same formula: *"So send forth with me the Children of Israel"* (7:105); *"So send forth with us the Children of Israel, and do not punish them"* (20:47). Every verse of the exodus cycle is built on this one demand; everything that follows — the signs, the plagues, the pursuit, the sea — is the universe enforcing it.

**"I Am a Trustworthy Messenger unto You"**

The second clause is Moses' credential: *innī lakum rasūlun amīn* — "indeed I am, for you, a trustworthy messenger." The formula is identical to the one carried by Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ (26:107, 125, 143, 162, 178) — a deliberate litany, the standard credential of prophethood across every nation. *Amīn* — trustworthy, faithful, the keeper of what is entrusted — names the essential office: the messenger does not compose the message, he delivers an entrusted deposit intact. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Moses' claim here is to *amānah* in both directions: faithful toward God in not altering or concealing what he was sent with, and faithful toward the people in not seeking their property or their destruction — *"I do not ask you for any payment; my payment is only with God"* (10:72, and the refrain across 11:29, 26:109, 34:47). The exegetes note with pleasure that the same title, *al-Amīn*, was the pre-revelational name of the Prophet ﷺ among the very Quraysh who later called him *"taught, possessed"* (44:14) — the trustworthiness certified by decades of their own commercial witness, against slander manufactured in a single season.

**"Unto You": The Messenger Belongs to the Audience**

One small word carries deep meaning: *lakum* — "unto you," "for you." Moses does not present himself as *against* Egypt, though Egypt will make him its enemy; he presents himself as *for* it. The messenger's coming is an offer of dignity to the very court that will try to destroy him, for the call is extended to Pharaoh's own household — *"Go, you and your brother, with My signs, and slacken not in My remembrance; go both of you to Pharaoh, for he has transgressed; yet speak to him a gentle word; perhaps he may be reminded, or fear"* (20:42–44). Even in this sūrah's compressed version, the gentleness survives in the address itself: Moses speaks to the empire as *unto you* — a messenger arrived for your sake, carrying your opportunity. That the opportunity was refused does not alter the character of the offer, and the sūrah's readers are meant to notice that Makkah's Messenger ﷺ stood in the identical posture toward his city: *for you*, until his city chose the smoke.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:19

> **And rise not against God; surely I have come to you with a manifest authority.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prohibition of Height Against Heaven**

*Wa lā taʿlū ʿalā Allāh* — "and do not rise high against God," or "do not exalt yourselves against God." The verb *ʿalā* — to rise, to ascend, to claim height — is the same verb Pharaoh will later use of himself in the Qur'an's verdict upon him: *"Indeed, he was high-handed (ʿāliyan) among the prodigal"* (44:31, as verse 31 will state: *"foremost among the prodigal"* — *min al-ʿālīn*). The prohibition therefore strikes at the exact posture of the regime: Pharaoh's state was an architecture of height — pyramids, towers, the claim *"I am your lord, the most high"* (79:24), the boast *"Am I not better than this one who is contemptible?"* (43:52). Moses' command tells the court, in its own vocabulary, that there is one direction in which height is impossible: upward, against God. Ibn Kathīr explains the verse as: do not be too arrogant to follow Him, to accept His proofs, and to believe in His signs; al-Qurṭubī adds that *ʿuluww* against God names every refusal that is rooted in self-magnification rather than in doubt — the refusal of one who knows but will not descend from his pedestal. The Qur'an treats this species of refusal as the primal sin: it was the reason Iblīs fell — *"he refused and was arrogant (istakbara)"* (2:34) — and it is why the Qur'an's central promise concerns precisely the proud: *"I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant in the earth without right"* (7:146). Height claimed against heaven is, in the Qur'an's physics, the only fall that begins at the top.

**"A Manifest Authority": The Evidence Arrives**

The verse's second clause gives the warrant for the first: *innī ataytukum bi-sulṭānin mubīn* — "indeed I have come to you with a manifest authority." *Sulṭān* — from *s-l-ṭ*, dominance, proof, warrant — is the Qur'an's technical word for the evidence that accompanies every messenger: *"We sent no messenger except with the signs of his people"* (14:4). For Moses the *sulṭān mubīn* was the two physical signs of the hand and the staff — *"And press your hand to your side; it will come out white without blemish — another sign; that We may show you some of Our greatest signs"* (20:22–23) — delivered in the court's own audience hall before its own magicians (20:56–70; 26:30–33; 7:106–117). The exegetes emphasize the procedural justice of this: God does not demand submission of Pharaoh on Moses' word alone; the demand for deliverance (v. 18) is backed by public, repeatable, court-witnessed proof. Al-Rāzī draws the epistemic principle: obedience to a messenger is not obedience to a man's opinion but compliance with demonstrated evidence — the *sulṭān* converts the encounter from a contest of wills into a trial of proof, which is why the court's only available answers were denial, magic-counterfeiting, and force — answers that concede the argument.

**Why Arrogance Is the True Crime**

The pairing of the two clauses teaches the sūrah's psychology of denial in miniature: the command *do not rise against God* is necessary precisely because the evidence *manifest authority* has already arrived. Where proof is absent, humility and arrogance are indistinguishable; where proof is present, every continued refusal is an act of self-elevation — the refuser is not withholding assent from the evidence but asserting that his position is higher than it. This is the structure the sūrah diagnosed in Makkah (*"playing in doubt,"* 44:9) and will diagnose again in the materialists of verse 34–36: not the absence of evidence but the presence of height. The modern reader knows the posture intimately — the refusal that cannot name its reason and therefore changes the subject, mocks the claimant, or legislates the question away. The verse names it with two words and forbids it: *lā taʿlū ʿalā Allāh* — stop climbing against the sky; the evidence is already on the ground, waiting for you to come down.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:20

> **Truly I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord, lest you stone me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet's Defensive Prayer**

*Wa innī ʿudhtu bi-rabbī wa rabbikum an tarjumūn* — "and I have sought refuge in my Lord and your Lord, that you do not stone me." The verb *ʿādh* — to seek refuge, to take shelter — is the posture of the one who knows the threat is real and knows also where protection lives. The threat named is *rajm*, stoning: the capital punishment of the ancient crowd, and the standing sentence pronounced by defiant peoples upon their prophets. The sister verses preserve the pattern: the people of Yā Sīn told their messengers, *"If you do not desist, we will surely stone you"* (36:18); Abraham's own father threatened him with the same word — *"If you do not desist, O Abraham, I will surely stone you"* (19:46); Shuʿayb's notables plotted expulsion and worse. Stoning is the democracy of the defiant crowd: no judge, no procedure, only the collective arm. That Moses names it aloud shows the realism of prophetic speech — he does not pretend the court is harmless; he assesses it accurately and then does the only thing that changes the equation.

**"My Lord and Your Lord": The Leveling Clause**

The verse's theological center is its double attribution: *rabbī wa rabbikum* — my Lord and your Lord. In Pharaoh's Egypt this clause is itself an act of war against the state religion. Pharaoh's regime claimed a vertical monopoly: divinity flowed down from the palace; the Pharaoh was the mediator between heaven and the Nile, between heaven and the people. Moses' sentence installs a horizontal correction in the middle of the throne room: the Lord to whom I take refuge is *the same* Lord who owns you. The tyrant and the prophet stand before one Master; the tyrant's claimed divinity is thereby demoted in a single clause to a subjecthood he cannot refuse without admitting the clause is true. Ibn Kathīr notes that Moses makes this attribution at the moment of maximum danger precisely to teach it: refuge is not taken in any power of the regime — not in its law, its patronage, or its tolerance — but over its head, in the One who is above it and above Moses alike. The exegetes also divide the two halves devotionally: *my Lord* — the intimacy of the one who knows Him and takes shelter in Him; *your Lord* — the summons to the opponents: He is not my partisan, He is your Owner too; you will answer to the same shelter I am entering.

**Two Readings of the Threat**

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī record two readings of *an tarjumūn*. The literal: lest you stone me — kill me by stoning; and the figurative: lest you assault me with words — curse, slander, false accusation. Both readings stood in the classical commentaries, and both were fulfilled in the careers of the prophets: the assault of tongues preceded the assault of hands in every prophetic confrontation, from Noah's "madman" (54:9) to Makkah's *"taught, possessed"* (44:14). The verse therefore arms the believer with a complete defense against both: against slander, refuge keeps the heart from being governed by it; against violence, refuge keeps the soul from fearing any hand more than the Hand above all hands. The Prophet ﷺ taught the same reflex in the words he used to open every matter of weight — the *istiʿādhah*, *"I seek refuge in God..."* — and in the sūrah of refuge itself: *"Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind"* (114:1). Moses' sentence is the doctrine in its oldest recorded form: when the crowd picks up its stones, the prophet picks up his Lord.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:21

> **"But if you believe me not, then keep away from me."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ethics of Withdrawal**

*Wa in lam tuʾminū lī fa-ʿtazilūn* — "and if you do not believe me, then withdraw from me," or "leave me alone." This is the final clause of Moses' address to the court, and it is among the Qur'an's great statements of the ethics of non-coercion. Moses does not threaten, does not bargain, does not curse; he offers the third option that every honest call must offer: separation. *Iʿtazilūn* — from *ʿ-z-l*, separation, withdrawal — asks for nothing but distance: do not believe, but do not persecute; keep your unbelief, and leave me my mission. Ibn Kathīr explains the verse as: if you do not accept what I bring, then at least refrain from harming me — *"let there be peace between us until God judges between us"*; al-Rāzī reads it as the declaration of mutual non-interference: I have nothing to do with you and you have nothing to do with me, until the Day of decision. The verse thereby draws the Qur'an's line between rejection and persecution: a people may disbelieve a messenger — belief cannot be compelled (*"There is no compulsion in religion,"* 2:256) — but it may not harm him, for harming the messenger is not the management of disagreement but the assault on the channel of mercy itself.

**The Prophet ﷺ's Identical Posture**

The sūrah's first audience would have heard the verse as a mirror, for the Prophet ﷺ stood toward Quraysh in exactly this offer. The Qur'an gave him the same vocabulary of separation: *"Say: O disbelievers... to you your religion, and to me mine"* (109:1, 6); *"And if they deny you, say: for me my deed, and for you your deed; you are innocent of what I do, and I am innocent of what you do"* (10:41); *"So leave me alone with whoever denies this discourse"* (68:44). The pattern is legislated, not incidental: revelation offers every community the standing choice of peaceful separation, and it records the choice's refusal as the crime that brings the punishment. Pharaoh's regime refused separation — it pursued, tortured, and planned the killing of the very boys of the Israelites (2:49; 40:25) — and Makkah refused it too, driving the believers into the mountain pass, into Abyssinia, and finally out of the city. In both histories the verse's offered peace was answered with escalation, and in both histories the escalation ended at the sea and at Badr.

**Why Separation Is a Mercy Even to the Refuser**

One further logic deserves statement. Withdrawal protects the refuser as well as the messenger. Coexistence without persecution leaves the refuser's door open: he remains able to observe, to reconsider, to convert — as the magicians of Pharaoh's court did, within the very audience where Moses spoke, falling prostrate the moment the evidence separated from the spectacle (20:70; 26:46–48). Violence against the messenger, by contrast, locks the door from the inside: the violent refuser has committed himself to a position he can no longer retreat from without admitting atrocity, and so escalates until the punishment arrives. The Qur'an's repeated offer of separation is therefore mercy administered to both parties at once — and the history recorded in the next verses is what happens when mercy's minimum terms are refused. Moses has now completed his address — demand, credential, prohibition, refuge, offer of peace. The court's answer will not be recorded; instead the sūrah passes directly to Moses' appeal over the court's head, in the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:22

> **Then he called unto his Lord, "These are a guilty people."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Complaint Ascends**

*Fa daʿā rabbahu anna hāʾulāʾi qawmun mujrimūn* — "Then he called upon his Lord: 'These are a guilty people.'" The court having answered with silence, threat, and refusal, Moses turns from the horizontal axis to the vertical one. The verb is *daʿā* — called, invoked, complained — and its object is not a request but a report: he does not ask for anything in this clause; he files the finding. *Mujrimūn* — criminals, those cut off from what is right, from *j-r-m*, to sever, to earn guilt — is the verdict of the trial the whole narrative has been conducting. The same sentence, the sūrah's introduction recalls, stands at the end of the previous sūrah as the echo of the Prophet's own lament — *"O my Lord, truly these are a people who believe not"* (43:88) — now returned in Moses' voice across four thousand years. The exegetes note the adab of the complaint: Moses does not ask for destruction — he states the guilt and leaves the sentence to the Judge. The pattern is the prophetic standard for the prayer of the oppressed: describe the wrong accurately, and hand the jurisdiction upward.

**The Sister Complaints of Noah and Moses**

The Qur'an preserves three great complaints of this kind, and reading them together yields the doctrine of the verse. Noah's, at the end of nine hundred and fifty years of refusal: *"My Lord, do not leave upon the earth from among the disbelievers an inhabitant"* (71:26) — the demand pronounced only after the term was announced complete. Moses' fuller version, in Sūrah Yūnus: *"Our Lord, You have given Pharaoh and his notables ornament and wealth in the life of this world, our Lord, that they may lead astray from Your way. Our Lord, blot out their wealth and harden their hearts, so that they will not believe till they see the painful punishment"* (10:88) — and the divine answer recorded in the next verse: *"He said: 'Your petition has been answered'"* (10:89). And the Prophet ﷺ's own instance, gentler in form but identical in structure: the report at Ḥunayn and elsewhere of his complaint to his Lord about his people, and the sūrah's own echo at 43:88. Al-Qurṭubī draws the juridical lesson from the family: the prayer against the oppressor is lawful for the oppressed prophet and believer alike, provided it follows the completion of the proof and the passing of the respite — for the Prophet ﷺ taught the principle in its universal form, in the ḥadīth of Ibn ʿAbbās sent to Muʿādh in Yemen:

> *"And beware the prayer of the oppressed, for between it and God there is no veil."* (al-Bukhārī 1496; Muslim 19)

**The Filing, Then the Sentence**

Verse 22 is therefore the hinge of the exodus cycle: everything before it is offer (demand, proof, prohibition, refuge, separation); everything after it is execution. The structure teaches that divine punishment never enters a history until a complaint of this order has been filed — the proof completed, the respite exhausted, the record sealed. It is a doctrine of terrible reassurance in both directions: for the oppressed, that their case has a court and their complaint a clerk; for the oppressor, that every day of unfiled respite is mercy still being administered, and that the filing, once made, is never answered with dismissal. The next verses open the execution phase: the night order, the sea left at rest, and the drowned host.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:23

> **"Set forth with My servants by night; you will indeed be pursued."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night Order**

*Fa asri bi-ʿibādī laylan innakum muttabaʿūn* — "Then set forth with My servants by night; you will indeed be pursued." The divine address now switches from Moses' complaint to God's operation, and its first word is an order to move. *Isri* — from *s-r-y*, traveling by night, the night-march; the same verb the Qur'an uses of the Prophet ﷺ's Night Journey (*"Glory to Him who took His servant on a night-journey"* — 17:1). The command carries the servants' title again — *ʿibādī*, "My servants" — the third time the sūrah's exodus cycle has named them as God's property (v. 18): the deliverance is framed as the Owner reclaiming what is His. And the command anticipates the enemy: *innakum muttabaʿūn* — "you will be followed." God does not deliver Israel into an empty landscape; He delivers them into a pursuit, with the pursuit itself scheduled into the plan. The exegetes, al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr among them, note the military precision of the revelation: the time of departure (night, covering the movement), the direction (toward the sea, as 26:52–53 and 20:77 will specify), and the certainty of pursuit — each element announced before it happens, so that when the chariots appear on the horizon at dawn, Israel will know this too was in the plan: *"And Pharaoh sent callers into the cities: 'These are indeed a small faction; and they have enraged us; and we are all vigilant'"* (26:53–56). The sister verse states the operation's timetable: *"And We revealed to Moses and his brother: 'Take for your people houses in Egypt, and make your houses a qiblah, and establish the prayer; and give good tidings to the believers'"* (10:87) — preparation, worship, then the night march.

**Why the Plan Includes the Pursuit**

The theological weight of *innakum muttabaʿūn* deserves its own pause, for it is one of the Qur'an's deepest lessons about deliverance. God could have delivered Israel without Pharaoh ever waking — an angel at every door, a deeper sleep over Egypt. Instead He ordained a deliverance with pursuit in it, ending at the sea with no visible exit, so that the deliverance would be remembered as deliverance. A rescue unnoticed is a rescue unremembered; a people carried out of slavery while asleep would have woken free without ever knowing the cost. The Qur'an states the resulting memory-law explicitly elsewhere: *"And remember when We parted the sea for you and saved you and drowned the people of Pharaoh while you looked on"* (2:50) — *while you looked on*. The pursuit was the instrument by which the deliverance became visible, and therefore memorable, and therefore transmissible — which is why the exodus is still recited in this sūrah four millennia later, and why the Prophet ﷺ fasted ʿĀshūrāʾ, the day God saved Moses from Pharaoh, and commanded its fast (al-Bukhārī 2004; Muslim 1130). The believer's trials are planned on the same principle: the pressure is not evidence of abandonment; it is the scheduled precondition of a deliverance designed to be witnessed.

**The Psychology of the Night March**

The command also teaches the discipline of the delivered. Israel left at night — in haste, with borrowed ornaments (20:87), dough still unleavened, moving under cover toward an appointment they could not see. Faith, at the moment of deliverance, looks like walking into darkness on a word. The Qur'an preserves Moses' instruction to his people in that hour in Sūrah Ṭā Hā: *"And indeed We revealed to Moses: 'Set forth with My servants by night and strike for them a dry path in the sea; you will not fear being overtaken, and you will not dread'"* (20:77). Two negations — no fear of overtaking, no dread of drowning — given in advance, before the path exists. This is the structure of every commanded movement in the Qur'an: the instruction arrives before the road, and the road appears under the first step.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:24

> **And leave the sea at rest; truly they will be a drowned host."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterintuitive Order**

*Watruki'l-baḥra rahwan innahum jundun mughraqūn* — "And leave the sea at rest (motionless, open); indeed, they are a host to be drowned." This is one of the Qur'an's most remarkable operational commands. Israel has crossed on the dry path struck through the sea (20:77); behind them Pharaoh's host is entering the same corridor. Every human instinct now orders Moses to close the trap: command the waters to return, drown the pursuers immediately, guarantee safety. The divine command is the opposite — leave the sea as it is, standing open, until the last of the enemy has entered it. Al-Ṭabarī explains *rahwan* as: still, at rest, its parted walls standing; Ibn Kathīr reports that Moses, having delivered his people, was inclined to strike the sea to close it — and was commanded not to, so that the entire host, to its last soldier, would be inside the corridor when the waters resumed. The verse thereby teaches a doctrine of divine timing: God's sentences are not executed in haste; they are executed in full. A trap closed early catches part of the enemy; a trap closed at the appointed moment catches the entire regime.

**The Drowned Host: The Verdict Executed**

*Innahum jundun mughraqūn* — "indeed, they are a drowned host." The sentence is announced in the same breath as the command to leave the sea open, so that the reader watches the drowning happen as already-decided fact. The Qur'an returns to this event repeatedly — *"And We drowned the people of Pharaoh while you looked on"* (2:50); *"And We seized him and his host and cast them into the sea"* (28:40); *"So We took him and his host and flung them into the sea, and he was blameworthy"* (51:40); *"And We drowned Pharaoh's people, all of them"* (26:66) — because the drowning is the archetype of every drowned regime after it: the superstructure, the chariots, the boast, the title "lord most high," all settled at the bottom of the same sea in a single morning. The sūrah will spend its next four verses (25–28) inventorying what that morning confiscated. Two details complete the scene. First, the Qur'an preserves Pharaoh's deathbed confession — *"I believe that there is no god but the One in whom the Children of Israel believe"* (10:90) — and its rejection: *"Now? And you had disobeyed before and were of the corrupters?"* (10:91) — the crisis-belief of 44:12 performed on the largest screen in history. Second, the Qur'an states the divine purpose of preserving the body: *"So today We will save you in your body, that you may be a sign for those after you"* (10:92) — the drowned king displayed across the centuries as the exhibit the sūrah is now reopening for Makkah.

**The Sea at Rest as a Mirror of the Believer's Heart**

The exegetes drew one further lesson from *leave the sea at rest*: the delivered servant is not to seize the instruments of vengeance into his own hand. Israel's safety was complete the moment the path opened; the destruction of the pursuer was God's affair, executed at God's minute. The believer's parallel posture is the one the Qur'an legislates everywhere: *"And whoever avenges himself after being wronged — upon such there is no blame"* is immediately balanced by *"but whoever pardons and reconciles, his reward is with God"* (42:41–42), and the Prophet ﷺ's own practice — never taking personal revenge, as ʿĀʾishah testified (al-Bukhārī 3560; Muslim 2327) — is the embodied commentary. Leave the sea at rest: walk your path, keep your appointment, and let the waters of justice close at the moment their Owner has written.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:25

> **How many gardens and springs did they leave behind,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory of Confiscation Begins**

*Kam tarakū min jannātin wa ʿuyūn* — "How many gardens and springs they left behind!" The particle *kam* — "how many" — is exclamatory, not interrogative: it does not ask for a count but marvels at one. The Qur'an's camera now pans slowly across abandoned Egypt, item by item, and the first items are the two foundations of every ancient civilization's wealth: gardens (*jannāt*, enclosed, irrigated estates — the word itself means "covered ground," a paradise walled in from the desert) and springs (*ʿuyūn*, the water-sources that made the gardens possible). The Nile's system of canals and basins had turned Egypt into the ancient world's granary; Pharaoh's boast in Sūrah al-Zukhruf cited it: *"Is not the kingdom of Egypt mine, and these rivers flowing beneath me?"* (43:51). The inventory now opens with precisely what the boast cited — and declares it left behind. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note that the verse's force lies in the combination of marvel and immediacy: estates that had passed through generations of ownership were vacated in a single morning, by a people who had planned, a day earlier, to keep them forever.

**The Doctrine of Leaving-Behind**

The verb *tarakū* — they left — is one of the Qur'an's great eschatological words, and al-Dukhān uses it in its historical form before deploying it in its personal one. Every human being leaves behind exactly this catalogue: property, position, pleasures, projects. The Qur'an states the law explicitly: *"You will not inherit the earth, nor any of it — you are merely temporary tenants"* is its sense in *"We have made you vicegerents upon the earth"* (6:165); *"And how many a generation have We destroyed before them, who were better in wealth and outward show?"* (19:74); *"And the inheritance of the heavens and the earth belongs to God"* (57:10). The Companions lived this verse's doctrine visibly: when the Prophet ﷺ died, his estate consisted of his mule, his weapons, and a piece of land he had given to charity (al-Bukhārī 2739, from ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥārith); and ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb wept when he saw the storehouses of the Persian treasury opened upon Madinah, saying: *"By God, these are not opened upon a people except that God sends enmity between them"* — and he was right (al-Bukhārī 3127, from ʿAmr ibn ʿAwf, in the fuller account). The gardens and springs of verse 25 are thus not archaeology but arithmetic: everything any reader owns is on loan, and the terms of the loan are this verse.

**Why the Inventory Begins with Beauty**

The sūrah begins its confiscation list with the beautiful and the productive — gardens, springs — rather than with palaces or armies, and the order is deliberate. Gardens and springs are wealth in its living form: they require tending, they yield year after year, they are the picture of a civilization at peace with its own future. To confiscate them first is to confiscate the future itself — for a people with no future, gardens are meaningless. The Qur'an's method throughout is to show the deniers not merely destroyed but *disinherited of time*: the gardens they planted for their grandchildren were inherited by strangers within a generation. The sūrah's opening doctrine supplies the frame: the year's affairs are distinguished by command from God's presence (44:4); a garden is a bundle of distinguished affairs — water scheduled, growth apportioned — and when the Owner recalls His commands, the garden remains but the gardener's tenure ends. Verse 25 is the first line of the audit; the next three verses will complete it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:26

> **sown fields and a noble dwelling,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Working Land and the Honored Residence**

*Wa zurūʿin wa maqāmin karīm* — "and sown fields and a noble station." The inventory moves from the estate's water and orchards to its two visible faces: *zurūʿ* — the sown fields, the grain under cultivation, the annual crop that fed the empire; and *maqām karīm* — the noble standing-place, the honored residence. Al-Ṭabarī reports that *maqām* here refers to the places of assembly, the pulpits of authority, the positions where the notables stood and sat in their rank; al-Qurṭubī adds the palaces and the dwellings themselves. The pairing covers the civilization's two products: food and honor — the field that sustains the body and the station that sustains the self-image. Both were left behind, and the sūrah's adjective for the second item is its quiet masterstroke: *karīm*, noble, honored — the same adjective the messenger bore in verse 17 (*"a noble messenger"*). The Qur'an has placed the nobility of the regime and the nobility of the prophet in the same verse-group, and the narrative has decided which one survived: the noble dwelling sank into history; the noble messenger's name is recited in every prayer of every day until the end of time.

**The Field as Time-Machine**

A sown field is the most honest image of postponed reward: seed buried in autumn against a harvest the sower may never see. Egypt's fields were the physical form of the regime's confidence in its own continuity — the state that plants for next year is the state that believes in next year. The drowning confiscated not the present but the future: the standing crop belonged to whoever survived, and the survivor was the people Egypt had enslaved. The Qur'an states the transfer explicitly in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ: *"So it was, and We made the Children of Israel inherit them"* (26:59); and in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: *"And We made the people who were oppressed to inherit the eastern and western parts of the land which We had blessed"* (7:137). Al-Rāzī draws the principle: sown fields left behind are the picture of every deferred plan of the heedless — the retirement, the empire, the legacy, the five-year plan of a soul that did not budget for its own departure. The field is left sown; the reaper is a stranger. The Prophet ﷺ stated the law in one sentence:

> *"If the Hour were to rise while one of you is holding a palm-seedling, and he is able to plant it before he stands up, let him plant it."* (Aḥmad 12902; al-Bukhārī in al-Adab al-Mufrad 479)

— not because the plan will outlive the planter, but because the act of planting, done as worship, is its own completed harvest. Egypt's error was not that it planted; it was that it believed the planting made it immortal.

**The Noble Dwelling's Descendants**

Every reader owns a *maqām karīm* of some scale: a title, an office, a reputation, a seat at a table. The verse's pedagogy is not the prohibition of such stations — the Qur'an does not forbid honor, and it itself conferred the title *karīm* upon the messenger — but the correction of their tenure. A noble station held as God's trust survives its holder as legacy; a noble station held as Pharaoh held his — *"Is not the kingdom of Egypt mine?"* (43:51) — is confiscated with the holder. The difference between the two tenures is exactly the difference the sūrah has been tracing from its first verse: the merciful descent of affairs from the Presence (44:4–6) versus the proud height claimed against it (44:19). The dwelling stays; the deed is rewritten.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:27

> **and prosperity in which they rejoiced?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pleasure Itself Confiscated**

*Wa naʿmatin kānū fīhā fākihīn* — "and the prosperity in which they were delighting." The inventory reaches its subtlest item. *Naʿmah* — softness, ease, the pleasantness of life, pleasure-as-condition — is distinct from the possessions that preceded it: gardens, fields, and dwellings are the instruments of enjoyment; *naʿmah* is the enjoyment itself. The Qur'an elsewhere uses the word for the same target: *"Then you will surely be asked that Day about the delight (naʿīm)"* (102:8). And the verse adds the verb that completes the diagnosis: *fākihīn* — delighting, taking pleasure, being amused in it; from *f-k-h*, fruit and its enjoyment, the same root as *fākihūn*, the fruit-eaters of Paradise (52:18) — but here the delighting is the worldly kind, the amusement of a civilization that had turned its prosperity into its religion. Al-Qurṭubī notes that *naʿmah* can also be read *niʿmah* — blessing — on which reading the verse confiscates the gratitude-structure itself: the blessing was given, and they rejoiced in the gift while ignoring the Giver.

**Rejoicing as Indictment**

Why is rejoicing indicted here? Not because joy is forbidden — the Qur'an commands its own joy: *"Say: in God's bounty and in His mercy — in that let them rejoice"* (10:58). The indictment is against rejoicing *in the wrong register*: delighting in prosperity as though it were the achievement and the destination of the self, rather than the distinguished affair of a single year (44:4) administered by the Lord of verses 6–8. The Qur'an gives this species of joy a name — *faraḥ* untethered from gratitude — and repeats its verdict: *"That was because you used to exult on earth without right, and because you used to be insolent"* (40:75); *"And when We let man taste mercy from Us, he rejoices in it"* (42:48) — rejoicing in the mercy while never asking about the Merciful. Pharaoh's regime had perfected this joy: its monuments were the architecture of amusement, its religion the sanctification of its own permanence. The confiscation of verse 27 is therefore the deepest of the four: the gardens can be replanted, the fields resown, the dwellings reoccupied — but the joy that made all of them an idol is confiscated without replacement, because it was never a possession to begin with; it was a posture of the heart, and the heart stood upon it when the waters returned.

**The Modern Prosperity's Same Audit**

The verse reads with surgical precision against the modern consumer order, which has institutionalized *naʿmah* as the meaning of life — entertainment, comfort, novelty, the permanent amusement economy. The Qur'an does not ask for the abolition of prosperity; it asks for its re-registration — rejoicing in it as bounty (10:58), spending from it as trust (2:3), and remembering, before the year's affairs are distinguished again, that every *naʿmah* is a lease. The closing question-mark of the verse in the sūrah's cadence — *and prosperity in which they rejoiced?* — is the audit's signature: an astonishment offered to every reader about his own delight, and an invitation to answer it before the morning in which one's own inventory is recited.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:28

> **Thus it was. And We bequeathed it unto another people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Transfer of the Estate**

*Kadhālika wa awrathnāhā qawman ākharīn* — "Thus it was, and We made another people inherit it." The particle *kadhālika* — "thus," "so it was" — is the Qur'an's seal upon a completed demonstration: the file is closed, the precedent certified. The verb that follows is *awrathnā* — from *w-r-th*, inheritance — and it names the transaction by which Egypt's confiscated catalogue changed hands. The Qur'an states the same transfer twice elsewhere: *"So it was, and We made the Children of Israel inherit them"* (26:59); *"And We made the people who were oppressed to inherit the eastern and western parts of the land which We had blessed, and the beautiful word of your Lord was fulfilled upon the Children of Israel because of what they patiently endured"* (7:137). The inheritor is described with maximum anonymity — *another people* — and the anonymity is the point: the estate does not pass to a named dynasty but to whoever the decree appoints; the previous owners are not even mourned, as the next verse will state.

**The Law of Inheritance**

From this verse and its sisters the exegetes extracted the Qur'an's law of civilizational succession, and Ibn Kathīr gathers the supporting texts: *"And if you turn away, He will substitute another people in your place; then they will not be the likes of you"* (47:38); *"He is able to remove you and bring a new creation in your place, and that is not difficult for God"* (35:16–17); *"My righteous servants shall inherit the earth"* (21:105). The law has three clauses. First, entitlement to the earth is tenancy, not ownership — *"We made you vicegerents upon the earth"* (6:165). Second, tenancy is conditioned on righteousness, not on power, race, or antiquity — Pharaoh's Egypt had all three, and lost the tenancy in one morning. Third, the transfer is executed by God alone, at His timing, by His instrument — here the sea, there the drought, elsewhere the invading army or the internal decay: *"How many a town We destroyed while it was wrongdoing, so it is fallen upon its roofs, and how many an abandoned well and a raised palace!"* (22:45). The verse is therefore at once history and warning: to Makkah, whose wealth the sūrah has just threatened with the same confiscation (*"their property could be taken from them at any moment,"* as the classical commentary puts it); and to every subsequent reader whose *maqām karīm* he mistakes for a deed in his own name.

**The Comfort Hidden in the Transfer**

The verse carries a second doctrine, for the oppressed as well as the oppressors. The estate of Egypt passed to the very people it had enslaved — the boys whose slaughter Pharaoh had ordered (2:49; 7:141) were the ones who inherited his gardens. The Qur'an states the principle explicitly: *"And We desired to confer favor upon those who were oppressed in the land, and to make them leaders, and to make them the inheritors"* (28:5). Patience under oppression, the sūrah teaches, is not passive waiting; it is the quiet accumulation of the inheritance, administered in heaven while the oppressor is still drafting his plans on earth. Verse 28 closes the inventory, and verse 29 delivers the final verdict of the drowned — the verse that made the classical scholars say that no tyrant's death in the Qur'an is recorded without this commentary attached.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:29

> **Neither Heaven nor earth wept for them, nor were they granted respite.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Haunted the Classical Commentaries**

*Fa mā bakat ʿalayhimu's-samāʾu wa'l-arḍu wa mā kānū munẓarīn* — "Neither the heaven nor the earth wept for them, nor were they granted respite." This is among the most discussed verses of the sūrah, because it attributes grief to the cosmos. The exegetes divided over its register, and al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr preserve both camps. The figurative reading: the expression is Arabic idiom for a death unmourned — so complete was their destruction that nothing lamented it; the greatest empire on earth died and the universe did not notice. The literal reading, carried by reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities: heaven and earth do in fact weep for the believer, and their weeping for him is withheld from the likes of Pharaoh's people. Ibn ʿAbbās's report, transmitted in the tafsīr literature, explains the mechanism: every servant has a gate in heaven through which his provision descends and his deeds ascend; when the believer dies, the gate misses him and weeps, and the places on earth where he prayed and remembered God weep for him — but Pharaoh's people had left no trace of worship on the earth and sent no good deed up to heaven, so nothing in either world had any reason to mourn.

**The Places That Remember**

The literal reading is supported by the Qur'an's own theology of creation's awareness — *"The seven heavens and the earth and whatever is in them glorify Him; there is not a thing that does not glorify Him with His praise, but you do not understand their glorification"* (17:44) — and by the reports the exegetes gathered: al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110/728), commenting on this very verse, said: *"They used to say: the earth weeps for the one who dies who used to worship upon her"*; and the narrations on the verse collected in al-Tirmidhī's tafsīr and in Ibn Kathīr include the tradition that when the believer dies, his places of prayer and his gates of ascent miss him. Even where the chains are discussed by the critics, the doctrine they carry is Qur'anically anchored: the cosmos is not inert backdrop but witness (41:20–21, *"Their ears, their eyes, and their skins will testify against them"*); the earth that received a forehead in prostration received a record (99:4, *"That Day it will report its news"*). The tyrant's tragedy is thus total: he used the earth and left no memory in it; he claimed the heaven and left no file in it. He dies unmourned in both worlds at once.

**"Nor Were They Granted Respite"**

The verse's final clause — *wa mā kānū munẓarīn* — "nor were they granted respite" — completes the sentence with procedural justice. Respite (*inẓār*) is the Qur'an's name for the mercy-administered delay the sūrah itself dramatized for Makkah: the smoke, the prayer, the lifted famine, the second chance (44:10–15). Pharaoh's people received their respite in full — the plagues, each with its promise of removal upon belief (7:133–135), the years of invitation, the magicians' public conversion as the last demonstration (26:46–50). When the term arrived, therefore, it arrived without further delay: the sea closed at its scheduled minute. Al-Qurṭubī draws the rule: respite is the mercy before the sentence, and its exhaustion is the sentence's justification. The verse's comfort runs in the opposite direction for the believer: the one whose places of prayer will weep for him has spent his respite building mourners in the earth and files in heaven — and that construction is available to every reader, today, in the next prostration.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:30

> **And We delivered the Children of Israel from a humiliating punishment—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deliverance Named as Humiliation's End**

*Wa laqad najjaynā Banī Isrāʾīla mina'l-ʿadhābi'l-muhīn* — "And We delivered the Children of Israel from the humiliating punishment." The exodus cycle now turns its camera from the drowned to the delivered, and the sūrah chooses its central adjective with care: the punishment was not merely severe but *muhīn* — humiliating, degrading. The two adjectives name different things: severe punishment breaks the body; humiliating punishment breaks the self. Pharaoh's regime administered both, but it is the humiliation the sūrah foregrounds, because humiliation is the instrument by which tyranny makes its victims consent to their own enslavement. The Qur'an describes the regime's mechanism in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ: *"Indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and divided its people into factions, oppressing a sector of them, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive; indeed, he was of the corrupters"* (28:4). Slaughter of sons destroys the future; the sparing of women into servitude destroys the household; the division into factions destroys solidarity. Three instruments, one product: a people taught to feel small. The deliverance is therefore named as the removal of humiliation before it is named as the removal of pain — for a people restored to dignity can rebuild every loss, while a people restored to comfort without dignity rebuilds nothing.

**The Anatomy of Humiliating Power**

Al-Ṭabarī reports that the *ʿadhāb muhīn* refers specifically to the forced labor, the slaughter of the male children, and the degradation of the Israelites under Pharaoh's taskmasters; Ibn Kathīr gathers the sister verses: *"And when We saved you from the House of Pharaoh, who inflicted terrible punishment upon you, slaughtering your sons and sparing your women — and in that was a great trial from your Lord"* (2:49; 7:141; 14:6). The exegetes note a recurring Qur'anic law: tyranny always degrades before it destroys, because the degraded do not resist. The counter-law the Qur'an legislates is the restoration of dignity as the first act of every divine deliverance — hence the exodus begins not with a battle but with a title-change: *"Deliver to Me My servants"* (44:18). The humiliated are recovered by being renamed: no longer Pharaoh's property but God's servants, and servitude to God is the only servitude that liberates, because it removes every human master from the soul's chain of command. The Prophet ﷺ stated the same principle in the ḥadīth of the equal line:

> *"O people, your Lord is one and your father is one; there is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except by reverence (taqwā)."* (Aḥmad 23536, from Abū Hurayrah, in the Farewell Sermon narrations)

**The Deliverance as Standing Doctrine**

The verse is recited, in the sūrah's design, not as a closed history but as an open precedent. Every community under humiliating power — political, economic, social — reads in it the same four-stage operation the exodus displayed: the complaint filed upward (44:22), the night march on a word (44:23), the sea left at rest until the term (44:24), and the inheritance transferred to the patient (44:28, with 28:5). The sūrah's pastoral contribution is its realism: deliverance is not promised in the victim's preferred time or form, but it is promised in the Owner's — and the Owner's record, as this sūrah has just certified it, has never once been broken.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:31

> **from Pharaoh. Truly he was foremost among the prodigal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tyrant Filed Under His True Category**

*Min Firʿawn; innahu kāna ʿāliyan mina'l-musrifīn* — "from Pharaoh; indeed, he was high-handed, of the prodigal." The verse names the source of the humiliating punishment and then strips its author of every title he claimed, replacing them with two of the Qur'an's verdict-words. *ʿĀliyan* — high, overbearing, the one who claimed height — is the same root the sūrah used in Moses' prohibition (*"do not rise against God,"* 44:19): the height Pharaoh claimed against heaven is now recorded as his defining attribute, and recorded as a crime. *Musrif* — from *s-r-f*, to exceed the bound, to waste, to be prodigal — is the Qur'an's name for the one who passes every limit: in spending, in killing, in claiming. Al-Ṭabarī explains *mina'l-musrifīn* as: of those who exceeded the bounds of God in disbelief and tyranny; al-Qurṭubī adds that Pharaoh's *isrāf* reached its summit in the claim of divinity — *"I am your lord, the most high"* (79:24) — the maximum possible excess, the creature's claim to be the Creator. The two words together form the Qur'an's complete diagnosis of tyranny: height claimed without right (*ʿuluww*), and every limit exceeded in maintaining it (*isrāf*).

**Pharaoh as Archetype, Not Anomaly**

The exegetes emphasize a point the sūrah's structure enforces: Pharaoh is recorded in this register not as history's monster but as its prototype — the pattern every defiant power recycles at whatever scale its century permits. The Qur'an states the recurrence explicitly: *"Has there not come to you the report of those before you — the people of Noah, and ʿĀd, and Thamūd, and those after them? None knows them but God"* (14:9); and it applies Pharaoh's specific crime to Makkah's chiefs by implication throughout al-Dukhān, as verse 37 will do by direct rhetorical question. The Prophet ﷺ applied the pattern explicitly in the report the exegetes cite on verse 49 of this sūrah — that Abū Jahl was called *"the Pharaoh of this ummah"* (recorded in the tafsīr literature; Ibn Kathīr cites it on 44:49): every generation produces its Pharaoh-scale denial, and the sea has a standing appointment with each of them. Ibn Kathīr also notes the justice of the record: the Qur'an does not merely drown Pharaoh; it classifies him, so that the classification outlives the dynasty — the name itself, *Firʿawn*, becoming in the languages of revelation a common noun for the type.

**The Prodigality of Power**

The word *isrāf* deserves its final pause, for it generalizes beyond Pharaoh. The Qur'an uses the same word for the wastrel of wealth (*"and do not waste; indeed, the wasteful are brothers of the devils,"* 17:26–27) and for the wastrel of blood and claim. The link is the doctrine of the bound: every created thing has a limit set by its Maker, and *isrāf* is the crossing of any limit — of spending, of eating, of punishing, of claiming. Pharaoh's *isrāf* was simply the most total version of the common human disease: the self that does not recognize a bound. The sūrah's answer to it has been running since verse 1: the affairs of every year are distinguished by command from the Presence (44:4) — the universe is administered by limits, and the one who lives against them lives against the structure of reality, and is eventually corrected by it. The drowned host of verse 24 was that correction in its complete form.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:32

> **We knowingly chose them over the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Election Performed with Knowledge**

*Walaqad ikhtarnāhum ʿalā ʿilmin ʿalā'l-ʿālamīn* — "And We chose them, upon knowledge, over the worlds." The verse states the election of the Children of Israel, and it states its manner: *ʿalā ʿilm* — upon knowledge, knowingly. The phrase is the verse's theology in miniature. Election in the Qur'an is never favoritism; it is assignment, made with full knowledge of the assigned — of their capacity, their history, and the conditions of the office. Al-Ṭabarī reports: We chose them knowing them and knowing that they deserved the choice at that time, over the worlds of their era; Ibn Kathīr specifies the time-bound: chosen above the other nations *of their time* in receiving the revelation, the messengers, and the religion; al-Qurṭubī adds the content of the office: *"He gave them what He gave no other nation of the worlds"* — the manna, the quails, the cloud, the parted sea, the scripture — as verse 33 will catalogue. The same election is stated elsewhere with its basis named: *"O Children of Israel, remember My favor which I bestowed upon you, and that I preferred you over the worlds"* (2:47, 122) — and the basis is always *favor* (niʿmah), not blood: the preference is the gift, and the gift carries its conditions.

**The Condition Attached to Every Chosenness**

The Qur'an's doctrine of election is conditioned from its first statement to its last, and the condition is obedience. *"And when his Lord tested Abraham with words and he completed them, He said: 'I will make you a leader of mankind.' He said: 'And of my offspring?' He said: 'My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers'"* (2:124) — election terminates in the wrongdoer, whatever his lineage. The sūrah states the positive law elsewhere in the exodus cycle: *"And the beautiful word of your Lord was fulfilled upon the Children of Israel because of what they patiently endured"* (7:137) — the inheritance was fulfilled *because of patience*, a causal clause that makes the tenure performance-based. And the negative law follows the same logic: *"And the Jews and the Christians say, 'We are God's sons and His beloved.' Say: then why does He punish you for your sins? Rather, you are human beings from among those He created"* (5:18) — sonship-claims are dissolved by the simple fact of ongoing punishment; *"And had the People of the Book believed and been reverent, We would have removed their misdeeds and admitted them to gardens of delight"* (5:65). Al-Rāzī draws the systematic conclusion from these texts: chosenness is an office of service, renewable by performance and forfeitable by breach; the world's error — repeated by every elect nation in history — is converting the office into an ethnic possession. Verse 32, read with its conditions, is thus simultaneously an honor and a warning: chosen, knowingly, upon a tenure with terms.

**The Chosenness of the Human as Such**

A minority reading deserves preservation, recorded by Maybudī and cited in the classical commentary on this verse: that the verse alludes also to the election of humanity itself — *"We have honored the Children of Adam, and carried them upon the land and the sea, and provided them with good things, and preferred them greatly over much of what We have created"* (17:70); *"We created man in the most beautiful stature"* (95:4). On this reading the verse enlarges the exodus's lesson: the God who chose the oppressed over the empire chooses, in principle, the human over the rest of creation — and the election of Israel is one exhibition of a standing preference of the species. The practical doctrine survives either reading: every reader lives inside an election — of humanity over creation, of this ummah over the nations (3:110), of the individual over the hour's temptations — and every election is tenure, and every tenure has terms, and the knowledge with which the choice was made includes what the chosen one will do with it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:33

> **And We gave them signs wherein was a manifest trial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Signs and Their Built-In Test**

*Wa ātaynāhum mina'l-āyāti mā fīhi balāʾun mubīn* — "And We gave them of signs that in which was a manifest trial." The chosen people were not merely elected; they were supplied with *āyāt* — signs — and every sign carried inside it a *balāʾ mubīn*, a manifest trial. The exegetes divide the signs into two catalogues, both correct. First, the signs of deliverance and provision: the parted sea (2:50), the drowned enemy watched from the far shore (26:63–66), the cloud that shaded the desert march, the manna and the quails sent daily (2:57; 7:160; 20:80–81). Second, the signs of demonstration: the hand of Moses shining white, the staff becoming a serpent, the plagues of Egypt — the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood — *"detailed signs, that perhaps they would return"* (7:133). Al-Ṭabarī reports that the *balāʾ mubīn* is the clear test embedded in all of it: a test by blessing for the recipients of the manna, and a test by punishment for the recipients of the plagues. The verse thereby states one of the Qur'an's deepest epistemic laws: signs do not compel belief; they test it.

**Why Signs Are Trials**

The law behind the verse is stated explicitly in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ: *"And We try you with evil and with good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned"* (21:35). The trial-function of signs follows from the structure of faith itself: faith credited only under compulsion is not faith, so the evidence God supplies must be sufficient to convince the willing and insufficient to compel the unwilling. The manna was bread — undeniable bread — but it was bread that required gratitude, and gratitude is precisely the posture the trial measures. The staff-serpent was undeniable spectacle — and Pharaoh's court answered undeniable spectacle with the counter-spectacle of the magicians, which the same court had summoned (20:56–64), because the will had already chosen its side before the evidence arrived. Ibn Kathīr gathers the outcome: the signs of Egypt failed to produce lasting belief in the recipients of the plagues — *"And when the punishment fell upon them they said: 'O Moses, invoke for us your Lord... we will surely believe and send the Children of Israel with you.' But when We removed the punishment from them for a term they were about to reach, behold they broke their word"* (7:134–135) — the identical respite-reversion cycle al-Dukhān diagnosed for Makkah in verses 12–15. The trial is thus constant across history: every sign is a mirror, and the mirror returns the face of the one looking.

**The Believer's Use of Signs**

The pastoral doctrine of the verse belongs to the recipient, not the recipient's enemy. Signs are given to be read, and reading them is an act of worship: *"Indeed, in that are signs for a people who reason"* is the Qur'an's refrain (13:4; 16:12; 30:24). The manifest trial of verse 33 is passed by the one who receives the manna with gratitude, the deliverance with humility, and the demonstration with surrender — and it is failed by the one who receives the manna as entitlement, the deliverance as leverage, and the demonstration as a contest. The sūrah closes the Moses cycle with this verse deliberately: the exodus was not a rescue operation alone but a curriculum, and the curriculum's examination has been administered to every subsequent community that received a sign — including the community that received the clearest sign of all, the Book sworn to in verse 2, and including every reader of this commentary, who is now taking the trial.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:34

> **Truly these say,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Camera Returns to Makkah**

*Inna hāʾulāʾi la-yaqūlūn* — "Indeed, these — they say." The verse is a hinge, one of the Qur'an's shortest, and its function is the sudden cut of the camera from the drowned empire back to the living audience. *Hāʾulāʾi* — "these" — is the demonstrative of presence, almost of contempt: these people here, in your own town, saying what the drowned empire said in theirs. The exegetes note the rhetorical force of the bare sentence: no content is given yet; the verse announces the speech and withholds it for one beat, so that the listener leans in — and the next verse delivers the content in the deniers' own quoted words. Al-Zamakhsharī observes that the construction — *inna* plus the emphatic *lām* — carries the sense of exasperated testimony: "these people are actually saying this." The Moses cycle has just run its complete arc — trial, messenger, refusal, respite, drowning, inventory, unmourned death — and the sūrah now shows the audience that the cycle has reopened: the same speech, the same structure, the same pending appointment.

**The Identical Grammar of Every Denial**

The verse's deeper lesson is its demonstration of history's rhyme. Between Pharaoh's court (v. 17–33) and Makkah's chiefs (v. 34 onward) stand four thousand years of civilization — and the denial is identical in its grammar. This is the Qur'an's constant testimony about the human heart: disbelief is not a culture but a posture, and postures repeat across centuries with only the costume changing. *"Likewise, no messenger came to those before them except they said: 'A magician or a madman'"* (51:52); *"Did they bequeath it to one another? Rather, they are a transgressing people"* (51:53) — the verse asking, with irony, whether denial is inherited tradition, and answering: no, it is the transgression of each generation rediscovered for itself. Verse 34 is therefore both introduction and diagnosis: what follows is not Makkah's original thought but its inherited posture, and the drowned archive of the preceding verses is its documented future.

**Why the Speech Is Quoted at All**

One final observation belongs to the method: the Qur'an quotes the deniers rather than merely describing them, and it does so throughout — preserving their exact words in the Book of God until the end of time. The purpose is twofold. First, the quotation is evidence: the denier's words, preserved verbatim, become the exhibit at his own hearing, as verse 50 will perform: *"This is that which you used to doubt."* Second, the quotation is inoculation: every later believer who hears the same sentence recycled — in every century's materialism, every age's mockery — recognizes it instantly, because the Book has filed it before. Verse 34 opens the file; verse 35 supplies the text.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:35

> **"There is naught but our first death, and we shall not be resurrected.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Materialist Creed in One Sentence**

*In hiya illā mawtatunā'l-ūlā wa mā naḥnu bi-munsharīn* — "There is nothing but our first death, and we shall not be raised." The verse quotes the creed of the Makkan deniers in their own words, and the words deserve close reading. The construction is double-negative, the strongest form of exclusion in Arabic: *in* (naught) + *illā* (but) — nothing exists except. And what they affirm is striking: they do not say "there is no God" — the sūrah has already recorded that they confess the Creator (44:7–8) — they say: there is no *second event*. One death, one life, no resurrection, no return. The formula appears in the Qur'an as the standard creed of the denial of the Hereafter, in a family of verses: *"And they say: there is nothing but our worldly life; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us except time"* (45:24); *"And they say: when we are bones and fragments, shall we really be raised up as a new creation?"* (17:49); *"They say: when we are dead and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be resurrected?"* (23:82). Al-Ṭabarī explains the phrase *our first death*: they meant — if there is any death that counts, it is this one, the only one; there is no death-and-return after it. Ibn Kathīr notes that the creed is identical to the philosophical materialism of every age: the universe is matter, consciousness is accident, death is terminus, and morality is preference.

**The Creed's Hidden Premises**

The verse's genius is that it quotes the creed without arguing with it yet — the argument arrives across the next five verses — but the premises of the creed can already be inspected. The creed rests on three assumptions, each of which the sūrah will dismantle. First: that first creation proves nothing about second creation — answered by the Qur'an's standing argument, *"Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time"* (36:79); *"And he presents for Us an example and forgets his own creation: 'Who will give life to bones when they have decayed?'"* (36:78). Second: that power is measured by visible repetition — answered by the sūrah's own opening: the One who distinguishes the affairs of every year by command from His presence (44:4) has demonstrated administrative capacity over the invisible future at every moment of the listener's life. Third: that the absence of a returning ancestor disproves the return — answered by verse 36's treatment, to come. The creed is thus not refuted by shouting but by inventory: the sūrah will count what the creed has ignored.

**Why the Denial Targets the Second Event Specifically**

The precision of the quoted creed deserves one further pause. The deniers do not primarily deny God's existence — their own phrase *our first death* concedes that someone administered the first one; death is not self-inflicted. What they deny is the *repetition*: the second death-and-life, the return, the accounting. And this is exactly where denial will always concentrate, for the second event is the one with moral consequences. A universe with one death is a universe without a courtroom; a universe with a return is a universe in which every deed is filed. The Qur'an identifies the motive explicitly elsewhere: *"Rather, man desires to sin freely before him; he asks: 'When is the Day of Resurrection?'"* (75:5–6). The denial of the second event is, in the Qur'an's autopsy, the intellectual form of the desire for consequence-free living — which is why the sūrah began with the blessed night and the distinguished affairs: a universe whose years are administered affair by affair (44:4) is a universe with a chancery, and a chancery keeps records, and records are opened. The creed of verse 35 is the attempt to live as if the chancery did not exist; the remaining verses of the sūrah are the chancery's reply.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:36

> **Bring us our fathers, if you are truthful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand for Empirical Resurrection**

*Faʾtū bi-ābāʾinā in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "Then bring our fathers, if you are truthful." The creed of verse 35 now produces its demonstration-demand: if the resurrection is real, produce one instance of it — revive our dead ancestors, here, now, before our eyes. The demand has the appearance of empirical rigor, and the Qur'an treats it with full seriousness, answering it on every level. First, the demand is historically identical to every skeptic's ultimatum: *"And they say: we will not believe you until you gush a spring from the earth for us... or you bring God and the angels as guarantors"* (17:90–92); *"And those who expect not the meeting with Us say: why were not angels sent down to us, or why do we not see our Lord?"* (25:21). The Qur'an's uniform diagnosis of these ultimatums is that they are not inquiries but escapes: the condition demanded is chosen precisely because it will not be met on the demander's terms, so that the refusal of the condition can be cited as the reason for refusing the message. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note the psychology: the demand "bring our fathers" converts an evidential question into a performance request, shifting the burden onto the messenger in a domain no messenger controls — resurrection is the exclusive act of God at the appointed Hour (36:78–83), and demanding its rehearsal on demand is like demanding that the king hold the coronation in the village square.

**The Demand's Own Refutation**

The Qur'an's second answer is more subtle: the demand refutes itself, because the evidence it asks for is already in the demanders' possession. Their fathers lived, and that very living — the first creation they are quoting as the only event (*"naught but our first death,"* 44:35) — is the evidence for the second: *"O mankind, if you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then indeed We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot... that you may know that God is the Truth, and that He gives life to the dead, and that He is over all things capable"* (22:5–6). The stages of every reader's own origin are the resurrection's demonstration, performed in every womb. And the third answer is eschatological: the fathers will indeed be brought — at the appointed time, not the demanded one: *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and behold, they will come forth from their graves to their Lord"* (36:51); *"The Day when they will hear the blast in truth — that is the Day of Emergence"* (50:42). The demand will be answered in full; it will simply not be answered *as a demonstration*, because demonstrations are for the time of capacity, and the time of capacity is the life the demanders are currently spending on the demand.

**The Modern Demand's Same Shape**

The verse remains precisely current: every generation's materialism produces its own "bring our fathers" — the demand that the unseen submit to the visible on visible terms, that God produce Himself for laboratory inspection, that the soul be weighed, that the Hereafter be televised. The Qur'an's answer is constant: the universe does not run on the demander's protocol; it runs on the chancery's (44:4). Evidence has been supplied in the only form that preserves the trial — first creation witnessed by all, conscience witnessed within, revelation witnessed in history — and the one who demands more is not seeking evidence but postponing decision. The sūrah will now answer the demand not with a revived father but with a rhetorical question about an entire extinct civilization — the people of Tubbaʿ — and the argument from the greater to the lesser that follows is the Qur'an's demolition of the demand's premise.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:37

> **Are they better, or the people of Tubbaʿ and those before them? We destroyed them. Truly they were guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from the Greater to the Lesser**

*Ahum khayrun am qawmu Tubbaʿin wa'lladhīna min qablihim; ahlaknāhum innahum kānū mujrimīn* — "Are they better, or the people of Tubbaʿ and those before them? We destroyed them; indeed, they were guilty." The verse answers the materialist demand of verse 36 with the Qur'an's characteristic historical logic: the question is not whether the Quraysh can demand a resurrected ancestor, but whether the Quraysh are stronger than the nations already destroyed. *Tubbaʿ* was the title of the kings of Ḥimyar, the south-Arabian dynasty that ruled Yemen and its dependencies for centuries — a civilization of dams, inscriptions, and long-range trade, far mightier in wealth, arms, and territory than the merchant town of Makkah. The rhetorical question expects its own answer: they are not better. And the verdict follows in three words: *ahlaknāhum* — "We destroyed them" — with the ground of the verdict: *innahum kānū mujrimīn*, "indeed, they were guilty" — the same verdict-word Moses filed against Pharaoh's people (44:22), closing the frame: guilt, then destruction, in both archives.

**Tubbaʿ in the Tradition**

The exegetes preserve substantial material on the name. In 50:14 the people of Tubbaʿ appear in the roll-call of destroyed deniers — *"Before them the people of Noah denied, and the people of the Rass, and Thamūd, and ʿĀd, and Pharaoh, and the brethren of Lot, and the people of the Wood, and the people of Tubbaʿ — all denied the messengers, so My threat came true"* — placing them in the highest register of punished civilizations. Two traditions govern the commentary. First, the report that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"I do not know whether Tubbaʿ was a prophet or not."* (recorded in the tafsīr literature; al-Ḥākim and others transmit variants; discussed by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī)

Second, the report:

> *"Do not revile Tubbaʿ, for he had embraced Islam."* (Aḥmad, from Sahl ibn Saʿd; al-Ḥākim graded it ṣaḥīḥ)

The exegetes reconcile the two thus: Tubbaʿ himself — the king the tradition describes as having journeyed toward Madinah, honored the site of the Prophet's future emigration, and left provisions for its future inhabitants — was among the righteous, perhaps even a prophet; what the sūrah destroys is *the people of Tubbaʿ*, the nation that rejected what their king carried, exactly as Pharaoh's people rejected the messenger though Pharaoh's own magicians believed. Ibn Kathīr records the historical traditions that Tubbaʿ clothed the Kaʿbah and that the scholars of his time warned him against attacking Madinah because it was the destined refuge of a coming prophet — traditions that, whatever their individual grading, carry the exegetical point: the people of Tubbaʿ were not ignorant of revelation; they were supplied with it, and they refused it, and their might did not postpone their appointment.

**The Standing Arithmetic**

The verse's arithmetic generalizes across the Qur'an's entire philosophy of history: *"Have they not traveled the earth and seen how was the end of those before them? They were greater than them in strength..."* (40:82; cf. 30:9, 47:13, 22:45, 6:6, 17:17, 21:11, 28:58, 38:3, 50:36). Every one of these texts runs the same computation: strength × denial = destruction, with the multiplier of strength increasing, not decreasing, the certainty of the outcome — because greater power means greater evidence used, greater respite consumed, greater account filed. Makkah, which could produce neither the gardens of Egypt nor the dams of Ḥimyar, was being asked to contemplate both archives at once. The modern reader is asked the same contemplation, with better archives: the ruins of every superstructure that pronounced itself permanent are open to inspection, and the verse's question — *are they better?* — has never once been answered in the affirmative by any civilization that has stood where Makkah stood.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:38

> **And We did not create the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is between them in play.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Universe Is Not Amusement**

*Wa mā khalaqnā's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa wa mā baynahumā lāʿibīn* — "And We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them playing." The verse is the Qur'an's direct answer to the materialist creed of verses 35–36, and it answers at the root: not by producing the demanded ancestor but by denying the premise on which the demand rests. The materialist creed assumes a universe without purpose — matter in motion, terminating in one death — and the verse denies the assumption with the verb of the deniers' own activity: *lāʿibīn*, playing. The sūrah has already diagnosed the deniers as players in doubt (44:9); now it declares the universe itself innocent of play. The same declaration recurs across the Book: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in play; We did not create them except in truth, but most of them do not know"* (21:16–17); *"Did you suppose that We created you frivolously, and that you would not be returned to Us?"* (23:115) — the question addressed, in the Qur'an's staging, to the denizens of Hell themselves, as the verse's audience is meant to realize before arriving there. Al-Ṭabarī explains *lāʿibīn* as: not created for amusement without purpose, command, prohibition, reward, or punishment; al-Rāzī develops the argument formally: a universe created in play would have no order, no law, and no consistent administration — but the heavens and the earth run upon laws that the same listeners confess in their agriculture, their navigation, and their calendar; therefore the creation is purposive; and a purposive creation with moral agents in it requires the completion of its purpose, which is the Resurrection.

**The Argument from the Administration Already Witnessed**

The verse's force is doubled by its position in the sūrah. The listener has just been told, in verses 3–6, that the affairs of every year are distinguished and dispatched by command from the Presence — that is, the listener already knows, from the revelation he is being asked to accept, that the universe is administered affair by affair, year by year. The verse now generalizes the same knowledge from the Qur'anic report to the visible cosmos: the administration you have been told about is the administration you are already living in. The stars' punctuality, the seasons' reliability, the embryo's stages (22:5, 23:12–14) are each *amr ḥakīm*, a wise affair, executed with the precision the word *ḥakīm* demands. Play produces no such precision; play leaves no such records; play does not administer the provisions of sixty years in the body of a single human being. The inference is the Qur'an's standing syllogism, and Ibn Taymiyyah's school stated it repeatedly: order witnessed entails an orderer, and an orderer who orders with wisdom does not abandon His order to terminate without judgment — for that would make the wisdom pointless, and pointless wisdom is a contradiction.

**Against the Century of Nihilism**

The verse reads with renewed urgency against the modern intellectual default, which has institutionalized the very play the verse denies: the universe as accident, consciousness as epiphenomenon, morality as preference, life as the interval between two nothings. The Qur'an does not argue with this default on its own terms; it denies its root and offers the alternative as the only coherent reading of the evidence. If the universe is play, then the deniers' play (v. 9) is the correct response to it — amusement answering amusement. But if the universe is not play — if every year's affairs are being distinguished by command from a wise Presence — then play is not the correct response to anything, and the one who plays in such a universe is not enjoying it but failing its examination. Verse 38 is thus the hinge of the sūrah's second half: from here to verse 42, the argument moves from creation's purpose to the Day that completes it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:39

> **We did not create them, save in truth. But most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Except in Truth": The Positive Clause**

*Mā khalaqnāhumā illā bi'l-ḥaqq* — "We did not create them except in truth." After the negation of play comes the affirmation of its opposite: *bi'l-ḥaqq*, in truth — by truth, with truth, for truth. The sister verses carry the same clause: *"And We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them except in truth"* (15:85; 46:3). Al-Ṭabarī reports the exegetical contents of *in truth*: for the establishment of justice, for the recognition of the Creator, for the reward of the righteous and the punishment of the denier — truth as the substance of creation's purpose, not merely its manner. Al-Qurṭubī adds the juristic reading: the heavens and the earth were created *with* truth in the sense that they are created bearing rights — rights of the Creator over the creation, and the creation's standing as evidence for the truth of its Maker: *"We will show them Our signs in the horizons and in themselves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (41:53). The verse thereby converts cosmology into obligation: a universe created in truth is a universe in which truth-telling, truth-doing, and truth-seeking are not preferences but alignments with the structure of reality, and falsehood is not merely wrong but un-real — out of register with the creation's founding clause.

**The Consequence the Verse Entails**

The exegetes draw the entailment explicitly, al-Rāzī foremost among them: if creation is in truth, then the Resurrection is necessary — not as a speculation but as a requirement of the truth. In this world the truthful are frequently defeated and the liars frequently enthroned; the oppressed die under the smoke of their oppressors' prosperity. A creation in truth cannot terminate with that ledger unbalanced, for an unbalanced final ledger would make the creation in play, which verse 38 has just denied. Therefore the Day comes — and verse 40 will announce its name — because truth demands the balancing of accounts, and the creation was made in truth. This is the Qur'an's moral proof of the Hereafter, and it is a proof addressed to the conscience rather than to the telescope: every human being possesses the premise — the immediate knowledge that the torturer and the tortured should not end identically — and the verse asks only that the premise be followed to its conclusion.

**"But Most of Them Know Not": The Epistemic Verdict**

The verse closes with *wa lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn* — "but most of them do not know." The verdict is not that the truth is hidden — the sūrah has spent its first eight verses and its Moses cycle establishing that the evidence was delivered in clarity (*mubīn*, vv. 2, 17) — but that it is not received. *Most* — not all; the door of the minority stands open, and the sūrah's final scene (vv. 51–57) will describe its occupants. *Know not* — not "cannot know," but "do not know," the absence located in the will rather than in the intellect: the knowledge was available in the blessed night, in the clear messenger, in the administration of the years, in the stages of one's own origin; what is missing is the attention. The verse is thus simultaneously the most severe and the most hopeful of the passage: severe, in declaring the majority's ignorance of the truth they live inside; hopeful, in naming the condition *ignorance* — a curable state — rather than incapacity. The cure is offered in the very word the verse withholds from its object: the knowledge exists; it descends; it is being distinguished affair by affair, year by year, in every blessed night since the first one.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:40

> **Truly the Day of Division is their tryst, all together.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Appointment Kept by Everyone**

*Inna yawma'l-faṣli mīqātuhum ajmaʿīn* — "Indeed, the Day of Division is their appointed tryst, all of them together." The argument of verses 38–39 now delivers its consequent: creation made in truth terminates in a Day named for the act that completes truth's requirement — *al-faṣl*, division, separation, adjudication. The Qur'an gives the Day this name in a family of verses: *"This is the Day of Division which you used to deny"* (37:21); *"Indeed, the Day of Decision is their appointed time, all of them"* (77:13); *"On the Day when the Spirit and the angels stand in rows"* (78:38, in the sūrah that asks *"What is the Day of Division? And what will make you know what the Day of Division is?"* — 77:13–14). And *mīqāt* — tryst, appointed time — is the verse's key word: the Day is not a possibility but an appointment, fixed as the year's affairs are fixed (44:4), and the appointment is universal — *ajmaʿīn*, all together: the doubter and the messenger, Pharaoh and Moses, the player in doubt and the prostrating servant. Every human being who ever lived holds the same date.

**Why "Division"**

The name *yawm al-faṣl* — from *f-ṣ-l*, to separate, to decide, to part — is explained by the exegetes on three levels, all preserved by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī. First, the division of judgment: the truth is separated from the falsehood, the believers from the deniers, the record balanced — *"And they will be called: 'Where are those you used to associate?'"* (40:73). Second, the division of relationships: the Day separates kin from kin, friend from friend, as verse 41 will state and as the Qur'an stages in full: *"The Day when a man will flee from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his wife and his children — for every man of them, that Day, will have an affair sufficient for him"* (80:34–36). Third, the division of destinies: one party in the Garden and one party in the Blaze (42:7), a division that — unlike every division in this world — is final: *"A community shall not enter another's place"* in the classical sense of the eternal fixity of the two destinations. The name thus compresses the Day's entire operation: what was mixed in this world — truth and falsehood, righteous and guilty, in one society, one family, one body's own record — is there separated, and the separation is the justice the creation was made in (44:39).

**The Tryst as Comfort and Terror**

The word *mīqāt* — tryst, appointment — carries both of the verse's faces. For the oppressed believer, the tryst is the comfort: every account unbalanced here is scheduled there; every smoke endured is on the docket; the appointment cannot be missed, postponed, or settled out of court. For the player in doubt, the tryst is the terror: the same universality that guarantees the believer's vindication guarantees the denier's hearing — *all together*, with no class exemption, no empire's delegation, no Pharaoh's chariot available. The sūrah's design places this verse immediately after the Tubbaʿ and Pharaoh archives precisely to universalize them: the destroyed nations were not destroyed into nothing; they were destroyed *into the appointment* — drowned, burned, and dismantled, but held, every individual of them, against the date. Verse 40 is thus the sūrah's answer to the creed of verse 35 in its final form: there is not one death but two, and between the second death and the endless life stands the Day whose name is Division, and whose appointment book has never once been empty.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:41

> **The Day when no friend will avail a friend in any way, nor will they be helped,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dissolution of Social Capital**

*Yawma lā yughnī mawlan ʿan mawlan shayʾan wa lā hum yunṣarūn* — "the Day when no protector will avail a protector anything, and they will not be helped." The verse is the Day of Division's first operational clause, and it strikes at the exact asset the deniers had been trading on. *Mawlā* — protector, patron, close ally, the one who stands for you — was the currency of Arabian society: the clan, the patron, the network of obligations within which every person lived and by which every person was defended. The verse announces the day that currency is demonetized: *nothing* — *shayʾan*, the accusative of minimum quantity, not one thing's worth — passes between friend and friend as aid. The Qur'an states the same dissolution across a family of verses, each from a different angle: *"The Day when there will not avail a soul for another soul anything, and no intercession will be accepted from it, nor any ransom taken"* (2:48); *"There shall be no kinship between them that Day, nor will they question one another"* (23:101); *"The Day when a man will flee from his brother"* (80:34); *"The guilty will wish to ransom himself from the punishment of that Day with his children, his spouse, and his brother, his kin who had sheltered him, and all who are on the earth, that it might save him"* (70:11–14). Al-Qurṭubī notes the verse's precision: it does not say friends will be absent — the deniers will have their company, as verse 43's *sinner* has his — but that the company will be *unavailing*: present and useless, which is the sharper punishment.

**Why Friendship Fails**

The failure of friendship on that Day is not cruelty but consequence, and the sūrah has already supplied its logic. Friendship avails in this world because this world runs on delegated power: money moves, judges are persuaded, doors open, prisons release. The Day of Division strips every delegation: *"The Day when they will come forth — nothing of them is hidden from God"* (40:16); *"And all will return to God that Day"* (24:43?). The only currency that survives the stripping is the one minted by the One who alone remains — which is why verse 42 will name the sole exception. Ibn Kathīr gathers the supporting texts: *"And they will be called: 'Where are those you used to associate besides God?' They will say: 'They have departed from us'*" (40:73–74); *"The Day when the wrongdoer will bite his hands, saying: would that I had taken with the Messenger a way! Woe to me — would that I had not taken such-a-one as a friend! He led me away from the remembrance after it had come to me"* (25:27–29). The last quotation states the doctrine completely: the friendship that fails on that Day is precisely the friendship that substituted for obedience in this one. Every bond built around shared denial is a bond against the soul, and the verse's *no friend will avail* is the maturity date of every such investment.

**The Friendship That Does Avail**

The verse's negative is therefore also a positive instruction for this world, and the exegetes drew it: build friendships that survive the Day — the friendship of mutual obedience, mutual reminder, mutual intercession by God's permission. The Qur'an models it in the believers' own speech: *"And the believers, men and women, are protecting friends of one another; they enjoin the right and forbid the wrong"* (9:71) — a friendship whose content is precisely what avails. And the Prophet ﷺ named the species of friendship that crosses the divide:

> *"A person is upon the religion of his close friend; so let each of you look to whom he takes as a close friend."* (Abū Dāwūd 4833; al-Tirmidhī 2378, who graded it ḥasan gharīb, from Abū Hurayrah)

Verse 41 is thus read in the tradition as both terror and curriculum: the Day's demonetization of social capital is announced now, in the time of capacity, so that the reader audits his own ledger before the audit — asking of every bond which way it is leading its parties, and converting, while conversion is possible, the friendships that fail into the friendships that will be seen again.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:42

> **save him unto whom God has been merciful. Truly He is the Mighty, the Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Single Exception**

*Illā man raḥima Allāh* — "except him to whom God has shown mercy." The universal clause of verse 41 — nothing avails, none are helped — opens here for exactly one exception, and the exception is named with maximum precision: it is not the friend, the deed, the lineage, or the intercessor that forms the exception's subject; it is *the one whom God has mercied*. The availing and the helping of that Day are both downstream of mercy, and mercy is downstream of God's own choice. Al-Ṭabarī reports: nothing will avail on that Day except the mercy of God; Ibn Kathīr adds the intercession clause that the Qur'an attaches everywhere mercy is mentioned at the judgment: intercession itself operates only within the exception — *"Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?"* (2:255); *"And they cannot intercede except for him with whom He is pleased"* (21:28); *"How many an angel is in the heavens whose intercession avails nothing except after God permits it for whom He wills and is pleased with"* (53:26). The doctrine the verse installs is thus complete: the Day is closed to every horizontal currency and open to exactly one vertical one, and that one is mercy — received, not purchased.

**The Economy of Mercy**

The verse's placement immediately after the friendship clause yields the sūrah's complete economics of salvation, and it is the same economics the opening section announced. The blessed night, the distinguished affairs, the command from the Presence, the continual sending — all of it *a mercy from your Lord* (44:6). The verse thereby unites the sūrah's beginning and end: the mercy that descended with the Book is the identical mercy that will avail at the Division. The ḥadīth qudsī supplies the metaphysical foundation:

> *"Indeed, My mercy overcomes My wrath."* (al-Bukhārī 7404; Muslim 2751)

and the Prophet ﷺ supplied its pastoral application:

> *"None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone." They said: "Nor you, Messenger of God?" He said: "Nor I — unless God envelops me with mercy from Him and favor."* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816)

The exegetes harmonized the two texts with the verses of reward-for-deeds thus: deeds are the *condition* of the mercy, not its *price* — the servant works toward the mercy as the traveler walks toward the water he was promised, and the water remains gift. Verse 42 is that doctrine at the judgment's door: the file that passes the door passes by mercy, and the verse announces it so that no one arrives at the Day trading on a horizontal asset — network, ancestor, reputation — while the one currency is still available for the asking.

**The Two Names That Seal the Passage**

The section closes with *innahu huwa'l-ʿazīzu'r-raḥīm* — "indeed, He is the Mighty, the Merciful." The pairing is deliberate and governs everything the sūrah has said from verse 9 onward. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty, the Unconquered — is the name under which the smoke descends, the sea closes, the striking falls, and the Division executes: none of it can be resisted, appealed, or escaped. *Al-Raḥīm* — the Merciful — is the name under which the warning was issued before every one of those events, the respite administered, the Book sent down, and the exception of verse 42 opened. The two names together are the sūrah's theology in miniature: might that warns because it is merciful, and mercy that warns because it is mighty. The reader stands between the two names for the length of his life, and the sūrah's remaining verses will now show what each name has prepared — first the hospitality of the Mighty for the unrepented player, then the hospitality of the Merciful for the reverent.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:43

> **Truly the tree of Zaqqūm**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tree Named**

*Inna shajarata'z-zaqqūm* — "Indeed, the tree of the Zaqqūm." The sūrah's fifth scene opens on the hospitality of the Fire, and it opens on its food-service — the tree the Qur'an names three times (37:62; 44:43; 56:52) and alludes to as *"the cursed tree in the Qur'an"* (17:60). *Zaqqūm* derives, the lexicographers report, from *zaqama* — to swallow with difficulty, to gulp down what the throat refuses; the word itself is a diagram of the eating it names. In the Arabs' own landscape the name was already known: the exegetes identify zaqqūm with one of the vilest, most bitter trees of the Tihāmah, whose milky sap burns and whose fruit is loathsome — the desert's own emblem of the inedible made food. The Qur'an takes the word from that landscape and transplants it into the unseen, where it names a tree growing in the bottom of the Blaze (37:64, *"Indeed, it is a tree that emerges from the bottom of the Hellfire"*), whose fruit Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt describes with the verse that made the Arabs' stomachs turn: *"Its fruit is like the heads of the devils"* (37:65). Ibn Kathīr reports that the Arabs had never seen the heads of devils; the comparison works by the imagination's own dread — the ugliest thing conceivable, made visible enough to be named and invisible enough to be limitless.

**The Tree as Counter-Hospitality**

The exegetes observe the sūrah's deliberate inversion of hospitality imagery. The Arabs' highest social institution was the guest's reception — the honored tree, the laden table, the host's pride. The Fire's hospitality is that institution inverted at every point, and al-Dukhān will complete the inversion across verses 43–50: the food is Zaqqūm, the drink is boiling liquid, the host is the command *"Seize him,"* and the host's toast is mockery — *"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble"* (44:49). Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt states the inversion's logic explicitly: *"Is that better as hospitality, or the tree of Zaqqūm?"* (37:62) — hospitality (*nuzul*) being the word the Arabs used for the guest's first entitlement. The doctrine behind the imagery is the Qur'an's law of fit recompense: *"Indeed, those who committed sin used to laugh at those who believed"* (83:29) is answered by its reversal; *"Is the recompense of good anything but good?"* (55:60) is answered by its mirror for evil. The tree is not arbitrary horror; it is the harvest of a sowing — the inedible fruit of a life that fed on denial.

**Abū Jahl's Mockery and the Tree's Authentication**

The classical commentaries preserve the tree's occasion. When the verses of Zaqqūm were recited in Makkah, Abū Jahl — who will appear by name in the commentary on verse 49 — mocked them publicly: *"He threatens us with a tree that grows in fire! Trees are burned by fire, not grown in it!"* and he ordered dates and butter brought, saying to his companions: *"Zaqqūm yourselves!"* — the episode al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr record from the reports on 37:62 and 44:43, and to which 17:60 refers: *"We made not the vision We showed you except a trial for the people, and the cursed tree in the Qur'an; and We frighten them, but it increases them only in great transgression."* The Qur'an's answer to the mockery is not botanical but theological: the Fire's contents are not the world's contents relocated; they are creations of the Hereafter, named by resemblance — as Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said of Paradise's furnishings: *"Nothing in Paradise resembles what is in this world except in names."* The one who measured the unseen by the Tihāmah's botany was measuring with the wrong instrument — exactly the error of verse 36's *"bring us our fathers"* — and the tree stands as one of the standing exhibits of the case the Day of Division will hear.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:44

> **is the food of the sinner,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sinner and His Portion**

*Ṭaʿāmu'l-athīm* — "the food of the sinner." The tree's diner is named with the intensive form *athīm* — from *i-th-m*, guilt — the one saturated in sin, the persistent wrongdoer, the professional of wrongdoing rather than its occasional visitor. Al-Ṭabarī reports that several of the early authorities identified the *athīm* here specifically with Abū Jahl — the man who had mocked the tree by name — while the general meaning stands for every soul of his register: the intensive form makes the description a category, and Abū Jahl its certified instance. The verse thereby performs the Qur'an's method of naming: the general warning is given, and a named individual is filed under it as the precedent, so that every later reader knows the file is populated, not hypothetical. The sister verse states the assignment explicitly: *"Indeed, the tree of Zaqqūm is the food of the sinner"* (37:62–63, with 44:44), and Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah completes the menu: *"Then indeed you, O misguided deniers, will eat from the tree of Zaqqūm, filling your bellies with it"* (56:51–53).

**The Logic of the Assignment**

The exegetes drew out the justice of the assignment, al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr among them. In this world the *athīm* fed himself on the forbidden — usury, orphans' property, the labor of the humiliated, the pleasures purchased with others' dignity — and fed his guests on mockery: Abū Jahl's dates and butter, served as a joke about revelation. The Hereafter's assignment is the same appetite, deprived of its objects and given their truth: hunger permanent (the eating never satisfies — *"they will eat from the tree of Zaqqūm, filling their bellies"* — 56:52–53, followed immediately by the boiling drink), food that is itself punishment, and no guest to mock but the self. The law is the one the sūrah has been executing since verse 9: what a soul plays with becomes its portion. The player in doubt (44:9) played with the warning; the mocker of the tree mocked the hospitality of the unseen; each receives, in the Fire's arithmetic, the exact content of his own joke. The ḥadīth the exegetes cite on the tree supplies the scale of the horror, from the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"If but one drop of Zaqqūm were to fall into the oceans of this world, it would pollute them for all their inhabitants. How shall it be, then, for the one whose nourishment it has become?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2585 — who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; Ibn Mājah 4325; al-Nasāʾī; Aḥmad)

**The Verse as Dietary Ethics**

The verse's instruction for this world is the inverse of its threat, and the tradition drew it: the believer's table is rehearsed here. The Prophet ﷺ taught the ethics of food at both poles — gratitude at the halal meal (*"God is pleased with the servant who eats a food and praises Him for it"* — Muslim 2734) and caution at the doubtful one — and the Qur'an frames the lawful table as the Paradise-table's ancestor: *"O you who believe, eat from the good things We have provided you"* (2:172). The *athīm's* Zaqqūm is what a table becomes when every rule of its construction — source, acquisition, gratitude, sharing — has been broken for a lifetime. Verse 44 is therefore read in the tradition as both exhibit and lesson: the Fire's food-service is displayed so that the world's food-service may be corrected, while the diner still has a choice of menus.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:45

> **like molten lead boiling in their bellies,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Molten Metal of the Fire**

*Ka'l-muhl* — "like the molten lead" (or: like molten metal, the dross of what is melted). The word *muhl* appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire in three contexts: as the drink of the denier — *"And if they call for water, they will be given water like molten lead that scorches their faces"* (18:29); as the garment — *"Their garments are of fire, and boiling liquid is poured over their heads"* (22:19); and here, as the consistency of the Zaqqūm's action in the belly. The exegetes report from Ibn ʿAbbās and others that *muhl* names the substance at the extreme of melting — the molten dross of metal, the thing that combines the two torments of heat and heaviness. The verse's syntax places the simile precisely: the Zaqqūm *acts in their bellies like molten lead* — the comparison is to the experience of swallowing and containing a substance that is at once food and metal, at the temperature of fire. Ibn Kathīr gathers the family: the boiling of the bellies (44:45–46), the scorching of faces (18:29), the melting of what is in the bellies and the skins (22:20) — one substance, three points of contact, a complete administration of the body.

**The Recitation Variant and Its Meaning**

The classical commentaries preserve a recitation detail worth recording, as al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī do: some read *yaghlī* — "it boils," with the masculine — applying the boiling to the *muhl* or to the *food*; others read *taghlī* — with the feminine — applying it to the *tree* (*shajarah*): "like molten lead, *the tree* boils in their bellies." Both readings stood in the transmitted recitations, and both enrich the image: on the first, the substance itself is in perpetual boiling inside the body; on the second, the tree's entire product carries the boiling quality into the eater. The scholarly treatment of such variants is itself a lesson in the tradition's temper: difference of reading is preserved, not suppressed, where the consonantal text supports it, because each reading adds a facet of the meaning — the Qur'an's images are cut like gems, and the recitations are their facets.

**Why the Body Is Addressed**

The verse's insistence on the belly — and verse 48's on the head, and 22:20's on the whole interior — deserves its pause, for it encodes the Qur'an's doctrine of embodied recompense. The sinner's sin was committed in and through a body: the hand that seized, the tongue that mocked, the belly that consumed the forbidden. The Fire's administration therefore addresses the body, point by point — *"The Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet will testify against them for what they used to do"* (24:24); *"Today We will seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify"* (36:65). The justice is anatomical: every member that served the wrong receives its file. At the same time the exegetes preserve the corrective of Ibn ʿAbbās's principle — the Hereafter's substances are named by resemblance, not identity — so that the believer holds both truths together: the punishment is real, bodily, and inescapable; and its reality exceeds the worldly materials its names borrow. The molten lead is not lead; it is what lead, at its worst, only hints at.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:46

> **like the boiling of boiling liquid.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Maximum Temperature**

*Kaghalī'l-ḥamīm* — "like the boiling of the ḥamīm." *Ḥamīm* — from *ḥ-m-m*, heat — names in the Qur'an the water (or liquid) brought to its absolute maximum: *"and they will be given boiling liquid to drink, that will cut their intestines to pieces"* (47:15); *"This — so let them taste it: boiling liquid and freezing cold"* (38:57); *"And the deniers will have garments of fire cut out for them, and boiling liquid poured over their heads"* (22:19). The word's usage across the Book establishes its definition, as al-Ṭabarī records: the ḥamīm is the liquid that has reached the end of heating — nothing hotter can be prepared — and the Qur'an pairs it in one verse with its opposite extreme (*"and freezing cold,"* 38:57) to indicate that the Fire's register includes the entire spectrum of torment, heat and its contrary, administered with the precision of a menu. The simile of verse 46 completes verse 45's: the Zaqqūm's action is *like the boiling of the ḥamīm* — the most intense boiling imaginable to the Arabs, the pot at the fire's peak that cannot be exceeded, made the standard measure of the interior punishment.

**The Rhetoric of the Known Measure**

The exegetes, al-Zamakhsharī foremost, note the pedagogy of the simile: the Qur'an describes the unseen by the maximum of the known. The Arab listener had watched the pot at full boil, had seen the molten dross, had flinched from the scald; the revelation takes the ceiling of his sensory experience and declares the Hereafter's floor to be there. This is not exaggeration but understatement — the same method the ḥadīth qudsī applies to Paradise (*"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and has not occurred to the heart of man"* — al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824, with 32:17): the known maximum names the unseen's minimum. The pastoral function is immediate: deterrence works through imagination, and the imagination is stocked by the senses; the verses therefore stock it with the two substances — molten metal and full-boil liquid — that every seventh-century body had reason to fear. The modern body, for whom open fire and molten metal have receded from daily contact, reads the same verses through its own maximums — the industrial accident, the chemical burn, the interior fever — and the principle survives: the Qur'an's similes point to the ceiling of whichever century reads them.

**The Boiling as Image of Inner State**

One further layer, drawn out by the spiritually-minded exegetes, deserves preservation: the belly's boiling is also the image of the sinner's interior in this world, externalized in the next. The heart that boiled with rage at the truth, with envy at the righteous, with contempt at the warning, is given its boiling made permanent and physical. The Qur'an states the continuity of identity across the two abodes explicitly: *"The Day when the Fire will envelop their faces, and they therein will smile with distorted lips"* is replaced by its reversal; and *"their skins, their hearing, their sight"* are returned as witnesses (41:20–21). The Fire does not import a stranger's torment; it materializes the resident's own history. Verse 46 is that doctrine in two words: the boiling you carried is the boiling you are given.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:47

> **"Seize him and thrust him into the midst of Hellfire!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imperative Scene**

*Khudhūhu fa-ʿtilūhu ilā sawāʾi'l-jaḥīm* — "Seize him, and drag him into the midst of the Blaze." The scene shifts from description to quotation: the verse records the command itself, and its addressees are the guardians of the Fire — the *zabāniyah*, the angels of punishment, whom the Qur'an names in Sūrah al-ʿAlaq: *"Let him beware; if he does not desist, We will drag him by the forelock — the lying, sinning forelock. Let him call his assembly; We will call the Zabāniyah"* (96:15–18). Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr report that the command is God's, issued to the guardians; al-Zamakhsharī notes its grammar: imperatives, plural, rapid — *khudhūhu, ʿtilūhu* — the syntax of a sentence already decided and immediately executed. The verb *ʿatala* means to drag violently, by force, without dignity — the same verb family the Qur'an uses of the Fire's general reception: *"The Day when they will be thrust into the Fire of Hell with a thrusting"* (52:13). And *sawāʾ al-jaḥīm* — the middle, the very midst of the Blaze — assigns the location: not the margin but the center.

**The Inversion of Every Arrest**

The exegetes observe that the verse inverts, point by point, every arrest the denier's regime had performed in this world. Pharaoh's regime seized Moses' people for torture; Quraysh seized the weak believers — Bilāl under his rock, the family of Yāsir, ʿAmmār's mother Sumayyah, the first martyr — for their confession. The Fire's arrest is the same act reversed in jurisdiction: the seizers are seized, and by officers no regime can bribe, intimidate, or out-rank. The Qur'an states the reversal's justice elsewhere: *"And do not think God unaware of what the wrongdoers do; He only defers them for a Day when eyes will stare fixedly"* (14:42); *"The Day when the wrongdoer will bite his hands"* (25:27). The verse also completes the hospitality inversion announced at verse 43: the guest is not welcomed but seized; the host's hall is not entered but dragged into; and the center of the hall — *sawāʾ al-jaḥīm* — is the seat assigned. Ibn Kathīr records the supporting description: *"When the shackles and the chains are around their necks, they will be dragged, in boiling liquid; then in the Fire they will be burned"* (40:71–72) — the dragging is a procession, and its route and destination are both specified in advance, as all of the Fire's operations are: nothing in the scene is improvised, because all of it was scheduled.

**The Verse as the Seizure of the Self**

The spiritually-minded commentators drew the interior reading: the command is executed, in its first form, at the moment of the soul's own death without repentance — *"Then on that Day you will be driven to your Lord"* (75:30); *"And if you could see when the angels take the disbelievers, striking their faces and their backs"* (8:50). The soul that spent its life seizing — property, pleasure, position, the dignity of others — is itself seized at the hour it had always postponed, and dragged toward the center of what it had always mocked. Verse 47 is thus the sūrah's answer, in scene form, to verse 36's demand: the deniers asked to be shown the resurrection on their terms; the verse shows them the resurrection's procedure on its terms — a command, plural officers, a violent drag, a specified destination, and no negotiation at any stage.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:48

> **Then pour the punishment of boiling liquid upon his head."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pouring from Above**

*Thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihī min ʿadhābi'l-ḥamīm* — "Then pour over his head from the punishment of the ḥamīm." The command continues, and its second clause completes the body's administration: the dragging delivered the condemned to the midst (v. 47); the pouring addresses the summit. The verb *ṣabba* — to pour, as water is poured — is domestic vocabulary turned judicial: the Fire's officers pour as a servant pours for a guest, and the liquid poured is named with terrible precision as *"the punishment of the ḥamīm"* — the boiling liquid itself being the punishment, not merely its instrument. Sūrah al-Ḥajj supplies the full description of the same operation: *"Boiling liquid will be poured over their heads, by which what is within their bellies will be melted, and their skins"* (22:19–20). The two verses together give the complete circuit: head, interior, surface — the body administered from above downward. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this verse and 22:19 are the only places the Qur'an describes the ḥamīm poured *upon the head* — elsewhere it is drunk (47:15; 56:54) — and draws the point: the Fire's service varies its delivery as a complete kitchen does, and each delivery is named separately so that no member can claim it was overlooked.

**The Head as Address**

The exegetes draw the head's significance. The head is the seat of the face — the face that was too proud to prostrate, the forehead of 96:15–18's *"lying, sinning forelock"* — and the pouring upon it is the head's own sentencing: the part that claimed height (*ʿalā*, 44:19, 31) receives its portion from the highest point of the body. Al-Rāzī and the later commentators also note the verse's phrase *min ʿadhābi'l-ḥamīm* — "from the punishment of the boiling liquid" — and its preposition *min*: the ḥamīm poured is *from* the punishment, one item of its inventory, implying an inventory without end: *"Indeed, those who disbelieved will be told: the punishment of God is greater than your punishment of yourselves"* is its echo in the tradition's register; and *"Every time their skins are roasted through, We will replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment"* (4:56). The ḥamīm is one punishment among many; the verse names it because it was the one the mocker of Zaqqūm and the player in doubt were best equipped to imagine.

**The Completed Sentence**

Verses 43–48, read as one unit, form the Fire's complete sentence upon the *athīm*: the food assigned (Zaqqūm), its action in the belly (molten boiling), the drink's temperature defined (ḥamīm at maximum), the arrest executed (seize, drag), and the head's portion poured. The sūrah's method throughout this unit is procedural — commands, officers, locations, sequences — and the procedure is the doctrine: the Fire is not chaos but administration, as precise as the chancery that distinguished the year's affairs in verse 4, because it is the same chancery executing its judgments. The hospitality inversion now awaits only its final course: the toast — and the sūrah serves it in the next two verses, with the irony the mocker had always demanded.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:49

> **"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Toast of the Fire**

*Dhuq innaka anta'l-ʿazīzu'l-karīm* — "Taste! Indeed, you are the mighty, the noble." The Fire's hospitality completes itself with the host's address, and the address is irony of the Qur'an's most surgical kind. The words *ʿazīz* (mighty, honored) and *karīm* (noble, generous) are quoted back at the condemned — and the classical commentaries preserve the occasion that makes them a quotation. Ibn ʿAbbās and a group of the early authorities, as al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr record, identified the addressee with Abū Jahl — the chief of Makkah who had held himself in high esteem among his people, claiming the titles of might and nobility in his own valley, and who had mocked the tree of Zaqqūm by name. Ibn Kathīr cites the report that the Prophet ﷺ called Abū Jahl *"the Pharaoh of this ummah"* — the sūrah thereby delivering, in this single verse, the verdict upon both Pharaohs at once: the drowned one of Egypt (vv. 17–33) and the struck one of Badr (v. 16). Some read *innaka* — "indeed you are" — as *annaka* — "because you are": taste *because* you were the mighty, the noble — the causative reading recorded by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Qurṭubī — so that the titles become the ground of the sentence rather than its mockery; both readings stood, and both sharpen the justice.

**The Anatomy of the Irony**

The exegetes dissect the verse's irony with care, and their analysis yields the doctrine. The words are the condemned's own titles, returned to him at the moment of their total negation: he who claimed might receives the seizure of verse 47 — might belongs to the One whose officers now drag him; he who claimed nobility receives the pouring of verse 48 — nobility belongs to the One whose hospitality he refused. The Qur'an stages the same reversal for every self-magnifier: *"And they will be humiliated; indeed, those who were arrogant..."* is its law; and the Prophet ﷺ stated it in the ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"Pride is My cloak, and greatness My garment; whoever contends with Me for either of them, I will cast him into the Fire."* (Muslim 2620, from Abū Hurayrah; Abū Dāwūd 4090, with the wording "I will punish him")

The irony of verse 49 is therefore not cruelty but arithmetic: the titles are read back from the ledger in which the condemned entered them himself, and the reading is performed at the counter where they are no longer legal tender. Al-Rāzī notes the further precision: the address is singular — *you are* — isolating the one who had always been surrounded by his assembly (96:17, *"let him call his assembly"*); the mighty man's final audience is one officer and one sentence.

**The Two Hospitality Halls, Now Separated**

The verse's placement executes the sūrah's architecture. *ʿAzīz* and *karīm* have both appeared earlier in al-Dukhān: the messenger came *noble* (v. 17, *rasūl karīm*), and Pharaoh fell as *high-handed* (v. 31, *ʿāliyan*). The two halls of hospitality now separate finally around these titles: the nobility that was carried — the messenger's, and the reverent's after him — enters the secure station of verse 51; the nobility that was claimed — Pharaoh's, Abū Jahl's, every player's — receives its toast in the midst of the Blaze. The verse is the sūrah's verdict upon every self-made title in every century: the titles survive their holders, and are read aloud, at the counter where they were never valid.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:50

> **This is that which you used to doubt."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Doubt Named and Returned**

*Hādhā'lladhī kuntum bihī tamtarūn* — "This is that concerning which you used to doubt." The toast closes with the file's last line, and the line is addressed to the doubt itself. *Tamtarūn* — from *m-r-y*, to doubt, to dispute — names not honest inquiry but the disputing, playing doubt the sūrah diagnosed at its opening: *"Nay, but they are playing in doubt"* (44:9). The verse returns the exact object: *this* — the smoke, the striking, the Zaqqūm, the pouring, the midst of the Blaze — *this* is the thing you doubted. Al-Ṭabarī reports: this punishment is the reality of what you disputed about in the world; al-Qurṭubī adds the alternative scope: the entire affair — judgment, reckoning, and punishment — about which they played. The sister verses perform the same return across the Book: *"The Day when they are thrust into the Fire of Hell with a thrusting: 'This is the Fire which you used to deny. Is this sorcery, or do you not see?'"* (52:13–15); *"And it will be said: 'Today We forget you as you forgot the meeting of this Day of yours'"* (45:34); *"This is that which you used to deny"* is the standing formula — the exhibit identified against the denial in evidence form.

**Why Doubt Is the Crime**

The verse deserves careful doctrine, for it names doubt as the condemned party's crime, and the modern reader must not misread it. The Qur'an does not punish doubt-as-inquiry; its own method is inquiry's constant summons — *"Have they not traveled the earth?"* (22:46), *"Have they not looked into the dominion of the heavens and the earth?"* (7:185), *"Will you not reason?"* (2:44). What the verse convicts is the species of doubt the sūrah has been documenting since verse 9: the doubt that *plays* — the doubt that refuses to investigate because investigation would conclude, the doubt maintained as a lifestyle rather than a stage, the doubt that answers a clear messenger with "taught, possessed" (44:14) rather than with questions. Ibn Kathīr gathers the family: *"That was because when God alone was called upon, you disbelieved"* (40:12); *"And they used to say: when we are dead and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be resurrected?"* (56:47) — doubt as the standing posture of a life, not the passing posture of a mind. The distinction is the verse's entire jurisprudence: honest doubt that seeks is a road; maintained doubt that plays is a residence, and the residence is what verse 50 is furnishing.

**The Doubter's Own Testimony**

One final precision completes the verse's justice. The condemned are not informed of something new; they are shown something they always knew. The Qur'an records the admission everywhere: *"They will say: 'Had we listened or reasoned, we would not have been among the people of the Blaze'"* (67:10); *"And they will say to their skins: 'Why have you testified against us?' They will say: 'God made us speak, who makes all things speak'"* (41:21); *"The Day when they will see the punishment, the wrongdoer will say: is there any way back?"* (26:227?). The file was inside the doubter all along — the conscience the sūrah appealed to in verse 39's *"but most of them know not"* — and verse 50 is the moment the file is opened and read by its own owner. The sūrah's two hospitality halls are now complete on the Fire's side; the camera turns, in the next verse, to the hall across the divide.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:51

> **Truly the reverent are in a station secure,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Secure Station**

*Inna'l-muttaqīna fī maqāmin amīn* — "Indeed, the reverent are in a station secure." The sūrah's second hospitality hall opens, and it opens with the opposite of everything the Fire's hall displayed: instead of the seized *athīm*, the *muttaqī* — the reverent, the one who practiced *taqwā*, that careful walking through the dangerous path of this life which the Qur'an has made the whole of practical religion; instead of the midst of the Blaze, a *maqām* — a standing-place, a residence, a station; and instead of the Fire's administration of torment, the station's defining adjective: *amīn* — secure, trustworthy, the place itself faithful to its occupants. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr gather the contents of the security: secure from death — *"they taste not death therein, save the first death"* (44:56); secure from Satan's entry; secure from removal — *"and they will not be expelled from it"* is the sense of 15:48's *"and they shall not be expelled"*; secure from grief, fear, and exhaustion: *"All praise to God, who has removed grief from us; indeed our Lord is Forgiving, Appreciative"* (35:34); *"The greatest terror will not grieve them"* (21:103); *"No fatigue shall touch them therein, and they will not be expelled therefrom"* (15:48). The recitation variant — *muqāmin*, "an abiding-place," recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī — adds the dimension of permanence: not merely secure while it lasts but secure *as* lasting.

**The Contrast the Sūrah Built**

The verse's full force is its position in the sūrah's architecture, and the classical commentary on 44:51–52 draws it explicitly: the gardens and springs Pharaoh's people left behind (vv. 25–26) were confiscated in a single morning, because they were gardens held against the Owner's titles; the gardens and springs the reverent enter (44:52) are held under the Owner's titles, and therefore cannot be confiscated. The *maqām karīm* — the noble station — Pharaoh's regime abandoned (44:26) has its true counterpart here: the nobility that was carried rather than claimed receives its permanent residence. The sūrah thereby completes its doctrine of tenure: every station in this world is held on revocable terms; the secure station is the only station held on irrevocable ones, and its deed is the mercy of verse 42 — the sole currency that survived the Day of Division.

**Taqwā as the Station's Title-Deed**

The verse's subject is *al-muttaqūn* — the practitioners of taqwā — and the sūrah has been teaching the word since its second verse-group. Taqwā, in the Qur'an's own definition, is the posture of one who places between himself and what harms him a protection: obedience between himself and punishment, the halal between himself and the haram, the remembered God between himself and the forgotten self. The Prophet ﷺ defined its curriculum in the ḥadīth the tradition recites as the word's commentary:

> *"Fear God wherever you are, follow a bad deed with a good one to erase it, and treat people with good character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987 — who graded it ḥasan — from Abū Dharr and Muʿādh)

— three clauses covering the vertical relation (fear God), the temporal relation (repair the past), and the horizontal relation (good character with people). The secure station of verse 51 is the ripeness of those three clauses; the verse announces it so that the reader knows the title-deed is available, its terms are published, and the station is being furnished while this commentary is being read.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:52

> **amid gardens and springs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confiscated Gardens Returned**

*Fī jannātin wa ʿuyūn* — "in gardens and springs." The words are identical to verse 25's — *jannātin wa ʿuyūn* — and the repetition is the sūrah's deliberate rhyme of justice: the gardens and springs the deniers left behind are the very species of the gardens and springs the reverent are given. The classical commentary states the contrast: Pharaoh's gardens were vacated in a morning because they were held in denial; the reverent's gardens are entered eternally because they were sought in reverence. The Qur'an's Paradise-gardens appear throughout the Book with their fixed equipment — rivers beneath (2:25), fruits within reach (76:14), shade (76:13), springs named individually (76:5–6, 83:27–28) — and al-Dukhān's pairing of *jannāt* with *ʿuyūn* reproduces the formula of the exodus inventory to state its reversal: what the drowned empire could not keep, the reverent are given to keep forever.

**The Psychology of the Garden**

The exegetes ask why gardens and springs are the standing furniture of the Paradise-description, and their answers yield the doctrine. First, the garden is the image of provision-without-anxiety: it yields without being driven, it renews without being replanted by its occupant, it is the opposite of the labor-cursed ground of this world — *"you will not be hungry therein, nor naked; nor thirsty therein, nor scorched by the sun"* (20:118–119). Second, the spring is the image of provision-without-exhaustion: the water that is always arriving and never running out, the antithesis of the hoarded store. Third, and deepest, the garden is the image of beauty-without-price: in this world beauty is purchased — by money, by effort, by compromise — and therefore becomes another theater of competition; the garden of the secure station is beauty without purchase, which is why its description closes the sūrah's hospitality section with *a bounty from your Lord* (44:57). The verse is thus the sūrah's answer, in landscape form, to the materialist creed of verse 35: there is not one death only; there is a garden after it, and springs, and the garden's permanence is the creed's refutation made into a residence.

**The Environmental Ethics This World Receives**

The verse's instruction for this world belongs to its image: the believer's relation to gardens and springs here is rehearsal for his relation to them there. The Prophet ﷺ legislated the rehearsal — *"If the Hour were to rise while one of you is holding a palm-seedling, let him plant it"* (Aḥmad 12902); *"Whoever plants a tree or sows a field, and a bird or a human or an animal eats from it, it is charity for him"* (al-Bukhārī 2320; Muslim 1553) — and the Qur'an made the earth's stewardship a standing appointment: *"It is He who made you vicegerents upon the earth"* (6:165). Pharaoh's gardens were held as ownership and confiscated; the believer's gardens — his own plot, his shade-tree, his well — are held as trust and converted. The verse promises the eternal garden; the ḥadīth converts the temporal one into its down payment.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:53

> **wearing fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Silks and the One Posture**

*Yalbasūna min sundusin wa istabraqin mutaqābilīn* — "They wear of sundus and istabraq, facing one another." The verse clothes the reverent and seats them, and both acts carry doctrine. *Sundus* — the fine, thin silk, the fabric worn against the skin — and *istabraq* — the heavy brocade, the silk woven with gold, the outer garment of display — together cover the wardrobe's two poles: comfort and honor, the intimate and the public. The Qur'an names the two fabrics in every Paradise-wardrobe verse (18:31; 22:23; 76:21), and the pairing states that the secure station supplies both registers of human delight. Then the posture: *mutaqābilīn* — facing one another. The exegetes, al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī among them, draw out its social meaning: none of them sits behind another; all are equally exalted; no hierarchy of seating remains, because no hierarchy of merit remains — the merit that earned the station is the same mercy (44:57) in every case. The sister verses complete the furnishing: *"Reclining upon couches lined with brocade"* (55:54); *"Upon thrones, facing one another"* (15:47; 37:44); *"And We remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment; as brothers, upon couches, facing one another"* (15:47).

**The Resentment Removed**

The facing-one-another of Paradise is the cure of the turning-away of this world, and the verse's deepest doctrine is its social one. Human society in this world runs largely on backs turned: envy turns faces away from the envied; rivalry turns faces toward the superior and away from the inferior; resentment archives its grievances and seats them at every table. The secure station removes the organ itself: *"We remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment"* (15:47; 7:43). Al-Rāzī notes that the removal is stated before the facing, because facing with resentment still in the breast is merely proximity; facing after its removal is company. The Prophet ﷺ legislated the rehearsal of this posture in this world: *"Do not envy one another, do not outbid one another in hatred, do not turn away from one another; be, O servants of God, brothers"* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah) — the ḥadīth dismantling, clause by clause, the machinery that produces turned backs. The silk of verse 53 is thus not merely fabric but social fabric: the wardrobe of a community whose faces have finally turned toward one another.

**The Silk Prohibited Here, Granted There**

The verse's jurisprudence for this world is precise, and the tradition preserved it. The Prophet ﷺ prohibited silk and gold for the males of his community in this life:

> *"These two are forbidden for the males of my ummah and permitted for their females."* (Ibn Mājah 3595; al-Tirmidhī 1720; Abū Dāwūd 4057, in variant wordings)

and

> *"Whoever wears silk in this world will not wear it in the Hereafter."* (al-Bukhārī 5633; Muslim 2073)

The prohibition and the grant together state the Qur'an's economics of pleasure: nothing genuinely good is ultimately forbidden; the forbidden here is the same species granted there, and the difference is the channel, not the substance. The believer who declines the silk of this world at the boundary of its rule receives the sundus and istabraq beyond it — the pleasure preserved by being postponed, and enhanced by being earned. The verse is thus both description and discipline: the wardrobe is being woven, and every boundary kept in this world is a thread in it.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:54

> **Thus it is. And We shall wed them to wide-eyed maidens.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thus It Is": The Seal of Incomparability**

*Kadhālika wa zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin ʿīn* — "Thus — and We wed them to wide-eyed maidens." The particle *kadhālika* — "thus," "so it is" — functions here, as the exegetes record, in two readings: as the seal of completion — "this is how the matter stands," the hospitality's description authenticated, the like of it beyond adequate telling (al-Qurṭubī cites this reading); and as the bridge of addition — "just as We gave them the gardens, the springs, the silk, and the facing company, *likewise* We honored them with this" (al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī). Both readings agree on the substance: the verse is the grant's continuation, and the grant is being itemized as a dowry is itemized — garden, spring, wardrobe, company, and now marriage. The verb is *zawwajnāhum* — We wedded them, We paired them — the language of lawful marriage, not of possession: the relation granted is the relation of covenant, with its entire Qur'anic freight of reciprocity — *"They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them"* (2:187) — applied at the summit of its species.

**The Wide-Eyed: The Phrase and Its Meanings**

*Ḥūr ʿīn* — the phrase appears across the Paradise-descriptions (44:54; 52:20; 55:72; 56:22) and the exegetes preserve its full philology. *Ḥūr* is the plural of *ḥawrāʾ* — the one whose *ḥawar* is intense: the extreme whiteness of the white of the eye and the extreme blackness of its pupil, the contrast that the Arabs counted the height of the eye's beauty and purity; *ʿīn* is the plural of *ʿaynāʾ* — the large-eyed, the eye itself being the face's seat of expression. The classical commentary on this verse adds the layers: al-Rāzī, on 52:20, observed that since the eye reflects the soul more clearly than any other member, the eye-description carries the meaning "rich of soul"; Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have read *ḥūr ʿīn* as referring to the righteous women of this world transformed; a minority reading identifies them with the women of Paradise generally; the majority hold them a creation of the Hereafter, described in the narrations the exegetes gather as formed from musk, camphor, and saffron, and in another report *"created from the glorifications of the angels"* — with the standing correction, also preserved by al-Qurṭubī and others, that the pious women of this world hold the higher station, on the reports of Umm Salamah and others recorded by al-Ṭabarānī and cited in the tafsīr literature. The phrase's function across all readings is one: the beloved described by the eye rather than the body, by purity and expressiveness rather than display — the Qur'an's consistent register for Paradise's intimacy, which names the soul-facing before anything else.

**The Cap on Imagination**

The exegetes cap the entire description with the ḥadīth qudsī, and it is the correct cap:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and has not occurred to the heart of man."* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824 — and recite, if you wish: *"No soul knows what comfort of eyes is hidden for them"* — 32:17)

The Paradise-descriptions, including this verse, are therefore read in the tradition as true in substance and surpassing in reality: the names are shared, the realities exceed. The verse's doctrine for this world follows immediately: the one who guards his eye and his channel here — *"Tell the believing men to lower their gaze"* (24:30); *"And whoever guards his chastity — those are the inheritors"* (23:5–6, 10–11) — is not renouncing the species of delight; he is transferring it across the divide, where it arrives, as this verse announces, in the form of covenant rather than consumption.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:55

> **Therein they call for every fruit, secure.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Request Without Anxiety**

*Yadʿūna fīhā bi-kulli fākihatin āminīn* — "They call therein for every fruit, secure." The verse completes the secure station's service, and its two words do the work. First, the service: *they call* — the fruit is not gathered by labor but summoned by request; the occupant of the secure station does not go out to his provision; his provision comes in at his word. The Qur'an states the same law of service across the Paradise-descriptions: *"And therein is whatever the souls desire and the eyes delight in"* (43:71); *"They will have therein whatever they wish"* (16:31; 25:16; 39:34); *"Fruits in abundance, neither cut off nor forbidden"* (56:32–33); *"And the fruits of what they choose"* (56:20). Second, and deeper, the state: *āminīn* — secure. The word returns the station's title (v. 51, *maqām amīn*) into the occupants' own posture: they are not only in a secure place; they are *secure in their requesting*. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr state the doctrine: the security is from interruption — the fruit is never cut off; from harm — no fruit of the secure station carries the poison this world hides in its sweetest things; and from deprivation — the request is never refused, postponed, or priced.

**Why Security Is Named Twice**

The verse's repetition of security — station secure (v. 51), calling secure (v. 55) — is the sūrah's pastoral point, and it names the exact wound of human pleasure in this world. Every delight here is eaten with fear in the hand: fear of its end, of its price, of its side-effect, of the rival who wants it, of the body that will fail to enjoy it. The philosophers of every century have diagnosed the shadow; the Qur'an legislates its removal. The secure station's fruit is the first pleasure in human experience that arrives without its accompanying grief, and the verse announces it so that the reader understands what is actually being offered: not more pleasure but *unshadowed* pleasure — the enjoyment separated from the anxiety that has always been sold with it. The sister verse states the removal at the root: *"All praise to God, who has removed grief from us"* (35:34); and the Prophet ﷺ defined the Paradise-gift's negative pole in the ḥadīth of the seven sheltered, whose company is completed by *"a man whose heart is attached to the mosques"* — the heart already rehearsing, in this world, the security it will inhabit in the next (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031).

**The Fruit as Image of Every Good**

The exegetes note that the verse names *fruit* — not bread, not meat — and draw the point: fruit is the food of delight, not of necessity; hunger is unknown in the secure station (20:118), so what is summoned there is summoned purely for enjoyment. The image thereby generalizes: *every fruit* is every good called for without cost to the soul — the conversation, the view, the company, the knowledge, the rest. The verse is the sūrah's final answer to the materialist's demand of verse 36: they asked to be shown the fathers; the answer shows the fruit — the resurrection's content delivered as an invitation rather than a demonstration, because invitations are answered by the willing, and demonstrations only by the compelled.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:56

> **They taste not death therein, save the first death. And He will shield them from the punishment of Hellfire—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deniers' Own Words, Inverted**

*Lā yadhūqūna fīhā'l-mawta illā'l-mawtata'l-ūlā* — "They taste not death therein, save the first death." The verse is among the Qur'an's most precise rhetorical returns: it quotes the materialist creed of verse 35 back at its refutation, transformed into the believer's reward. The deniers had said: *"There is naught but our first death"* (44:35) — one death, and no resurrection, as their entire hope. The verse answers: you asked for exactly one death — and exactly one death is what the reverent receive; but where the creed meant "one death and then nothing," the grant means "one death and then everything." The same death, two completions: for the denier the death that ends in the Fire's "taste!" (44:49); for the reverent the death that ends in the station's security (44:51). The Qur'an states the removal of death from Paradise elsewhere as its standing law: *"They will not taste death therein, save the first death"* is this verse; and the ḥadīth supplies the scene of death's final abolition, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī:

> *"Death will be brought on the Day of Resurrection as a speckled ram and made to stand between Paradise and the Fire, and it will be said: 'O people of Paradise, do you know this?' ... and it will be said: 'O people of the Fire, do you know this?' ... Then it will be slaughtered, and it will be said: 'O people of Paradise, eternally, with no death; and O people of the Fire, eternally, with no death.'"* (al-Bukhārī 4730; Muslim 2850)

**The Shielding from the Fire**

The verse's second clause — *wa waqāhum ʿadhāba'l-jaḥīm* — "and He shielded them from the punishment of the Blaze" — states the mercy's negative operation, and the verb *waqā* — shielded, protected, guarded against — is the same family as *taqwā* itself: the reverent (*muttaqūn*) who practiced the shielding of themselves in this world are shielded by God in the next. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the shielding is integral to the Mercy of the Divine Nature, which precedes the Wrath — the ḥadīth qudsī's *"My mercy overcomes My wrath"* (al-Bukhārī 7404; Muslim 2751) — and he cites the ḥadīth the classical commentary places on this verse, the Prophet ﷺ's demonstration with the nursing mother:

> *"Do you think this woman would allow her child to be thrown into the fire?" They said: "By God, no, insofar as she is able." He said: "And God is more tender toward His servants than this woman toward her child."* (al-Bukhārī 5999; Muslim 2754)

The shielding is thus not the exception to the Fire's administration but the mercy's own standing office: the Fire is real, its hospitality furnished (vv. 43–50); and the shielding is real, its file populated — and the two realities stand in the sūrah exactly as the two names stand at verse 42: *the Mighty, the Merciful*. The verse's pastoral function is the removal of the believer's last anxiety: the reverent man carries, beneath all his obedience, the fear that it may not be enough; verse 56 answers the fear at its root — it is not the servant's grip on God that completes the salvation, but God's shielding of the servant, and the shielding is promised in the Book.

**The Taste That Ends**

The verb *yadhūqūna* — they taste — deserves its final pause, for the Qur'an uses tasting for the Fire's punishment too: *"Taste! Surely you are the mighty, the noble"* (44:49); *"Taste the touch of Saqar"* (54:48). The symmetry states the doctrine: both abodes are *tasted* — experienced fully, bodily, personally — but one tasting has no second course, and the verse announces it: the reverent's tasting of death is completed in the first one, and every tasting after it is of the fruit (44:55). The materialist creed of one tasting is thus honored in the only place it could ever be true — in the station of the reverent — and refuted in the place the creed chose for it: the denier's one death opens into a tasting that the sūrah has just furnished in full.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:57

> **a bounty from thy Lord; that is the great triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bounty, Not Wages**

*Faḍlan min rabbik; dhālika huwa'l-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm* — "as a bounty from your Lord; that is the great triumph." The verse states the entire hospitality section's ground, and it states it in one word: *faḍl* — bounty, grace, favor unowed. The exegetes are unanimous on the doctrine, al-Rāzī stating it most fully: everything the preceding verses itemized — the secure station, the gardens, the springs, the silk, the facing company, the marriage, the fruit, the death's removal, the shielding — is bounty, not wage; none of it is *deserved* by the reverent as such, because the faith by which they believed and the obedience by which they acted were themselves God's bounty upon them. The ḥadīth supplies the doctrine's prophetic statement:

> *"None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone." They said: "Nor you, Messenger of God?" He said: "Nor I — unless God envelops me with mercy from Him and favor."* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816)

The scholarly harmonization of this with the Qur'an's reward-verses (*"and you will be repaid in full for what you used to do"* — 66:11 and its family) preserves both truths: deeds are the condition and the sign of the bounty, not its price; the servant works as the traveler walks toward the promised water, and the water remains gift. The doctrine is the sūrah's final answer to every economy of self-salvation — Pharaoh's (*"is not the kingdom of Egypt mine?"* — 43:51), Abū Jahl's (*"the mighty, the noble"* — 44:49), and every reader's private version: the station is secure because its deed is bounty, and bounty, unlike wages, cannot be reduced, audited away, or revoked for the employer's convenience.

**The Great Triumph Defined**

The verse's second clause names the result: *al-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm* — the great triumph. The Qur'an uses the triumph-formula at four summits (9:72; 23:111; 44:57; 85:11), and defines it once, juridically: *"Everyone will taste death, and you will be repaid in full on the Day of Resurrection; whoever is moved away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has triumphed"* (3:185). The triumph has thus two constituent acts — removal from the Fire (the shielding of 44:56) and admission to the Garden (the station of 44:51) — and the verse pronounces both complete. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the triumph is called *great* because it is the only triumph that cannot be reversed: every worldly victory — the gardens, the springs, the noble station of verse 25–26 — was a triumph with confiscation scheduled in it; the great triumph is the one whose deed is bounty and whose station is secure. The phrase stands at the end of the hospitality section as the audit's closing line: the file of the reverent is not merely balanced; it is pronounced victorious, by the One whose pronouncement no court can review.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:58

> **We have only made this easy upon thy tongue, that haply they may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ease as Evidence**

*Fa innamā yassarnāhu bi-lisānika laʿallahum yataẓakkarūn* — "Indeed, We have only made it easy upon your tongue, that perhaps they may remember." The sūrah returns to its opening subject — the Book sworn to in verse 2 — and closes the circle with the Book's accessibility. The verb *yassarnā* — We made easy — is the Qur'an's standing testimony about itself: *"And indeed We have made the Qur'an easy to remember — so is there any who will remember?"* (54:17, 22, 32, 40, the refrain of Sūrah al-Qamar); *"We have only made it easy upon your tongue that you may give glad tidings to the reverent thereby, and warn thereby a contentious people"* (19:97). The ease operates on every level the exegetes record: ease of recitation — the Book is pronounced as it was revealed, by an unlettered tongue made its perfect instrument; ease of memorization — no book of its length has ever been memorized whole by so many millions, most of them outside its language; ease of meaning — its guidance in the essentials is available to the unlettered and the scholar alike; and ease of transmission — *"upon your tongue"*: the Book's primary vehicle is not the page but the living voice, and the verse's second person names the first of the tongues. Al-Rāzī notes that the ease is itself part of the evidence: a speech this easy to carry, this impossible to imitate, this sustained in content, testifies by its properties to the descent the sūrah announced in verse 3.

**"Upon Thy Tongue": The Oral Constitution of Revelation**

The phrase *bi-lisānika* — upon your tongue — deserves its pause, for it states the Qur'an's oral constitution, and the pastoral consequences the tradition drew from it. The revelation was delivered as speech to be spoken, not as text to be archived; the Prophet ﷺ received it in his tongue, recited it in his tongue, and taught it into the tongues of his Companions, and the written muṣḥaf is the servant of that transmission, not its master — which is why the recitation-rules (*tajwīd*), the variant readings, and the memorizer's chain remain the Book's primary science. The ḥadīth supplies the reciter's charter:

> *"The best of you is the one who learns the Qur'an and teaches it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān)

and

> *"Recite the Qur'an, for on the Day of Resurrection it will come as an intercessor for its companions."* (Muslim 804, from Abū Umāmah)

The verse's purpose-clause — *that perhaps they may remember* — states the ease's object: remembrance (*tadhakkur*), the recovery of what the soul already knows. The Qur'an does not describe itself as information imposed but as remembrance offered — *"And remind, for the reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55) — and the ease is administered to that end: every barrier removed so that the remembering can happen — the language made clear, the messenger made trustworthy, the recitation made easy, the memory made available. The verse is thus the sūrah's answer to every excuse of inaccessibility: the Book is on the tongue, the tongue is yours, and the remembering is one recitation away.

**The Second-Person Comfort**

The verse also carries the pastoral second person the sūrah has reserved for its moments of maximum tenderness — *"a mercy from your Lord"* (44:6) — and it applies it now, at the sūrah's close, to the Prophet ﷺ himself: after the smoke, the drowning, the Zaqqūm, and the Division, the address bends to the one who carried all of it to his city: *We made it easy upon your tongue*. The ease is the messenger's consolation: the message he delivered under mockery, famine-prayer, boycott, and war was carried for him, in the very organ of its delivery, by the One who sent it. Every reciter since inherits the clause: the Book is easy upon the tongue because its first tongue was made its ease.

## Sūrah al-Dukhān 44:59

> **So be on the watch, for they, too, are on the watch.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Frame Closes: The Two Watch-Posts**

*Fartaqib innahum murtaqibūn* — "So watch; indeed, they are watching." The sūrah ends on the verb with which it commanded its first watching: *fartaqib* opened the smoke-promise in verse 10 — *"So keep watch for a day when the sky brings forth manifest smoke"* — and now closes the chapter, enclosing the entire argument in a frame of vigilance. The verb *irtaqib* — from *r-q-b*, the sentinel's fixed watch — appears twice and only twice in the sūrah, at its two termini, and the symmetry is the sūrah's final doctrine: everything between verse 10 and verse 59 — the blessed night, the distinguished affairs, the Moses cycle, the Tubbaʿ archive, the Day of Division, the two hospitality halls — is the content of what the two parties are watching *for*. Al-Ṭabarī reports the exegetical contents of the command: watch for the aid I have promised you against them; al-Qurṭubī adds: they watch for your death and your downfall, and you watch for what God has promised you — and the sister verses carry the same construction across the Book: *"And wait; indeed, they are waiting"* (52:31); *"Each party is awaiting"* is the sense of 11:122's *"And wait; indeed, We are waiting"*; *"Say: wait; we too are waiting with you"* (10:102; cf. 6:158; 32:30).

**What Each Party Watches For**

The verse's genius is its documentation that both parties are already watching — the difference between them is not vigilance versus negligence, but the object of the vigil. The deniers watch for the believer's end: the smoke they were promised to disprove, the community they were promised to outlast, the death they were promised to see; their watching is the continuation of the play of verse 9 by other means. The believers watch for the promise's fulfillment: the respite's expiry, the striking's arrival, the Division's docket, the station's furnishing. And the sūrah, having furnished both hospitality halls in full, leaves the reader at his own watch-post with the complete inventory of what each vigil is waiting for — so that the final question is not whether to watch (everyone watches; the verse says so) but what to watch for. The Prophet ﷺ's own watching is the tradition's embodied commentary: the night-long vigil at Badr, the iʿtikāf of the last ten nights, the standing supplication *"O God, I ask You for the coolness of Paradise"* — the sentinel whose post was worship and whose horizon was the appointment.

**The Sūrah's Standing**

Al-Dukhān closes, then, not with a period but with a posture. The Book sworn to (v. 2) has been delivered; the blessed night (v. 3) has been dated; the affairs have been distinguished (v. 4); the mercy has been named (v. 6); the doubt has been diagnosed (v. 9); the smoke has been announced (vv. 10–11); the Pharaoh archive has been opened (vv. 17–33); the materialist creed has been quoted and answered (vv. 34–39); the Day of Division has been scheduled (v. 40); both hospitality halls have been furnished (vv. 43–57); and the ease has been placed upon the tongue (v. 58). What remains is the watch. Every reader of this commentary stands, at this sentence, in the interval the sūrah was revealed to administer — the interval between the announcement and the arrival, between the distinguished affairs of this year and the next, between the smoke's promise and the smoke's day — and the sūrah's last word assigns him his position in it: *fartaqib* — watch, for they are watching; and what each party watches for is already written, affair by affair, in the chancery of the blessed night.
