# Sūrah al-Jumuʿah (Chapter 62) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah That Names the Gathering**

Sūrah al-Jumuʿah, "The Congregation" or "The Gathering," is the sixty-second chapter of the Qur'an, an eleven-verse Madinan sūrah that takes its name from the Friday congregational prayer commanded in verse 9. Although a minority of early authorities counted it among the Makkan revelations, the internal evidence — above all the sustained address to the Jews in verses 5–8 and the legislation of the Friday prayer in verses 9–11 — places it firmly in the Madinan period, when a functioning Muslim community already existed with its markets, its mosque, and its covenantal neighbors. It is the fourth of the group known as *al-Musabbiḥāt*, "The Glorifiers" — the sūrahs that open with the glorification of God: numbers 57, 59, 61, 62, and 64. To recite their openings in sequence is to hear the same cosmic chorus announced five times, each time with a different grammatical tense and a different theological accent, as though the Book itself were teaching the believer that glorification is not an event but an atmosphere.

**A Bridge Between Two Sūrahs**

The classical commentators noticed that al-Jumuʿah is joined to the sūrah before it, al-Ṣaff ("The Ranks"), by more than adjacency. Both chapters weigh the commerce of this world against the commerce of the next. Al-Ṣaff ends by summoning the believers to a trade that saves from painful punishment — belief in God and striving in His way — while al-Jumuʿah commands the believers to suspend their literal trading for the Friday prayer and then contrasts worldly commerce with what is with God. Some commentators add a second link: al-Ṣaff praises those who fight in God's way drawn up in ranks, and al-Jumuʿah summons the believers to stand in ranks behind their imam in the congregational prayer. War and worship share a geometry in this vision — the straight rank — because both are disciplines in which the individual self dissolves into a single directed body. The sūrah after it, al-Munāfiqūn ("The Hypocrites"), completes the triptych: gathering in the mosque is easy, but the next chapter warns that bodies can gather while hearts stay away.

**The Prophet's Own Practice on Fridays**

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ honored this sūrah in his own devotional practice. It is authentically reported that he would often recite Sūrah al-Jumuʿah and Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn together in the Friday congregational prayer itself — the chapter commanding the gathering, and the chapter warning against hollow gathering, read aloud to the gathered. Muslim records from Ibn ʿAbbās and from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of God ﷺ used to recite in the Friday prayer *Sūrah al-Jumuʿah* and *al-Munāfiqūn*, and in the two ʿĪd prayers he would recite Sūrah Qāf and *Iqtarabati al-sāʿah* (Muslim 879). The pairing was deliberate pedagogy: week after week, the community heard first the honor of its gathering and then the exposure of gathering without sincerity. No congregation could hear both and remain comfortable in mere attendance.

**The Day Itself: Why Friday Matters**

Two well-known prophetic reports fix the rank of the day this sūrah sanctifies. Muslim records:

> *"The best day over which the sun has risen is Friday: on it Adam was created, and on it he entered the Garden, and on it he was removed from it. And the Hour will not come save on Friday."* (Muslim 854, from Abū Hurayrah)

And concerning the name of the day, the commentaries preserve the report that it is called *al-jumuʿah*, "the Gathering," because on that day the physical constitution (*khalq*) of Adam was gathered together — God assembled the human creature on the day He assembled the week around worship. The logic is beautiful: the day on which humanity was gathered into existence became the day on which humanity gathers in worship. Every Friday reenacts, in miniature, the gathering of mankind itself.

**The Architecture of Eleven Verses**

Though brief, the sūrah moves in three deliberate movements. Verses 1–4 open with the universal glorification of God and the sending of the Messenger ﷺ among the unlettered as an act of pure divine bounty — the gift. Verses 5–8 present the warning example of those who were entrusted with revelation before and failed to carry it — the Jews who bore the Torah as a donkey bears books — and challenge their claim of exclusive divine friendship with the test of longing for death — the warning. Verses 9–11 then turn to the believers with the positive legislation: leave trade for the remembrance of God when the call is made, then return to the earth seeking His bounty while remembering Him much — the way. Gift, warning, way: the whole logic of revelation is compressed into eleven verses. The commentary that follows treats each verse as an independent discourse, in strict order, without compression — for as the sūrah itself teaches, the Book must be *carried*, not merely borne on the back.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:1

> **Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God, the Sovereign, the Holy, the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chorus That Never Falls Silent**

The sūrah opens with a sentence that contains no command, no story, and no argument — only a fact about the universe: *yusabbiḥu li-llāhi mā fī al-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — "Whatsoever is in the heavens and whatsoever is on the earth glorifies God." The verb *yusabbiḥu* is in the present tense, and the grammarians note the significance: glorification is not something creation did once, nor something it will do one day, but something it *does* — continuously, presently, without intermission. The same declaration opens Sūrah al-Ḥadīd (57:1) in the past tense (*sabbaha*, "has glorified"), Sūrah al-Ḥashr (59:1) and al-Ṣaff (61:1) likewise in the past, and Sūrah al-Taghābun (64:1) in the present like ours. Taken together, the five Glorifiers teach that creation glorified God before man existed, glorifies Him now, and will glorify Him after man is gone. Man is not the choir's founder; he is its late-arriving member, invited to join a song already in progress.

**What It Means for Things to Glorify**

But what does it mean for unconscious things — stones, stars, seas — to "glorify" God? The Arabic root *s-b-ḥ* carries the sense of swimming, floating, moving freely through a medium; from it comes *tasbīḥ*, declaring God free — free of every defect, every partner, every unworthy attribute. The classical commentators offered two complementary understandings, and both are true at once. First, the glorification of *state* (*tasbīḥ al-ḥāl*): every created thing, by its very existence, order, and dependence, testifies that it has a Perfect Maker. The precision of a honeycomb, the orbit of a planet, the'acqua of the eye — each is a silent sentence declaring that blind chance could not have authored it. Second, the glorification of *speech* (*tasbīḥ al-maqāl*): God has given all things a mode of utterance we cannot comprehend. The Qur'an states this explicitly:

> *"The seven heavens glorify Him, and the earth, and whosoever is therein. And there is not a thing but that it glorifies Him with praise, but you understand not their glorification. Truly He is Forbearing, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 17:44)

And of the mountains and birds glorfying with David:

> *"And We made the mountains subject, glorifying with him at eventide and sunrise, and the birds gathered — all turning unto Him."* (Qur'an 38:18–19)

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on these verses, affirms that all creatures genuinely glorify in ways suited to their natures, known fully only to God. Al-Ṭabarī records from the early generations that even the pebbles glorified in the Prophet's ﷺ hand, and the food glorified as it was eaten — authenticated in the report of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd:

> *"We used to hear the food glorifying God as it was being eaten."* (al-Bukhārī 3579)

The believer who grasps this never feels alone in worship. When he says *subḥāna Allāh*, the ground beneath him, the air around him, and the stars above him are saying it in their own tongues. Solitary prayer is a physical impossibility; every *dhikr* is congregational.

**Four Names That Govern the Sūrah**

The verse closes with four divine Names — *al-Malik, al-Quddūs, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm*: "the Sovereign, the Holy, the Mighty, the Wise." Each Name governs something that follows in the sūrah, and the commentators delight in tracing the threads. *Al-Malik*, the Sovereign — from *m-l-k*, ownership and kingship — is the King whose summons no subject may ignore; it is His Friday call that will interrupt all trading in verse 9. *Al-Quddūs*, the Holy — from *q-d-s*, purity and transcendence, related to the word for Jerusalem (*al-Quds*, "the Holy") and to the Holy Spirit — is the One utterly free of defect; it is His holiness that creation's glorification declares, and His holiness that demands the purification (*tazkiyah*) of verse 2. *Al-ʿAzīz*, the Mighty — the All-Powerful whom none can resist or humiliate — will recur at the end of verse 3, sealing the promise that the Prophet's ﷺ mission will reach all later generations; no power can block it. *Al-Ḥakīm*, the Wise — who places everything in its perfect position — will recur with the Mighty in verse 3 and stands behind the wisdom of sending an unlettered Messenger (verse 2) and behind the legislation of Friday (verses 9–11). Four Names, eleven verses: the sūrah is sealed at both ends by its theology. Ibn Taymiyyah frequently observed that the Qur'an's pairings of divine Names are never ornamental; each pairing is itself an argument. Might joined to Wisdom means power exercised with perfect judgment — the precise combination a heart needs to trust when commanded to abandon its livelihood for an hour of prayer.

**Why Begin a Sūrah of Friday Law with Cosmology**

There is a deliberate logic in opening legislation about Friday commerce with the glorification of the cosmos. Before God tells man when to trade and when to pray, He shows man where he stands: a small creature inside a vast worshipping universe, addressed by its Sovereign King. The Friday command will therefore not feel like an arbitrary rule but like an invitation into proportion — the part aligning itself with the whole. The modern reader, drowning in noise and notifications, may feel this most sharply: the verse proposes that beneath theMarket's clamor there is a deeper sound, the silent *tasbīḥ* of all things, and that the Friday prayer is the weekly appointment where man tunes his hearing back to it. The merchant who closes his shop at the call is not leaving reality for ritual; he is leaving one small transaction to join the only transaction in which the entire universe is engaged.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:2

> **He it is Who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from among themselves, reciting unto them His signs, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom, though before they were in manifest error,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Unlettered: An Insult Turned Into an Honor**

*Huwa alladhī baʿatha fī al-ummiyyīna rasūlan minhum* — "He it is Who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from among themselves." The word *ummī* (plural *ummiyyūn*) comes from *umm*, "mother" — one who remains as his mother bore him, untaught in reading and writing. Al-Ṭabarī records that in this context it denotes the idolatrous Arabs who possessed no scripture, in contrast to the Jews and Christians, the "People of the Book"; others, such as al-Rāzī, extend it to all who had no revealed Book. The Qur'an itself uses the term in this contrast:

> *"And among them are unlettered ones who know not the Book, save wishful fancies, and they do naught but conjecture."* (Qur'an 2:78)

Two profound points follow. First, the Messenger was *minhum*, "from among themselves" — not an angel, not a foreigner, not a king from a distant empire, but one of their own, speaking their tongue, known in his lineage, his honesty, and his character. This is the divine pedagogy: guidance comes dressed in the familiar so that none can plead estrangement. Second, and more wonderfully, God chose illiteracy as the vessel of the final revelation. The Arabs' lack of a Book was, in worldly eyes, backwardness; in God's arrangement, it became the clearest proof of the revelation's divine origin. A man who never studied under any teacher, who could neither read the scriptures of others nor write his own, suddenly recited a Book that humbled the masters of Arabic eloquence, narrated the histories of ancient prophets, and legislated for a civilization. The Qur'an itself draws the argument:

> *"And thou didst not recite any book before it, nor didst thou write it with thy right hand; else would the falsifiers have doubted."* (Qur'an 29:48)

Ibn Kathīr stresses this as among the greatest of the Prophet's ﷺ miracles: the unlettered teacher of all teachers. For the modern believer, the lesson endures: God is never constrained by human credentials. He raises whom He wills from whatever station, and the honor of a people lies not in their libraries but in their Lord.

**Four Functions of the Messenger**

The verse assigns the Messenger ﷺ a fourfold mission: *yatlū ʿalayhim āyātihi wa yuzakkīhim wa yuʿallimuhum al-kitāba wa al-ḥikmah* — "reciting unto them His signs, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom." Each term is a science in itself. *Tilāwah*, recitation, comes from *t-l-w*, "to follow" — the reciter causes the hearer to *follow* the revealed words; the Prophet ﷺ was the first reciter, and every recitation since follows his. *Tazkiyah*, purification — from *z-k-w*, growth and purity — is, as the early commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī) explain, purification from idolatry, from corrupt belief, and from base character; it is both cleansing and cultivation, like pruning a tree so it grows. Then comes the double teaching: *al-Kitāb*, the Book — the Qur'an itself — and *al-Ḥikmah*, the Wisdom. This pairing appears repeatedly:

> *"Our Lord, and raise up among them a Messenger from among them, who shall recite unto them Thy signs, and teach them the Book and Wisdom, and purify them. Truly Thou art the Mighty, the Wise."* (Qur'an 2:129 — the supplication of Abraham and Ishmael)

> *"Certainly God conferred a favor upon the believers when He raised up among them a Messenger from among themselves, reciting unto them His signs, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom, though before they were in manifest error."* (Qur'an 3:164)

Note that 3:164 is nearly identical to our verse — God repeats the favor so that none forgets it. What is *al-Ḥikmah*? Many early authorities, including al-Shāfiʿī, understood it as the Sunnah — the Prophet's ﷺ teaching, example, and judgment, which explain and apply the Book; others, like al-Ṭabarī, take it more broadly as all divinely inspired knowledge of this world and the next, including understanding (*fiqh*) of the Book itself. The two views unite in practice: the Book without the Wisdom would be a text without a teacher, and the Prophet ﷺ was sent to be both reader and explainer. This is also the fulfillment of the primordial covenant God took from all prophets:

> *"Should a messenger then come to you confirming that which is with you, you shall surely believe in him and you shall help him."* (Qur'an 3:81)

The teaching of Book and Wisdom is likewise attributed to Abraham (4:54), to Jesus (5:110), and to Muhammad ﷺ (4:113) — one curriculum, many classrooms, until the final classroom encompassed all mankind.

**"Though Before They Were in Manifest Error"**

The verse ends with a backward glance: *wa in kānū min qablu la-fī ḍalālin mubīn* — "though before they were in manifest error." *Ḍalāl*, error, is literally straying from the road, like a traveler lost in the desert; *mubīn* means open, evident, undeniable. The Arabs before Islam worshipped stones they carved with their own hands, buried their daughters alive, waged wars of forty years over a camel or a horse race, drank and gambled away their wealth, and knew no law above tribal vengeance. The Qur'an does not romanticize this past; it names it plainly so that the magnitude of the favor is felt. The psychological wisdom here is profound: gratitude requires memory. A people who forget what they were cannot value what they have become. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly commands remembrance of the "before" — as with the believers of Madinah:

> *"And remember God's Blessing upon you, when you were enemies and He joined your hearts together, so that by His Blessing you became brethren."* (Qur'an 3:103)

The convert's testimony, the repentant sinner's tears, the community's founding story — all draw their power from this contrast. Modern Muslims, born into the favor, must deliberately study the *jāhiliyyah* — the pre-Islamic ignorance — not out of contempt for ancestors but out of honesty about what revelation changed. The man who thinks he was always guided has no gratitude; the man who remembers his error treasures his guidance like a rescued drowning man treasures the shore.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:3

> **and others among them, who have not yet joined them. And He is the Mighty, the Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Grammar of Inclusion**

This short verse is grammatically joined to the one before it: *wa ākharīna minhum lammā yalḥaqū bihim* — "and others among them, who have not yet joined them." The word *ākharīn*, "others," follows upon *al-ummiyyīn*, "the unlettered" — meaning the Messenger was sent not only to the Arabs of his generation but to "others" who had "not yet joined them." The verb *yalḥaqū*, from *l-ḥ-q*, means to catch up with, to join, to attain — as a rider catches up with a caravan. The image is of a caravan already on the road (the first believers) with more travelers destined to catch up across the centuries. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others hold that this encompasses everyone who will enter Islam from the Prophet's ﷺ time until the Day of Resurrection, in every land and every age — so that the Prophet's ﷺ functions of reciting, purifying, and teaching extend to all later generations through the preserved Book and the transmitted Sunnah. When a believer in Malaysia or Morocco or Minnesota opens the Qur'an today, this verse is being fulfilled in him: he is one of the "others" who has now joined.

**The Persians in the Prophecy**

The most celebrated occasion attached to this verse is preserved in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. When it was revealed, the Companions asked, "Who are these others, O Messenger of God?" The Prophet ﷺ placed his hand upon Salmān al-Fārisī — the Persian seeker who had journeyed through Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and slavery to Islam — and said:

> *"Were faith to be at the Pleiades, men from among these would surely reach it."* (al-Bukhārī 4897; Muslim 2546, from Abū Hurayrah)

In some narrations: *"men from among the Persians."* Ibn Kathīr cites this ḥadīth as decisive proof that the verse embraces the non-Arabs (*al-ʿajam*) who would enter Islam — the Persians, the Turks, the Indians, the Africans, the Europeans, and all peoples. Consider the daring of the prophecy: at the moment of its utterance, the Muslim community was a small Arab minority, and Persia was a mighty Zoroastrian empire that had just received — and torn up — the Prophet's ﷺ letter of invitation. To declare that *Persians* would one day be the bearers of this faith, reaching for it though it hung among the stars, was humanly absurd and divinely certain. Within a generation, Salmān's people produced scholars like al-Bukhārī, Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, Abū Ḥanīfah, al-Ghazālī, and Rūmī — men whose names now define Islamic learning itself. The verse is thus a standing demolition of every ethnic monopoly on Islam: no race owns this religion, and every race is invited to its front rank.

**The Followers and the Ladder of Generations**

Many commentators, including al-Baghawī and al-Rāzī, see in this verse a specific reference to the generation immediately after the Companions — the *Tābiʿūn*, the Followers — whose piety and scholarship carried Islam through its first expansion. On this reading, verse 2 establishes the preeminence of the Companions and verse 3 establishes the merit of those who follow them in excellence — a merit the Qur'an elsewhere confirms:

> *"And the foremost, the first among the Emigrants and the Helpers, and those who followed them in excellence — God is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him."* (Qur'an 9:100)

And the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The best of my community are my generation, then those who follow them, then those who follow them."* (al-Bukhārī 3651; Muslim 2533, from ʿImrān ibn Ḥuṣayn)

The two interpretations — all future Muslims, and specifically the Followers — do not conflict; the nearer meaning (the Followers) is contained within the wider (all who ever join). The logic of the verse establishes a ladder: excellence is measured by nearness to the source, yet the door of joining never closes. Every generation is invited to "catch up with the caravan" — and the caravan, by God's promise, cannot be stopped.

**Sealed with Might and Wisdom**

The verse closes: *wa huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ḥakīm* — "And He is the Mighty, the Wise," echoing two of the four Names from verse 1. The placement is precise. That an unlettered Arab Messenger's teaching should reach Persians and Romans and peoples not yet born — that the caravan should keep growing until the Last Day — requires both Attributes: *might*, so that no empire, no persecution, no forgetting can block it; and *wisdom*, so that each people receives the message in the manner suited to it. Al-Rāzī notes that whenever the Qur'an mentions the spread of Islam to distant peoples, it seals the promise with these Names, as if to answer the doubter's question — "How can this be?" — with: by Might that none resists and Wisdom that never errs. Fourteen centuries of continuous expansion, from one city in the Hijaz to every country on earth, stand as the empirical commentary on this seal.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:4

> **That is the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will. And God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bounty, Not Wages**

*Dhālika faḍlu Allāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ* — "That is the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will." The demonstrative *dhālika*, "that," points back to everything verses 2–3 described: the sending of the Messenger, the recitation, the purification, the teaching, and the inclusion of all future peoples in the mission. All of it — every letter of it — is *faḍl*, bounty: a gift beyond desert, grace beyond wages. The word *faḍl* (from *f-ḍ-l*, surplus, excess) denotes what is given above and beyond what is due. Guidance is not a salary the Arabs earned by their eloquence, nor the Persians by their civilization, nor any believer by his intelligence or ancestry. It is sheer gift. The same sentence appears verbatim elsewhere, confirming its status as a Qur'anic refrain:

> *"That is the Bounty of God, which He gives to whomsoever He will. And God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty."* (Qur'an 57:21; cf. 5:54)

Al-Rāzī notes that in this context the Bounty may specifically denote the Prophet ﷺ himself, or prophethood in general — the greatest gift God bestows upon mankind, since all other gifts perish while guidance saves forever. The implication rebukes two opposite diseases: the despair of the sinner who thinks himself too far gone (bounty is given to *whomsoever He wills* — and He wills to give to the repentant), and the arrogance of the guided who thinks himself entitled (it was given, not earned — and gifts can be withdrawn from the ungrateful).

**"To Whomsoever He Will": The Question of Divine Choice**

The phrase *man yashāʾ*, "whomsoever He will," has occupied theologians for a thousand years, and the verse requires honest treatment. Why is one man guided and another left? The Qur'an's answer is two-sided, and both sides must be held together. On one side, God's will is absolute and unconstrained: He owes guidance to none, and His distribution of gifts — wealth, health, intelligence, and faith itself — is His sovereign prerogative. On the other side, God's will is never arbitrary: He guides those who turn toward guidance and leaves those who turn away. The Qur'an states both principles plainly:

> *"And those who strive for Us — We shall surely guide them to Our paths."* (Qur'an 29:69)

> *"So when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate."* (Qur'an 61:5)

Ibn Taymiyyah resolved the tension with his characteristic precision: God's will (*mashīʾah*) and God's wisdom (*ḥikmah*) are never separated; He wills in accordance with His perfect knowledge of hearts. The rain falls by God's will, but it benefits only the plowed field — and the plowing is man's. *Faḍl* is free, but it is received by the open hand, not the clenched fist. This is why the verse that declares bounty to be God's free gift sits immediately before the verse that condemns those who *refused* to carry their gift: the gift was free, but the carrying was their responsibility.

**"Possessed of Tremendous Bounty"**

*Wa Allāhu dhū al-faḍli al-ʿaẓīm* — "And God is Possessed of Tremendous Bounty." The word *ʿaẓīm*, tremendous, shares its root with *ʿaẓm*, bone — the structural greatness of a thing, its massiveness. God's bounty is not merely generous; it is *structurally* vast, mountainous, oceanic. Consider what it has already encompassed in this sūrah alone: a Messenger to the unlettered, purification from idolatry, a Book and Wisdom, and an open door for every people until the Last Day. And the Prophet ﷺ taught the arithmetic of this bounty:

> *"God has written down the good deeds and the evil deeds, then explained that: whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it down as ten good deeds, up to seven hundredfold, up to many multiples. And whoever intends an evil deed and does not do it, God writes it down with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it down as one evil deed."* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

Ten-to-seven-hundred for good, one-for-one for evil: this is what *tremendous bounty* looks like in accounting. The believer who truly absorbs this verse lives between two liberating truths: nothing he has is his own achievement, so pride is impossible; and nothing he needs is beyond God's giving, so despair is impossible. Gratitude and hope — the verse plants both in a single sentence.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:5

> **The parable of those [who were] made to bear the Torah, then did not bear it, is that of an ass bearing books. How evil is the likeness of the people who denied God's signs! And God guides not wrongdoing people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Entrusted with Gold, Carrying It Like Straw**

The sūrah pivots from gift to warning with one of the most striking parables in the Qur'an: *mathalu alladhīna ḥummilū al-tawrāta thumma lam yaḥmilūhā ka-mathali al-ḥimāri yaḥmilu asfārā* — "The parable of those who were made to bear the Torah, then did not bear it, is that of an ass bearing books." The verb *ḥummilū*, "were made to bear," is in the passive of causation: they were *loaded*, entrusted, charged — as al-Qurṭubī explains, "enjoined to act in accord with it." To *bear* (*ḥamala*) a scripture in the Qur'anic sense is not to memorize its letters or to carry its scrolls in a saddlebag; it is to implement its teachings, to let it judge one's disputes, restrain one's appetites, and shape one's character. These people received the loading but refused the carrying: *thumma lam yaḥmilūhā* — "then did not bear it." They kept the scrolls and abandoned the submission. The tragedy is captured in the tense: the entrusting is past and complete (*ḥummilū*), the failure is ongoing (*lam yaḥmilūhā*).

**Why a Donkey — and Why Books**

The parable's vehicle is deliberately humbling: *al-ḥimār*, the donkey or ass — the beast proverbial in Arab (and biblical) speech for carrying burdens without comprehending them — *yaḥmilu asfārā*, "bearing books." The word *asfār* (plural of *sifr*) denotes large books, tomes, volumes of writing; some linguists connect it to *safar*, journey, because books unveil (*yusfiru*) distant knowledge as dawn unveils the road — or because great books were companions of travel. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both stress the point of comparison (*wajh al-shabah*): the donkey feels only the *weight* of the volumes on its back; it has no share in their *meaning*. Whether it carries cookbooks or scriptures, poetry or tax registers, its condition is identical — sweat without wisdom, burden without benefit. So it is with the scholar who memorizes revelation but does not act upon it: the Book is on his back, not in his heart; it tires him on the Day of Judgment without having guided him in the world. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same disease in its general form:

> *"Do you enjoin righteousness upon people while you forget yourselves, though you recite the Book? Will you not reason?"* (Qur'an 2:44)

And the Prophet ﷺ warned of the learned tongue joined to the heedless heart:

> *"The most severely punished of people on the Day of Resurrection will be the scholar whom God did not benefit by his knowledge."* (al-Ṭabarānī, al-Bayhaqī — narrated from Abū Hurayrah; its meaning is corroborated by numerous authentic texts on acting upon knowledge)

The parable thus draws a line that cuts through every religious community: between *carrying* revelation as a burden of pride or profession, and *being carried by* revelation as a passenger is carried by his mount toward salvation.

**A Charge Older Than Islam**

The source commentary rightly observes that this charge — reciting scripture without embodying it — is the same indictment the Hebrew prophets brought against the priests of their own age. Isaiah declares: *"This people draw near with their mouth and honor Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me"* (Isaiah 29:13); Jeremiah laments that *"the lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie"* and that the wise are put to shame (Jeremiah 8:8–9); Ezekiel charges the priests with failing to distinguish between the holy and the common (Ezekiel 22:26); Hosea warns that *"my people are destroyed for lack of knowledge"* (Hosea 4:6). The Qur'an's parable therefore stands in conscious continuity with the biblical prophetic tradition: Muhammad ﷺ is saying to the possessors of the Torah what Isaiah and Jeremiah said before him. This continuity is itself part of the argument — a true prophet confirms his predecessors, and the confirmation here takes the form of repeating their rebuke. For the Muslim reader, the sobering corollary follows: if the disease could afflict those who bore the Torah, it can afflict those who bear the Qur'an, for the human heart has not changed its susceptibilities.

**The Warning Turns Toward the Muslims**

Most pointedly, the classical commentators insist this verse is not merely about Jews; it is a mirror held up to Muslims. The Prophet ﷺ himself turned it around. It is reported that he once warned his community of coming calamities that would befall when religious understanding was lost and falsified, and when a Companion asked how that could happen while Muslims would continue teaching the Qur'an generation after generation, he replied:

> *"May your mother weep for you! Do you not see these Jews and these Christians? They read the Torah and the Gospel but do not act in accord with them."* (Reported by al-Dārimī and others; cf. Aḥmad)

And in the famous eschatological warning:

> *"There will soon come upon the people a time in which nothing of the Qur'an remains save its trace (*rasmuhu*), and nothing of Islam remains save its name. Their mosques will be full, though they will be devoid of guidance. Their scholars will be the worst people under the sky; from them strife will emerge, and to them it will return."* (al-Bayhaqī, Shuʿab al-Īmān; ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib — widely cited by the commentators)

*Nothing of the Qur'an save its trace*: the letters remain on the page and the recitation upon the tongue — beautiful printing, melodious chanting, competitions and certificates — while judgment by it, weeping over it, and transformation through it have vanished. The donkey's load has been transferred. Every Muslim community must therefore ask itself the verse's question with trembling: are we *bearing* the Book, or merely *transporting* it? The signs of true bearing are concrete: the Qur'an's prohibitions restrain our business, its commands order our households, its promises calm our fears. Where these are absent, the parable applies — whatever our claims.

**"How Evil Is the Likeness"**

*Biʾsa mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyāti Allāh* — "How evil is the likeness of the people who denied God's signs!" The exclamation *biʾsa* is the language of disgust and censure — "how vile, how wretched." Note the escalation: the verse began with failure to *act* (*lam yaḥmilūhā*) and ends with *denial* (*kadhdhabū*). The commentators explain the inward connection: persistent refusal to act upon acknowledged truth hardens, by slow degrees, into denial of the truth itself. A man first neglects the command while admitting it; then he resents the command because it condemns him; then he reinterprets the command until it condemns no one; finally he denies the commander. Neglect is the seed of denial — which is why the verse closes: *wa Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn* — "And God guides not wrongdoing people." *Ẓulm*, wrongdoing, is literally putting a thing in other than its place — and what greater misplacement than putting the Book on the back instead of in the heart? Guidance is bounty (verse 4), but bounty is withdrawn from those who wrong it. The chain of the sūrah is now complete: bounty given (verses 2–4), bounty wronged (verse 5) — and the wrong, unrepented, becomes the seal.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:6

> **Say, "O you who are Jews! If you claim that you are friends unto God apart from [other] people, then long for death, if you are truthful."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Claim Put to the Test**

*Qul yā ayyuhā alladhīna hādū in zaʿamtum annakum awliyāʾu li-llāhi min dūni al-nās fa-tamannaw al-mawt in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "Say: O you who are Jews! If you claim that you are friends unto God apart from other people, then long for death, if you are truthful." The address *alladhīna hādū*, "those who are Jews" (literally "those who turned," from *h-w-d*, returning — either returning to God or, as some say, the name taken from Judah, *Yahūdhā*), is answered on its own terms. The claim under examination is *wilāyah*: that they are *awliyāʾ*, intimate friends and beloved protégés of God, *min dūni al-nās*, "apart from other people" — exclusively, to the exclusion of the unlettered Arabs and their new Prophet. The Qur'an records this claim elsewhere in their own reported words:

> *"And the Jews and the Christians say, 'We are the children of God and His beloved.' Say: 'Why then does He punish you for your sins? Nay, you are mortals among those He created.'"* (Qur'an 5:18)

The logic of the challenge is airtight and universally applicable: *if* you are truly God's exclusive friends, *then* the meeting with Him should be your dearest desire — for the friend longs for the Friend, and death is the door of the meeting. The conditional (*in... fa...*) is a standing test of every claim to divine favoritism in every age: show me your longing for the meeting, and I will show you the sincerity of your love.

**Why Longing for Death Proves Love**

The reasoning rests on a psychological truth no one can evade: men love what benefits them and fear what harms them. Whoever is certain that Paradise awaits him — that his Friend holds gifts, honors, and eternal nearness behind the veil of death — must logically welcome death's arrival, as a traveler welcomes the road that ends at home. Conversely, whoever dreads death betrays, in that dread, his uncertainty about what follows. This is why the Qur'an issues the same challenge in Sūrah al-Baqarah with added precision:

> *"Say: 'If the Abode of the Hereafter with God is purely yours, apart from other people, then long for death, if you are truthful.' But they will never long for it, ever, due to that which their hands have sent forth. And God knows the wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 2:94–95)

And the Prophet ﷺ stated the underlying principle as a general law of the heart:

> *"Whosoever loves to meet God, God loves to meet him; and whosoever abhors meeting God, God abhors to meet him."* (al-Bukhārī 6507; Muslim 2683, from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit — and from Abū Hurayrah, ʿĀʾishah, and others)

When ʿĀʾishah asked whether this meant the natural dislike of death — "for all of us dislike death" — the Prophet ﷺ explained that the reference is to the hour of clarity: the believer, given glad tidings at death of God's mercy and pleasure, loves the meeting; the disbeliever, given tidings of punishment, abhors it (al-Bukhārī 6507). The verse's challenge must be read with this subtlety: it does not condemn the natural human reluctance to die — even the prophets felt death's weight — but the *absence of all longing* for the meeting in those who profess exclusive friendship. The true friend may tremble at the journey yet yearn for the arrival; the false claimant neither trembles rightly nor yearns at all.

**The Challenge in History**

Ibn ʿAbbās and other early authorities report that the Prophet ﷺ declared: had the Jews longed for death that day, they would have died — every one of them — and the challenge was thus also a standing miracle (*taḥaddī*), like the challenge to produce a sūrah like the Qur'an: issued publicly, never taken up. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr record similar reports concerning 2:94 — that had they uttered the longing, they would have choked on it and perished on the spot. Whether or not one presses the miraculous reading, the historical fact is eloquent: no Jewish community, then or since, has responded to this verse by professing longing for death. The silence of fourteen centuries is the verse's corroboration. And the challenge generalizes with surgical fairness: it applies equally to any Muslim who claims God's exclusive favor for his sect, his school, his nation, or his bloodline while his heart clings to the world with both hands. The Qur'an's tests are never tribal; they are examinations of the heart, and every heart may sit them.

**Longing for Death — The Lawful and the Unlawful**

A necessary clarification: Islam forbids *wishing* for death out of despair or worldly distress. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Let none of you wish for death on account of a harm that has befallen him. But if he must do so, let him say: 'O God, keep me alive so long as life is better for me, and take me to Thyself when death is better for me.'"* (al-Bukhārī 5671; Muslim 2680, from Anas)

What the verse demands is not suicidal despair but *shawq*, loving yearning for the meeting — the state of the great saints who, having prepared their provision, awaited the Beloved's summons as a bride awaits her wedding. The distinction is between fleeing *from* life (despair, forbidden) and longing *for* God (love, commanded in its season). The believer's healthy station combines both: he does not hasten death, for every additional day is a field for good deeds — *"The best of you are those who live longest and do best"* (al-Tirmidhī 2330) — yet he keeps his heart packed for the journey, his debts settled, his repentance current, and his longing alive. The Jews' failure in this verse was not that they cherished life — life is God's gift — but that their alleged certainty of salvation produced *no* longing whatsoever, exposing the claim as counterfeit.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:7

> **But they will never long for it due to that which their hands have sent forth. And God knows the wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prophecy Disguised as a Sentence**

*Wa lā yatamannawnahu abadan bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim* — "But they will never long for it, ever, due to that which their hands have sent forth." The *lan* of absolute negation (*lā yatamannawnahu abadan* — "they will never, ever long for it") transforms the verse from description into prophecy. God declares, concerning an entire community across all future time, that they will not take up the challenge. This is the same structure as the challenge of Sūrah al-Baqarah (2:24) — *"But if you do not — and you will never"* — produce a sūrah like it: a prediction about human behavior issued with divine certainty, falsifiable by a single counterexample, never falsified. Any Jew in Madinah could have stood up and said, "I long for death," and the verse would have collapsed. None did. The early commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr) treat this as among the Qur'an's evidences: only the Knower of hearts could gamble a revelation on the future silence of thousands of hostile witnesses — and win, century after century.

**"That Which Their Hands Have Sent Forth"**

The verse diagnoses *why* they will not long for it: *bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim* — "due to that which their hands have sent forth." The deed is figured as a traveler dispatched ahead: a man's actions go before him to the Hereafter like advance baggage, and he knows — however much he denies it publicly — what he has packed. The early commentators specify the contents of these particular bags: the denial of Muhammad's ﷺ prophethood despite recognizing him (as the Qur'an says, *"those to whom We gave the Book know him as they know their own sons"* — 2:146), and the distortion (*taḥrīf*) of the scriptures — concealing passages, altering rulings, selling verses for worldly price (see 4:47; 5:13, 41). The phrase *"that which their hands have sent forth"* recurs throughout the Qur'an as the standard explanation of Hereafter-dread:

> *"But they will never long for it, ever, due to that which their hands have sent forth. And God is Knowing of the wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 2:95)

> *"And if evil befalls them for that which their hands have sent forth — then truly man is ungrateful."* (Qur'an 42:48)

The psychology is universal and verifiable in every conscience: guilt dreads audit. The employee who has embezzled fears the auditor; the student who has not studied fears the examiner; the soul that has "sent forth" treachery fears the arrival. Their refusal to long for death is therefore an involuntary confession — the conscience testifying against the tongue. They *claim* exclusive friendship (verse 6) but *feel* the truth (verse 7): the heart is the witness that cannot be bribed.

**The Conscience as God's Courtroom Witness**

Here the verse opens one of the Qur'an's deepest psychological teachings: that man carries within himself a pre-judgment of the Judgment. The Qur'an calls it the self-reproaching soul:

> *"And I swear by the self-reproaching soul."* (Qur'an 75:2)

And declares that on the Last Day no external prosecutor will even be necessary:

> *"Read thy book! Thou suffices this day as reckoner against thyself."* (Qur'an 17:14)

Al-Ghazālī develops this theme at length: the punishments of the Hereafter are not arbitrary inflictions but the unveiling of what the soul already carries — the miser meets his miserliness as a strangling chain, the arrogant meets his arrogance as crushing weight. The Jews' dread of death in our verse is a down payment of that unveiling: their souls already know the verdict, and the knowledge leaks out as fear. Modern psychology, with its language of repression and anxiety, circles the same fire from afar: the guilt that is buried alive never dies; it resurfaces as dread, insomnia, and the frantic noise with which men drown out silence. The Qur'anic remedy is not to silence the conscience but to *satisfy* it — through repentance (*tawbah*), restitution, and reform — so that the advance baggage is repacked before arrival. The Prophet ﷺ gave the daily instrument:

> *"By God, I seek God's forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance more than seventy times a day."* (al-Bukhārī 6307, from Abū Hurayrah)

If the sinless Prophet ﷺ repacked his baggage seventy times daily, what excuse has the sinner for traveling with his unexamined?

**"And God Knows the Wrongdoers"**

*Wa Allāhu ʿalīmun bi-al-ẓālimīn* — "And God knows the wrongdoers." The seal is quiet and terrible. *ʿAlīm* — from *ʿ-l-m*, knowledge — denotes perfect, encompassing, intimate knowledge; nothing in the wrongdoer's heart or history escapes it. The wrong (*ẓulm*) here is the comprehensive wrong: wronging revelation by distorting it, wronging the Prophet ﷺ by denying him, wronging their own souls by forfeiting salvation, and wronging the truth by claiming a friendship they do not possess. The sentence functions on three levels at once: as *threat* (your concealment is known), as *consolation* to the believers (your opponents' reality is known — you need not despair at their outward confidence), and as *theodicy* (none will be wronged in the final judgment, for the Judge knows). Ibn Taymiyyah often observed that the Qur'an's closing divine Names are the verse's own commentary: here, *ʿAlīm* answers the unspoken question — "But they hide it so well; who can prove what is in their hearts?" — with: God can. The courtroom already has its omniscient Witness; the trial is a formality of justice, not an investigation of facts.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:8

> **Say, "Truly the death from which you flee will surely meet you; then you shall be returned to the Knower of the Unseen and the seen, and He will inform you of that which you used to do."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fugitive and the Appointment**

*Qul inna al-mawta alladhī tafirrūna minhu fa-innahū mulāqīkum* — "Say: Truly the death from which you flee will surely meet you." The imagery is unforgettable: death is not a pursuer chasing from behind, whom a fast runner might outdistance; it is an appointment waiting ahead, *mulāqīkum* — "meeting you," coming face to face. The form *mulāqī* (from *l-q-y*, meeting) is mutual: as you walk toward your future, your future walks toward you, and the meeting is certain. Fleeing is therefore geometrically absurd — every road leads to the rendezvous. The Qur'an states the same certainty elsewhere with fortress imagery:

> *"Wheresoever you may be, death will overtake you, though you be in lofty towers."* (Qur'an 4:78)

And the Prophet ﷺ illustrated life's brevity with two fingers held together and a parable of the traveler resting briefly under a tree:

> *"What have I to do with this world? I am in this world but as a rider who rests in the shade of a tree, then departs and leaves it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2377; Ibn Mājah 4109, from Ibn ʿAbbās — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The verse's *fa-innahū* — "then truly it" — carries the emphasis of oath: *certainly*, without exception, without delay when the term arrives. Al-Qurṭubī notes the sweet irony of the grammar: the more they flee (*tafirrūna*, present continuous — they keep fleeing), the more certain the meeting; flight itself is motion toward the appointment. The modern world, with its billion-dollar industries of youth-preservation and death-denial — the euphemisms ("passed away," "lost"), the cosmetic concealments, the frantic distractions — is the most elaborate fleeing civilization in history, and this verse stands over it like a quiet judge: *it will meet you*.

**Four Dispositions Toward Death**

Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī (d. 505/1111), commenting on this verse in his *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* (Book of the Remembrance of Death), distinguished four human dispositions toward death, and his taxonomy has been treasured by preachers ever since. *First*, those engrossed in the world, for whom the remembrance of death only increases bitterness and distance from God — of them the Qur'an says:

> *"For mankind, their reckoning draws nigh, while they turn away heedless."* (Qur'an 21:1)

Their end is unfortunate, for *"whosoever abhors meeting God, God abhors meeting him"* (al-Bukhārī 6507). *Second*, the penitent (*al-tāʾib*): he does not abhor death itself, but fears the meeting because he knows his deficiencies — so he busies himself with preparation, and his fear is healthy, like the student's nervousness before an exam he has studied for. *Third*, the gnostics (*al-ʿārifūn*): they remember death constantly and yearn for it as a lover yearns for union with the Beloved — this is the lawful *shawq* discussed under verse 6. *Fourth and highest*, those who yearn for neither death nor life in themselves, but only for whatever pleases their Lord — their will dissolved in His, they say with complete sincerity: if He keeps me, I serve; if He takes me, I come. The verse's fleeing Jews stand at the farthest remove from all four stations: they neither prepare like the penitent, nor yearn like the lover, nor submit like the saint — they only flee. Every reader must locate himself in al-Ghazālī's fourfold map, for one of the four is his address.

**Returned to the Knower of the Unseen and the Seen**

*Thumma turaddūna ilā ʿālimi al-ghaybi wa al-shahādah* — "then you shall be returned to the Knower of the Unseen and the seen." The verb *turaddūna*, "you shall be returned," teaches that death is not a departure into nothingness but a *return* — the soul goes back to its Origin, the trust goes back to its Owner, the servant goes back to his Master. And the One to whom all return is *ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa al-shahādah* — Knower of the hidden and the witnessed alike: the secret intention and the public act, the whispered conspiracy and the open deed, the atom's weight of good and the atom's weight of evil. Al-Ṭabarī stresses that in this context the Name carries a precise threat: the intentions behind actions — the motives no historian can recover and no court can prove — cannot be hidden from Him. The Qur'an elaborates the terror and the comfort of this total knowledge:

> *"And He is God in the heavens and on the earth; He knows your secret and your open, and He knows what you earn."* (Qur'an 6:3)

For the wrongdoer, this is the end of every hiding place; for the sincere sufferer whose good was unseen by all, it is the guarantee that nothing was ever truly unseen. Every unthanked kindness, every secret charity, every tear shed alone in night prayer — all were *shahādah*, witnessed, to Him.

**"And He Will Inform You"**

*Fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "and He will inform you of that which you used to do." The verb *yunabbiʾu*, from *n-b-ʾ*, denotes informing of weighty news — *nabaʾ* is momentous tidings, not small talk. At the Final Judgment, God will "inform" each soul of the true nature of its deeds: what they really were beneath their excuses, what they really caused across their ripples, what they really deserve in recompense. This divine "informing" is mentioned in numerous verses (5:14, 105; 6:60, 108, 159; 9:94, 105; 10:23; 24:64; 29:8; 31:15, 23; 39:7; 41:50; 58:6–7; 64:7; 75:13) — among them:

> *"On a Day when God will resurrect them all together, and inform them of that which they did. God has reckoned it, though they forgot it. And God is Witness over all things."* (Qur'an 58:6)

*"God has reckoned it, though they forgot it"* — the ledgers of heaven forget nothing that the ledgers of memory dropped. Al-Ṭabarī explains that God will then requite the good with good and the evil with what it merits — the informing is itself the beginning of the recompense, for to *see* one's life truly at last is, for the wicked, a punishment before the punishment, and for the righteous, a joy before the joy. The practical conclusion is the one ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb drew for a lifetime:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed."* (Attributed to ʿUmar; Tirmidhī, and widely cited in the commentaries)

The believer who audits himself nightly — what did I send forth today? — will meet the divine informing as a confirmation, not an ambush.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:9

> **O you who believe! When you are called to the congregational prayer, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trade. That is better for you, if you but knew.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call That Outranks the Market**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nūdiya li-l-ṣalāti min yawmi al-jumuʿati fa-sʿaw ilā dhikri Allāhi wa dharū al-bayʿ* — "O you who believe! When you are called to prayer on the day of congregation, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trade." The address *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — "O you who believe" — is the Qur'an's summons of honor, used only in Madinah, only for those within the covenant, and always before something weighty. Friday in Madinah was market day: caravans arrived, stalls filled, and the bustle of buying and selling reached its weekly peak — precisely when the call to prayer sounded. God's command therefore stages a direct contest between two summons: the market's call (*the profit is now, the caravan leaves today*) and the minaret's call (*the prayer is now, God summons today*). The verse settles the contest by royal decree: when the two calls collide, the King's call prevails. This is not a Sabbath of rest like the Jewish Saturday or the Christian Sunday — Islam prescribes no full day of idleness; the suspension covers only the period of the congregational prayer itself. The wisdom is characteristic: Islam consecrates *time within* the working week rather than abolishing the week, teaching that devotion and livelihood are not enemies but neighbors with appointed boundaries.

**"Hasten" — With the Heart Before the Feet**

*Fa-sʿaw ilā dhikri Allāh* — "so hasten to the remembrance of God." The verb *isʿaw* (from *s-ʿ-y*, striving, endeavoring, walking swiftly with purpose) has been lovingly examined by the commentators. It does not mean *run* — the Prophet ﷺ explicitly forbade rushing to prayer in a breathless, undignified scramble:

> *"When you come to prayer, do not come hastening in disarray; come to it walking, and with calmness (*wa ʿalaykum al-sakīnah*). Whatever you catch, pray; and whatever you miss, complete."* (al-Bukhārī 636; Muslim 602, from Abū Hurayrah)

Al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Rāzī therefore explain *saʿy* here as earnest endeavor: to go with intention, preparation, and longing — outwardly by performing ablution, wearing one's best garments, and arriving early; inwardly by emptying the heart of market-arithmetic and filling it with humility. Some of the early readers (Ibn Masʿūd among them) even read *fa-mḍū*, "so go" — confirming that the essence is purposeful going, not sprinting. *Dhikr Allāh*, "the remembrance of God," is understood by most commentators as the Friday prayer itself together with its sermon (*khuṭbah*), for the sermon is pure remembrance — praise, exhortation, Qur'an, and supplication. The command thus means: when the call sounds, redirect the entire apparatus of striving — feet, hands, heart — from the market's remembrance (prices, profits, rivals) to God's remembrance. The man who stands in the Friday rank while his mind remains at his stall has obeyed with his feet and disobeyed with his heart; the verse demands both travelers.

**"Leave Off Trade" — The Jurisprudence of the Hour**

*Wa dharū al-bayʿ* — "and leave off trade." The command is absolute in its hour: buying, selling, bargaining, contracting — all commercial activity is suspended from the call until the prayer's end. The jurists built a small edifice upon these three words. *First*, the prohibition binds those upon whom Friday is obligatory: free, adult, sane, resident Muslim men; travelers, women, children, and the sick are excused from the obligation (though welcome at the prayer). *Second*, most schools hold that a sale contracted during the hour is *sinful* but legally valid — the sin attaches to the timing, not the contract — while some (notably the Mālikīs in one narration) void it, to seal the breach entirely. *Third*, the prohibition extends by analogy to every worldly occupation that distracts from hastening — the factory line, the classroom, the bureaucrat's desk — for trade is named only as the chief example, the likeliest rival. The underlying principle, as al-Rāzī formulates it, connects this verse to the previous passage: those who flee death do so from love of the world; God therefore trains the believers in the opposite motion — to *flee the world* for a measured hour, proving weekly that their hearts are not owned by their incomes. It is a commerce that will never perish set against a commerce that perishes daily:

> *"Truly those who recite the Book of God, establish prayer, and spend from that which We provided them, secretly and openly, hope for a commerce that will never perish."* (Qur'an 35:29)

**How Friday Prayer Began**

Al-Qurṭubī preserves the precious history of the Friday prayer's institution. Before the Prophet's ﷺ arrival in Madinah, two of the earliest Madinan converts — Asʿad ibn Zurārah (d. 1/623) and Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr (d. 3/625), the Prophet's ﷺ envoy to Madinah — gathered the Muslims for a day of prayer and remembrance, that they might have a weekly congregation like the Jews' Saturday and the Christians' Sunday. The Helpers (*Anṣār*) thus began the Friday gathering before the Hijrah; the Prophet ﷺ institutionalized it upon arrival; and these verses then gave it divine sanction. The Prophet ﷺ led the first Friday prayer himself during his migration: after twelve days in Qubāʾ, just south of Madinah, he set out on a Friday, and when the hour came he prayed with the people in a seasonal riverbed (*wādī*) they used as a mosque — and there delivered the first congregational sermon (*khuṭbah*). From a dry riverbed to two billion worshippers: such is the arithmetic of blessed beginnings. The sermon itself became, in the Prophet's ﷺ practice, a weekly university — as Jābir ibn Samurah reported:

> *"The Prophet's ﷺ prayer was moderate, and his sermon was moderate: he would recite verses of the Qur'an and remind the people."* (Muslim 866)

And he warned, balancing mercy with majesty, against abandoning the gathering:

> *"Let people cease neglecting the Friday prayers, or God will surely seal their hearts, and they will surely be among the heedless."* (Muslim 865, from Ibn ʿUmar and Abū Hurayrah)

> *"Whoever abandons three Fridays out of neglect, God seals his heart."* (Abū Dāwūd 1052; al-Tirmidhī 500; al-Nasāʾī 1369 — from Abū al-Jaʿd al-Ḍamrī; graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim and others)

Three missed Fridays — three ignored royal summons — and the heart is sealed: the jurisprudence of the verse and the warnings of the Sunnah agree that this hour is not decoration but foundation.

**"That Is Better for You, If You But Knew"**

*Dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūn* — "That is better for you, if you but knew." The verse ends not with a threat but with an appeal to intelligence: leaving a profitable hour for prayer *is better for you* — better in this world (for the disciplined soul, the united community, and the blessed provision are worth more than one hour's margin) and better in the next (where one accepted Friday prayer outweighs a lifetime of ledgers). The conditional *"if you but knew"* (*in kuntum taʿlamūn*) is gentle irony: you pride yourselves on market-knowledge — on knowing prices, trends, and opportunities — yet you are ignorant of the one exchange rate that matters, the rate between an hour and eternity. True knowledge (*ʿilm*) in the Qur'an is not information but valuation: knowing what a thing is *worth*. The believer who understands this verse becomes the shrewdest of traders — he sells sixty minutes and buys Paradise's pleasure, a margin no market on earth can match.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:10

> **And when the prayer is completed, disperse throughout the land and seek the Bounty of God, and remember God much, that haply you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Permission, Not Exile**

*Fa-idhā quḍiyati al-ṣalātu fa-ntashirū fī al-arḍi wa-btaghū min faḍli Allāh* — "And when the prayer is completed, disperse throughout the land and seek the Bounty of God." Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī all emphasize that *fa-ntashirū*, "so disperse," is not a *command* obliging departure — one may remain in the mosque in remembrance if one wishes — but a *permission* lifting the suspension: the sanctuary-hour is over, the markets reopen, the roads are lawful again. The elegance of the sequence is the whole Islamic view of work in miniature: *gather, then scatter; ascend, then descend; pray, then earn*. Worship is not escape from the world but preparation for it — the believer charges his heart in congregation, then carries the charge into the marketplace. Monasticism says: abandon the world to find God. Materialism says: abandon God to master the world. This verse says: find God first, *then* master the world in His Name. The dispersal (*intishār* — from *n-sh-r*, spreading, unfolding, like a scroll opened or bones resurrected) even hints at resurrection: the congregation scatters from the mosque as mankind will scatter from the graves — each carrying what he earned in the gathering.

**"Seek the Bounty of God" — Commerce Consecrated**

*Wa-btaghū min faḍli Allāh* — "and seek the Bounty of God." The commentators offer two readings that unite beautifully. Most (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī) understand *faḍl Allāh* here as lawful provision (*rizq*) through commerce and labor: work itself, resumed after prayer, is reframed as *seeking God's bounty* — not "making money" but receiving gift. The wage is thereby consecrated: the same market that was forbidden for an hour becomes an act of devotion when re-entered in God's Name, with honest scales and a grateful heart. Others read *faḍl* as knowledge, or as every good of this world and the next. And Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have given the verse its most tender reading: that the dispersal is not (only) for worldly seeking, but for visiting the sick, attending funerals (often held after Friday prayer), and visiting one's brethren for God's sake (al-Qurṭubī, al-Suyūṭī, al-Ṭabarī). All three readings together draw the complete Friday afternoon: earn honestly, learn humbly, and love actively — the market, the study circle, and the sickbed, each a field of God's bounty for whoever enters it remembering Him.

**"Remember God Much" — The Verse's True Command**

*Wa-dhkurū Allāha kathīrā* — "and remember God much." Here is the verse's only true imperative of devotion, and the commentators (notably al-Baghawī) stress its timing: remembrance is commanded precisely *in dispersal* — in the market, on the road, at the desk — because dispersal is where remembrance dies. In the mosque, surrounded by worshippers, remembrance is easy; in the bazaar, surrounded by bargaining, it is the trial of trials. *Kathīrā*, "much" — abundantly, frequently, in all states — sets no ceiling: the Qur'an commands *much* remembrance here and elsewhere:

> *"O you who believe! Remember God with much remembrance. And glorify Him morning and evening."* (Qur'an 33:41–42)

> *"And remember thy Lord much, and glorify Him at eventide and at dawn."* (Qur'an 3:41)

The Prophet ﷺ explained what "much" means in practice — that the tongue should never dry:

> *"Let your tongue remain moist with the remembrance of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3375; Ibn Mājah 3793, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Busr — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

And he taught the market's own liturgy — a remembrance so weighty that one utterance in the marketplace outweighs years of heedlessness:

> *"Whoever enters the marketplace and says: 'There is no god but God alone, without partner; His is the sovereignty and His is the praise; He gives life and causes death, and He is Living and dies not; in His Hand is all good, and He is Powerful over all things' — God writes for him a million good deeds, effaces from him a million evil deeds, and raises him a million ranks."* (al-Tirmidhī 3428; Ibn Mājah 2235, from ʿUmar — graded ḥasan by several authorities)

A million for one sentence: because remembrance uttered *where God is forgotten* is the dearest remembrance of all. The practical program of verse 10 is therefore exact: pray in congregation (verse 9), work honestly and visit mercifully (verse 10a), and through all of it keep the heart's thread tied to God (verse 10b). The mosque-hour and the market-hours are not two religions but one — the religion of the remembered God.

**"That Haply You May Prosper"**

*Laʿallakum tufliḥūn* — "that haply you may prosper." *Falāḥ* (from *f-l-ḥ*, splitting, cleaving — as the farmer *fallāḥ* splits the earth to plant, and as dawn splits the night) denotes true prosperity: success that splits open and endures — the harvest after the plowing, the daylight after the dark. The Qur'an uses it as the summary-word of salvation (*"the believers have prospered"* — 23:1; *"and whoever is saved from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has prospered"* — paraphrased in 3:185). Note the causal chain the verse constructs: prayer completed → bounty sought → God much remembered → prosperity hoped. Remove the middle term — work without remembrance — and prosperity collapses into mere accumulation, the rich poverty of the distracted heart. Al-Ghazālī observed that the marketplace is the battlefield of remembrance: whoever remembers God there has passed the examination that the mosque-goer has only prepared for. Friday thus becomes a weekly rehearsal of the whole religious life: gather to remember, scatter to earn, remember while earning — and the harvest, God willing, is *falāḥ* in both abodes.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:11

> **And when they see some commerce or diversion, they scatter toward it and leave thee standing. Say, "That which is with God is better than diversion and commerce; and God is the best of providers."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Caravan, a Drum, and an Emptying Mosque**

*Wa idhā raʾaw tijāratan aw lahwan infaḍḍū ilayhā wa tarakūka qāʾimā* — "And when they see some commerce or diversion, they scatter toward it and leave thee standing." The verse descends upon a remembered embarrassment, preserved in the two Ṣaḥīḥs from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh: the Prophet ﷺ was delivering the Friday sermon when a caravan arrived from Syria — laden with food and goods in a season of scarcity — and, at the sound of its announcing drum, the congregation poured out of the mosque toward it until only twelve men remained (al-Bukhārī 936; Muslim 863). Picture the scene: the Messenger of God ﷺ standing upon the pulpit, mid-sermon, the words of revelation upon his lips — and the rustle of departing sandals, the emptying rows, the twelve steadfast faces in a hollowed mosque. *Infaḍḍū*, "they scatter" (from *f-ḍ-ḍ*, breaking apart, dissolving — as a seal breaks or a crowd dissolves), captures the disintegration: a congregation — a *jumuʿah*, a gathering — un-gathering itself before the prayer is done. *Wa tarakūka qāʾimā*, "and leave thee standing" — standing in the sermon, standing in the prayer, standing alone in dignity while the world runs past. The pathos of the phrase has moved fourteen centuries of readers: the Prophet ﷺ, abandoned mid-address, yet never abandoning the address. Al-Qurṭubī adds the legislative sequel: before this incident the Prophet ﷺ would pray first and preach after; after it, he preached first and prayed after — so that none could claim his prayer discharged while skipping the sermon. The practice of sermon-before-prayer, observed by Muslims to this day, was born from twelve men's steadfastness and a caravan's temptation.

**Commerce *or* Diversion — The Two Great Distractions**

*Tijāratan aw lahwan* — "commerce or diversion." The pairing is precise and permanent. *Tijārah* (trade, commerce — from *t-j-r*, turning over, circulating goods) is the *serious* distraction: livelihood, ambition, the plausible necessity — "I must earn." *Lahw* (diversion, play, amusement — from *l-h-w*, that which diverts and occupies heedlessly) is the *frivolous* distraction: entertainment, spectacle, the drum and the crowd — "I must see." Every rival of worship in every age is one of these two or their union: the stock ticker and the sports screen, the overtime shift and the streaming series, the "urgent" deal and the "unmissable" show. The Qur'an names them together elsewhere as the definition of the worldly life itself:

> *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion (*laʿibun wa lahw*), adornment, mutual boasting among you, and rivalry in wealth and children."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The order in our verse — commerce *before* diversion — reflects the occasion (a trade caravan), but the sūrah's closing reverses it (*"better than diversion and commerce"*) — as if to say: whether your idol is profit or pleasure, necessity-claim or pastime, what is with God outranks it. The diagnostic question for every believer is therefore double: what is *my* caravan — the income I fear to lose by praying? And what is *my* drum — the amusement that empties my rows? Honest answers to these two questions map the exact state of the heart.

**"That Which Is with God Is Better"**

*Qul mā ʿinda Allāhi khayrun mina al-lahwi wa mina al-tijārah* — "Say: That which is with God is better than diversion and commerce." The comparison is between two treasuries: *mā ʿinda Allāh* — "what is with God," the reward of the congregational prayer, the blessing upon honestly paused provision, the Paradise prepared for the obedient — and the twin treasuries of earth, profit and pleasure. *Khayr*, "better," here means better in every dimension the two rivals claim: more lasting (they perish, it abides), more certain (they may fail, it is guaranteed), more delightful (their joy is mixed with anxiety, its joy is pure), and greater in sheer quantity (they are measured in coins and hours, it in eternities). The Qur'an makes the general principle explicit:

> *"What is with you perishes, and what is with God abides."* (Qur'an 16:96)

> *"And what is with God is better and more lasting for those who believe and trust in their Lord."* (Qur'an 42:36)

Al-Qurṭubī notes that *"what is with God"* includes the provision God grants in *this* world to those who honor His hour — the mysteriously blessed livelihood of the prayer-guarded merchant, whose wealth suffices though his hours are shorter and whose heart rests though his rivals scramble. The early community tested this promise empirically: the twelve who remained became the seed of a civilization, while the caravan's goods were consumed within weeks. Every Friday since re-poses the wager, and every faithful congregation collects the winnings.

**"God Is the Best of Providers" — The Sūrah's Final Name**

*Wa Allāhu khayru al-rāziqīn* — "and God is the best of providers." The sūrah's final words answer the fear that emptied the mosque: *if I stay for prayer, I will lose my share of the caravan's goods*. The answer: provision (*rizq*) does not come from caravans; it comes from God, and He is the *best* of providers — *khayr*, best in generosity, in timing, in suitability, in blessing. Men provide wages, caravans provide goods, markets provide opportunities — all are secondary channels; the Source is One. The Qur'an elsewhere names Him so in parallel passages (5:114; 22:58; 23:72; 34:39), and the Prophet ﷺ taught the theology of the marketplace:

> *"If you were to trust in God with true trust, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out hungry in the morning and return full in the evening."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; Ibn Mājah 4164, from ʿUmar — graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The birds do not attend caravans; they attend the sky, and the sky's Lord feeds them. Al-Baghawī draws the verse's final inference: whoever hastens to God seeking His provision will find that his provision does not pass him by — what was written for him reaches him in the mosque as surely as in the market, for the market never owned it. The sūrah thus ends where it began: verse 1 proclaimed God the Sovereign (*al-Malik*) — the King whose summons interrupts all trade; verse 11 proclaims Him the Provider (*khayr al-rāziqīn*) — the King who compensates all obedience. Between the two Names lies the whole argument: the cosmos glorifies (1), the Messenger was sent (2–3) by pure bounty (4), the bearers-before failed their trust (5–8) — so *you*: answer the call (9), work in remembrance (10), and never trade the Best of Providers for a passing caravan (11). The gathering is complete. *And God is the best of providers.*
