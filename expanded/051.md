# Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt (Chapter 51) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Winds That Scatter, and a Sūrah Named From Its First Oath**

Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt takes its name from its opening word, *wa'l-dhāriyāti* — "By those that scatter" — the wind-borne particles of dust and seed that God swears by in the very first verse. The name is drawn, as is the convention with Qur'anic sūrahs, not from the chapter's subject alone but from a distinctive word at its head, and it sets the tone for the whole: a sūrah of oaths, of subtle signs in nature, of four swiftly told histories of divine judgment, and of a single piercing declaration about why human beings were created. It belongs to the Makkan period of revelation, most likely its middle-to-later stretch, when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ still faced a Quraysh that mocked the idea of resurrection and demanded a date for the Judgment he announced. The sūrah's sixty verses answer that mockery at every level — through oath, through argument, through history, and through the calm, unanswerable statement of purpose that stands near its end: *"I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me"* (51:56).

**Where the Sūrah Sits in the Order of Revelation**

Al-Dhāriyāt follows naturally upon the sūrah that precedes it in the written arrangement, Qāf (Sūrah 50), which closed with the Resurrection, the Gathering, and the warning that the earth will split open and humanity will hasten forth. The first passage of al-Dhāriyāt takes up exactly that thread: its opening oaths conclude in verses 5–6 with the twin guarantee — *"verily what you are promised is true, and truly the judgment shall come to pass."* The classical commentators read this as a deliberate seam: Qāf described the scene of the end; al-Dhāriyāt now swears an oath that the scene is real. The sūrah therefore opens the way a courtroom opens a solemn testimony — with an oath designed to compel attention before the verdict is announced.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

Read as a whole, the sūrah unfolds in a clear five-part movement. It begins (vv. 1–6) with the oaths and their conclusion, the certainty of the promised judgment. It then turns (vv. 7–14) to the intellectual roots of disbelief — the scattered and self-contradictory claims of the deniers, their guessing, their stupor, and their sarcastic demand for a date — answered by the image of the fire upon which they will be "tried." There follows (vv. 15–19) the portrait of the reverent, who will dwell among gardens and springs, who slept but little of the night, who sought forgiveness before dawn, and in whose wealth there was a recognized share for the beggar and the deprived. The sūrah then pivots (vv. 20–23) to the signs — on the earth, within the human soul, in the sky, and in the Lord's own oath — that the promise is as certain as the listener's own power of speech. The middle section (vv. 24–46) recounts the honored guests of Abraham, the deliverance of the believers of Sodom and the ruin of the rest, Moses and Pharaoh, ʿĀd and the barren wind, Thamūd and the thunderbolt, and Noah — a chain of precedent establishing that mockery of a prophet has never ended well. Finally (vv. 47–60) the sūrah returns to the heavens and earth as signs, issues its twin commands — *flee unto God* and *set up no other god* — comforts the Prophet with the knowledge that every messenger was called "a sorcerer or one possessed," and closes with the purpose of creation and the final warning: *"Woe unto those who disbelieve on account of the Day that they are promised."*

**The Two Voices of the Chapter**

A striking feature of al-Dhāriyāt, noted by the scholars of the Qur'an's rhetoric, is the alternation of voice. Most of the sūrah speaks *about* the disbelievers in the third person; but verses 50–58 swing suddenly to direct address — *"So flee unto God. Truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him"* — placing the reader, not merely the seventh-century Quraysh, in the position of the one being warned. The shift performs the sūrah's argument: after watching ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh fall from the safe distance of narrative, the listener is made to realize that the warning is aimed at himself. This is the Qur'an's characteristic way of turning history into summons.

**The Place of the Sūrah in the Prophet's Own Life**

The tradition remembers specific moments in which al-Dhāriyāt was recited. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from the Companion al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib that he heard the Prophet ﷺ reciting *"By the fig and the olive"* (Sūrah 95) in the sunset prayer, and Muslim records the Prophet reciting in the Friday prayer such sūrahs as Sūrah al-Jumuʿah (62), al-Munāfiqūn (63), and al-Ghāshiyah (88). But the most celebrated report touching this sūrah concerns verse 47, the Prophet's prayer of *qunūt*, and the invocation said in witr: the Prophet ﷺ taught his grandson al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī a supplication that includes words drawn directly from al-Dhāriyāt's theme of fleeing to God and seeking refuge in Him. The sūrah is thus woven into the devotional practice of the community from the first generation onward.

**How the Commentary Is Structured**

What follows treats each of the sixty verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order. Verses that the source commentary handles in pairs or clusters — the opening oaths, the reverent's reward, the signs in earth and soul, Abraham's guests, Lot's deliverance, the spread of the sky and earth, God's self-sufficiency — are here separated, so that every verse receives its own full treatment. Each verse is quoted individually, then expanded through the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Ālūsī, and others), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of the teaching, and its bearing upon modern life. Nothing has been compressed because of the chapter's length: the sūrah's own theme — that the signs are there for whoever will look — is honored in the manner of the reading, unhurried and complete, from *wa'l-dhāriyāti* to *"Woe unto those who disbelieve on account of the Day that they are promised."*

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:1

> **By the scatterers as they scatter,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Begins the Chapter and Names It**

The sūrah opens with an oath — *wa'l-dhāriyāti dharwan* — "By the scatterers as they scatter." The construction is the Qur'anic oath formula, the particle *wāw* of swearing joined to a feminine plural participle, *al-dhāriyāt*. The word is a feminine active participle of the root *dh-r-w* (*dharā*), whose primary sense is to winnow, scatter, and disperse a thing into the air — as the farmer casts grain to the wind so that the chaff is carried off, or as the wind itself lifts dust and spreads it. The cognate noun *dhurwa* means a summit or peak; the scattered particles of a thing are called *dharr*, the fine motes of dust visible in a shaft of sunlight, which the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the smallest of all things: *"And whosoever does an atom's weight (mithqāla dharratin) of good shall see it"* (99:7). The very letters of the oath, then, belong to a family of words meaning the finest, most dispersed elements of the created world.

**What the Oath Swears By: The Classical Interpretations**

The commentators divide, with mutual respect, over what exactly the *dhāriyāt* are, and their readings are preserved together rather than treated as rivals. The majority and most famous view — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and adopted by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Jalālayn, and al-Ṭabarī — is that the scatterers are the *winds*: the winds that scatter dust and soil across the face of the earth, that winnow the chaff, and that carry the pollen and the seed. The image is of God's invisible agents moving over the landscape, spreading particles so fine that no human hand could direct them. A second view, also traced to Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Bayḍāwī, reads the scatterers as the *women* — or, more precisely, the generations of mothers — who scatter, that is, who bring forth progeny and disperse offspring across the earth. This reading rests on the observation that the same root describes the casting abroad of seed, so that human reproduction itself becomes a kind of divine scattering. The two interpretations are not finally in conflict: both describe forces that spread what was gathered, that sow what was held, and in doing so both witness to a plan that no scattering could proceed from chance.

**Why God Swears by the Wind**

The Qur'an frequently swears by phenomena of nature — the sun, the moon, the night, the dawn, the fig and the olive, the sky and its builder — and each oath carries an implicit argument. God may swear by whatever He wills, for oaths are His prerogative; yet the classical scholars note that when God swears by a created thing, He draws attention to the sign it contains. The wind is invisible, yet its effects are unmistakable; it cannot be grasped, yet it can move mountains of sand grain by grain; it obeys no human command, yet it fertilizes every orchard and fills every sail. To swear by the scattering winds is therefore to swear by a mercy that works invisibly, continuously, and without human participation. Al-Ṭabarī links the oath to the same logic found in 2:164:

> *"And the shifting of the winds, and the clouds subdued between the sky and the earth, are surely signs for a people who understand."* (Qur'an 2:164)

The winds that scatter dust also scatter rainclouds and spread them, and by that spreading the dead earth is revived — a miniature resurrection enacted before the eyes every season. This is the oath's quiet thrust: the same God who scatters the dust can scatter the dust of the dead and regather it.

**The Grammar of Multiplicity and Its Meaning**

That the oath's objects are feminine plurals — *al-dhāriyāti, al-ḥāmilāti, al-jāriyāti, al-muqassimāti* — is itself worth noticing. Arabic forms these participles in the feminine plural to describe not one wind or one ship but uncountable multitudes: winds beyond number, clouds beyond count, ships beyond tally, commands beyond measure. The plural performs what the wind performs: it disperses attention across an entire created order rather than fixing it on a single instance. The grammatical form is the plural of *dhāriya*, and the *wāw* of the oath repeats before each of the four objects, binding them into one ascending chain of testimony, each link sworn by God. The listener is made to hear a solemn litany — scatterers, bearers, coursers, apportioners — a procession of witnesses filing in before the verdict that the chapter is about to pronounce.

**The Logical Movement: From Scattering to Gathering**

The deepest point of the oath emerges when one asks what a thing that scatters has to do with the resurrection, which the sūrah is about to guarantee. The answer, developed by the commentators, is that scattering is never the end of God's acts, only their beginning. The wind scatters the seed, and the seed dies in the earth, and the earth returns it multiplied. The cloud scatters the rain, and the rain revives what was dead. The mother scatters her children, and the generations are thereby renewed. Each scattering in the created order is a preparation for a regathering — which is precisely the structure of the resurrection itself, where what death has dispersed, God will recollect. Ibn Kathīr observes that the oaths of this sūrah were taken on the winds and the clouds and the like because they demonstrate the One who brings life to the dead earth, and thus testify to His power to bring life to the dead bodies of men. The oath, then, is not decorative: it is the first argument of the sūrah, hidden inside a single line.

**A Modern Eye on the Ancient Oath**

For a reader in any age, the opening verse offers a lesson in attention. The modern mind moves through the world without noticing the wind, except when it inconveniences a journey or knocks down a fence. The Qur'an trains a different habit: to see in the most ordinary and invisible of forces a signature. The same air that carries a seed across a field also carried the pollen that made the fruit that feeds a city; the same breeze that disperses dust disperses the clouds that water the valley. The sūrah's first word asks the listener to pause before the wind and recognize that scattering is an instrument of providence. In this it exemplifies the whole Makkan method of the Qur'an: it does not begin with abstract proof but with the world in front of the eyes, and it asks only that the hearer look at it honestly.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:2

> **and by those that bear a burden,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Witness in the Oath's Chain**

The oath continues: *fa'l-ḥāmilāti wiqran* — "and by those that bear a burden." The particle here is *fāʾ* ("and then"), which links the second oath to the first in a sequence of ascent rather than mere repetition: first the scatterers, then the bearers. The noun *al-ḥāmilāt* is again a feminine plural active participle, from the root *ḥ-m-l*, "to carry, to bear, to lift." Its object, *wiqr*, is a specific word for a heavy load — not the lighter *ḥiml* that a man might carry on his shoulder, but a weight of real consequence, the load of a beast or a vessel. The same root yields *ḥamala*, "to become pregnant," and *ḥaml*, "pregnancy," so that the word's associations reach in two directions at once: outward toward the clouds freighted with rain, and inward toward the mother carrying a child. The oath deliberately chooses a vocabulary that can hold both meanings.

**The Clouds That Carry Moisture**

The dominant and most famous interpretation — shared by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī — is that the bearers of a burden are the *clouds*. The cloud is a burden-bearer in the most literal sense: it is a vast reservoir of water suspended in the sky, carried by the wind from the sea to the land, holding a weight that would flatten a city if it fell at once, yet dispersed as merciful rain. The Qur'an itself draws exactly this picture when it describes the winds as drivers of the clouds:

> *"And it is God Who sends the winds, and they stir the clouds, and We drive them to a dead land and revive thereby the earth after its death. Even so shall be the Resurrection!"* (Qur'an 35:9)

The same verse makes the connection this sūrah's oath relies upon: the cloud that carries water to a dead land and revives it is the very pattern of the resurrection. To swear by the burden-bearing clouds is to swear by a daily, observable act of reviving the dead — rain falling on barren earth until it is covered in green.

**The Women Who Carry New Generations**

The second reading, recorded by al-Bayḍāwī and al-Qurṭubī and traced to the first generations, understands the bearers as *pregnant women* — the mothers who carry the unborn, the heaviest and most honored of burdens. This reading is strengthened by the word *wiqr*: pregnancy is classically described as a weight, and the Qur'an speaks of the mother's travail with exactly this vocabulary:

> *"And We have enjoined upon man goodness toward his parents: his mother carried him in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is in two years."* (Qur'an 31:14)

On this view, the oath moves in a beautiful sequence: the winds that scatter the seed, and the mothers who carry the new life that seed becomes. The first oath describes the sowing, the second the bearing, the third (the ships) the journeying, and the fourth (the angels) the apportioning of every destiny. The chain thus recapitulates the whole arc of a life carried by God's design, from conception to appointed end.

**The Theology Hidden in the Image of Carrying**

Why does the Qur'an swear by bearers of burden? Because burden-bearing is, in the Qur'an's anthropology, the universal human condition — and the one thing from which God alone is exempt. The same root appears at the climax of the most discussed verse on human accountability:

> *"No bearer of burden shall bear the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164; 17:15; 35:18; 39:7; 53:38)

Man carries the weight of his own deeds; the cloud carries the weight of the rain; the mother carries the weight of the child; and over all of them stands the One who carries all burdens while being carried by none. The oath thus sets up, at the very threshold of the sūrah, the contrast that will be made explicit at its end, where God declares that He desires no provision from His creatures — He is the Provider, the Possessor of Strength (51:57–58). Everything in creation is a carrier, and everything carried points to the One who carries without need.

**From Weather to Womb: The Unity of the Two Readings**

The two interpretations should not be forced into rivalry. The Arabic word was chosen, the commentators note, precisely because it holds both the cloud and the womb within its range of meaning, and the Qur'an frequently intends a word's full semantic field. The cloud is the womb of the sky, carrying water the way a mother carries life; both pour forth what they carry as mercy. And both are "burdens" only from the human point of view — from God's point of view they are effortless acts of continuous providence. The oath invites the hearer to stand between sky and womb and see the same signature in each: a carrying that sustains the world, performed ceaselessly, invisibly, and free of charge.

**A Lesson for the Hurried Reader**

For the modern reader the verse is a small course in the Qur'anic art of looking. The modern habit is to see the cloud only as a weather system and pregnancy only as a biological process, and so to see nothing else. The Qur'an's oaths are an antidote to this flattening of the world: they take the cloud and the womb and demand that the hearer see in them what they actually are — instruments, signs, witnesses. The verse does not argue that a Designer exists; it simply swears by the bearers of burden, and lets the honest eye complete the argument. Whoever has never seen a cloud as anything but vapor, or a mother as anything but an organism, has not yet looked.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:3

> **by those that course with ease,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Oath and the Picture of Gentle Motion**

The litany rises again: *fa'l-jāriyāti yusran* — "and by those that course with ease." The participle *al-jāriyāt* comes from the root *j-r-y*, whose basic sense is "to run, to flow, to course." A *jāriya* is anything that runs — running water, a running horse, and, in the most natural application, a *ship* gliding upon the sea, for the Qur'an uses the verb *jarā* of ships repeatedly: *"And His are the ships running (al-jawāri) upon the sea, like mountains"* (55:24). The word *yusran* is a noun of ease — "with ease, effortlessly, gently" — and the pairing of "coursing" with "ease" is deliberate: the oath swears not merely by things that move, but by things that move through their element without strain, as a fish through water or a ship over a calm sea.

**The Ships That Glide Upon the Ocean**

The dominant interpretation, shared by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭabarī, is that the coursers are the *ships* that run upon the surface of the ocean. The image is one of the Qur'an's favorites, because a ship is a standing miracle that its passengers rarely notice. A vessel of iron and timber, heavier than the water that bears it, laden with cargo and hundreds of souls, moves across a surface that would swallow any man in an instant — and it moves "with ease." The Qur'an draws the lesson explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And of His signs are the ships upon the sea like mountains. If He wills, He stills the wind, and they remain motionless on its back. Truly in that are signs for every patient, grateful soul."* (Qur'an 42:32–33)

The ease of the ship's course is not the ship's own achievement; it is a suspended act of providence, renewed at every moment. The sea could, at a word, cease to carry — and the sūrah will shortly remind its hearers of a sea that did exactly that, when Pharaoh's hosts pursued Moses and the water closed over them (51:40).

**The Planets That Run Through Their Stations**

A second reading, preserved by al-Bayḍāwī, understands the coursers as the *heavenly bodies* — the planets and stars that run their fixed courses through the sky. This reading is linguistically sound, for the same root describes the sun and moon in their orbits: *"And the sun runs (tajrī) to a resting place of its own; that is the decree of the Mighty, the Knowing"* (36:38). On this view the oath moves outward from earth to heaven: the scatterers (the winds), the bearers (the clouds), the coursers (the planets), and finally the apportioners (the angels). The sequence becomes a single sweep of the eye across the whole creation — from the dust underfoot to the orbits overhead — each level a witness. The planets "course with ease" because their motion is perfectly governed, never colliding, never deviating, never tiring, which is itself a sign of the Governor.

**Ease as a Signature of the Divine**

The word *yusran* deserves its own attention, for ease is one of the Qur'an's names for the manner of God's work. The revelation declares of itself: *"We have made the Qur'an easy (yassarnā) for remembrance"* (54:17). The Prophet ﷺ was assured: *"So truly with hardship comes ease"* (94:5). And here the created order is described as running *yusran* — with ease. The pattern is consistent: whatever God does is done without strain, and whatever God commands is made manageable for the one who obeys. Al-Ṭabarī connects the ease of the ship's course to the ease of the believer's path — both are gifts of the same Provider. The oath thus prepares the hearer for the sūrah's later command: *"So flee unto God"* (51:50) — a flight that, however difficult in the world's reckoning, is described as the one motion that leads to ease rather than away from it.

**The Contrast That Will Come: Motion Eased and Motion Destroyed**

The third oath also plants a contrast the chapter will harvest later. The ships course *with ease* — but in the story of Pharaoh (51:40) the sea that carried ships will be turned into a grave. The heavenly bodies run their courses undisturbed — but for ʿĀd (51:41–42) the wind will not course gently but blast with barren fury, and for Thamūd (51:44) the thunderbolt will seize them. The sūrah's oaths describe the world as it is when God's mercy governs it: scattering, bearing, coursing, apportioning — all in order and ease. The histories that follow describe what happens when a people sets itself against that order. The oaths are thus the calm before the storm, and their calm is itself the argument: this is how the world runs, and it runs so only by the One who can also stop it.

**Learning to See the Hidden Support**

For the modern reader, the third oath is a summons to notice the supports that daily life silently relies upon. A person boards a ship or an airplane and trusts, without a thought, the buoyancy of water or the lift of air — forces no passenger created, no pilot commands, and no engineer guarantees. The ease with which the world carries us is so complete that it becomes invisible, and its invisibility becomes, for the heedless, an excuse for forgetting. The Qur'an's answer is this oath: *by the things that course with ease* — as if to say, do not mistake the reliability of providence for its absence. The very ease is the sign.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:4

> **and by those that apportion the Command,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Oath and Its Climax**

The opening litany reaches its summit: *fa'l-muqassimāti amran* — "and by those that apportion the Command." The participle *al-muqassimāt* is from the root *q-s-m*, "to divide, to distribute, to apportion," the root from which *qismah* (a share), *qasam* (an oath), and *maqsūm* (a thing allotted) all derive. The object *amran* is "a command, an affair, a matter" — the whole business of destiny. The oath thus swears by beings whose office it is to divide and distribute the divine decree: to apportion rain to this land and withhold it from that one, to carry the appointed provision to each creature, to execute the judgment written for every soul. This is the fourth and highest of the witnesses, and it names directly what the first three only implied — that behind the scattering winds, the bearing clouds, and the coursing ships stands an order of *command* that is distributed, measure by measure, by obedient agents.

**The Angels Who Apportion What God Ordains**

The majority interpretation — recorded by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Jalālayn, and al-Ṭabarī — is that the apportioners are the *angels*. This is the natural reading in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, for the angels are described precisely as dividers of the command:

> *"By those who pluck out violently, and those who draw out gently, and those who glide, and those who race ahead, and those who govern the Command (al-mudabbirāti amran)."* (Qur'an 79:1–5)

And again:

> *"And by those who range in ranks, and those who drive away, and those who recite the Reminder"* (Qur'an 37:1–3) — *"truly your God is One."*

The angels are God's appointed executors: they carry the revelation down to the prophets, they write the deeds, they take the souls, they distribute mercy and calamity according to the decree. To swear by them is to swear by the entire invisible government of the world, the administration that runs the universe while its visible inhabitants sleep.

**The Winds That Apportion the Rain**

A second reading, preserved by al-Bayḍāwī, takes the apportioners as the *winds* themselves, which apportion the rain by driving the clouds now here, now there — so that one valley is watered and another left dry, exactly as God has decreed. On this reading the four oaths fold back into a single subject viewed from four angles: the wind that scatters, the wind's clouds that bear, the wind's ships that course, and the wind's apportioning of rain across the earth. The unity is attractive, for it makes the opening verses a meditation on a single invisible force — the wind — through which God scatters, carries, courses, and distributes the life of the world. Both readings are sound, and the classical commentators, as is their custom with oaths, record them side by side, since the Qur'an's oaths often carry more than one true reference at once.

**Why the Oath Climbs to "the Command"**

The word *amr* — command — is the hinge on which the whole opening turns. The scatterers scatter, the bearers bear, the coursers course, but none of them does so of its own accord: each executes an *amr*. The wind is not free to blow where it pleases; it is "subdued" (*musakhkhar*) — a word the Qur'an uses repeatedly of the winds, the sea, and the heavenly bodies, all of them *musakhkharāt* by God's command:

> *"And He has subjected to you the night and the day, and the sun and the moon, and the stars are subjected by His Command. Truly in that are signs for a people who understand."* (Qur'an 16:12)

The oath's climax is therefore a piece of precise theology: the universe is not a set of blind forces but a set of obedient servants carrying out a distributed command. This is why the very next verse can leap from winds and clouds to the resurrection: if the whole visible world is the execution of a command, then the command to resurrect is of exactly the same order, and no more difficult for its Commander than the command that scatters dust.

**The Connection Between Oath and Destiny**

The root *q-s-m* hides a further link that the commentators notice. The word for an oath, *qasam*, and the word for apportioning, *qismah*, are the same root — an oath is literally a "cutting" or "division" of speech, a binding of oneself by a divided, solemn formula. By swearing *by those that apportion the Command*, God swears by the apportioners in a formula whose very name is "apportioning." The device is subtle but real: the oath by the dividers of destiny is itself an act of division, drawing the hearer into the very reality it invokes. Language, in the Qur'an's hands, enacts what it states.

**The Practical Lesson: Every Share Is Measured**

For the modern reader the fourth oath teaches the doctrine of measured provision — the *qadar* — in its most livable form. Nothing in one's life arrives unmeasured; rain, health, wealth, opportunity, and hardship are all portions of a command distributed by agents who never err. The Qur'an states the principle elsewhere with a vow of its own:

> *"There is no creature on the earth but that its provision is upon God, and He knows its dwelling and its repository."* (Qur'an 11:6)

This is not fatalism but its opposite. To know that one's share is apportioned is to be freed from the desperate grasping that poisons modern life — the envy, the overwork, the terror of scarcity. The one who believes the apportioners exist can work with all his strength and yet sleep with a quiet heart, because the outcome was never in his hands to begin with. The sūrah's opening oaths, so easy to pass over as poetic decoration, are in fact a complete education in the peace that comes from recognizing a governed world.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:5

> **verily what you are promised is true,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath's Answer Arrives**

After four oaths, the sentence to which they are all sworn now arrives: *innamā tūʿadūna la-ṣādiq* — "verily what you are promised is true." The structure is emphatic in three places at once. The particle *inna* ("verily") introduces certainty; the restricting particle *mā* (*innamā*) narrows the whole statement to one unqualified claim; and the *lām* of *la-ṣādiq* is the oath's own answer — the *lām al-qasam*, "the lām of swearing," which grammarians call the response to an oath. The verse is thus grammatically welded to the four oaths before it: "By the scatterers, and the bearers, and the coursers, and the apportioners — verily what you are promised is true." It is a single sentence stretched across five verses, and the listener who follows it to the end receives the verdict the oaths were summoned to deliver.

**What Is Promised: Resurrection, Judgment, Reward, and Punishment**

The phrase *tūʿadūn* — "you are promised" — is a passive form of the verb *waʿada*, "to promise," and in the Qur'an the promise (*waʿd*) par excellence is the promise of the Hereafter: the resurrection of the body, the standing for judgment, the scales, the Bridge, the Garden, and the Fire. Al-Jalālayn glosses the verse as the promise of "resurrection and all that follows it," and al-Ṭabarī expands the same point: the final accounting, the reward, and the punishment are all inevitable. The sūrah has thus, in its first five verses, traveled the entire distance from dust to destiny: from the winds that scatter soil to the Day that will scatter all human certainties, from the clouds that revive dead earth to the Day that revives dead bodies, from the ships that carry cargo to the Day that will carry every soul to its verdict. The oaths were never about weather; they were about the resurrection, seen in miniature.

**The Logic of the Argument: A Repeated Pattern**

Why should winds and clouds prove the resurrection? The commentators unfold the reasoning with care. The resurrection, reduced to its essence, is an act of *bringing life out of death* — and this act is performed before human eyes every year. The Qur'an makes the parallel explicit:

> *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humble; then when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells. Surely He Who revives it is the One Who gives life to the dead. Truly He is Powerful over all things."* (Qur'an 41:39)

The wind scatters the seed into the earth's tomb; the cloud bears the water; the rain falls; and the seed — dry, lifeless, apparently dead — is returned as a living plant. If God performs this resurrection annually, on a scale of continents, without any difficulty, then the resurrection of human bodies is no harder for Him, but easier, for it is the same act. Al-Ṭabarī notes that this is precisely why the sūrah opens with the winds and clouds: they are the evidence, already accepted by every farmer and every Bedouin of the Quraysh, that the dead can be revived. The Qur'an argues from what its hearers already believe about the world to what they deny about the Hereafter.

**The Epistemology of the Oath: Why Swear at All?**

An oath is the strongest instrument of conviction available to human speech, and its presence here deserves reflection. When a man swears, he calls a witness greater than himself to guarantee his truthfulness; when God swears, He calls His own creation to witness, and His creation cannot refuse. Al-Rāzī and the later scholars observe that the frequency of oaths in the Makkan sūrahs corresponds to the intensity of denial they faced: the Quraysh did not merely doubt the resurrection, they ridiculed it, and against ridicule the Qur'an does not argue with syllogisms alone but with the solemnity of sworn speech. The oath does not add information; it adds weight. It says, in effect: this matter is so certain that it merits being sworn upon, and so important that the whole of creation is summoned to stand surety for it.

**The Psychology of Being Promised**

There is also a deep comfort in the verse's passive voice — *you are promised*. The hearer is not asked to produce the resurrection or to achieve it; he is told it is *promised to him*. A promise, unlike a threat alone, engages the Promiser's faithfulness, and the Qur'an grounds the certainty of the Hereafter precisely in God's truthfulness: *"God does not break His promise"* (3:9; 13:31; 30:6; 39:20). The verse's word *ṣādiq* — "true" — is the adjective of truthfulness (*ṣidq*), the quality of one who does not lie. The entire hope of the believer, and the entire dread of the denier, rest on this single attribute: the Promiser is *true*. If God is truthful, then the Garden is as real as the rain; if He is truthful, then the Fire is as real as the drought. The verse is thus not a philosophical argument but a personal one — an appeal to the character of the One who promised.

**For the Modern Reader: Living Inside a Promise**

The modern age has not abolished the need for this verse; it has sharpened it. A culture that believes nothing is *promised* — that life is an accident and the future a void — must manufacture meaning by ceaseless effort, and its exhaustion is everywhere visible. The verse offers a different posture: the future is not empty but *promised*, and promised by One who is true. This does not invite passivity, for the promise is also a summons; but it does invite rest of a kind the modern mind has forgotten — the rest of trusting a promise from a source that cannot lie. The believer who recites this oath and lets it settle does not merely affirm a doctrine; he exchanges anxiety for certainty, and certainty for the energy to live the only life worth living: the one lived in preparation for what has been promised.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:6

> **and truly the judgment shall come to pass.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Half of the Double Guarantee**

The oath's conclusion is completed: *wa-inna al-dīna la-wāqiʿ* — "and truly the judgment shall come to pass." The verse stands parallel to the one before it, each beginning with the emphatic *inna*, each carrying the oath's answering *lām*, so that the two verses form a matched pair: *the promise is true, and the judgment shall fall.* The word *al-dīn* here is not "religion" in the narrow sense but the Day of Judgment, the Recompense — the Qur'an uses the phrase *yawm al-dīn*, "the Day of Judgment," for the day of requital (1:4; 82:15). And the word *wāqiʿ* is a participle of the root *w-q-ʿ*, "to fall, to occur, to befall" — the same root from which the Qur'an takes the name *al-Wāqiʿah*, "the Occurrence," for Sūrah 56, which opens with the same guarantee:

> *"When the Occurrence (al-wāqiʿah) occurs, none shall deny its coming to pass (waqʿatahā)."* (Qur'an 56:1–2)

The participle *wāqiʿ* is a promise cast in the form of an accomplished fact: the judgment is not something that *may* happen or *will perhaps* happen; it is, in the grammar itself, *falling*, *occurring*, already in motion toward its hour.

**The Meaning of "the Judgment" in This Context**

Al-Dīn, as the commentators explain, gathers several meanings that converge on a single reality. It is the *recompense* — the returning of every deed upon its doer; it is the *reckoning* — the setting up of the scales and the account; and it is the *judgment* — the verdict that follows the weighing. The verse thus announces not merely that the world will end, but that it will end in *justice*. This is the point that the Makkan idolaters found hardest to accept, not because they doubted that death was real, but because they could not believe that their actions would ever be called to account. The Qur'an answers that denial with the most unqualified certainty in its vocabulary: *inna* and *la-* and the participle of occurrence, all stacked upon one another, as if to say — this is not negotiable.

**The Cross-Reference to the Oath of the Mountain**

The source commentary cross-references Sūrah 52:7, where the same guarantee appears:

> *"Truly the punishment of thy Lord shall come to pass (la-wāqiʿ)."* (Qur'an 52:7)

The two sūrahs, adjacent in the written order, thus reinforce one another. Al-Dhāriyāt swears by winds and clouds that the *judgment* shall fall; al-Ṭūr swears by the mountain, the inscribed book, and the frequented house that the *punishment* shall fall. The parallel is part of a larger Qur'anic strategy: the certainty of the Hereafter is announced from every angle — through oaths, through parables, through the histories of ruined nations, and through direct appeal — until no angle of approach is left unguarded. The Makkan hearer who finished one sūrah met the same truth in the next, framed differently but identically insistent.

**"Shall Come to Pass" and the Certainty of What Falls**

The root *w-q-ʿ* is worth pausing over, because it gives the verse its distinctive feel. The word describes what *falls* — a blow that lands, an arrow that strikes, an event that descends. It is the vocabulary of gravity, not of mere possibility. A thing that is *wāqiʿ* has already left the hand and is in the air; its arrival is a matter of time, not of chance. This is precisely the Qur'an's theology of the Hour: its time is hidden, but its occurrence is settled. The Prophet ﷺ was asked again and again, "When is the Hour?" and the revelation trained him to answer not with a date but with a certainty:

> *"They ask thee about the Hour, when it will come to pass. Say: knowledge thereof is with my Lord alone; none shall manifest it at its time but He."* (Qur'an 7:187)

The hiddenness of the *time* and the certainty of the *event* are both essential: a judgment whose date were known could be gamed, postponed against, repented of at the last instant; a judgment whose date is hidden but whose coming is certain keeps every day weighty. The verse holds both truths in a single word: it shall *fall* — and no one knows when.

**Justice as the Deepest Need of the Human Heart**

Beneath the verse's grammar lies a psychological truth the Qur'an builds upon throughout. Human beings cannot live sanely in a world where wrongs are never righted. The tyrant dies in his bed, the victim dies in the ditch, and if death is the end, then the ledger of history is permanently unbalanced — which is why the twentieth century, which tried to live without the Hereafter, produced the most desperate substitutes for it: the revolution, the utopia, the reckoning in history, each promising a justice that always receded. The Qur'an's answer is simpler and finally inescapable: the judgment *shall fall*. There will be a court where no bribe is taken, no witness silenced, no deed lost, where the victim and the tyrant stand on equal ground before the Judge. The verse's certainty is not a threat to the innocent but their deepest consolation — the promise that nothing is finally wasted and no one is finally forgotten.

**From Oath to Verdict: The Opening Section Completed**

With this verse the opening movement of the sūrah closes. Four oaths have been sworn; a double guarantee has been delivered. The winds scatter, the clouds bear, the ships course, the angels apportion — and all of it converges on two facts: the promise is true, and the judgment shall fall. The listener has been brought, in six verses, from the dust of the earth to the threshold of the Hereafter. The sūrah will now turn from the guarantee to its hearers, and the next verse will describe what those hearers do with this truth: *"truly you are of differing claims."* The stage is set, the verdict announced; what remains is the response.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:7

> **By Heaven possessed of paths,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New Oath, and a Word of Many Wonders**

A fresh oath opens the next section: *wa'l-samāʾi dhāti al-ḥubuk* — "By Heaven possessed of paths." The word *ḥubuk* is the crux of the verse, and its range of meaning is so rich that the commentators devote sustained attention to it. The root *ḥ-b-k* carries the sense of "to weave, to make firm and well, to plait"; a garment woven tightly and beautifully is *ḥabk*, and the verb *ḥabaka* means "to make a thing strong and well-ordered." Al-Ṭabarī records that *ḥubuk* indicates the ripples that the wind makes upon sand or water — the fine, parallel waves that pattern a dune or a pond — and also the streaks that run along a mountain's face. Al-Ṭabarī then draws the threads together: in this context *ḥubuk* refers to the subtle paths through which the heavenly bodies move, the invisible tracks of sun, moon, and stars across the sky. The single word thus holds three images at once — woven fabric, wind-rippled sand, and the orbital paths of the heavens — and the Qur'an chooses it deliberately, so that all three shimmer behind the oath.

**The Sky Itself, or the Heavens Above It**

The word *samāʾ* can mean either "sky" (the visible canopy of cloud and atmosphere) or "heaven" (the loftier realm of the celestial bodies), and the commentators allow both. Read as "sky," the *ḥubuk* are the streaks and bands of cloud that pattern the atmosphere; read as "heaven," they are the paths and orbits of the stars. Al-Ṭabarī, who favors the loftier reading, notes that the underlying verb *ḥabaka* — "to make well and firm" — gives the verse its true keynote in either case: the oath swears by the *beauty and sound structure* of the created order. The sky is not a chaos of weather but a woven thing, a fabric of ordered streaks and moving lights, and its order is precisely what makes it a sign. The verse thus continues the sūrah's method: it does not argue, it points — at a sky whose structure any night-watcher can verify with his own eyes.

**The Fabric Metaphor and the Maker's Craft**

The weaving image deserves emphasis, for it is one of the Qur'an's favorite ways of describing the heavens. Elsewhere the sky is *woven* with stars:

> *"And by the sky, and the night-visitant — and what will convey to thee what the night-visitant is? — the piercing star."* (Qur'an 86:1–3)

And the celestial order is described as a roof raised without visible pillars, a firmament made firm:

> *"And We made the sky a roof, preserved."* (Qur'an 21:32)

The metaphor of weaving carries a precise theological point: a fabric does not weave itself. Where there is warp and weft, there is a weaver; where there is structure, there is a structurer. The streaks of cloud and the paths of planets are the visible "weave" of a work whose maker the eye cannot see but the mind cannot miss. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss — that *ḥabaka* means "to make well and firm" — is thus not merely lexical but argumentative: the sky's firmness and beauty are the signature of the One who made it firm.

**The Oath's Target: The Hearts That See and the Hearts That Deny**

Why does the sūrah swear by the streaked, woven sky at this point? Because the verses that follow (51:8–9) describe the disorder in the *hearers* — their "differing claims," their turning away — and the oath sets the order of the sky against the disorder of the human heart. The sky is a *ḥubuk*: woven, structured, coherent. The disbelievers are its opposite: scattered, contradictory, turned about. The contrast is the sūrah's silent accusation: God gave you a sky whose every streak declares its Maker, and you respond with self-contradiction. The oath is thus not ornamental but pointed — it holds up the order of creation as a mirror in which the disorder of denial is exposed.

**The Star Paths and the Guidance They Symbolize**

The interpretation of *ḥubuk* as the paths of the heavenly bodies carries a further, practical meaning for the sūrah's original hearers. The Arabs of the desert navigated by the stars; the fixed paths of the constellations were their maps across trackless sand. A sky "possessed of paths" is a sky that *guides* — the same word-family as *hudā*, guidance, that runs through the Qur'an. The oath thus whispers a promise: the same God who set paths in the sky for the traveler's body has set a path for the traveler's soul, and the sūrah is about to point to it — *"So flee unto God"* (51:50). The physical paths of the stars are a parable of the spiritual path of the revelation: both are set by the same Maker, both are reliable, and both are there for whoever will follow them.

**For the Modern Reader: The Lost Habit of Looking Up**

The modern reader lives under a sky that he seldom sees — drowned out by city lights, blocked by ceilings, ignored by a generation that looks down at screens. The verse is an invitation to recover a lost habit: to look up, and to see not empty space but *ḥubuk* — structure, pattern, path. Astronomers now describe the heavens with the same vocabulary the Qur'an used fourteen centuries ago: the stars move in orbits, the galaxies in filaments, the cosmos itself a woven web of dark matter and light. The sūrah does not ask the modern reader to abandon his science but to complete it: to see, in the very order that science maps, the signature that science cannot. The sky possessed of paths is, in the end, an address — and the next verse will turn from the sky to its addressee.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:8

> **truly you are of differing claims.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath's Answer: A Diagnosis of the Hearers**

The oath by the woven sky receives its response: *innakum la-fī qawlin mukhtalif* — "truly you are of differing claims." The *lām* of *la-fī* is again the oath's answering lām, so the grammar binds verse 8 to verse 7 exactly as verse 5 was bound to verses 1–4: *By the sky possessed of paths — truly you are of differing claims.* The sky is coherent; you are not. The verse is a diagnosis, and it lands with the precision of a physician naming a disease: the hearers of the Qur'an, far from standing on any single solid position, are scattered across a field of mutually contradictory claims about the Prophet, the Qur'an, and the Hereafter.

**What the Differing Claims Were**

The commentators enumerate the contradictions with relish, because the list itself is the argument. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Jalālayn, and al-Ṭabarī record that the Quraysh could not agree among themselves about what the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was. Some said he was a poet (*shāʿir*); others that he was a sorcerer (*sāḥir*); others that he was possessed (*majnūn*); still others that he was a soothsayer (*kāhin*). These labels were not only false; they were incompatible — a poet does not speak like a soothsayer, a sorcerer does not compose like a madman — and the fact that the same man drew such contradictory accusations was itself the proof that none of the accusers believed any of them. The Qur'an seizes on the contradiction elsewhere:

> *"Consider how they strike for thee parables, and so go astray, and cannot find a way."* (Qur'an 17:48)

The differing claims also extended to the Qur'an and the resurrection: some denied the resurrection outright, others admitted it but misdescribed it, and none could hold a position for long, because the truth they were fleeing kept breaking in upon their inconsistencies.

**The Psychology of Contradictory Denial**

Beneath the historical detail lies a timeless observation about the psychology of disbelief. When a person rejects a truth that his conscience recognizes, he cannot rest in a single false position, because no single false position can hold his conscience at bay for long. He must keep moving — poet, sorcerer, madman, soothsayer — inventing a new label whenever the old one collapses. The contradiction is not a sign of intellectual sophistication but of flight: a man running from the truth cannot run in a straight line. Al-Ṭabarī's commentary implies exactly this when he shows the Quraysh's claims collapsing into one another. The Qur'an's diagnosis is thus not merely historical but anthropological: falsehood is self-fragmenting, and the fragmenting of the deniers' claims is the evidence of the truth they deny.

**The Contrast with the Certainty of the Believers**

The verse's force comes from its contrast with what precedes it. The sky is *ḥubuk* — woven, firm, coherent. The believers, in verses 15–19, will be described with the same coherence: they sleep little, pray much, seek forgiveness, and give from their wealth — a single, integrated life. The disbelievers, by contrast, are *mukhtalif* — differing, divided, at war with themselves. The sūrah's implicit scale is clear: truth produces unity and consistency; falsehood produces fragmentation and contradiction. This is a test the Qur'an invites every age to apply, and it is one of the most persuasive of its arguments: examine the lives and claims of the truthful and of the deniers, and see which group is coherent and which is scattered.

**"Differing Claims" as a Standing Human Condition**

The verse also describes a condition that long outlives seventh-century Makkah. Every age produces its own versions of the differing claims — a marketplace of mutually incompatible accounts of reality, each loudly held, none able to stand examination. The modern world speaks of "your truth" and "my truth," as if truth were a possession to be divided; it produces a thousand theories of human purpose that cancel one another out; it offers, in the same hour, the claim that life is meaningless and the claim that meaning is whatever one chooses. The Qur'an's answer to this ancient, recurring condition is not to add another opinion to the pile but to point to the one position that does not crumble under examination — the truth sworn by the sky and guaranteed by its Maker. The differing claims, the verse implies, are not evidence that truth is unavailable; they are evidence that truth has been refused.

**A Call to Self-Examination**

Finally, the verse turns the diagnosis upon the reader. It is possible to be *fī qawlin mukhtalif* — "in a differing claim" — without realizing it: to hold a set of beliefs that do not cohere, to profess one thing and live another, to affirm God in theory and serve idols in practice. The sūrah's address — *truly you are of differing claims* — was aimed at a specific audience, but its grammar is open, and the tradition has always read it as a standing summons to examine one's own consistency. The believer who takes the verse seriously will ask: is my life woven, like the sky, or scattered, like the claims? The question is the verse's gift, and the next verse will show where the scattering leads.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:9

> **Whosoever has been turned away shall from it be turned away.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Whose Grammar Mirrors Its Meaning**

This is one of the sūrah's most compact and most discussed verses: *yuʾfaku ʿanhu man ufik* — literally, "turned away from it is he who has been turned away." The verb *afaka / yuʾfaku* is the root of *ifk*, the word for a lie, a perversion, a thing turned from its proper course. The root's core meaning is *to turn*, and from it the Qur'an derives both *ifk* (falsehood, slander — the accusation against ʿĀʾishah is called *al-ifk* in Sūrah 24) and *al-muʾtafikāt*, "the overturned" — the cities of Lot's people, which were literally turned upside down (9:70; 69:9). The verse uses the root twice, once in the passive and once in what may be read as the passive or the active, and the doubling is itself the point: the one who has been *turned* (perverted, deflected from truth) shall be *turned away* (deflected from the Qur'an and the Prophet). The grammar enacts a law: perversion begets exclusion.

**The Two Readings: Passive and Active**

The commentators record a genuine grammatical divergence with real theological weight. Read as a passive — "whosoever has been turned away (ufika) shall be turned away (yuʾfaku)" — the verse describes a settled consequence: those whose intellects and souls have already been perverted will continue in their perversion. Ibn Kathīr transmits this reading and its explanation: just as the sower reaps what he sows, the soul that has turned from truth finds itself turned ever further. Mujāhid, the great student of Ibn ʿAbbās, explains it as a statement about weakness of judgment: whoever is weak in intellect and discernment will, by that weakness, be turned away from religion. But al-Bayḍāwī and al-Rāzī also record an active reading of the first verb — "whosoever *lies* (afaka) shall be turned away from it" — so that the verse becomes a direct causal law: the liar is turned away from the truth precisely by his lying. Both readings converge on the same moral: falsehood is self-perpetuating, and the punishment of turning from truth is to be left in the turning.

**What "It" Is: The Qur'an, the Prophet, the Truth**

The pronoun *ʿanhu* — "from it" — has an antecedent the commentators supply with slight differences that all point the same way. Al-Ṭabarī takes "it" to be the *Qur'an*; al-Jalālayn, the *Prophet and the Qur'an* together; others simply the *truth*. The differences are trivial because the objects are inseparable: to be turned from the Prophet is to be turned from the Book he brought, and to be turned from the Book is to be turned from the truth. The verse thus completes the diagnosis of verse 8: the differing claimants are not merely confused but *turned* — deflected by an inner perversion from the very message that stands before them. The verse answers the question that every preacher's experience raises: why, when the truth is clear, do some still not see it? Because seeing is not a matter of light alone but of the eye, and the eye that has willed its own turning cannot be forced to look.

**The Principle: God Does Not Wrong, the Soul Wrongs Itself**

This verse is the Qur'an's recurring principle of *divine justice* stated in its most compact form. God does not turn a soul away arbitrarily; the turning is the consequence of the soul's own turning. The Qur'an states the law in both directions:

> *"And when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate."* (Qur'an 61:5)

> *"So because they broke their covenant, We cursed them and made their hearts hard."* (Qur'an 5:13)

And with equal clarity the other way:

> *"And those who strive for Us, We shall surely guide them upon Our paths."* (Qur'an 29:69)

The two movements — turning away breeds further turning away; striving breeds guidance — are one law seen from two sides. This is not predestination against the will but the Qur'an's account of how the will, once bent, stays bent: every act of refusal digs the channel a little deeper, until the channel becomes a canyon the soul can no longer climb. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both stress that the verse describes God's *just* response to the soul's own choice, never an arbitrary decree. The doctrine at stake is the very one the sūrah's later verses will rest upon: human responsibility is real, and its consequences are real.

**The Psychology of the Habit-Formed Heart**

The verse's deepest insight is psychological, and modern experience confirms it at every point. A man tells one lie, and it is hard; he tells a hundred, and it is easy; he tells enough lies, and he reaches the state where he believes them — the *ifk* has turned him, and he can no longer be reached by the truth he has buried. The same holds for every vice: the first betrayal costs a sleepless night, the fiftieth costs nothing. The Qur'an names this process with precision: the heart is *turned* — not in a single dramatic instant but by the accumulation of small, chosen turnings, until the turning has become its nature. This is why the Qur'an so insistently warns against the *first* step of defiance, and why repentance is urged *now*, while the heart is still soft enough to turn back. The verse is not a cold statement of fate; it is a warning issued while there is still time.

**The Warning's Modern Shape**

For the modern reader the verse diagnoses a familiar figure: the person who has heard the truth a hundred times and is further from it after each hearing — not because the truth is weak but because the heart has been progressively turned. The Qur'an's account explains a phenomenon that no amount of "better arguments" alone can fix: resistance to truth is rarely intellectual at its root; it is a settled disposition, built by a thousand small refusals. And the same law works in hope's direction, which the sūrah itself will soon offer: *"So flee unto God"* (51:50) — the one command that can reverse the turning, if the soul will take the first step. The verse leaves the reader with a single urgent question: which way, this very day, am I turning?

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:10

> **Perish those who conjecture!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Curse That Is Also a Diagnosis**

The sūrah's voice sharpens into an imprecation: *qutila al-kharrāṣūn* — "Perish those who conjecture!" The verb *qutila* is the passive of *qatala*, "to kill," and in this construction it is a curse meaning "may they be killed," "may they perish," or, as some render it, "cursed be they" — the strongest form of condemnation in the Qur'anic idiom, used elsewhere of the one who curses his parents, of the slanderer, and of those who deny the truth after seeing it. The word *al-kharrāṣūn* is the active participle of the root *kh-r-ṣ*, whose basic sense is "to guess, to estimate, to conjecture without knowledge," and which also yields *kharrāṣ*, "one who lies by claiming to know what he does not," and *takharrus*, "to invent false accusations." The verse thus curses a specific type of person: not the merely mistaken, but the one who *speaks without certainty as though he had it* — who guesses, opines, and fabricates on the greatest questions of existence, and passes off his guesses as knowledge.

**The Conjecturers: Who They Were and What They Claimed**

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr identify the conjecturers as those who said that human beings would not be resurrected, that there is no reckoning, that the Prophet's promise of the Hereafter was invention. Their defining trait is not that they lacked certainty — every finite creature lacks certainty about the unseen — but that they *replaced* certainty with guesswork and then acted upon the guesswork as if it were sure. The root's flavor matters: *kharrāṣ* is the man who prices the harvest by squinting at the field, the fortune-teller who invents a fate, the accuser who fabricates a charge. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the word also describes those who *yatakharraṣu* — fabricate lies against the Prophet, saying of his message what he never said. The curse falls, then, on the whole habit of mind that prefers an invented certainty to an honest unknown, and a comfortable lie to an uncomfortable truth.

**Why Conjecture Is Cursed: The Sin of Pretending to Know**

At first the severity may surprise: why should guessing be cursed? The answer lies in the Qur'an's account of what conjecture does. Conjecture about the unseen is not a harmless pastime; it is the *substitute* for revelation. Where God has sent certainty, the conjecturer supplies his own, and thereby sets himself up as his own prophet. The Qur'an makes the contrast explicit:

> *"They follow naught but conjecture, and conjecture avails nothing against the truth."* (Qur'an 53:28)

> *"And most of them follow naught but conjecture; truly conjecture avails nothing against the truth. Surely God knows what they do."* (Qur'an 10:36)

The issue is not ignorance, which can be remedied, but *false certainty*, which cannot — the man who is sure he is right has closed the door that the man who knows he is ignorant still has open. The curse is thus not on doubt but on its counterfeit: on the confidence that has no ground, the opinion dressed as fact, the guess that will send a man into the Fire while he protests that he "knew."

**The Conjecturers in the Context of the Sūrah**

Within the sūrah's argument, the verse lands as the climax of a descending diagnosis. Verse 8 named the differing claims; verse 9 traced them to a turned heart; verse 10 now names the turn's product — conjecture, the *kharrāṣ*'s substitute for certainty. And the sūrah has already shown what certainty looks like: the oath by the scatterers and the woven sky, the guarantee that the promise is true. Against that sworn certainty stands the conjecturer's guess. The contrast is complete: God swears; man guesses. The verse's curse is the Qur'an's verdict on the man who, offered a sworn truth, prefers his own hunch.

**The Verse's Psychology: The Danger of the Untested Opinion**

Modern life has not abolished the *kharrāṣ*; it has multiplied him. The age is full of confident pronouncements about God, the soul, the afterlife, and human purpose made by people who have never examined their grounds — the casual atheism and the casual belief, the hot take and the inherited prejudice, all delivered with the certainty of expertise. The verse's warning is that this habit is not neutral; it is *cursed*, because the opinions we hold without examination become the rails on which a life runs, and a life run on guesses ends in ruin. The Qur'an's alternative is not the absence of conviction but conviction earned: belief built on the evidence of the signs, the coherence of the revelation, the testimony of the oaths. The verse asks, in effect, a single question of every confident modern voice: is this *certainty*, or is it *kharrāṣ*?

**From Curse to Portrayal**

The curse is a bridge. Having pronounced it, the sūrah will now turn from the conjecturer's sin to his condition — "those who blunder along in a stupor" (51:11) — and from his condition to his sarcastic question about the Day of Judgment (51:12). The curse is thus not an isolated outburst but the hinge of a section: it names the disease, and the following verses will describe its symptoms. The reader who has followed the argument is left with the sūrah's implicit challenge ringing in his ears: do not be of the *kharrāṣūn* — do not guess about the matters on which your whole existence turns, when the truth has been sworn and offered.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:11

> **Those who blunder along in a stupor,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conjecturer's Condition Described**

The sūrah now portrays the conjecturers it has just cursed: *alladhīna hum fī ghamratin sāhūn* — "those who, in a stupor, are heedless." The word *ghamrah* is a flood, an overwhelming deluge, a depth of water in which a man is submerged and loses his footing; by extension it means any condition in which one is drowned, immersed, and cut off from clear sight — drunkenness, confusion, the press of an overwhelming crowd. Al-Jalālayn glosses the verse: the conjecturers "roam about in stupefying ignorance." The word *sāhūn* is the active participle of *sahā*, "to be heedless, to forget, to be distracted" — the root from which *sahw* (forgetfulness) and the prostration of forgetfulness in prayer take their name. The two words together paint a precise picture: a man submerged in a flood of distraction, heedless of what is coming and of what is required, moving through life as a sleeper moves through a dream.

**"In a Stupor" — the Flood Imagery Unpacked**

The choice of *ghamrah* is deliberate and vivid. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the conjecturers are plunged into a depth of ignorance that covers them as water covers a drowning man — they are not merely mistaken but *submerged*, unable to see the shore, unable to hear the warning, carried this way and that by currents they never chose. The imagery connects to the sūrah's own vocabulary of drowning, for later verses will show Pharaoh and his hosts cast into the sea (51:40) and will describe the deniers as *fī ghamrah* here — the same flood that drowned the tyrant at the sea's surface is, in the moral realm, the flood of heedlessness that drowns the heart. The word also carries the sense of "a great company, a throng" — the heedless are not alone in their stupor but pressed together in a crowd, each reassuring the other, the manyness of their number mistaken for proof.

**Heedlessness: The Root Word and Its Reach**

The second word, *sāhūn*, names the verse's central idea, and the Qur'an builds an entire moral vocabulary upon it. Heedlessness (*ghaflah*, *sahw*) is, in the Qur'an's anthropology, the great human disease — the state of being so absorbed in the present and the visible that the eternal and the unseen drop from view. The Qur'an warns:

> *"They know an outward aspect of the life of this world, but of the Hereafter they are heedless (ghāfilūn)."* (Qur'an 30:7)

> *"The reckoning of mankind draws nigh, while they turn away in heedlessness."* (Qur'an 21:1)

The difference between the two roots matters: *ghaflah* is the absence of attention, while *sahw* is the lapse of it — the mind that was awake and has drifted, the man who means to remember and forgets. The verse chooses *sahw* precisely because the conjecturers are not wholly ignorant; they have heard the message, and they have *drifted* from it, letting the flood of the world carry their attention away. This is the tragedy the verse holds up: they are not the enemies of the truth so much as the forgetters of it.

**Why Heedlessness Is Worse Than Hostility**

A subtle but crucial point emerges here. The Qur'an treats open hostility as a smaller obstacle than heedlessness, because the hostile man is at least *engaged* — he is thinking about the message, even if to fight it — while the heedless man is not thinking at all. A man asleep cannot be argued with; he can only be woken. This is why the Qur'an's own method is so often to *startle* — by oath, by imagery, by the sudden shift of voice — and why the sūrah's later command to the Prophet is *"And remind, for truly the Reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55), and its address to humanity is the abrupt, wake-up call *"So flee unto God"* (51:50). The verse diagnoses a condition for which the only cure is to be startled awake, and the sūrah is itself the startling.

**The Stupor of the Modern World**

The description has lost none of its force with time; if anything, the modern world has perfected the art of the *ghamrah*. The flood in which the contemporary man is submerged is not water but stimulation — the endless scroll, the endless news, the endless commerce of distraction that keeps the mind so full of the immediate that the eternal never gets a hearing. The word *sāhūn* names the modern condition with uncomfortable precision: not wickedness but *forgetfulness*, not defiance but *drift*. And the verse's implicit warning is that the flood is fatal precisely because it feels harmless — no one drowns in a stupor by noticing it; one drowns by not noticing it at all. The sūrah's answer, standing right here in the verse's wake, is the same in every age: lift your head out of the flood and look at what is coming.

**The Verse as a Mirror**

Like verse 8, this verse invites self-examination, and its grammar does the work: the heedless are described in the third person, but every reader knows the description could be his own. The question the verse leaves behind is not "are there heedless people?" but "am I one of them?" — a question to be answered not by protest but by the test the sūrah itself provides: does the coming Day, sworn and certain, actually shape how I spend this day? The next verse will show the heedless in their characteristic motion, asking the question that exposes them — *"When is the Day of Judgment?"* — a question asked not to learn but to mock.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:12

> **asking, “When is the Day of Judgment?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Is Not a Question**

The heedless are now quoted in their own voice: *yasʾalūna ayyāna yawmu al-dīn* — "they ask, 'When is the Day of Judgment?'" The word *ayyāna* is an interrogative of time — "when?" — and *yawm al-dīn* is the Day of Recompense, the same phrase with which Sūrah al-Fātiḥah names the Day of Judgment (1:4). The sūrah is quoting the deniers' refrain, and the commentators are unanimous about its tone: this is not a question asked in the spirit of inquiry, from a mind that genuinely wants to know, but a question asked in the spirit of derision — the skeptic's shrug, the mocker's taunt, the demand for a date as if the failure to provide one disproved the thing promised. Ibn Kathīr states it plainly: the disbelievers do not ask in order to learn but in mockery and denial, and the Qur'an records the question precisely to expose it.

**The Recurring Taunt Across the Qur'an**

The sūrah itself notes, in the source commentary, that this question and its variants run through the Qur'an like a refrain, and each occurrence is a window into the psychology of denial. The same taunt appears in Sūrah Yūnus:

> *"And they say, 'When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?'"* (Qur'an 10:48)

In Sūrah Sabaʾ:

> *"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?' Say: yours is the tryst of a Day which you cannot postpone by an hour nor bring forward."* (Qur'an 34:29–30)

In Sūrah al-Mulk:

> *"And they say, 'When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?' Say: knowledge is with God alone, and I am but a clear warner."* (Qur'an 67:25–26)

And in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah:

> *"He asks, 'When is the Day of Resurrection?'"* (Qur'an 75:6)

The repetition is itself the argument: the taunt was the constant companion of every prophet, in every age, and the constancy of the taunt is the constancy of the disease — the demand that the unseen submit to the timetable of the scoffer.

**Why the Date Is Hidden, and Why the Demand Is Dishonest**

The Qur'an's answer to the taunt is consistent and profound. The knowledge of the Hour's timing is withheld — deliberately. The revelation instructs the Prophet:

> *"They ask thee about the Hour, when it will come to pass. Say: knowledge thereof is with my Lord alone; none shall manifest it at its time but He."* (Qur'an 7:187)

The withholding is not a defect in the message but a mercy and a discipline. A Day whose date were published would be exploited: the sinner would sin freely and repent at the last hour; the saint would be robbed of the daily urgency that keeps his life straight. The hidden hour keeps every hour weighty — a truth the modern world, which lives by deadlines and calendars, should understand immediately: men work hardest not when the deadline is far but when it is unknown and could be near. And the demand for a date is dishonest on its face, because the scoffer who asks "when?" would not believe if he were told; the date is not what he lacks, but the will to believe. The Qur'an exposes this by never once obliging the demand — the answer is always "knowledge is with God," never a date, because the question was never really about time.

**The Irony the Sūrah Is About to Deliver**

The question is placed here with deliberate dramatic irony, because the very next verse answers it in a way the askers never intended: the Day of Judgment they mockingly demand is the day *"when they are tried upon the Fire"* (51:13), and the taste of that trial will be told them in the very words of their own mockery — *"This is what you sought to hasten"* (51:14). The sūrah's structure is a rhetorical trap: the scoffer asks for the Day to be hastened, and the answer is that the Day, when it comes, will not be a date on a calendar but a reality on the skin, and that the hastening they demanded will be the first thing they taste. The question is thus not answered but *turned* — flung back upon the asker with the weight of his own words.

**The Modern Scoffer and the Same Question**

The taunt has not disappeared; it has only changed costume. The modern voice asks, "When?" in its own idiom — demanding empirical proof of the afterlife, a timestamp for the unseen, as if the limits of the laboratory were the limits of reality. The verse's lesson is that the demand itself misdiagnoses the situation: the issue was never the calendar but the heart. The one who genuinely wants to know whether the Day is real is invited to examine the evidence the sūrah has already laid down — the signs in the earth and sky and soul, the oaths, the histories of ruined nations — and to see that the question "when?" is a question about time, while the answer that matters is about truth. The scoffer's "when?" is met, in the end, not with a date but with a decision: *"So flee unto God"* (51:50).

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:13

> **A day when they are tried upon the Fire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer the Scoffers Never Wanted**

The verse supplies the answer to the question just quoted, and the answer is a reversal: *yawma hum ʿalā al-nāri yuftanūn* — "a day when they are tried upon the Fire." The scoffers asked "when is the Day of Judgment?" and the revelation answers not with a date but with a scene — the day they will be *upon the Fire*, *tried* by it. The grammar sharpens the reversal: the word *yawma* ("a day") is in the accusative of time, answering the interrogative "when?" directly, as if the reply to their taunt were simply: "on the day when you are tried upon the Fire." The question and its answer stand in the same breath, and the mockery is answered by the very reality it mocked.

**The Root of "Tried": The Furnace of the Goldsmith**

The key word is *yuftanūn*, the passive of the verb *fatana*, whose root *f-t-n* means "to test, to try, to prove," and which in its most concrete sense describes the *smelting of gold* — the placing of ore in the fire to separate the pure metal from the dross. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both draw out this etymology: the disbelievers will be "tried upon the Fire" as gold is tried in the furnace, the fire exposing and burning them. The root is the same from which *fitnah* derives — trial, temptation, persecution — the word the Qur'an uses for the tests of this world. The verse thus sets up a devastating parallel: in this world, human beings are *tried* by God's tests (the *fitnah* of wealth, of children, of persecution, of ease and hardship); on that Day, those who failed the trial will be *tried upon the Fire* — the same word, but now the trial is not a test that can be passed but a punishment that has begun. The trial of this world was the chance; the trial of the Fire is the consequence.

**Why "Upon the Fire" and Not "In the Fire"**

The prepositional phrase *ʿalā al-nāri* — "upon the Fire" — deserves notice, for it is more precise than "in the Fire." The commentators explain that the disbelievers will be placed *upon* the Fire — over it, on its surface, exposed to its heat — so that the burning is a *trial* visited upon them, a direct, felt contact with the very thing they denied. Some readings connect the phrase to the next verse's command, *"Taste your trial"* (51:14), where the tasting makes explicit what "upon the Fire" implies: the punishment is not remote or symbolic but a direct sensory reality, as immediate as the touch of a flame. The image is deliberately physical because the denial it answers was physical — they denied a bodily resurrection, and the answer is a bodily, tangible experience of the Fire's trial.

**The Irony of the Word "Trial" Turned Against the Scoffer**

The deepest irony of the verse is lexical. The scoffers used their worldly *fitnah* — their persecution and mockery of the believers, their trials against the Prophet — as a weapon; the word *fitnah* in the Qur'an describes both the persecution the believers suffered and the test of wealth and power that seduces the heedless. Now the word is turned back upon them: the very root of the trial they inflicted and the temptations they enjoyed becomes the name of their punishment. They who tried others will be tried upon the Fire; they who tempted others from the truth will taste the temptation's end. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss — that *yuftanūn* means "punished and burned in the Fire" — captures the word's full arc: the test has ended, and only its burning remains.

**The Justice of the Scene**

Beneath the warning's severity runs the sūrah's constant theme of justice. The verse does not describe an arbitrary torment but a *correspondence*: the Fire is what the deniers themselves demanded when they asked for the Day to be hastened; it is the logical terminus of a life spent in heedlessness; it is the trial that answers the trial. The Qur'an's doctrine of requital is always proportionate — *"Whosoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whosoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:7–8) — and here the proportion is laid bare: they mocked the unseen Day, and the unseen Day becomes their seen, felt, unanswerable reality. The verse is not a threat for its own sake but the Qur'an's way of making the denier confront, in advance, the logic of what he has chosen.

**For the Modern Reader: The Trial That Begins Now**

The verse also carries a warning that reaches the present tense. The trial upon the Fire is, in the Qur'an's account, the continuation and completion of a trial already underway — and the trial already underway is this very life, in which every soul is being tested right now by what it is given and what it is denied. The modern reader who shrugs at the Hereafter is not escaping a trial but failing one. The verse's function is to make the future vivid enough to alter the present — which is, after all, the purpose of every Qur'anic warning. The next verse will complete the reversal by handing the sentence to the deniers in the imperative: *"Taste your trial!"* — the very thing they hastened, now served to them.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:14

> **Taste your trial! This is what you sought to hasten!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imperative of Retribution**

The scene reaches its climax in a command addressed to the deniers: *dhūqū fitnatakum* — "Taste your trial!" — followed by the explanation that gives the command its sting: *hādhā alladhī kuntum bihi tastaʿjilūn* — "this is what you sought to hasten." The imperative *dhūqū* — "taste!" — is the Qur'an's characteristic verb for punishment's arrival, because tasting is the most intimate of the senses: one can look at a thing from a distance, but one cannot taste it without taking it in. The same command resounds through the Qur'an's accounts of judgment:

> *"Taste the punishment for what you used to earn."* (Qur'an 3:106; 8:50; 22:22)

> *"Taste the burning!"* (Qur'an 3:181; 8:50)

The command is addressed to those who, in this world, tasted only what they desired and refused to taste what they owed. Now the tongue that denied is made the first instrument of the truth it denied.

**"Your Trial": The Punishment They Called a Lie**

The phrase *fitnatakum* — "your trial" — completes the wordplay begun in verse 13. The root *f-t-n*, which described the smelting of gold and the tests of this world, is here turned into a possession: *your* trial — the trial that belongs to them, that they earned, that is theirs alone. The Qur'an's doctrine of requital is written into the possessive pronoun: punishment is not an external thing imposed from nowhere but the harvest of one's own deeds, the "trial" a soul prepared for itself. The same possessive logic runs through the Qur'an's accounts of the Fire's inhabitants, who are told:

> *"This is what your hands sent ahead."* (Qur'an 22:10)

The possessive pronoun is the Qur'an's way of insisting that the Fire's reality is the soul's own work returned to it, no more alien to the sinner than the fruit is alien to the tree.

**"What You Sought to Hasten": The Taunt Thrown Back**

The verse's second half is the sūrah's masterstroke of reversal. The deniers spent their lives asking, mockingly, *"When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"* — and the Qur'an records their exact demand:

> *"And they say, 'When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?' Say: That which you seek to hasten is not within my power. Judgment belongs to God alone; He relates the Truth, and He is the best of deciders."* (Qur'an 6:57)

Now the demand is answered in the imperative: *this* — the very thing before you — *is what you sought to hasten*. The taunt "hurry it up, then!" is repaid with the thing hurried. The source commentary draws the full contrast with the believers' attitude, quoting Sūrah al-Shūrā:

> *"Those who believe not in it seek to hasten it, and those who believe are wary of it and know that it is the truth."* (Qur'an 42:18)

The deniers raced toward the Day as a jest; the believers trembled before it as a certainty. Each receives what his attitude prepared: the mocker receives the thing mocked, and the wary receives safety from the thing feared.

**The Psychology of Hastening Punishment**

There is a profound psychological truth in the Qur'an's repeated portrait of the denier "seeking to hasten" the punishment. The man who does not believe in a consequence treats its announcement as a bluff, and the longer the consequence delays, the more confirmed he feels in his unbelief — which is why the denier grows bolder with every passing day of impunity. The Qur'an names this dynamic elsewhere:

> *"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?' Say: perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close behind you."* (Qur'an 27:72)

And it warns that the delay is not neglect but respite, a mercy extended and, when refused, converted into a heavier reckoning. The verse's irony is the irony of every delayed consequence: the debtor who laughs at the creditor's patience learns that patience is not cancellation. The deniers "sought to hasten" the Day precisely because they thought it would never come; the verse reveals that the hastening, when it comes, will be the one prayer of theirs that was ever answered.

**The Hereafter's Realism and the Sūrah's Method**

This verse also reveals the Qur'an's method of making the unseen real: it does not describe the Fire in abstract adjectives but in *address* — in the direct, second-person imperative that places the reader inside the scene. "Taste your trial!" is not reportage about others; it is speech aimed at the hearer, and its power lies in that aim. The Makkan sūrahs, which faced the most hardened denial, are built of such direct addresses, and al-Dhāriyāt is among the sharpest: the scoffer is quoted, then answered, then commanded. The reader who has followed the sūrah to this point has been made to hear, in advance, the sentence that the heedless will hear in truth. The verse is thus not a prediction but a rehearsal — a mercy that lets the living hear the sentence before it is final, while the command *"So flee unto God"* (51:50) still stands open as the way of escape.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:15

> **Truly the reverent shall be amidst gardens and springs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turning of the Scene**

The sūrah pivots on a single word: from the Fire of the deniers to the Garden of the reverent, from *"Taste your trial!"* to *inna al-muttaqīna fī jannātin wa-ʿuyūn* — "truly the reverent shall be amidst gardens and springs." The conjunction is the strongest available — *inna*, "truly" — and the contrast is absolute: the same verse-pair that condemned the conjecturers now exalts the reverent, and the two destinies are set side by side so that the hearer must choose between them. The word *al-muttaqīn* — "the reverent, the God-wary" — is the Qur'an's master-term for the whole of the religious life, and its placement here, immediately after the portrait of the heedless, makes the contrast the verse's whole point: the heedless are drowned in their stupor, the reverent dwell in gardens and springs.

**Who Are the Muttaqūn?**

The word *muttaqīn* is the active participle of *ittaqā*, from the root *w-q-y*, "to guard, to protect," and it names the one who has placed a *wiqāyah* — a shield, a protection — between himself and what he fears. The God-wary is thus, in the very letters of his name, the one who protects himself from God's displeasure by obeying God's command, who walks through life as one walks through a place of danger with his shield raised. The Prophet ﷺ was asked what most admits people to Paradise, and answered with the two qualities that define the *muttaqī*:

> *"Taqwā of God and good character."* (al-Tirmidhī 2004; Ibn Mājah 4246; graded ḥasan)

And the Qur'an itself defines the reverent in the verse that stands at the head of the Book's longest sūrah, as those who *"believe in the Unseen, and perform the prayer, and spend from what We have provided them"* (2:3). The *muttaqūn* are not a special class of saints but the ordinary believers whose faith has taken the form of caution, obedience, and the constant remembrance of the Day they will meet.

**"Amidst Gardens and Springs": The Geography of Safety**

The reward is described first as a *place*: *fī jannātin wa-ʿuyūn* — "amidst gardens and springs." The word *jannah* is the garden, the hidden orchard whose branches shade and whose soil is watered; and *ʿuyūn* is the plural of *ʿayn*, the spring, the living source of water. The pairing is deliberate, for in the waterless deserts of Arabia no image spoke more directly of safety, rest, and life than the walled garden with its running spring — the oasis that ends the journey's thirst and the shade that ends the sun's fury. The Qur'an uses the pairing repeatedly for Paradise, as in the verse the source commentary cross-references:

> *"Truly the reverent shall be amidst gardens and springs — 'Enter them in peace, secure.'"* (Qur'an 15:45–46)

And again:

> *"Truly the reverent shall be in a station secure, amidst gardens and springs."* (Qur'an 44:51–52)

The spring is the image of unending, effortless provision — water that does not need to be carried or hoarded but flows of its own accord — and the garden is the image of shelter and fruit. The two together are the Qur'an's picture of a life in which every need is met without toil, the opposite of the Fire's trial.

**The Reward as Correspondence**

The sūrah's doctrine of correspondence governs this verse as it governed the last. The deniers were "in a stupor" (*ghamrah*) — a drowning flood — and their end is the trial of the Fire; the reverent were heedful — awake through the night, as the next verses will show — and their end is the garden's living spring. The heedless *forgot* the Day, and they are tried upon the Fire; the reverent *remembered* the Day, and they are brought to the springs. The correspondence is exact, and it makes the reward not an arbitrary gift but a fitting return. The Qur'an states the principle of correspondence in its simplest form:

> *"Is the recompense of goodness anything but goodness?"* (Qur'an 55:60)

The gardens and springs are the recompense of the heedfulness, and the heedfulness is described — in the verses that follow — as nights of little sleep and dawns of seeking forgiveness and wealth in which the poor had a known share.

**The Psychology of the Promised Garden**

For the believer, this verse is a source of a specific kind of strength. The life of *taqwā* is, in the world's eyes, a life of restraint — restraining the hand from the forbidden, the eye from the haram, the tongue from the lie, the night's sleep for the night's prayer. Such a life needs a horizon, and the verse supplies it: the restraint is not an end but a path, and the path ends in a place where restraint is no longer needed because every desire is lawful and every spring flows. The believer who feels the cost of taqwā in this world is being told, with the sūrah's full emphasis, that the cost is temporary and the garden is not. This is the Qur'an's consistent method — never to command restraint without holding up its destination — and the method is the mercy: the garden is described in advance so that the road to it can be walked.

**A Note on the Verse's Address**

One further subtlety: the verse is in the third person — "the reverent shall be" — not yet the second person of direct address. The sūrah has been speaking *to* the deniers; now it speaks *about* the reverent, and the effect is to widen the frame: the hearer is given two portraits, side by side, and left to see which is his own. The sūrah's rhetoric is a mirror held up twice — the heedless in their stupor, the reverent in their gardens — and the hearer stands between the two, already choosing. The next verses will fill in the portrait of the reverent with the concrete habits that earned the garden: their nights, their dawns, and their wealth.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:16

> **partaking of that which their Lord has given them. Truly they were virtuous aforetime.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verbs of Receiving and Giving**

The verse completes the portrait of the reverent in Paradise: *ākhidhīna mā ātāhum rabbuhum* — "taking what their Lord has given them" — followed by the explanation that anchors the whole scene: *innahum kānū qabla dhālika muḥsinīn* — "truly they were, before that, doers of good." The first word, *ākhidhīn*, is an active participle of *akhadha*, "to take," and it pictures the reverent in the act of receiving — not waiting, not asking, but *taking* what is handed to them, because in the Garden the gifts are so abundant and so certain that the hand simply reaches and takes. And the giver is named: *rabbuhum* — "their Lord" — the possessive pronoun carrying the whole tenderness of the scene; it is not an anonymous paradise but the hospitality of a Lord who raised them, guided them, and now serves them from His own hand.

**"Before That": The Sequence That Makes Sense of the Reward**

The phrase *qabla dhālika* — "before that" — is the hinge of the verse's logic. The scene in the Garden is not the beginning of the reverent's story but its harvest; "before that" — before the gardens and springs — there was a life, and that life is named in a single word: *muḥsinīn*, doers of good. The root *ḥ-s-n* means "to do well, to make beautiful," and *iḥsān* is the highest rank of action — the doing of the good, and the doing of it beautifully. The Prophet ﷺ defined it in the most famous definition in the tradition, when Gabriel asked him, "What is *iḥsān*?" and he answered:

> *"It is that you worship God as though you see Him; for if you do not see Him, truly He sees you."* (Muslim 8; al-Bukhārī 50)

The *muḥsin* is thus not merely the one who does good deeds but the one who does them *in the presence of God* — who performs every act as if watched by the Beloved, and therefore performs it well. The verse says: the ones who take from their Lord's hand in the Garden are the ones who lived as though they saw Him in the world.

**The Wordplay That Joins the Two Halves**

The commentators notice a subtle symmetry in the verse's vocabulary. The reverent are *ākhidhīn* — "taking" — what their Lord *ātā* — "gave." In the world, they were the *givers*: they gave of their wealth (as the next verses will detail), they gave their nights to prayer, they gave their dawns to seeking forgiveness. Now the roles are exchanged: those who gave in the world are given to in the Hereafter, and their taking is the counterpart of their giving. The Qur'an states this exchange as a universal law:

> *"And whatever good you send ahead for your souls, you will find it with God — better and greater in reward."* (Qur'an 73:20)

The Garden's gifts are not a fresh creation but the return of what was sent ahead, grown and multiplied. The verse's two halves are thus not a scene and a footnote but a cause and its effect: because they were *muḥsinīn*, therefore they take.

**"They Were Virtuous": The Grammar of a Settled Character**

The verb form *kānū muḥsinīn* — literally "they were doers of good" — uses the verb *kāna* ("to be") with the participle, a construction that in Arabic describes not a passing action but a settled, habitual state. They did not do good once or occasionally; they *were* doers of good — goodness was their character, not their occasional gesture. This is the Qur'an's consistent emphasis: the Hereafter rewards what a person *was*, the settled disposition of the heart expressed in habitual action, not the isolated deed performed for show or under pressure. The same construction governs the next verses — "they slept but little," "they sought forgiveness" — all in the imperfect and habitual forms, painting a portrait of lives whose goodness was as constant as their breath.

**The Source Commentary's Point: The Garden's Delights and Their Price**

The source commentary captures the verse in a single line: the reverent will partake of all the delights of the Garden *because they had been virtuous in this world*. The connection is causal and complete — nothing in the Garden is arbitrary, nothing is unearned, and nothing earned is lost. This is the Qur'an's answer to the human suspicion that the righteous life is a bad bargain: the bargain is, in fact, the only sound one ever struck, for it trades the temporary for the permanent at a rate no market can match. The Prophet ﷺ pressed the same point in the starkest terms:

> *"The world is the believer's prison and the disbeliever's paradise; and the Hereafter is the believer's paradise and the disbeliever's prison."* (Muslim 2956, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verse is that bargain rendered as a scene: the prison of restraint exchanged for the garden of taking, the life of giving exchanged for the eternity of receiving.

**For the Modern Reader: The Dignity of the Good Life**

The verse also speaks to a modern anxiety — the fear that a life of duty and self-restraint is a life wasted. Against that fear the verse sets a picture of the good life's true end: not loss but abundance, not deprivation but *taking* from the Lord's hand. And it defines the good life with a single, portable word — *iḥsān*, doing the beautiful, doing it as though seen. The modern reader is not asked to become a monk but a *muḥsin*: to do whatever he does — his work, his worship, his charity, his speech — as though God were watching, because He is. The next verses will specify the three habits that marked the reverent "aforetime," and the first of them is the habit that most strikes the modern reader: their nights were almost sleepless, spent standing before their Lord.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:17

> **Little of the night did they slumber,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Habit of the Reverent: The Night Vigil**

The sūrah now specifies, in three short verses, exactly what the *muḥsinīn* did "aforetime" — and the first habit is the night prayer: *kānū qalīlan mina al-layli mā yahjaʿūn* — "little of the night did they slumber." The verb *yahjaʿūn* is from *hajaʿa*, "to sleep at night," and the construction is emphatic: "of the night, little was it that they slept" — meaning, as al-Ṭabarī explains, "much of the night did they pray." The verse is an understatement that says everything: their nights were not given to sleep but to standing, recitation, and remembrance, and the sleep they did take was the small remainder, not the main portion. This is the first mark of the reverent — not their wealth, not their knowledge, but their nights — and the sūrah puts it first because the night vigil is the deed that no one sees and everyone needs.

**What the Night Prayer Was and When It Was Performed**

Al-Ṭabarī records the early interpretations of "little of the night." Some of the first commentators understood it as the interval between the sunset prayer (*maghrib*) and the night prayer (*ʿishāʾ*) — a period the earliest believers kept alive with prayer and remembrance, resisting the pull of sleep the moment darkness fell. Others read it more broadly: the reverent slept little at any hour of the night, rising in its depths for the *tahajjud*, the night vigil. Both readings converge on the same picture: a community whose relationship to the night was not the world's — for whom the night was not a time of unconsciousness but of wakefulness before God. The night prayer is so central to the Qur'an's portrait of the believers that it recurs again and again, and the sūrah's source commentary cross-references the two most important passages: the command to the Prophet, *"And keep vigil with it during the night, as a supererogatory act for thee"* (17:79), and the charge that opens Sūrah al-Muzzammil, *"O thou enwrapped one! Stand in the night, save a little"* (73:1–2).

**Why the Night, and Why in Secret?**

The Qur'an's insistence on the *night* — as opposed to the day — for this voluntary worship carries a precise spiritual logic. The night is when the world is quiet, when the eyes of men are closed, when the pretense of the day is laid down; it is the hour in which a soul is alone with its Lord with no audience but Him. Prayer in the night is therefore the prayer of sincerity, the deed done when no one is watching, and it is for this reason that the tradition treasures it above almost all voluntary acts. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Hold fast to the night prayer, for it is the practice of the righteous before you, a means of drawing near to your Lord, an expiation for sins, and a barrier against transgression."* (al-Tirmidhī 3549; graded ḥasan)

The night vigil is the believer's private audience, and the Qur'an makes it the first mark of those who earned the Garden precisely because it is the deed least capable of being counterfeited: a man may pray publicly for reputation, but no one rises in the cold hours before dawn for anyone but God.

**The Prophet's Own Nights**

The source commentary notes that the Prophet ﷺ was himself the living embodiment of this verse: he slept little, rising for the supererogatory prayers before dawn, and he encouraged others to the same. The tradition preserves the testimony of his household. ʿĀʾishah, his wife, was asked about his night prayer and answered:

> *"Do not ask about its beauty or its length. He would pray at night until his feet swelled."*

And when she asked him why he stood so long when God had forgiven him all his sins, he answered:

> *"Shall I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 1130; Muslim 2820)

The night vigil, in the Prophet's own practice, was not a penance but a *gratitude* — the natural response of a heart that has been given everything to the Giver. This is the verse's deepest point: the reverent did not lose sleep out of fear alone but out of love, and the loss of sleep was, to them, no loss at all.

**The Night Prayer and the Structure of the Believer's Day**

There is also a practical wisdom in the verse that modern readers often miss. The night prayer orders the whole day: the one who rises before dawn has already set his heart before the world can set it for him, and the day that begins in prostration is a day that has chosen its center before its distractions begin. The modern world runs on a different schedule — late nights, exhausted mornings, a life in which sleep is the last refuge of the overworked — and the verse offers a counter-pattern: a life in which the night is given, in part, to the One who gave the day. The commentators note that the night prayer was never meant to destroy health — the sūrah itself says "little of the night did they slumber," not "they never slept" — but to carve from the night a portion for the soul, and that portion, small as it may be, is the seed from which the garden of the verse's first half grew.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:18

> **and ere dawn would seek forgiveness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Habit: Seeking Forgiveness at the Last Hour of Night**

The portrait continues with the verse's most tender detail: *wa bi'l-asḥāri hum yastaghfirūn* — "and in the hours before dawn they would seek forgiveness." The word *asḥār* is the plural of *saḥar*, the last portion of the night, the still darkness just before the first light of morning — the hour the Qur'an elsewhere names among the attributes of the believers:

> *"Those who are patient, and truthful, and devout, and who spend, and who seek forgiveness in the hours before dawn (bi'l-asḥār)."* (Qur'an 3:17)

The verb *yastaghfirūn* — "they seek forgiveness" — is the tenth form of *gh-f-r*, and it names not a single act but a continuous practice: the reverent did not merely ask pardon once but lived in the habit of *istighfār*, the constant turning back to God with the request that He cover, erase, and overlook. The verse joins the night vigil of the previous line with its natural fruit: those who stand in the night end their vigil, at its deepest hour, with the prayer for pardon.

**Why Forgiveness, and Why at This Hour?**

The commentators pause over the pairing, because it teaches something essential about the reverent's self-understanding. The same people whom the sūrah has called *muḥsinīn* — doers of good — are described as *seeking forgiveness*: their goodness did not make them self-satisfied but self-accusing. This is the Qur'an's consistent portrait of the true believer, who never sees his own worship as a completed achievement but always as a shortfall in need of pardon. The Prophet ﷺ, whose sins were forgiven, is himself described as seeking forgiveness more than seventy times a day, and he taught:

> *"By God, I seek forgiveness from God and turn to Him in repentance more than seventy times a day."* (al-Bukhārī 6307, from Abū Hurayrah)

The seeking of forgiveness is thus not the act of the fallen only but the act of the beloved — the constant, humble acknowledgment that whatever one has done falls short of what the Lord deserves. And the hour is chosen deliberately: the last third of the night, when the world is asleep and the heart is at its most sincere, is the hour in which the prayer for pardon rises most purely.

**The Descent of the Lord in the Last Third of the Night**

The source commentary adjoins to this verse the most famous ḥadīth in the tradition concerning the hour before dawn, transmitted by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah, in which the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Our Lord, blessed and exalted be He, descends every night to the heaven of this world when the last third of the night remains, and says: 'Who calls upon Me, that I may answer him? Who asks of Me, that I may give him? Who seeks My forgiveness, that I may forgive him?'"* (al-Bukhārī 1145; Muslim 758)

The tradition, which the scholars of ʿaqīdah discuss at length, teaches that the hour before dawn is the appointed time of divine nearness, the window in which the asking soul is met with the answer, the seeking soul with the gift, and the repenting soul with the pardon. The reverent of this verse had learned the schedule of their Lord's mercy and arranged their nights around it: they slept little, and when the deepest hour came, they were awake, asking. The night vigil and the seeking of forgiveness are thus one single act seen from two sides — the standing and the asking — and the sūrah places them together as the complete anatomy of the believer's night.

**The Meaning of Istighfār: More Than an Apology**

The word *istighfār* deserves its full weight, for it is often flattened in translation into a mere "saying sorry." The root *gh-f-r* means "to cover" — the *mighfar* is the helmet that covers the head, the *ghufrah* the covering of a thing — and to seek God's forgiveness is to ask that He *cover* one's sins: to veil them, to erase their trace, to shield the sinner from their consequence. The seeking of forgiveness is therefore an act of hope and confidence, not of despair: the one who asks for pardon believes that the Pardoner exists and pardons. The Qur'an joins the command to seek forgiveness with the promise of its acceptance:

> *"And seek forgiveness of your Lord, and turn unto Him in repentance; He will give you goodly enjoyment for a term appointed."* (Qur'an 11:3)

And the Prophet ﷺ taught the believer to say, in the master-prayer of forgiveness:

> *"O God, You are my Lord, there is no god but You. You created me and I am Your servant, and I uphold Your covenant and Your promise as best I can. I seek refuge in You from the evil of what I have done; I acknowledge Your favor upon me, and I acknowledge my sin, so forgive me, for none forgives sins but You."* (al-Bukhārī 6306)

The verse's *yastaghfirūn* is this entire attitude compressed into one word.

**For the Modern Reader: The Lost Hour**

The hour before dawn is, for the modern world, the hour of deepest sleep or of anxious waking — the hour of the alarm clock and the last dream. The verse offers it back as what it was meant to be: the one hour in the day when the soul can stand before its Lord with nothing between them. The modern reader need not abandon his schedule to receive the verse's gift; the *saḥar* is a portion, not a lifetime, and the seeking of forgiveness in it is a matter of minutes. What the verse asks is a reordering of the heart's priorities — that the day be framed, at its darkest edge, by the prayer for pardon, so that whatever the day then brings, the soul has already placed its center where it belongs. The next verse completes the portrait with the third habit, the one that reaches outward from the night's solitude into the daylight world of others: the wealth in which the poor had a known share.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:19

> **And in their wealth was a due for the beggar and the deprived.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Habit: The Wealth That Carried a Recognized Right**

The portrait of the reverent is completed with the habit that reaches outward into society: *wa fī amwālihim ḥaqqun li'l-sāʾili wa'l-maḥrūm* — "and in their wealth was a due for the beggar and the deprived." The word *ḥaqq* is the key: it means a *right*, a *due*, a thing owed — not a favor, not a gift, not a charity performed from the giver's magnanimity, but an obligation recognized in the very structure of the wealth. The Qur'an's economics begins precisely here, in the redefinition of ownership: the reverent understood their wealth as carrying, within it, a share that belonged to others, and their giving was not generosity but *the payment of a debt*. The verse is the daylight counterpart of the previous two — the night prayer turned the heart to God, and the due of the wealth turned the hand to the neighbor.

**The Beggar and the Deprived: Two Faces of Need**

The verse names two recipients with precision. The *sāʾil* is the *beggar* — the one who asks, who stretches out his hand and makes his need known. The *maḥrūm* is the *deprived* — the one who is in need but does not ask, whether from dignity, from inability, or from silence; the root *ḥ-r-m* carries the sense of being *forbidden* or *cut off* from provision, and the *maḥrūm* is the person whom fortune has passed by and who will not or cannot announce it. Al-Jalālayn and al-Ṭabarī gloss the pair exactly so: the beggar asks, the deprived does not. And al-Rāzī widens the second term to include not only human beings but *all living creatures* unable to voice their needs — the stray animal, the bird in winter, the creature whose hunger has no speech. The verse thus trains a double attention: to the visible need that announces itself, and to the hidden need that must be sought out.

**The Due: Obligatory Alms or Something Wider?**

The commentators discuss whether the *ḥaqq* here is the obligatory zakāh or a broader due, and al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī lean toward the obligatory alms, while others allow that it includes both the required and the voluntary. The cross-reference the source commentary supplies settles the matter's spirit by showing the verse's companion:

> *"And those in whose wealth is a known right (ḥaqqun maʿlūm) for the beggar and the deprived."* (Qur'an 70:24–25)

There the right is *maʿlūm* — "known," fixed, apportioned — the language of zakāh's fixed share. But the sūrah's own word, *ḥaqq* without the qualifier, is deliberately wider, and the tradition has read it as the principle beneath every form of giving: the poor have a *right* in the wealth of the rich, and no society is sound until that right is honored as a debt. For the list of those who hold such a right, the source commentary points to Sūrah al-Baqarah's enumeration:

> *"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the East and the West; but righteous is the one who believes in God and the Last Day, and the angels, and the Book, and the prophets, and gives wealth, for love of Him, to kinsfolk, and orphans, and the needy, and the wayfarer, and the beggars, and for the freeing of captives..."* (Qur'an 2:177)

The principle is one: the wealth of the believer is a trust in which others hold shares, and the believer's task is to identify those shares and deliver them.

**The Logic of the "Right": Ownership as Trusteeship**

The verse's choice of the word *ḥaqq* over *ṣadaqah* (charity) is a deliberate piece of moral philosophy. Charity is the language of the giver's virtue; a *right* is the language of the recipient's entitlement. By calling the poor's share a *ḥaqq*, the Qur'an reframes the entire transaction: the rich man who gives is not being generous to the poor; he is discharging an obligation that inheres in the wealth itself, because the wealth was never wholly his. The Qur'an states the principle at its root:

> *"And spend of that whereof He has made you trustees (khulafāʾ)."* (Qur'an 57:7)

The human owner is a *khalīfah*, a steward, and the steward's first duty is to distribute the estate according to the Owner's instructions, which include the share of the beggar and the deprived. This is why the verse sits naturally beside the night prayer: both are acts of *recognizing* — the prayer recognizes the Lord of the wealth, and the due recognizes the poor's share in it, and neither is a favor but both are the honest response to how things actually stand.

**The Modern Shape of the Deprived**

The verse's second term, the *maḥrūm*, has never been more relevant. The modern world is full of the deprived who do not beg: the elderly person too proud to ask, the working poor whose wages do not reach the month's end, the neighbor whose need is hidden behind a kept-up door, the refugee without a language in which to ask. The verse trains the believer to look for them — to understand that the *right* in his wealth extends to needs that never announce themselves, and that the highest giving is the giving that seeks out the silent. The Prophet ﷺ pressed this exact point:

> *"The one who strives on behalf of the widow and the poor is like the one who strives in the path of God"* — and the narrator said, "I think he said: like the one who stands in prayer at night without tiring, and like the one who fasts without breaking." (al-Bukhārī 6006; Muslim 2982, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verse's three habits — the night vigil, the pre-dawn forgiveness, and the due of the wealth — are thus one life viewed from three angles: the soul's private worship, the heart's humility, and the hand's justice. The sūrah will now turn from this portrait of the reverent to the evidence that the whole creation lays before every eye, believer and denier alike: the signs on the earth and within the soul.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:20

> **Upon the earth are signs for those possessing certainty,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn to the Evidence**

The sūrah now returns to its opening method — the appeal to the signs in creation — but with a new precision: *wa fī al-arḍi āyātun li'l-mūqinīn* — "and upon the earth are signs for those possessing certainty." The word *āyāt* is the plural of *āyah*, the Qur'an's master-word for "sign," "verse," "proof" — the same word it uses for its own sentences and for the phenomena of the world, so that the book of revelation and the book of creation share a single vocabulary. And the verse names its audience with the sūrah's characteristic exactness: the signs are *for the mūqinīn* — those possessing *yaqīn*, certainty — the settled, tested conviction that the opening oaths were designed to produce. The verse thus states the sūrah's method in a single line: the earth is not mute; it is covered in signs, and the only question is whether the eye that looks at it is the eye of certainty or the eye of heedlessness.

**Why the Signs Are "Upon the Earth"**

The preposition *fī al-arḍ* — "in" or "upon the earth" — is chosen because the earth is the one text that every human being, believer and denier, farmer and philosopher, reads every day. The Qur'an does not begin its argument in the heavens alone but in the soil underfoot, because the soil is the evidence that cannot be escaped. The sūrah's own opening oaths were drawn from the earth's processes — the winds that scatter, the clouds that bear, the rain that revives — and now the argument is made explicit: these processes are *signs*. The Qur'an enumerates them across its pages:

> *"And in the earth are signs for those possessing certainty — and in your own selves; will you not then see?"* (Qur'an 51:20–21)

> *"And among His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your tongues and colors. Truly in that are signs for those who know."* (Qur'an 30:22)

The earth is a text written in water, wind, seed, and soil, and its grammar is the grammar of resurrection: the dead seed reviving, the barren land greening, the dry riverbed flooding again — each one a sign that the God who does this annually can do it finally.

**"For Those Possessing Certainty": The Qualifying Phrase**

The verse's second half is its sharpest edge. The signs are *there* — upon the earth, before every eye — but they are signs only *for the mūqinīn*. The same earth that teaches the certain is mute to the heedless; the same rain that proves the resurrection to one man is only weather to another. The Qur'an's epistemology is here laid bare: evidence does not compel by itself; it requires a *reader*, and the reader it requires is the one whose heart has not been turned away (the very word of verse 9). The verse is thus both an invitation and a diagnosis: the signs are offered to everyone, but only the certain will receive them, and the certainty is itself the fruit of looking honestly. The sūrah's logic is circular in the best sense — the signs produce certainty, and certainty opens the eye to the signs — and the circle is the Qur'an's account of how faith grows: look, and you will see; see, and you will look more.

**The Cross-References: The Theme Across the Qur'an**

The source commentary cross-references the two passages where this theme reaches its full statement. The first is Sūrah Fuṣṣilat, where the signs in the horizons and the souls are promised as the proof of the Qur'an's truth:

> *"We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth."* (Qur'an 41:53)

The second is Sūrah al-Jāthiyah, where the signs are catalogued:

> *"Truly in the heavens and the earth are signs for the believers; and in your creation, and in the beasts He has spread abroad, are signs for a people who are certain."* (Qur'an 45:3–4)

The recurrence of the phrase *li'l-mūqinīn* — "for those possessing certainty" — in both passages shows that the Qur'an ties the signs to certainty by design: the universe is not a proof that works on anyone regardless of disposition but a proof that completes the disposition that is already turning toward it. The sūrah's verse is the shortest statement of this longest theme.

**The Earth as the First Revelation**

There is a further depth to the verse that the commentators touch: the earth is, in a sense, the *first* revelation, the scripture that preceded all scriptures. Every prophet was sent to a people who already held the book of nature in their hands, and the Qur'an's argument is that the two books — nature and revelation — are written by one Author and read each other. This is why the sūrah can swear by the winds and clouds with the same solemnity with which it quotes the prophets: both are divine speech, one in the medium of matter and one in the medium of language. The verse invites the modern reader, who has often been taught to see science and scripture as rivals, to see them as the Qur'an saw them: two readings of one text, the one mapping its mechanism, the other naming its Author.

**For the Modern Reader: Recovering the Habit of Reading the Earth**

The modern reader, like the modern city-dweller, has largely lost the habit of reading the earth — not because the earth has stopped speaking but because the reader has stopped attending. The verse is a summons to recover the attention: to notice the seed that dies and returns, the rain that raises the dead land, the season that turns without fail — and to see in each a *sign*, an *āyah*, a verse of the same Book one holds in hand. The certainty the verse promises is not a mood but a *possession* — the *yaqīn* that comes from having looked at the evidence until it could no longer be doubted. The next verse will turn the reader's eye inward, from the earth's signs to the soul's, and ask the question that the sūrah has been preparing from its first word: *will you not then see?*

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:21

> **and within your souls. Do you not then behold?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inward Turn: From the Horizon to the Self**

The verse completes the pair by turning the gaze inward: *wa fī anfusikum* — "and within your souls" — followed by the challenge that has been gathering force through the whole sūrah: *a-fa-lā tubṣirūn* — "do you not then behold?" The word *anfus* is the plural of *nafs*, the self, the soul, and the phrase "within your souls" can be read as "within yourselves" — in your own bodies, minds, and natures. The movement of the two verses is deliberate and beautiful: the previous verse pointed *out* to the earth; this one points *in* to the self. The outer sign and the inner sign are two halves of one proof, and the sūrah now asks its hearer to complete the circle by looking at the place he has looked at least of all — himself. The question *a-fa-lā tubṣirūn* — "will you not then see?" — is the sūrah's recurring demand for vision, the verb *baṣara* (to see with insight) rather than mere *naẓara* (to glance), for the seeing required here is not the eye's glance but the mind's recognition.

**The Signs Within the Self**

What are the signs within the soul? The commentators enumerate them with a richness that the verse's brevity conceals. The most immediate is the *body itself* — the human frame, with its limbs, joints, senses, and systems, whose intricacy is a standing argument for its Maker. The source commentary records the saying of the great commentator and ḥadīth scholar Qatādah:

> *"Whoever meditates upon his own creation recognizes that he was only created for worship, and that his joints were only made flexible for worship."*

The body is not merely a machine but a *liturgy* — its joints bend for prostration, its tongue shapes remembrance, its hands extend for giving — and the one who studies his own frame honestly will see that he was made for a purpose and by a Maker. The commentators also read "within your souls" as the *diversity of human nature* — the variation of languages, forms, colors, and temperaments that no race or nation could have designed; and as the *lowliness of the body's origin and end*, the fact that the self is made of a drop of fluid, sustained by food that becomes waste, and yet reasons, loves, and knows God — a combination of frailty and dignity that points beyond itself. Al-Ālūsī records both readings, and al-Ghazzālī, in his *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, takes "within your selves" as far as the waste the body contains, the hidden uncleanness that humbles the proud. The point of all these readings is one: the self is a *sign*, and the sign is legible to whoever will look.

**The Qur'an's Argument From the Self**

The argument from the self is the Qur'an's most intimate proof, because it requires no travel and no scholarship — only honesty. The Qur'an develops it in full in Sūrah al-Ṭāriq:

> *"So let man consider from what he was created: he was created from a gushing fluid, issuing from between the loins and the ribs. Truly He is able to return him."* (Qur'an 86:5–8)

The logic is the same as the sūrah's opening oaths: the one who made the self from a drop can remake it from the dust; the resurrection is not harder than the creation, but the same act repeated. And the Qur'an presses the argument further in Sūrah Fuṣṣilat, promising that the signs within the self will be shown until the truth is plain:

> *"We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth."* (Qur'an 41:53)

The self is thus not a neutral object but a *promised* sign, an argument that God Himself undertook to unfold before the eyes of the skeptical — and the sūrah's verse is one of the places where that promise is cashed.

**"Do You Not Then Behold?": The Question as an Argument**

The closing question deserves its full rhetorical weight. The Qur'an does not say "therefore believe" but "will you not then see?" — because the issue is presented as one of *vision*, not of will alone. The signs are there; the earth is there; the self is there; the only missing element is the *looking*. The question form is the Qur'an's way of returning the responsibility to the hearer: it has placed the evidence before him, and now it asks him to do the one thing no one can do for him — to open his eyes. The question is also a rebuke softened into an invitation, and its frequency in the Qur'an — *afalā taʿqilūn* (will you not reason?), *afalā yatadabbarūn* (will you not reflect?), *afalā tubṣirūn* (will you not see?) — shows that the Qur'an treats the failure of the deniers as a failure of *attention*, not of intelligence. The evidence was never the problem; the looking was.

**The Modern Reader and the Unread Self**

For the modern reader the verse is a quiet revolution. The age has mapped the body to its last cell and the mind to its last synapse, and yet knows the self less than any age before — because knowing the self's parts is not knowing the self's *significance*. The verse proposes a different kind of looking: not the microscope's but the contemplative's, the gaze that asks not "how does this work?" but "whose work is this, and what is it for?" The answer it promises is the *yaqīn* of the previous verse — the certainty that comes when the outer sign and the inner sign are seen together, as two halves of a single proof. The sūrah will now crown the argument with the sign that joins them both — the provision in the sky and the promise it carries — before swearing by the Lord of both heaven and earth.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:22

> **And in Heaven is your provision and that which you were promised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign Above: Provision and Promise in the Sky**

The argument rises from earth and self to the sky: *wa fī al-samāʾi rizqukum wa mā tūʿadūn* — "and in Heaven is your provision and that which you were promised." The word *rizq* is the Qur'an's term for *provision* — everything by which a creature is sustained: food, water, air, light, livelihood, and, in its widest sense, every good that God apportions to His creation. The verse places this provision *in the sky* — a statement that sounds strange to the modern ear until one unpacks it, for the commentators read it in two complementary ways. Read narrowly, the sky's provision is *rain* — the water that descends and upon which every green thing and every living thing depends; and, as the source commentary notes, also *light and wind*, the other gifts that come down from above. Read broadly, the verse means that the source of all provision is *above* — that is, with God, Who is above all, and that what reaches the creature comes down from His treasury. Al-Rāzī explains the first sense, and al-Ṭabarī the second, and the verse carries both at once.

**Provision "In the Sky": The Narrow and the Wide Reading**

The narrow reading is the Qur'an's own, for it repeatedly locates the cause of earthly life in what descends from the sky:

> *"And in the sky is your provision and that which you are promised. So by the Lord of the sky and the earth, it is indeed true, as it is that you speak."* (Qur'an 51:22–23)

> *"And We sent down from the sky water in due measure, and lodged it in the earth; and truly We are able to take it away."* (Qur'an 23:18)

Rain is the literal *rizq* of the earth: without it, no seed, no plant, no animal, no bread. The Makkan hearer, a desert man, understood this with his whole body — the sky was the source of his food in the most direct sense, and to say "your provision is in the sky" was not poetry but survival. The wide reading is equally Qur'anic, for the Qur'an names God as the Provider and makes every provision descend from His command:

> *"And there is no creature on the earth but that its provision is upon God."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The two readings meet in the sūrah's own theology: the rain is the visible, earthly face of the invisible, heavenly provision, and the one who provides by rain is the One who provides by all means. Al-Quṭubī's gloss — that the provision is brought down by the angels — joins the two: the sky is the door through which God's provision enters the world, and the angels are its couriers.

**"That Which You Were Promised": The Promise Joined to the Provision**

The verse's second half is the sūrah's recurring theme returning in a new key: *wa mā tūʿadūn* — "and that which you are promised." The source commentary records the interpretations: it is the promise of the *Hereafter* — the resurrection, the judgment, the reward and punishment — the very promise the opening oaths guaranteed. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as all that God has apportioned for every person, and al-Jalālayn draws the same conclusion: the verse indicates the manner in which God determines all things. The joining of *provision* and *promise* in a single verse is the sūrah's quiet masterpiece: the same sky that sends down your daily bread will send down the Day of Judgment; the same God who sustains you today holds your tomorrow; the provision you can see is the guarantee of the promise you cannot. The verse is the Qur'an's way of saying: you trust the sky for your bread without a moment's doubt — trust it also for your return, for both come from the same hand.

**The Logic of the Twofold Sign**

The argument embedded in the verse is a form of reasoning the Qur'an uses everywhere: from the seen to the unseen, from the lesser to the greater. The provision of the sky is the *seen* — rain falls, bread arrives, no one doubts it. The promise is the *unseen* — the resurrection, the judgment, the Hereafter. The verse's logic is that the two are *of one kind*: both are things that descend from God's command, both are certain, and the certainty of the seen is the measure of the unseen. The Qur'an states this "from the lesser to the greater" argument explicitly elsewhere:

> *"Does man think that We shall not reassemble his bones? Nay, We are able to order perfectly his fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

The sky that never fails to send the rain will never fail to send the judgment, for both are the execution of one decree. The verse is thus not a mere observation about weather but a proof of the Hereafter, compressed into a single sentence.

**The Verse's Comfort and Its Warning**

The verse carries both faces of the sūrah's message. To the believer it is pure comfort: the provision that has never failed him is a standing guarantee of the promise that will not fail him; the sky he looks at every morning is a daily reminder that he is provided for and awaited. To the denier it is a warning wrapped in a proof: the same sky from which he takes his bread is the sky from which the judgment will descend, and the very certainty of the one should have taught him the other. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to see the provision itself as a sign of the Provider, and to meet it with the words:

> *"O God, none can withhold what You give, and none can give what You withhold, and the efforts of the striver avail him nothing against You."* (al-Bukhārī 844; Muslim 593)

The verse is that recognition set into the fabric of the sūrah — the sky as the visible treasury of an invisible Lord.

**For the Modern Reader: The Forgotten Sky**

The modern reader who lives by the supermarket and the tap has forgotten what the desert man knew in his bones — that all provision comes from above. The verse's gift is to restore that forgotten knowledge: to make the rain, the light, and the air visible again as *rizq*, as gifts descending from a Giver, and to make the daily bread a daily proof of the promised return. The sūrah will now crown the whole argument with its most solemn oath — by the Lord of sky and earth — and with the verse's unforgettable comparison: the promise is as true as the fact that you speak.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:23

> **So by the Lord of Heaven and earth, it is indeed true—as it is that you are endowed with speech.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crowning Oath of the Argument**

The sūrah's argument from the signs reaches its summit in an oath: *fa-wa-rabbi al-samāʾi wa'l-arḍi innahu la-ḥaqqun mithla mā annakum tanṭiqūn* — "So by the Lord of Heaven and earth, it is indeed true — as it is that you are endowed with speech." The oath is the sūrah's greatest, for it swears not by a creature but by the *Lord of the creatures* — by the Creator of both heaven and earth, the very Being whose existence the signs were assembled to prove. The particle *fa-* ("so") ties the oath to everything before it: because the signs are in the earth and the soul, because the provision and the promise are in the sky, *therefore* — by the Lord of all of it — the matter is true. And the word *ḥaqq* — "true, real, established" — is the verse's verdict, the heaviest word for reality the Qur'an possesses: the promise is not opinion or hope or metaphor but *ḥaqq*, the solid truth on which the whole of the argument rests.

**What "It" Is: The Promise Made Certain**

The pronoun *innahu* — "it is true" — refers, as the source commentary records, to the matters the sūrah has been establishing: the *promise* of the Hereafter (as in verse 22), the Qur'an as a whole, and the Judgment (as in verse 6). The commentators differ only in scope, not in substance, for all three referents are one: the truth of what God has promised and revealed. Al-Jalālayn and al-Rāzī take "it" as the promise of verse 22; al-Rāzī also records the reading of "it" as the entire Qur'an; and the reference to the Judgment stands behind all of them. The verse is the sūrah's moment of summation: everything the oaths swore, everything the signs showed, everything the histories will recount — all of it is gathered into a single pronoun and certified with a single word: *ḥaqq*.

**The Comparison: As Sure as Your Own Speech**

The verse's masterstroke is its comparison — *mithla mā annakum tanṭiqūn* — "just as it is that you speak." The word *tanṭiqūn* is from *naṭaqa*, "to speak," the root from which *manṭiq* (logic, speech) and *nāṭiq* (the speaking one) derive, and the comparison is deliberately chosen for its unanswerability. Al-Bayḍāwī explains the point: just as you have no doubt whatsoever that you speak — that your tongue moves and your words exist — so you should have no doubt in the reality of the promise. The verse borrows the *most certain fact* in the hearer's possession — his own speech, the one thing he cannot deny without using it — and measures the promise against it. The logic is devastating in its simplicity: you are certain of your speech beyond all doubt; be equally certain of the promise, for it is from the same Lord who gave you the tongue. The comparison is the Qur'an's rhetorical answer to the skeptic's demand for proof: it does not offer a syllogism but a *standard* — the certainty you already have is the certainty you are being asked to extend.

**The Rhetoric of the Unanswerable**

The comparison's force lies in its psychological precision. A man who says "I doubt I am speaking" has already refuted himself, for the doubt itself is speech. The verse thus seizes the one fact no denier can deny — his own ongoing act of speaking — and makes it the measure of the promise's certainty. This is the Qur'an's genius for arguing from what its opponent cannot escape: the denier who denies the resurrection is, in the very act of denying, using a tongue given him by the Lord of the resurrection. The verse does not argue *down* to the skeptic from an authority he rejects; it argues *up* from a fact he inhabits. It is, in the classical rhetoricians' terms, an argument from the *a fortiori* and the *undeniable* at once — the promise is no less certain than the speech by which you try to doubt it.

**The Lord of Heaven and Earth: The Oath's Object as the Argument's Conclusion**

There is a final perfection in the oath's object. The sūrah began by swearing by the *scatterers, the bearers, the coursers, the apportioners* — the creatures — and it ends this section by swearing by their *Lord*. The progression is the argument's completion: the creatures pointed to the Creator, and now the Creator Himself is invoked to certify the promise. To swear by the Lord of heaven and earth is to swear by the One whose existence the whole preceding argument has demonstrated, so that the oath is not a leap of faith but the conclusion of a proof. The classical theologians note that God swears by His creatures in the Qur'an to honor them as signs, and by His own Lordship to make the oath's certainty absolute — and here the sūrah does both in sequence, moving from the sign to the Signified, from the witnesses to the Lord of the witnesses.

**For the Modern Reader: The Standard of Certainty**

The verse leaves the modern reader with a question that is also a standard. What are you *certain* of? The modern mind is certain of many things — its own speech, its own existence, the laws of nature — and doubtful of the one thing the sūrah says is as certain as the rest. The verse's invitation is to apply the same standard across the board: the certainty you grant to your own speaking, grant also to the promise, for both rest on the same foundation — the Lord of heaven and earth. The verse is thus not a demand for blind belief but for *consistent* belief: a consistency in which the certainty one already possesses becomes the bridge to the certainty one is asked to reach. With this, the argument from the signs is complete, and the sūrah turns to the histories — Abraham, Lot, Moses, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Noah — the living evidence of what the promise has already done in the world.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:24

> **Hast thou heard tell of Abraham’s honored guests,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn to History: A Story Asked as a Question**

The sūrah changes its register from argument to narrative with a question: *hal atāka ḥadīthu ḍayfi Ibrāhīma al-mukramīn* — "Hast thou heard tell of Abraham's honored guests?" The form *hal atāka* — "has there come to you" — is the Qur'an's characteristic way of introducing a story that the hearer already half-knows, drawing him in with the promise that what follows is a *ḥadīth*, a report, an account worth the hearing. The Prophet ﷺ, who had never read the previous scriptures, is being asked — rhetorically — whether the account has reached him, and the answer is that it has, by revelation, which is itself a sign of the Qur'an's source: the unlettered Prophet narrates the story of Abraham's guests as one who has heard it from the One who sent them. The question-form is thus, at its opening, already an argument for the Qur'an's divine origin.

**The Guests and Their Honor**

The word *ḍayf* — "guest" — is one of Arabic's richest hospitality-words, for it originally names the *inclining* of the guest toward the host, and the Arabs built an entire ethics of honor upon it: the guest's arrival was a test of the host's nobility, and the host's treatment of the guest was the measure of his worth. The guests here are *al-mukramīn* — "the honored" — the passive participle of *akrama*, "to honor," so that the verse names them not "the angels" but "the honored ones," guests who were themselves deserving of honor and whom Abraham honored in turn. The commentators explain that they were a group of angels — three, or ten, or twelve, among them Gabriel — who came to Abraham in human form, and the sūrah will recount their mission in the verses that follow. The scene is the same as that told in fuller detail in Sūrah Hūd (11:69–76) and Sūrah al-Ḥijr (15:51–60), and the source commentary directs the reader to those passages for the complete account, while noting the Biblical parallel in Genesis 18.

**Abraham as the Pattern of Hospitality**

The story is chosen at this point in the sūrah for a precise reason: Abraham is the Qur'an's supreme example of the *reverent* — the *muḥsin* whose portrait the sūrah has just drawn in verses 15–19 — and his treatment of the guests is that portrait enacted. The same Abraham who slept little and sought forgiveness and gave of his wealth will now be shown rising to honor strangers, hastening to feed them, and fearing when they will not eat. The sūrah's structure is thus seamless: the reverent's habits were described in the abstract, and now the greatest of the reverent demonstrates them in the concrete. The honored guests, moreover, arrive with a double purpose — to give Abraham the glad tidings of a son and to carry out the punishment of Lot's people — so that the story holds together the sūrah's two great themes at once: mercy to the believer and judgment upon the denier, both descending from the same sky whose provision and promise were just sworn.

**The Qur'anic Art of the Abbreviated Narrative**

The sūrah tells the story in its own characteristically compressed way, and this verse is the first stroke of that compression. The Qur'an does not narrate for narration's sake; it selects the moments that carry the lesson and passes over the rest in silence, and the hearer is expected to fill the gaps from the fuller tellings elsewhere in the Book. This economy is itself a doctrine: the Qur'an's stories are *ʿibar* — lessons — not chronicles, and every detail retained is retained because it teaches. The verse's single adjective, *al-mukramīn*, already contains the story's moral: these were guests worthy of honor, and the one who honored them was the one whom God honored. The next verse will show the honor in action, as the guests enter and Abraham greets them with the greeting of Paradise.

**The Historical and Scriptural Setting**

The source commentary notes that the Qur'anic account is close to that of Sūrah Hūd and Sūrah al-Ḥijr, and that for other aspects of Abraham's story the reader should turn to Sūrah Ibrāhīm (14:35–41) and Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt (37:83–109), while the Biblical account stands in Genesis 18. The convergence and divergence of the two scriptures are both instructive: the Qur'an and the Bible agree on the arrival of the guests, on the promise of a son to the aged couple, and on the mission of judgment against the cities of the plain; they differ in the details the Qur'an reframes — most notably, as a later verse will show, in whether the guests ate the food Abraham served. The Qur'an's account is thus not a borrowing but a correction and a completion, and the sūrah's telling is the version the Prophet was taught by revelation, given not to compete with the earlier scripture but to restore its truth.

**For the Modern Reader: The Opening of a Portrait**

For the modern reader, the verse is an invitation to watch a master-class in hospitality about to unfold — and to notice, behind the story's surface, the sūrah's method at work. The verse does not moralize; it narrates, and lets the narration teach. The honored guests, the question-form, the single loaded adjective — all of it is the Qur'an's way of making the hearer *lean in*, of turning an ancient story into a present scene. The next verse will enter the scene itself, and the reader is about to witness, in Abraham's greeting, the first act of the reverent life described in verses 15–19: the hand opened in welcome, the tongue speaking peace.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:25

> **when they entered upon him and said, “Peace!” he said, “Peace—an unfamiliar folk.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exchange of Greetings and Its Grammar**

The scene opens: *idh dakhalū ʿalayhi fa-qālū salāman qāla salāmun qawmun munkarūn* — "when they entered upon him and said, 'Peace!' he said, 'Peace — an unfamiliar folk.'" The verse is a masterpiece of compressed drama, and the commentators draw out its every syllable. The guests' greeting, *salāman*, is in the accusative, and the grammarians explain that this form carries the sense of a wish being performed — "we wish you peace," a greeting offered in passing, as one casts a greeting to a stranger. Abraham's reply, *salāmun*, is in the nominative, and the nominative here is the form of *establishment* — "peace be *established* upon you," a greeting that settles and remains. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī note the difference: Abraham returned a *fuller, firmer* greeting than he received, and in doing so he fulfilled the very command the Qur'an would later give his descendants:

> *"And when you are offered a greeting, respond with a greeting that is better, or return it."* (Qur'an 4:86)

The guests offered peace in passing; Abraham answered with the peace that abides — and the sūrah records that Abraham's reply, *salāmun*, is the very greeting of the people of Paradise:

> *"Their greeting therein shall be 'Peace!'"* (Qur'an 56:26)

**"An Unfamiliar Folk": The Host's Wariness**

Abraham's second phrase — *qawmun munkarūn* — "an unfamiliar folk" — is the verse's quiet hinge. The word *munkarūn* is from *nakira*, "to be unknown, unrecognized," and it carries the host's honest assessment: these are strangers, men I do not know, travelers whose business I cannot guess. The phrase is spoken to himself, as the commentators note, a private thought overheard — Abraham sizes up his guests, sees that he does not know them, and yet does not let his wariness diminish his hospitality. The detail is crucial to the story's moral: Abraham's virtue was not that he trusted everyone but that he honored everyone *despite* his uncertainty. The stranger was not yet proven a friend, and still the host rose to serve him — which is the whole point of the scene the sūrah is about to unfold. The word also foreshadows the fear that will come in verse 28: the guests' unfamiliarity is real, and Abraham's instinct that something about them was not ordinary will prove correct.

**The Ethics of Hospitality in the Qur'an and the Prophet's Example**

The verse is the Qur'an's finest portrait of the greeting as an act of worship. The greeting of peace — *salām* — is not a social formality but a prayer and a covenant: to say "peace" to a stranger is to pledge him safety from one's own hand, tongue, and harm. The Prophet ﷺ made the spread of the greeting the very definition of a community's faith, and among his most quoted instructions:

> *"You will not enter Paradise until you believe, and you will not believe until you love one another. Shall I not guide you to something which, if you do it, you will love one another? Spread the greeting of peace among yourselves."* (Muslim 54, from Abū Hurayrah)

And the tradition records that the greeting was to be given to the known and the unknown alike — precisely Abraham's situation here, where the guests were strangers and the greeting was given all the same. The verse thus enacts, in a single line, the two great duties of the believer toward the stranger: the tongue's peace and the hand's welcome.

**The Greeting That Precedes the Meal**

Structurally, the verse is the first of three movements in the story — greeting, feeding, and fearing — and it establishes the pattern the Prophet's community was taught to follow: the greeting comes first, before anything is known of the guest, before any request is made, before any exchange of goods. The *salām* is the door through which the stranger enters the host's protection, and only after it is spoken does the host turn to the duty of the meal. The sūrah's order is deliberate: greeting, then the hastening to the family, then the food. The reverent life the sūrah drew in verses 15–19 — the life of worship, humility, and giving — is here shown in its social form, and the first of its social acts is the word of peace spoken to the unknown.

**For the Modern Reader: The Lost Art of the Greeting**

The modern world has largely forgotten the theology of the greeting — it says "hello" without meaning "I wish you peace," and it treats strangers as threats rather than guests. The verse recovers the lost art: the greeting as a *pledge*, the stranger as a *guest*, the unknown as an opportunity for honor rather than a cause for fear. Abraham's wariness — his honest "unfamiliar folk" — is permitted; what is not permitted is letting the wariness close the door. The verse's lesson is as practical as it is profound: honor the stranger with the word of peace, and let the rest follow. The next verse will show the rest following at once, as Abraham turns quietly to his family and returns with the best of his house — the fattened calf.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

> **Then he went quietly to his family and came with a fattened calf.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Discretion of Generosity**

The host acts: *fa-rāgha ilā ahlihi fa-jāʾa bi-ʿijlin samīn* — "then he went quietly to his family and came with a fattened calf." The verb *rāgha* is the sūrah's most delicate word: it means "to slip away quietly, to withdraw without being noticed, to move with stealth." Abraham did not announce his hospitality, did not summon servants with a flourish, did not make the guests wait through ceremony; he *slipped away* to his household, so quietly that the guests — had they been ordinary men — would not have known the feast was coming. The word teaches the Islamic ethics of giving at its deepest point: the left hand's ignorance of the right hand's gift, the generosity that conceals itself. The Qur'an praises exactly this hiddenness in its portrait of the believer's giving:

> *"If you disclose your charities, how good they are; but if you conceal them and give them to the poor, it is better for you."* (Qur'an 2:271)

Abraham's *rāgha* is that concealment enacted — the host who disappears quietly and returns with abundance, so that the gift arrives before the guests even know they are being given to.

**The Fattened Calf: The Best of the House**

What Abraham brings is specified with care: *ʿijlin samīn* — a fattened calf. The commentators note that this is the *best* of Abraham's provision, the animal he had kept and fattened against the great occasions, and that hospitality in the Abrahamic tradition means bringing the finest, not the leftover. The detail is echoed in the fuller account of Sūrah Hūd, where the guest-meal is described as "a roasted calf" (*ʿijlin ḥanīdh*):

> *"And certainly Our messengers came to Abraham with glad tidings. They said, 'Peace!' He said, 'Peace!' and hastened to bring them a roasted calf."* (Qur'an 11:69)

The *fattened* calf is the sign of a host who gave from his abundance and his best — the very opposite of the grudging hospitality that offers the guest what the house itself would not eat. The sūrah's earlier portrait of the reverent is here fulfilled: *"in their wealth was a due for the beggar and the deprived"* (51:19), and Abraham's wealth now performs its due for the stranger at the door. The calf is the wealth's *ḥaqq* made flesh — the share of the guest served as the centerpiece of the host's table.

**The Unhurried Speed of Service**

There is a beautiful tension in the verse between the *quiet* of Abraham's going and the *speed* of his return — a tension the commentators resolve by noting that both are aspects of one virtue. He went quietly (so as not to boast or alarm), and he returned quickly (so as not to keep the guests waiting); the discretion and the haste are the two halves of perfect service. The Prophet ﷺ distilled the same teaching into the community's ethics of hospitality:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47, from Abū Hurayrah)

And the honor of the guest, as the Prophet's companions understood it, meant speed in serving and excellence in what was served — the guest is not to be kept waiting, and the guest is not to be served less than the host would serve himself. Abraham's quiet haste is the model of both.

**The Contrast With the Biblical Account**

The source commentary draws the instructive comparison with Genesis 18, where the angels — in the Biblical telling — eat the meal Abraham sets before them, while in the Qur'anic account they do not, and it is precisely their refusal to eat that will trigger Abraham's fear in the verses to come. The difference is not trivial: the Qur'an's angels are pure spirits who do not eat, and their refusal is the first *sign* by which Abraham recognizes that his guests are not ordinary men. The Qur'an thus uses the meal not as a shared banquet but as a *test* — the food is offered in perfect hospitality, and the guests' inability to eat it becomes the moment of revelation. The story, in the Qur'an's hands, is reframed around its own central themes: hospitality as the reverent's habit, and the angel's refusal as the first glimmer of the supernatural breaking into the ordinary.

**For the Modern Reader: The Theology of the Quiet Gift**

The verse is a small catechism in the ethics of giving for any age. It teaches that generosity is best when it is *quiet* — that the hand that gives should not sound a trumpet before it; that the gift should be the *best* — not the surplus one can spare but the portion one values; and that service should be *swift* — for a delayed kindness is half a refusal. The modern reader, in an age of performed generosity and photographed charity, may find in Abraham's *rāgha* a rebuke and a relief: the truest gift is the one that slips away unnoticed and returns with abundance. The next verse will place the feast before the guests, and the story will turn from the host's virtue to the guests' refusal — and to the fear that refusal awakens.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:27

> **He placed it close to them, saying, “Will you not eat?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Meal Served, and the Gentle Invitation**

The hospitality reaches its full gesture: *fa-qarrabahu ilayhim qāla a-lā taʾkulūn* — "then he placed it close to them, saying, 'Will you not eat?'" The verb *qarraba* means "to bring near," and its use here is tender: Abraham did not leave the food at a distance or point the guests toward it; he *drew it close* to them, placing it within their reach, so that no effort was required of them. And his invitation is not a command but a gentle question — *a-lā taʾkulūn* — "will you not eat?" — the form a host uses to urge a reluctant guest without pressing him, to say "eat, please" with the softness of a question rather than the force of an order. The whole verse is the picture of hospitality perfected: the food brought near, the invitation offered with warmth, and — as the story is about to reveal — the guests' silence in response.

**The Sign the Refusal Conceals**

The verse's dramatic work is done by what does *not* happen: the guests do not eat. The commentators explain that Abraham's question is the story's turning point, because the refusal to eat is, in the ancient Arab world — and in the ancient world generally — a sign of hostility or ill will. A guest who would not share the host's food declared, by that refusal, that he was not a guest at all: that he had come as an enemy, or a messenger of judgment, and would accept no bond of the table. The source commentary records this precisely: refusing the offered food was read as a declaration of enmity, and it is this reading that makes Abraham's next response — the fear of verse 28 — entirely natural. The verse thus plants the seed of the story's climax in a question asked over a meal: the host offers, the guests decline, and the bond of hospitality is broken by the very beings who had come on a mission no hospitality could forestall.

**The Custom Behind the Fear**

To understand the verse fully, one must recover the ancient custom it assumes. In the world of Abraham, the sharing of food was the sharing of *protection*: to eat a man's salt was to place oneself under his safeguard, and to refuse it was to serve notice that no such bond was being made. The guest who would not eat was announcing that he was there for another purpose — as a raider, a herald of war, or, as the story will reveal, a messenger of judgment. Abraham, the perfect host, had performed every rite of welcome — the greeting, the haste, the best of his herd, the food drawn near — and the refusal of his offered bond was the first intimation that these were no ordinary travelers. The Qur'an's narrative economy is here at its finest: a single unanswered question carries the whole weight of the story's turning.

**The Angelic Nature of the Guests**

The refusal also carries a theological point the commentators draw out: the guests were *angels*, and angels do not eat. The Qur'an's teaching on the nature of angels is consistent — they are created of light, they neither eat nor drink nor marry, and their appearance in human form is a *tamaththul*, a taking-on of a likeness, not a real assumption of human need. The Qur'an elsewhere shows the same scene and the same refusal, with the angels' own explanation:

> *"And when he saw that their hands did not reach for it, he found them strange and conceived a fear of them. They said, 'Fear not; we have been sent against the people of Lot.'"* (Qur'an 11:70)

The refusal to eat is thus the *sign* by which the supernatural is recognized: the guest who looks like a man but will not eat like one is not a man at all. The sūrah's question — "will you not eat?" — is the question that reveals the angels for what they are, and the story moves, in the next verse, from the host's hospitality to his fear.

**The Hospitality of the Prophet as the Verse's Living Echo**

The verse's ethic was not left in Abraham's tent; the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ carried it into the life of his community, and the tradition is full of its echoes. The Prophet ﷺ taught his companions to share food and to consider the guest's comfort a right:

> *"The food of two suffices for three, and the food of three suffices for four."* (al-Bukhārī 5392; Muslim 2058)

And his own practice was to serve his guests himself, drawing the food near with his own hand, so that the host's dignity was in the *serving*, not in the being-served. Abraham's *fa-qarrabahu ilayhim* — "he drew it close to them" — is the ancestral form of this same courtesy, the host lowering himself to raise his guest.

**For the Modern Reader: The Question Over the Table**

The verse leaves the modern reader with a scene to inhabit and a lesson to take. The lesson is that hospitality is completed in the *serving*, not in the cooking — the food must be drawn near, the invitation must be warm, the guest must be made to feel that the table is his own. And the scene is a reminder that the table has always been a place of revelation: what a man does with the food set before him — and what his guests do with it — has never been a small matter. The next verse will turn the story's key, as Abraham, the perfect host, watches his perfect guests decline his perfect feast, and feels the first stirring of the fear that their next words will both confirm and calm.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:28

> **Then he conceived a fear of them. They said, “Fear not!” and gave him glad tidings of a knowing son.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Host's Fear and Its Cause**

The story turns: *fa-awjasa minhum khīfatan* — "then he conceived a fear of them." The verb *awjasa* means "to feel something in the depths of oneself," a fear that is *conceived* and hidden before it is shown, and the noun *khīfah* is the inward dread that rises when the unfamiliar becomes threatening. The cause of Abraham's fear is the refusal recorded in the previous verse: his guests would not eat, and in the custom of his world a guest who declined the host's food declared himself an enemy. The source commentary records this as the primary explanation — the refusal of food as a sign of hostility — and adds a second, deeper reading reported by al-Ālūsī: Abraham feared that these were angels sent to punish him, that the judgment of God had come for his own household. Both readings converge on the same psychology: the perfect host, his hospitality refused, reads in the refusal the approach of something dreadful, and the fear he conceals is the fear of a man who has honored strangers and now wonders whether they have come to destroy him.

**The Immediate Reassurance: "Fear Not!"**

The guests' response is instantaneous and tender: *qālū lā takhaf* — "they said, 'Fear not!'" The reassurance is the Qur'an's characteristic word to those who meet the angels, spoken to Abraham here, to Mary at the annunciation, and to the prophets throughout:

> *"He said, 'I am but a messenger of thy Lord, to grant thee a pure boy.'"* (Qur'an 19:19)

The angels, having tested the host's hospitality and found it complete, now lift the fear their test had caused. The sequence is the story's deepest lesson about the relationship between human virtue and divine nearness: Abraham's fear was the *natural* response to the supernatural, and the angels' "fear not" is the supernatural's own answer — those who honored God's messengers are never the objects of their mission's dread. The fear and its removal are, together, the proof that Abraham's hospitality had been accepted by the very Lord whose guests he served.

**The Glad Tidings: A Knowing Son**

The reassurance is immediately followed by the gift the guests had come to deliver: *wa bashsharūhu bi-ghulāmin ʿalīm* — "and they gave him glad tidings of a knowing son." The verb *bashshara* means "to give good news" — the root of *bashīr*, the bearer of glad tidings — and the news is the fulfillment of Abraham's lifelong prayer for an heir. The son is described as *ʿalīm* — "knowing" — and the commentators explain the adjective: he would be a son of knowledge, a prophet, one who knew God and was known for wisdom — the word pointing to Isaac, the son promised to the aged couple, and through him to the line of prophecy that followed. The same description appears in Sūrah al-Ḥijr:

> *"They said, 'Fear not; we give thee glad tidings of a knowing son.'"* (Qur'an 15:53)

And the sūrah notes, in its cross-reference to Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt, that the giving of glad tidings is attributed both to the angels (here) and to God directly — *"We gave him glad tidings"* (37:112) — the two verses together teaching that God gives through the angels as His intermediaries, the glad tidings descending from the Lord by the hands of His messengers.

**The Question of the Son: Isaac or Ishmael?**

The source commentary records the scholarly discussion the verse raises. The majority of the commentators maintain that the "knowing son" is *Isaac*, the child promised to Sarah in her old age — and the fuller account in Sūrah Hūd supports this, where Sarah herself is told of Isaac and after him Jacob (11:71). But others, including some reports from the first generations, see the son as *Ishmael*, the ancestor of the Arabs and of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabarī record both positions with the classical commentators' characteristic care, and the difference turns on which of the two announcements of a son the verse describes. The Qur'an itself, in its conciseness, leaves the door open, and the tradition has lived with both readings — the point of the verse being, in either case, the same: the aged, childless prophet is given the news of a son who will be a prophet, and the news is the mercy that balances the judgment the guests are about to announce.

**The Twofold Mission: Mercy and Judgment in One Visit**

The verse, read with the next, reveals the guests' mission as double: to Abraham they bring *mercy* — the son, the reassurance — and to the cities of Lot they bring *judgment* — the stones, the overthrow. The same angels, the same visit, carry both, and the sūrah's placement of the story is now clear: it is the living demonstration of the two destinies the sūrah has just described. The reverent — Abraham — receive glad tidings and the words "fear not"; the guilty — the people of Lot — receive the messengers of destruction. The story is the sūrah's argument from history: the promise and the threat, the garden and the fire, are not abstractions but have already been delivered, once, to a single household and a single city, by a single embassy of angels.

**For the Modern Reader: The Fear That Mercy Answers**

For the modern reader, the verse holds a personal teaching beneath its narrative surface. Abraham's fear is the fear that any human being feels when the divine draws near — the dread that one's own unworthiness has finally caught up with him — and the angels' answer is the answer the Qur'an gives to every such fear: *lā takhaf*, "fear not," for those who have served God with sincerity are met not by His wrath but by His mercy. The verse teaches that the one who honors God's guests — who keeps his door and his table open to the stranger — will find, when the unseen comes to his own door, that it comes with glad tidings. The next verse will turn to Sarah, and the glad tidings will meet the first human response of astonishment — the cry and the struck face of the aged, barren wife.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

> **Then his wife came forward with a loud cry; she struck her face and said, “A barren old woman!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sarah's Astonishment and Its Human Truth**

The glad tidings reach Sarah, and the verse paints her response with the Qur'an's unsparing realism: *fa-aqbalati imraʾatuhu fī ṣarratin fa-ṣakkat wajhahā wa-qālat ʿajūzun ʿaqīm* — "then his wife came forward with a loud cry; she struck her face and said, 'A barren old woman!'" The word *ṣarrah* is a cry, a clamor, the raised voice of astonishment — and the word *ṣakkat* is the striking of the face with the palm, the gesture by which the Arab women of that world expressed wonder and dismay. Sarah's exclamation — *ʿajūzun ʿaqīm*, "an old woman, barren!" — is not a denial but an astonishment: she does not disbelieve the promise so much as she is staggered by it, measuring the news against the facts of her own body — her age, her barrenness — and finding the gap between them unbridgeable. The Qur'an records the human response in its full color, because the human response is part of the sign: the promise is not diminished by the astonishment but magnified by it.

**The Ages of the Couple**

The source commentary supplies the traditional figures: Abraham was said to be a hundred and twenty years old at this time, and Sarah ninety-nine. The numbers, transmitted by al-Jalālayn from the earlier commentators, are not the point in themselves but the measure of the miracle: a woman of ninety-nine, barren her whole life, is told she will bear a son — and the impossibility of the news is precisely what makes it a sign. The Qur'an's fuller account in Sūrah Hūd gives Sarah's own words at greater length:

> *"And his wife was standing by, and she laughed; then We gave her glad tidings of Isaac, and after Isaac, Jacob. She said, 'Woe unto me! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman, and this husband of mine is an old man? That would surely be an astounding thing!'"* (Qur'an 11:71–72)

The laughter and the cry are one response — the human frame's recoil from the impossible — and the Qur'an records both without censure, because astonishment at God's power is not unbelief but the natural prelude to faith.

**The Miracle of the Barren Made Fruitful**

Beneath the verse's drama lies one of the Qur'an's most insistent themes: God's power to grant offspring to the barren and the aged, the reversal of every natural expectation. The theme is repeated across the Qur'an's histories — Abraham and Sarah, Zechariah and his wife, and, at the culmination, Mary herself — and each repetition makes the same point: the laws of nature are God's servants, not His masters, and the One who set them can suspend them. The Qur'an states the principle in the words spoken to Zechariah in exactly the same situation:

> *"He said, 'My Lord, how shall I have a son when old age has overtaken me and my wife is barren?' He said, 'Thus does God do whatsoever He wills.'"* (Qur'an 3:40)

Sarah's cry — "a barren old woman!" — is the human question, and the answer, given in the next verse, is the divine one: *"Thus has thy Lord decreed."* The two verses together are the Qur'an's complete teaching on the matter: the impossible is impossible only until God wills it otherwise.

**The Psychology of Astonishment in the Qur'an's Method**

The verse also reveals something of the Qur'an's method in telling its stories: it does not shrink from the *emotional* truth of its scenes, because the emotion is the doorway to the lesson. Sarah's cry and her struck face make the promise *real* to the hearer — the hearer is made to feel the weight of ninety-nine years of barrenness, and therefore the weight of the promise that overcomes it. The Qur'an's narratives are thus not legends told from a distance but scenes rendered from within, and the rendering is the vehicle of the faith the story is meant to produce. The same method governs the whole sūrah: the scoffer's taunt is quoted, the denier's fear is described, the reverent's night is shown — because the Qur'an knows that truth is received through the heart's recognition, not only the mind's assent.

**Sarah as a Sign for the Sūrah's Argument**

The sūrah has been arguing, from its first verse, that the God who scatters the dust and revives the dead earth can revive the dead. Sarah's body is now folded into that argument: a barren, aged womb is as dead a thing as the desert, and the granting of the son is a resurrection enacted in a single life. The story of Abraham's guests is thus not a digression from the sūrah's theme but its embodiment — the promise of verse 5, that what is promised is true, is already being fulfilled in the tent of the aged couple, before the judgment on the cities is even announced. The next verse will give the divine answer to Sarah's astonishment, and in that answer the whole theology of the sūrah is compressed into a single sentence.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:30

> **They said, “Thus has thy Lord decreed. Truly He is the Wise, the Knowing.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to Astonishment: The Decree of the Lord**

The angels answer Sarah's cry with the words that answer every human astonishment at divine power: *qālū kadhāliki qāla rabbuki innahu huwa al-ḥakīmu al-ʿalīm* — "they said, 'Thus has thy Lord decreed. Truly He is the Wise, the Knowing.'" The phrase *kadhāliki* — "thus it is," "so shall it be" — is the Qur'an's characteristic formula for the divine decree that overrides all human expectation, and the source commentary cross-references the exact parallel in the annunciation to Mary, where the angel Gabriel answers her wonder at the birth of Jesus with the same words:

> *"He said, 'Thus shall it be. Thy Lord says, "It is easy for Me." And [it is thus] that We might make him a sign unto mankind, and a mercy from Us. And it is a matter decreed.'"* (Qur'an 19:21)

The formula teaches the sūrah's central theology in miniature: what God decrees *is*, and the impossibility is entirely on the side of the human measure, never on the side of the decree. The aged and barren will bear, because the Lord of the aged and the barren has said so.

**The Two Names That Seal the Decree**

The verse closes the promise with two of God's names, chosen with precision: *al-Ḥakīm* — the Wise — and *al-ʿAlīm* — the Knowing. The commentators explain why these two: the granting of a son to the barren is an act of *wisdom*, because it serves a purpose the human eye cannot see — the line of prophecy, the continuation of the covenant, the mercy to a nation yet unborn — and an act of *knowledge*, because only the One who knows every possibility can bring forth what all natural expectation rules out. The pairing of the two names is the Qur'an's answer to the "how" and the "why" of every miracle: the "how" is answered by His knowledge, which comprehends what the creatures cannot; the "why" is answered by His wisdom, which ordains what the creatures do not yet see. The verse thus teaches the believer to receive every divine decree with the double confidence that its Author knows all and wills well.

**The Theology of the Decree and Its Tender Address**

One word of the verse deserves special notice: *rabbuki* — "thy Lord." The angels do not say "the Lord" but "thy Lord," addressing Sarah directly, and the possessive pronoun is the verse's tenderness: the same Lord who decreed the barrenness is the Lord who decrees the son, and He is *hers* — the Lord who has known her, kept her, and now rewards her patience. The Qur'an's theology is here at its most intimate: the decree is not an impersonal fate but the personal act of a personal Lord, addressed to a particular woman in a particular tent, and the answer to her cry is not an explanation but a *relationship* — "thy Lord has said it." The whole of the sūrah's doctrine of providence is contained in that possessive: the decrees of God are the acts of the One who is each believer's own Lord.

**The Lesson of the Impossible Promise**

The verse is placed here not only for Sarah but for every hearer of the sūrah. The sūrah has been arguing for the resurrection — the most "impossible" of all promises to the Makkan ear — and the story of Sarah is the argument made personal: if the barren womb can bear by the word "be," then the dead can rise by the same word, for the resurrection is no harder for the Lord of the worlds than the son was for the Lord of Sarah. The verse's two names do the theological work: the One who *knows* the dust's dispersal (the sūrah's opening oaths) and *wisely* decrees its regathering is the One who speaks here. The "thus has thy Lord decreed" of the angels is, in the sūrah's architecture, also the answer to the scoffer's "when is the Day of Judgment?" — the decree is not subject to the calendar but to the Decree.

**For the Modern Reader: Receiving the Impossible**

The modern reader, no less than Sarah, measures the promises of God against the facts of the world and finds the gap unbridgeable — and the verse's answer is unchanged. "Thus has thy Lord decreed" is the believer's resting place in the face of every impossibility: the illness the doctors have named terminal, the door that every calculation has closed, the dead end that no strategy can open. The verse does not promise that every worldly wish will be granted, but it grounds the believer in the truth that nothing is impossible to the Lord, and that His decrees are wise and His knowledge complete. The next verse will turn the story from the promise to the mission, as Abraham asks the guests the question that will lead them to announce the judgment on the cities — and the sūrah's two destinies, mercy and wrath, will be seen arriving in a single embassy.

---

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:31

> **He said, “What is your errand, O messengers?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Turns the Story Toward Judgment**

The glad tidings delivered, Abraham now asks the guests the question their arrival has been awaiting: *qāla fa-mā khaṭbukum ayyuhā al-mursalūn* — "he said, 'What is your errand, O messengers?'" The word *khaṭb* is a weighty one: it is an affair, a matter of consequence, a business of importance — the word used for a grave undertaking, and later for the public address (khuṭbah) that treats serious matters. Abraham, reassured about his own household, now turns to the *purpose* of the visit, for he has understood that these honored guests did not come only with glad tidings: the glad tidings were the prelude, and the errand — the *khaṭb* — is still to be told. And in calling them *al-mursalūn* — "O messengers" — Abraham acknowledges what the encounter has revealed: these are sent ones, emissaries of God, and their business is God's business.

**Abraham the Intercessor: The Question's Hidden Hope**

The source commentary cross-references the fuller account in Sūrah al-Ḥijr, where Abraham's question leads into his plea for the people of Lot:

> *"He said, 'What is your business, O messengers?' They said, 'We have been sent to a guilty people...'"* (Qur'an 15:57–58)

The question is asked because Abraham suspects the errand, and the suspicion stirs the intercessor in him: the same Abraham who argued with God's messengers about the fate of the cities, who pled for the righteous among them, whose heart would not rest while a single believer might be swept away. The tradition preserves the picture of Abraham as the first great intercessor, the friend of God (khalīl Allāh) whose compassion reached even to the guilty, and this verse is the opening of that compassion's plea. The question "what is your errand?" is thus not mere curiosity but the first movement of a heart that will not accept the destruction of a people without asking, at least, whether any of them might be spared.

**The Rhetoric of the Question in the Sūrah's Structure**

The verse also performs a structural service in the sūrah's narrative. The story has moved from greeting to feeding to fearing to glad tidings, and the question now gathers all the threads and points them toward the second half of the guests' mission. The sūrah's two destinies — mercy to the believer and judgment to the denier — are about to be spoken side by side, and Abraham's question is the hinge on which the story turns from the one to the other. The question-form, like the sūrah's opening "hast thou heard tell?", is the Qur'an's way of making the hearer lean forward: the errand is about to be named, and the hearer is made to ask along with Abraham, "what is it?" The narrative's pull is deliberate — the sūrah wants its hearer to be *awaiting* the announcement of judgment, so that the announcement lands with its full weight.

**The Manners of the Prophet in Questioning**

There is also a lesson in the *manner* of Abraham's question that the commentators preserve: the respectful address — *ayyuhā al-mursalūn*, "O messengers" — the acknowledgement of the guests' rank, the calm inquiry rather than the demand. Abraham, having seen the signs of their nature, does not interrogate them as a host might interrogate suspicious strangers but asks them as one asks honored emissaries, with the courtesy due to their office. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was taught the same courtesy in his own dealings with revelation and its bearers, and the verse models for every generation the manner of seeking knowledge from those who carry it: with respect for the office, and with the heart already inclined to accept the answer.

**For the Modern Reader: The Question We All Must Ask**

For the modern reader, the verse is an invitation to ask the same question of the sūrah itself: what is *its* errand, what is the *khaṭb* it has come to deliver? The sūrah's answer, unfolding from this verse onward, is that its errand is the same as the guests': to announce the judgment that follows denial, and the deliverance that follows faith. Abraham's question is thus the reader's own, and the next verse's answer — "we have been sent unto a guilty people" — is the answer the sūrah has been preparing from its first oath. The hearer who has followed the story to this point stands where Abraham stood, about to be told what the messengers have come to do — and the telling will divide the story, and the sūrah, and the world, into the saved and the ruined.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:32

> **They said, “We have been sent unto a guilty people,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Errand Named: A Mission of Judgment**

The guests answer: *qālū innā ursilnā ilā qawmin mujrimīn* — "they said, 'We have been sent unto a guilty people.'" The verse names the errand in three words that carry the whole weight of the story. *Innā* — "truly we" — the emphatic particle asserting the mission's reality. *Ursilnā* — "we have been sent" — the passive of *arsala*, the verb of apostleship, the same word by which every prophet is described, so that these angels are *messengers* in the full sense, sent by the same Sender who sends every warner. And *qawmin mujrimīn* — "a guilty people" — the word *mujrimūn* being the active participle of *ajrama*, "to commit a crime," from the root *j-r-m*, which yields *jurm* (a crime, an offense) and *jarīmah* (the same). The word is the Qur'an's precise term for the one who has cut himself off from God by his deeds — the criminal against the divine order — and the source commentary identifies the people at once: the people of Lot.

**The Word "Guilty" and What It Names**

The choice of *mujrimīn* over other words for the sinful is deliberate. The Qur'an has many terms for wrongdoing — *ẓālimūn* (the unjust), *fāsiqūn* (the disobedient), *kāfirūn* (the disbelievers) — but *mujrimūn* is the word of *crime*, and it is reserved for those whose deeds have reached the level of open, defiant transgression. The people of Lot had not merely disbelieved; they had committed the specific enormity the Qur'an names as their sign — the approach of men with lust in place of women — and had compounded it with highway robbery and open defiance of their prophet:

> *"And Lot, when he said to his people, 'Do you commit an indecency that none in all the worlds ever preceded you in? You come unto men with lust, and you cut off the road, and you commit in your assemblies what is deplorable.'"* (Qur'an 29:28–29)

The word *mujrimūn* gathers all of this into a single verdict: they were not merely mistaken but *guilty*, and the mission of the angels is the execution of the sentence their guilt had earned.

**The Mission of the Angels in the Qur'an's Economy**

The verse also teaches a central piece of the Qur'an's doctrine of the unseen: God acts in the world through His angels, and the great judgments are carried out by angelic hands. The destruction of a people is not a random natural disaster but a *mission* — angels *sent* with a *purpose*, as precisely as a prophet is sent with a warning. The Qur'an's picture of the universe is of a creation governed at every level by obedient emissaries, and the same root *r-s-l* that describes the prophets describes these angels of punishment: both are *mursalūn*, sent ones, the one to warn, the other to execute. The sūrah's opening oath — *"by those that apportion the Command"* (51:4) — now stands revealed: these guests are among the apportioners, and the command they apportion to the cities is the command of destruction.

**The Contrast the Verse Sets Up**

The verse, standing beside the glad tidings just delivered, sets the sūrah's two destinies in the sharpest possible contrast within a single visit. To Abraham and Sarah — the believers — the messengers bring a *son* and the words "fear not." To the cities of Lot — the guilty — the same messengers bring *judgment*. The difference is not in the messengers but in the recipients: the same embassy is mercy to the one and wrath to the other, and the sūrah's hearer is made to see that the divine message is always this double-edged thing, salvation and ruin depending entirely on the receiving. The verse is thus the historical proof of what the sūrah's opening promised: *"truly the reverent shall be amidst gardens and springs"* (51:15) and the guilty shall be *"tried upon the Fire"* (51:13) — both destinies already delivered, once, by a single errand.

**The Patience of God Before the Sentence**

There is a further teaching hidden in the verse's brevity: the angels say "we have been sent," but the sending was long in coming. The people of Lot had been warned by their prophet for years; the angels arrive only at the end, when the warning has been exhausted and the guilt has ripened. The Qur'an's theology of judgment is always this: the sentence is delayed until the warning is complete, and the destruction is never the first word but the last. The sūrah's own hearers, the Makkan deniers, are being told the same thing by implication: the messengers are patient, the warning is long, but the errand of judgment, when it comes, will come for the guilty as surely as it came for the cities. The next verse will name the instrument of that judgment — the stones of clay — and the story will move from the mission's announcement to its execution.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:33

> **to send upon them stones of clay**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instrument of Judgment Named**

The angels specify the instrument of the sentence: *li-nursila ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min ṭīn* — "to send upon them stones of clay." The word *ḥijārah* is the plural of *ḥajar*, the stone, and *ṭīn* is clay — the soft, molded earth — so that the verse names a weapon of peculiar irony: stones of *clay*, the soft made hard, the earth itself turned into the missile of its own punishment. The source commentary cross-references the fuller accounts, where the same stones are described as *sijjīl*, baked clay, and where the order of the punishment is given in full:

> *"So when Our Command came, We made its uppermost to be its lowermost, and We rained down upon them stones of baked clay, in layers."* (Qur'an 11:82)

The stones of clay are thus part of a double judgment: the city overturned from its foundations, and a rain of baked clay poured from the sky — the earth below and the sky above both turned against a people who had defied the Lord of both.

**The Irony of the Clay**

The material of the punishment is chosen with the Qur'an's characteristic precision, and the commentators pause over its meaning. The people of Lot were dwellers in a land of clay and stone, builders of cities from the very earth; and the sin they are most remembered for is one the Qur'an describes as a reversal of the created order — men taking the place of women, lust diverted from its natural channel. The punishment answers the crime with its own image: the clay they built with becomes the clay that buries them; the order they inverted is inverted upon them, the city's high made low and the earth's soft made hard. The Qur'an's doctrine of correspondence — that the punishment fits the crime — is here enacted in the very substance of the judgment: *"Whosoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it"* (99:8).

**The Stones in the Wider Qur'anic Account**

The stones of clay are a recurring element of the Qur'an's telling of Lot's people, and each mention adds a layer of meaning. In Sūrah al-Ḥijr, the same punishment is described with its precise mechanism:

> *"And We turned it upside down, and rained upon them stones of baked clay."* (Qur'an 15:74)

And in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, the rain of stones is joined to the finality of the sentence:

> *"And We rained upon them a rain. So behold how was the end of the guilty!"* (Qur'an 7:84)

The sūrah's own verse, in its brevity, assumes the hearer knows the fuller story and selects only the image that carries the lesson — the stones, the clay, the descent from the sky. The Qur'anic method is here visible in its purest form: the detail is not omitted for lack of knowledge but chosen for its weight, the single image of the clay-stones standing for the whole catastrophe.

**The Sky's Provision and the Sky's Judgment**

The verse also completes a connection the sūrah has carefully built. Earlier the sūrah declared: *"And in the sky is your provision and that which you were promised"* (51:22). The provision descends from the sky as *rain* — water, the mercy that gives life. Now the sky descends upon the guilty as *stones* — the judgment that takes life. The same sky, the same descent, the same verb of "sending" (*arsala / anzala*): the rain and the stones are one divine act seen from two sides, mercy to the earth that receives it in faith and wrath to the earth that receives it in defiance. The sūrah's opening oaths — the clouds that bear the rain (51:2), the winds that apportion it (51:4) — are thus revealed in their full significance: the sky's cargo is not only water but judgment, and the apportioners apportion both.

**The Theology of a Sudden, Material Judgment**

Finally, the verse teaches a truth about the *manner* of divine judgment that the sūrah's Makkan hearers needed above all: the judgment, when it comes, is *material* and *sudden*. The Makkans mocked the idea of a punishment from the unseen; the verse answers that the unseen's punishment is not a metaphor but a physical reality — stones, clay, a city overturned — and that it arrives without warning, at the moment of the command. The Qur'an's doctrine of the Hereafter is built on this same realism: the Fire is as real as the stones, the resurrection as real as the overturning. The story of Lot's people is the sūrah's proof that the threatened judgment is not a preacher's invention but a historical fact, repeated in the record of the ruined cities, and the next verse will add the detail that makes the judgment personal — each stone marked for its target.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:34

> **marked by thy Lord for the prodigal.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Marked Stones: Judgment Made Personal**

The verse completes the angels' announcement with a detail of terrifying precision: *musawwamatan ʿinda rabbika li'l-musrifīn* — "marked by thy Lord for the prodigal." The word *musawwamah* is the passive participle of *sawwama*, "to mark, to brand, to sign," and it describes stones that are not random but *marked* — each one assigned, named, destined for a particular target. The source commentary records the interpretation of al-Ālūsī and al-Jalālayn: each stone bore the name of the person at whom it was to be hurled, so that the judgment was not a general destruction but an individual accounting, the sky raining down not anonymous stones but a stone for each guilty soul, addressed by name. And the word *musrifīn* — "the prodigal" — is the active participle of *asrafa*, "to exceed the bounds, to go beyond every limit," the root *s-r-f* naming the one who has spent his life's capital in excess, who has crossed every line God set and gone on crossing.

**The Individuality of Judgment**

The marking of the stones is the verse's deepest teaching, and it belongs to the Qur'an's doctrine of personal accountability. The judgment on the cities was a single event, but within it every soul was judged individually — the stone that fell on each head was *that* head's own stone, marked by name. The Qur'an's doctrine of the Hereafter is built on the same principle writ large: the resurrection is a mass event, but the reckoning is individual, and no soul bears the burden of another:

> *"And no bearer of burden shall bear the burden of another; and if a heavily laden soul calls for its load to be carried, nothing of it will be carried, even by a near kinsman."* (Qur'an 35:18)

The marked stones are the principle made visible in history: the crowd was destroyed, but the destruction was a million individual sentences, each one delivered to its own address. The modern reader, who fears being lost in the mass of humanity, finds here the Qur'an's constant reassurance and warning — no one is anonymous to God, and no one's judgment is confused with another's.

**The Prodigal: The Word That Names the Crime**

The choice of *musrifūn* — "the prodigal, the excessive" — over other words for the sinners is precise. The word names not a particular sin but a *posture*: the one who exceeds the bounds, who takes the freedom God gave and spends it without limit, who treats the divine limits as obstacles rather than protections. The Qur'an uses the word for the people of Lot in the fuller account:

> *"We shall surely bring down upon the people of this town a punishment from the sky for the iniquity they have been committing."* (Qur'an 29:34)

The *isrāf* — the excess — of Lot's people was their defiance of the natural order and their persistence in it after the warning. The word is chosen because it captures the essential nature of all sin: sin is not merely an error but an *excess*, an overstepping of the bounds that God, in His wisdom, set for the creatures' own good. And the marked stones are the answer to the excess: the ones who would not be bounded by God's limits are, at the end, bounded by His judgment.

**"Marked by Thy Lord": The Possessive That Localizes the Judgment**

The phrase *ʿinda rabbika* — "with thy Lord," "by thy Lord" — deserves its full weight. The angels are speaking to Abraham, and the marking of the stones is said to be *with thy Lord* — the same Lord who just promised Abraham a son, the same Lord whose wisdom and knowledge closed the glad tidings in verse 30. The possessive binds the two halves of the mission into one source: the mercy of the son and the wrath of the stones both issue from *Abraham's Lord*, and the believer is taught to receive both from the same hand. The stones are not the work of a blind fate but of a Lord who marks each one, and the marking is the sign that the judgment is not chaos but order, not rage but justice, executed with the same precision with which the son was promised.

**For the Modern Reader: The Lesson of the Marked Stone**

The verse's warning reaches across the centuries in a single image: no one is forgotten, no one is anonymous, no sin is lost in the crowd. The modern world's deepest comfort — the sense that one can hide in the mass, that the individual is too small for the universe to notice — is here dissolved: the stone is marked, the soul is known, the judgment is personal. And the same truth, turned around, is the believer's deepest consolation: the mercy is personal too, the son was promised to a particular woman by name, and the Lord who marks the stones marks also the reward. The sūrah's two destinies — the gardens for the reverent, the fire for the guilty — are both, at bottom, this single truth: nothing and no one is ever anonymous to God.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:35

> **So We brought forth those among them who were believers;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Deliverance Before Destruction: The Order of the Divine Act**

The narrative shifts from the angels' announcement to God's own telling of the event, in the first-person plural of majesty: *fa-akhrajnā man kāna fīhā mina al-muʾminīn* — "so We brought forth those among them who were believers." The verb *akhrajnā* — "We brought out" — is the verb of extraction and deliverance, and the verse's logic is the logic of divine justice stated in its most basic form: before the judgment falls, the believers are removed. The source commentary states it plainly: God removed the believers in order to spare them from the punishment intended for the disbelievers. The order matters, and the sūrah presents it as fixed: first the extraction, then the destruction — the believer is never left to share the fate of the denier, and the mercy of the Lord reaches into the very city of judgment to pull His own out of it before the stones fall.

**The Principle: No Soul Is Destroyed With the Guilty It Did Not Join**

The verse embodies a principle that runs through the Qur'an's accounts of every judgment: the punishment is *discriminating*. It falls on the guilty and passes over the innocent, because the Judge's knowledge reaches each soul individually — the very point the previous verse made with the marked stones. The Qur'an states the principle explicitly:

> *"And thy Lord would not destroy the towns unjustly while their people were righteous."* (Qur'an 11:117)

> *"And We were not to punish until We had sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

The deliverance of the believers from the doomed cities is the principle in action: even amid the most corrupt people, the righteous are known, counted, and saved. This is the Qur'an's answer to the age-old anxiety of the believer surrounded by a corrupt society — the anxiety that one will be swept away with the flood of judgment. The verse answers: the sweep is selective; the believer is extracted; the corruption of the many does not drag down the few who stood apart.

**The Believers in the Cities of Lot**

Who were these believers? The next verse will name them — "but one house of submitters" (51:36) — and the fuller accounts identify them: Lot himself and his family, his two daughters, the household that believed and followed. The word *muʾminīn* — "believers" — is the Qur'an's term for those who have faith, and its use here is precise: in a city whose sin was the rejection of God's messenger and God's order, the believers were those who accepted the messenger and kept the order. The verse does not tell us their number, because the number is not the point; the point is that they *existed* amid the corruption, that faith survived even there, and that God knew them by name. The Qur'an's histories always preserve this detail — the remnant, the few, the ones who believed when belief was costly — because the remnant is the seed of the mercy that follows the judgment.

**The Root of "Bringing Out": Exodus and Salvation**

The verb *akhraja* — "to bring out" — is the Qur'an's verb of salvation, the same verb by which it describes every great deliverance: the bringing of the Children of Israel out of Egypt, the bringing of Noah's people into the ark, the bringing of Lot's family out of the doomed city. The word is the root of the very idea of *khurūj*, the going-out, and the Qur'an's vocabulary of salvation is built upon it: to be saved is to be *brought out* — out of the city, out of the sea, out of the darkness — by a hand that reaches in from outside the doom. The believer is never saved *in place*; he is always *brought out*, because salvation is a movement, an exodus, a leaving behind of the condemned place. The verse's *akhrajnā* is that exodus in a single word, and the next verse will show where the exodus ended — in the one house of submission that alone was found in the city.

**The Comfort and the Warning in One Verse**

The verse holds both of the sūrah's faces at once. To the believer it is the deepest comfort: the judgment that will one day fall on the world will fall selectively, and the ones God knows will be brought out of it — as Lot's family was brought out, as Noah's ark was raised, as the Prophet's companions were led out of Makkah. To the denier it is a warning wrapped in the same fact: the deliverance of the believers is the sign that the judgment is coming, for the extraction is the first act of the sentence, and the cities were given no second chance once the believers were out. The sūrah's Makkan hearers, who saw the believers as a small, despised remnant in a corrupt city, are being shown their own situation in the mirror of Lot's: the remnant will be brought out, and the city that remains will be judged.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:36

> **yet We did not find therein but one house of submitters.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Remnant Counted: One House in a City of Ruin**

The verse completes the accounting with a number that stuns by its smallness: *fa-mā wajadnā fīhā ghayra baytin mina al-muslimīn* — "yet We did not find therein but one house of submitters." After the believers were brought out, the sūrah tells us what the city had contained: not a congregation, not a community of the faithful, but a single *house* — *bayt*, one household — of *muslimīn*, those who submitted. The word *muslimūn* is the active participle of *aslama*, "to submit," and it names the believers by their essential act: they had *submitted* to God, surrendered their wills, and in a city where every other house had refused that surrender, one house alone had made it. The verse's force lies in the contrast between the two words — *ghayra baytin*, "but one house" — the whole city weighed and found to contain a single household of the surrendered.

**Believers and Submitters: The Two Words and Their Order**

The source commentary draws attention to the sūrah's deliberate sequence: verse 35 spoke of *muʾminīn* — "believers" — and verse 36 speaks of *muslimīn* — "submitters" — and al-Ālūsī explains the progression as a reference to those who combine *faith* and *action*. Faith (*īmān*) is the inner conviction, the assent of the heart; submission (*islām*) is the outward surrender, the obedience of the limbs. The one house was both: it believed in the messenger and it submitted to the command, and the sūrah names the two aspects separately because both were required — a faith that did not submit would not have been saved, and a submission without faith would not have been faith. The verse thus teaches, in its two-word sequence, the Qur'an's complete doctrine of the religious life: the heart's belief and the limbs' surrender, the inner and the outer, the *muʾmin* and the *muslim* joined in the one household that alone was found.

**The House: Lot and His Daughters**

The source commentary identifies the house: Lot and his two daughters, who were saved from the punishment, while Lot's wife was not. The fuller accounts give the detail in full — the command to Lot to depart with his family by night, the prohibition against looking back, and the fate of the wife who disobeyed:

> *"So We saved him and his family, all — save an old woman among those who stayed behind."* (Qur'an 26:170–171)

> *"And We saved him and his family, except his wife; We decreed her to be among those who stayed behind."* (Qur'an 27:57)

The house was thus not saved by blood but by submission: Lot's wife shared his roof but not his faith, and the roof did not save her — the submission did. The detail is the verse's sharpest point: proximity to the righteous is not righteousness, and the judgment that passed over the house of the submitters fell on the member of that very house who had not submitted. No one is carried to safety by another's virtue; the submission must be one's own.

**The Smallness of the Remnant as a Lesson**

The verse's number — *one house* — is itself a teaching, and the sūrah presents it without embarrassment. The truth has never been a matter of majority; the believer is often the remnant, the few, the single household in a city of deniers. The Qur'an repeats this lesson across its histories — Noah's few in the ark, the few who believed with the prophets, the *"few of My servants who are thankful"* (34:13) — and the lesson is the believer's consolation and his warning: he is not to measure truth by its popularity, nor to despair of the truth because its bearers are few. The one house of Lot's city was enough for God to spare, and its fewness was no obstacle to the mercy that reached it; what mattered was not the number but the submission. The sūrah's Makkan hearers, a small community in a city of idolaters, heard in this verse the description of their own situation and their own hope.

**For the Modern Reader: The Loneliness of Faith and Its Honor**

The modern reader, no less than the Makkan, knows the loneliness of conviction — the sense of being one house in a city that has chosen otherwise. The verse honors that loneliness by showing what it has always been worth: the one house was *found* — noticed, counted, saved — by the Lord whose judgment was about to fall on everything around it. The believer is not asked to be many; he is asked to be *muslim*, submitted, and the verse's promise is that the submitted are never overlooked, even when they are one house against a city. The next verse will turn from the saved to the sign they left behind — the ruins of the city, left standing as a warning for all who fear the painful punishment.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:37

> **And We left therein a sign for those who fear the painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ruins Left as a Warning**

The account of the cities closes with the purpose of their remains: *wa taraknā fīhā āyatan li'lladhīna yakhāfūna al-ʿadhāba al-alīm* — "and We left therein a sign for those who fear the painful punishment." The verb *taraknā* — "We left" — is the verb of deliberate preservation: the ruins were not merely abandoned but *left*, kept, displayed, for a purpose. The *āyah* — the sign — is, as the source commentary explains, a double thing: the physical ruins of the overturned cities, still visible on the trade routes of Arabia, and the *story* of the punishment itself, preserved in revelation and passed down through the generations as a warning. The two senses converge: the stones in the sand and the account in the scripture are one sign, the material and the verbal witnesses to the same judgment, left standing so that later generations might read them and fear.

**The Audience Named: Those Who Fear the Painful Punishment**

The verse's final phrase names the sign's intended audience with the sūrah's characteristic precision: *li'lladhīna yakhāfūna al-ʿadhāba al-alīm* — "for those who fear the painful punishment." The sign is not for everyone in the same way; it is for the *fearers* — those whose hearts are capable of the fear that turns into caution. The word *yakhāfūn* is the present-tense participle of *khāfa*, "to fear," and the *ʿadhāb al-alīm* — the painful punishment — is the Qur'an's standard name for the torment of the Hereafter, the same punishment the sūrah's opening threatened to the deniers. The verse thus closes the story of Lot's people by joining it to the sūrah's central warning: the ruins are a sign of the *painful punishment* that awaits every denier, and their purpose is to teach the fear that saves. The one who reads the sign and fears is the one for whom the sign was left; the one who passes it by is the one the sign was never able to reach.

**The Double Sign: Ruins and Revelation**

The source commentary's two senses of the sign are both essential to the Qur'an's method. The *physical* ruins — the cities of the plain, their remains known to the caravan travelers who passed them on the road to Syria — were a standing argument: the ground itself testified that a people had been destroyed, and the traveler who asked "why?" found the answer in the stories that surrounded the ruins. The *verbal* sign — the account preserved in the Qur'an — is the permanent form of the same argument, immune to the erosion that wears down the stones. The Qur'an explicitly joins the two when it points its hearers to the ruins:

> *"And indeed you pass by them in the morning and by night. Will you not then understand?"* (Qur'an 37:137–138)

The ruins and the revelation are one sign in two media, and the sūrah's verse is the statement of their single purpose: to teach the fear that leads to safety.

**Why Fear Is the Sign's Purpose**

The verse's choice of *fear* as the sign's fruit deserves reflection, for the Qur'an is often misread as offering only fear or only hope. The fear named here is not terror for its own sake but the *khashyah* — the reverent fear — that the Qur'an always pairs with knowledge and hope, the fear of a loving heart that dreads nothing so much as the loss of its Lord's pleasure. The sign of the ruins is meant to produce this fear because this fear is the beginning of salvation: the one who fears the painful punishment *avoids* it, and the avoiding is the purpose of the warning. The Qur'an states the sequence explicitly:

> *"And warn thereby those who fear that they shall be gathered unto their Lord."* (Qur'an 6:51)

The ruins are a warning, and the warning works only on the fearer — which is why the verse, like the sūrah as a whole, addresses itself to "those who fear." The sign is not a threat against the hopeless but a mercy to the heedful.

**For the Modern Reader: Reading the Ruins**

The modern reader may never see the physical ruins of the plain, but the *verbal* sign is in his hands, and its purpose is unchanged. The verse invites him to read the story of the destroyed cities not as ancient history but as a present warning — to see in the stones of clay and the overturned town the shape of the painful punishment that the sūrah has been announcing from its first verse, and to let the reading produce the fear that turns the heart back to its Lord while there is still time. The sign was left *for* the fearers, and the question the verse leaves behind is the one the sūrah has been asking throughout: is the reader among those for whom the sign was left? The next verse will move from the cities of Lot to the story of Moses, and the sūrah's chain of precedents will lengthen.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:38

> **And in Moses [We left a sign], when We sent him to Pharaoh with a manifest authority.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain of Signs Continues: Moses**

The sūrah's litany of precedents advances from Lot to Moses: *wa fī Mūsā idh arsalnāhu ilā Firʿawna bi-sulṭānin mubīn* — "and in Moses [We left a sign], when We sent him to Pharaoh with a manifest authority." The verse's opening phrase, *wa fī Mūsā* — "and in Moses" — carries forward the verb of verse 37, so that the sense is "and in Moses We left a sign," the sign being the story itself and its outcome. The name of Moses is the sūrah's doorway to its longest single thread, for Moses is the prophet whose name appears most often in the Qur'an — some one hundred and thirty-six times — and whose story, as the source commentary notes, is featured more prominently than that of any other prophet. The sūrah, like the other Makkan chapters that treat Moses (40:23–45; 43:46–56; 44:17–31; 79:15–25), tells his story not as chronicle but as *example* — a spiritual mirror held up to the Prophet Muhammad's own struggle, in which Pharaoh stands for every tyrant and Moses for every warner.

**"Manifest Authority": The Word and Its Range**

The phrase *bi-sulṭānin mubīn* — "with a manifest authority" — is the verse's key, and the commentators unfold its meanings. The word *sulṭān* derives from the root *s-l-ṭ*, which carries the sense of power, dominion, and the ability to prevail, and in the Qur'an it names both *authority* in the abstract and the *proof* by which authority is established. The source commentary records the two interpretations: first, the *miracles* associated with Moses — the staff that became a serpent, the hand that came forth white, the parting of the sea — which provided a clear indication of the truth of his message; and second, the *authority* that God grants His messengers over whomsoever He will, as the Qur'an states:

> *"And God gives authority (sulṭān) over whomsoever He wills."* (Qur'an 59:6)

The two senses converge: Moses was sent with a *manifest* authority, a power and a proof so clear that no honest observer could mistake them, and the authority was itself the sign that his mission was from God. The adjective *mubīn* — "manifest, clear, self-evident" — is the Qur'an's word for what needs no interpreter, and the pairing *sulṭān mubīn* is the Qur'an's formula for the incontestable proof.

**Pharaoh: The Type of the Denier**

The verse names Moses' opponent, and the name is loaded. Pharaoh is not, in the Qur'an, merely a historical king but the *type* of all who claim divinity against God, the archetype of the ruler who says "I am your lord most high" and demands worship of his subjects. The Qur'an's portrait of Pharaoh is built over many chapters — his oppression of the Israelites, his claim to godhood, his pursuit of Moses to the sea — and the sūrah compresses it into a single encounter: the messenger with the manifest authority against the tyrant with the manifest army. The source commentary notes that Pharaoh's response to Moses — the accusation of "sorcerer or possessed" in the next verse — is the same accusation the Quraysh leveled at the Prophet Muhammad, so that the story of Moses is, at every point, the story of the Prophet's own situation told in advance. The sūrah's method is here at its clearest: history is not recounted for its own sake but as the mirror in which the present hearer sees his own face.

**The Sign "In Moses": What the Story Leaves Behind**

The verse's opening — "and in Moses We left a sign" — names the story itself as a sign, and the sign's content is the whole of the Moses narrative: the deliverance of a people and the drowning of a tyrant, the proof that God's messenger prevails and the denier perishes. The sign is left, like the ruins of Lot's cities, *for those who fear the painful punishment* — and the sūrah's chain is thus building a cumulative case: Abraham's guests, the cities of the plain, and now Moses and Pharaoh, each a sign, each pointing to the same conclusion. The source commentary lists the fuller accounts of Moses for the reader who wishes the complete story (2:49–61; 7:103–55; 10:75–93; 17:101–4; 20:9–97; 26:10–66; 27:7–14; 28:3–46), and the sūrah itself will tell only the two moments that carry its lesson: the accusation of the tyrant and the drowning of his hosts.

**For the Modern Reader: The Persistence of the Type**

The modern reader may think Pharaoh's story belongs to a dead past, but the sūrah presents it as a living type: the *sulṭān mubīn* — the manifest authority of truth — against the *sulṭān* of worldly power, and the outcome that has never once varied. Every age produces its Pharaohs — the powers that claim the loyalty that belongs to God, that answer argument with accusation, that pursue the warner to the sea — and every age's Pharaoh ends as this one ends. The verse's lesson for the modern believer is twofold: the truth's authority is *manifest*, needing no force to be true; and the tyrant's force, however manifest, cannot outrun the judgment that follows it. The next verse will quote the tyrant's accusation, and the hearer will hear in Pharaoh's voice the very words the Prophet's own opponents spoke.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:39

> **But he turned away with his court and said, “A sorcerer or one possessed.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tyrant's Response: Turning Away With His Court**

The verse records Pharaoh's answer to the manifest authority: *fa-tawallā bi-ruknihi wa-qāla sāḥirun aw majnūn* — "but he turned away with his court and said, 'A sorcerer or one possessed.'" The verb *tawallā* is the Qur'an's word for the turning of the back — the deliberate, bodily refusal of the one who will not face the truth — and it is the same verb the sūrah used in verse 9 for the turning away of the denier. And the word *rukn* — rendered here "his court" — is worth its full unpacking, for the source commentary devotes careful attention to it. *Rukn* literally means "a support, a pillar, a corner," the part of a thing upon which it leans and by which it stands; by extension it names what a man *relies upon* — his strength, his allies, his hosts. Al-Shawkānī glosses it as might and strength; al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabarī as "his people," "his companions," the hosts who surrounded him, because he *relies upon them* (yurkinu ʿalayhim) and derives his strength from them. The picture is precise: Pharaoh turned away *leaning on his court* — his back to Moses, his weight on his nobles and his army — the tyrant's classic posture, refusing the truth while propping himself on the power that the truth is about to shatter.

**The Accusation: "A Sorcerer or One Possessed"**

Pharaoh's answer is not an argument but a label: *sāḥirun aw majnūn* — "a sorcerer or one possessed." The two words are the Qur'an's summary of the denier's entire repertoire, and the sūrah's own next section will point out their recurrence: the Quraysh said the same of the Prophet Muhammad — *"A sorcerer or one possessed"* — and so did every people of every warner before them (51:52). The source commentary notes the internal logic: Pharaoh must turn to his court and to the accusation precisely because he *cannot argue* against the proofs Moses has brought. The staff that became a serpent, the hand that came forth white — these were not refutable by argument, and so the tyrant, having no answer, resorts to the smear. The label "sorcerer" is the refuge of the one who has seen the proof and will not accept it: if the miracle is magic, then it demands no submission, and the magician can be dismissed with a word. The label "possessed" is its twin: if the messenger is mad, then his message needs no answer. The two accusations are the denier's escape hatch, and the sūrah quotes them to expose them.

**The Parallel With the Prophet Muhammad's Situation**

The source commentary draws the parallel explicitly, and it is the verse's central purpose in the sūrah's architecture. Pharaoh's accusation of Moses — *"a sorcerer or one possessed"* — is the *same* accusation the Quraysh leveled at the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, recorded throughout the Makkan sūrahs:

> *"And they say, 'O thou upon whom the Reminder has been sent down, truly thou art possessed.'"* (Qur'an 15:6)

> *"And they say, 'He is but a man possessed.'"* (Qur'an 23:25)

The parallel is the sūrah's comfort to the Prophet: he is being shown that his accusers are not original but are repeating, word for word, the accusation of the first tyrant against the first messenger of his kind — that the smear is itself a tradition, passed down from Pharaoh to Quraysh, and that it says nothing about the messenger and everything about the smearing. The Prophet's situation is thus normalized, historicized, and robbed of its power to wound: if Pharaoh said it of Moses, and Moses was God's messenger, then the accusation is not evidence of falsehood but its opposite.

**The Psychology of the Unanswerable and the Smear**

The verse is a study in the psychology of denial that the modern reader will recognize at once. When a truth cannot be answered, it is *labeled*; when an argument cannot be refuted, its speaker is *discredited*; and the stronger the proof, the louder the smear. Pharaoh's "sorcerer or possessed" is the ancestor of every ad hominem, every dismissal of the messenger in place of the message, every time the truth has been met not with an answer but with an insult. The sūrah's exposure of the pattern is its cure: to see that the accusation is the *sign* of the accuser's failure, not of the accused's fault. The believer who is smeared for the truth can read this verse and know that he is in the company of Moses and Muhammad, and that the smear has always been the last weapon of those who have lost the argument.

**For the Modern Reader: Leaning on the Rukn**

The verse's final gift is its picture of the *rukn* — the support on which the denier leans as he turns away. Pharaoh leaned on his court, his army, his throne, and the sea swallowed them all. The modern denier leans on his own versions of the *rukn* — wealth, status, the majority, the consensus of the like-minded — and the sūrah's warning is that the support is not a support at all but a weight that drags down. The one who turns from the truth *with his court* finds, at the end, that the court cannot hold him above the water. The next verse will show the drowning, and the *rukn* — the leaning — will be revealed as the tyrant's final, fatal mistake.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:40

> **So We seized him and his hosts and cast them into the sea, for he was blameworthy.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Judgment on the Tyrant**

The story of Moses closes in a single, swift verse: *fa-akhadhnāhu wa-junūdahu fa-nabadhnāhum fī al-yammi wa-huwa mulīm* — "so We seized him and his hosts and cast them into the sea, for he was blameworthy." The verb *akhadhnā* — "We seized" — is the Qur'an's verb of sudden, inescapable judgment, the same word it uses for the seizure of every ruined people, and the verb *nabadhnā* — "We cast" — carries the force of a flinging, a discarding, as of something worthless thrown away. The word *al-yamm* is the deep sea, and the final clause, *wa-huwa mulīm* — "for he was blameworthy" — states the judgment's cause in a single adjective: *mulīm*, the active participle of *alāma*, "to be blameworthy," the one who has earned the blame, whose deeds have made him deserving of the sentence. The verse is the Qur'an's epitaph for the tyrant, and it is an epitaph of four movements: seize, cast, drown, and the reason why.

**The Parting of the Sea and the Full Account**

The source commentary supplies the fuller narrative that the verse compresses: the casting into the sea refers to the parting of the sea for the Children of Israel and its closing over Pharaoh and his hosts, who were in pursuit — the scene the Qur'an describes in Sūrah al-Baqarah:

> *"And when We parted the sea for you and so delivered you, and drowned the House of Pharaoh as you looked on."* (Qur'an 2:50)

The drowning of Pharaoh and his army is one of the Qur'an's most repeated scenes, told in 7:136, 8:54, 17:102–3, 43:55–56, and 44:22–24, each telling adding a detail: the sea parting into walls, the water returning, the tyrant's last words of belief answered too late. The sūrah's version is the shortest of all, and its brevity is its power: the whole catastrophe — the pursuit, the parting, the closing, the drowning — is compressed into the verb *nabadhnā*, "We cast them," as if the sea were less a battlefield than a hand that opened and closed.

**"Blameworthy": The Word That Names the Verdict**

The final word, *mulīm*, is the verse's theological keystone, and it deserves the emphasis the sūrah gives it by ending the verse there. The word is not "disbelieving" or "oppressive" — though Pharaoh was both — but *blameworthy*, the one upon whom the blame falls and rests. The root *l-w-m* is the root of *lawm*, blame, and of *la nafs al-lawwāmah*, the self-reproaching soul of Sūrah al-Qiyāmah (75:2). The choice of *mulīm* makes the judgment's justice explicit: Pharaoh was not drowned by an arbitrary power but *because he was blameworthy* — the blame was his, the sentence followed the blame, and the drowning was the execution of a verdict he had authored. The Qur'an's doctrine of requital is here stated with the utmost economy: the seizure is always explained, and the explanation is always the same — *for he was blameworthy* — because God does not wrong, but the wrongdoer wrongs himself.

**The Pattern of Every Tyrant's End**

The verse is placed in the sūrah's chain of precedents to make a point that the chain has been building: every denier ends the same way. Lot's people were overturned and stoned; Pharaoh was seized and drowned; and the sūrah will now add ʿĀd and Thamūd and Noah — a catalogue of ruins, each ending in the same verb of seizure, each closed by the same verdict of blameworthiness. The chain is the sūrah's answer to the Makkan scoffer who asked "when is the Day of Judgment?": the judgment is not a future hypothesis but a past fact, repeated so often that the ground of the earth is a museum of its precedents. The tyrant's hosts — the *rukn* on which he leaned — are revealed in this verse as no support at all: the army that gave Pharaoh his confidence became the weight that pulled him to the sea floor, and the leaning of the previous verse is answered by the drowning of this one.

**For the Modern Reader: The Verdict That Outlives the Throne**

The modern reader, living in an age that has seen its own Pharaohs — the tyrants of the last century, each with his court and his hosts and his claim to final power — may read this verse as the Qur'an's permanent commentary on their end. The *rukn* always fails; the sea always closes; the verdict is always *mulīm*. And the verse's deeper lesson is for the individual: the blameworthiness it names is not reserved for Pharaohs but is the condition of every soul that turns from the manifest truth and leans on what cannot hold it. The sūrah's chain now passes from the tyrant of Egypt to the tribes of Arabia — ʿĀd and the barren wind, Thamūd and the thunderbolt — and the catalogue of the blameworthy grows, each entry a sign left for those who fear the painful punishment.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

> **And in ʿĀd [We left a sign], when We sent upon them the barren wind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain Moves to Arabia: The Tribe of ʿĀd**

The litany of precedents now turns from Egypt to Arabia's own pre-Islamic tribes: *wa fī ʿĀdin idh arsalnā ʿalayhimu al-rīḥa al-ʿaqīm* — "and in ʿĀd [We left a sign], when We sent upon them the barren wind." The verse's opening, *wa fī ʿĀdin* — "and in ʿĀd" — again carries the verb of verse 37, so that the sense is "and in ʿĀd We left a sign," the sign being the tribe's story and its ruins. ʿĀd was the first of the great Arabian peoples the Qur'an holds up as an example — a tribe of enormous physical strength, builders of high towers, dwellers in the sands of al-Aḥqāf, who were sent the prophet Hūd and rejected him. The source commentary points to the fuller accounts: 7:65–72, 11:50–60, 41:15–16, and 54:18–21. The sūrah, true to its method, selects only the single image that carries the lesson — the *wind* — and the word it chooses for that wind is the verse's whole point.

**The Barren Wind: The Word ʿAqīm and Its Meaning**

The wind is described as *al-ʿaqīm* — "the barren" — and the word is chosen with the Qur'an's exacting precision. *ʿAqīm* is the word for the barren woman, the womb that bears no child, and by extension it names anything that produces nothing, that gives no benefit, that brings forth no fruit. The source commentary explains the choice: this wind was *barren* because it carried no rain and pollinated no trees — unlike the winds the sūrah opened with, the scatterers and bearers that fertilize the earth, this wind passed over the land and left it dead. Al-Ālūsī and al-Jalālayn both record the point, and the contrast with the sūrah's opening is deliberate and devastating: the same wind that is the earth's midwife — scattering the seed, bearing the rain — was, for ʿĀd, a barren destroyer, a wind that brought forth only death. The word *ʿaqīm* binds the two ends of the sūrah: the wind is a servant of God, and the same servant that gives life to the grateful becomes the executioner of the defiant.

**The Winds the Sūrah Contrasts**

The sūrah's architecture places this verse against its first four, and the contrast is the verse's deepest teaching. The opening oaths swore by the winds that *scatter* and *bear* and *apportion* — the winds of mercy, through which the dead earth is revived. Now the sūrah shows the wind's other face: the *barren* wind, sent not to revive but to destroy. The two faces of the wind are one fact: the created order serves God, and its service is mercy or wrath according to the receiving. The Qur'an states the principle in Sūrah al-Rūm, where the same wind is described as both bringer of glad tidings and instrument of punishment:

> *"And among His signs is that He sends the winds as bearers of glad tidings, to let you taste of His mercy, and that the ships may sail by His command... and if We send a wind and they see it turn yellow, they become ungrateful after that."* (Qur'an 30:46, 51)

The wind of ʿĀd is that second wind — the mercy withdrawn, the servant turned executioner — and the sūrah's hearer is made to see that the very forces he relies upon for life are the forces God can turn against the denier.

**The Tribe of ʿĀd and Their Sin**

The source commentary supplies the background the sūrah compresses: ʿĀd was a people of giants and builders, and their sin was the sin of the powerful — arrogance, the worship of false gods, and the rejection of their prophet Hūd. The Qur'an records their boast and its answer:

> *"As for ʿĀd, they waxed proud upon the earth without right, and said, 'Who is mightier than we in strength?' Did they not see that God, Who created them, is mightier than they in strength?"* (Qur'an 41:15)

The wind was the answer to the boast: the tribe that said "who is mightier than we?" was met by a force that no strength could resist — the invisible wind, against which the mightiest body is a dry leaf. The lesson is the sūrah's constant one: strength is not in the arms but in the favor of God, and the wind that a man cannot see or seize can lay waste to the strongest people on earth in a handful of days.

**For the Modern Reader: The Wind's Two Faces**

The modern reader, who lives in a world of weather forecasts and climate science, may read this verse as the Qur'an's reminder that the forces of nature are not neutral but *governed* — servants of a Lord who sends the rain and can withhold it, who sends the breeze and can send the storm. The barren wind of ʿĀd is not a myth but a permanent possibility, written into the very forces the modern world has learned to predict but not to command. And the verse's moral is the sūrah's throughout: the same provision that sustains the thankful becomes the judgment of the thankless, and the difference is never in the wind but in the hearts that receive it. The next verse will show the wind's work in full, and the image of the bones decayed will complete the picture of what the barren wind leaves behind.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

> **Naught did it leave that it came upon, but that it made it as bones decayed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wind's Total Work**

The verse completes the picture of the barren wind's destruction: *mā tadharu min shayʾin atat ʿalayhi illā jaʿalathu ka'l-ramīm* — "naught did it leave that it came upon, but that it made it as bones decayed." The construction is absolute and total: the wind left *nothing* — not a dwelling, not a field, not a living thing — upon which it came, except that it reduced it to *al-ramīm*. The word *ramīm* is the decayed bone, the bone that has crumbled to powder, the residue of a thing after the flesh and the strength and the shape are gone — the same word the Qur'an uses for the skeptic's objection to the resurrection, *"Who shall revive the bones when they are decayed (ramīm)?"* (36:78). The irony is deliberate and sharp: the same word the denier uses to dismiss the resurrection as impossible — "bones decayed" — is here the very image of what God's judgment leaves of the denier's own people. The wind reduced ʿĀd to the *ramīm* they thought could never be revived — and the One who can make the decayed bone live is the One who made the living tribe into it.

**The Contrast With the Resurrection's Logic**

This irony is the verse's deepest point, and the sūrah plants it carefully. The Makkan deniers argued: "when we are bones decayed, how can we be resurrected?" The Qur'an answers by turning the image back upon them: God's power over the *ramīm* is absolute in both directions — He can reduce the mighty to decayed bones in a wind's passage, and He can raise the decayed bones to life at a word. The same root of power that destroyed ʿĀd is the power that will resurrect them and their like for the judgment. The verse thus folds the story of ʿĀd into the sūrah's central argument: the resurrection is not harder than the destruction, and the destruction that the hearer can verify in the ruins of ʿĀd is the proof of the power that will perform the resurrection. The wind that made bones decayed is the wind's Creator demonstrating, in history, exactly the power the denier says He lacks.

**The Wind in the Fuller Accounts**

The source commentary cross-references the other descriptions of this wind, and together they build the complete picture. In Sūrah Fuṣṣilat the wind is called *ṣarṣar* — a howling wind — sent upon them for days of ill omen:

> *"So We sent upon them a howling wind in days of ill omen, to let them taste the punishment of disgrace in the life of this world."* (Qur'an 41:16)

In Sūrah al-Qamar it is the same:

> *"Truly We sent upon them a howling wind on a day of continuous calamity."* (Qur'an 54:19)

And in Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah the description reaches its fullest intensity:

> *"So as for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a howling, raging wind, which He imposed upon them for seven nights and eight days in succession, so that you could see the people therein lying low, as if they were stumps of hollow palm trees."* (Qur'an 69:6–7)

The sūrah's own version, with its *ramīm*, is the shortest and most concentrated of all — the whole catastrophe compressed into the single image of the decayed bone, the end-point of every living thing that defied its Lord.

**The Lesson of the Total Judgment**

The verse's absoluteness — *mā tadharu min shayʾ* — "it left nothing" — carries a theological weight the sūrah wants its hearer to feel. The judgment on ʿĀd was not partial or negotiable; the wind came upon *everything* in its path, and the tribe's strength, their towers, their numbers, their boasts — all of it was reduced to the same residue. The Qur'an's doctrine of judgment is here stated in its starkest form: when the command comes, there is no fortress, no wealth, no might that the command cannot reduce. And the same totality, turned to mercy, is the believer's hope: the resurrection will be equally total, equally certain, equally beyond the power of any creature to prevent. The verse's single word *ramīm* holds both the warning and the hope — the decay is total, and so is the revival.

**For the Modern Reader: The End of Every Boast**

The modern reader, in an age of unprecedented human power, may find the verse a needed correction: the boast of ʿĀd — "who is mightier than we?" — is the boast of every age that trusts its towers and its technology, and the barren wind is the standing answer — the invisible force that no might can resist, the reminder that human strength is held on loan from the Lord of the winds. The verse does not call for the fear of the elements but for the fear of their Lord, and its lesson is the sūrah's throughout: the same world that sustains us is the world that will judge us, and the decayed bone is the destination of every body until the Lord who decayed it calls it back. The next verse will pass from ʿĀd to Thamūd, and the catalogue of the judged will lengthen.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:43

> **And in Thamūd [We left a sign], when it was said unto them, “Enjoy yourselves for a time.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain Reaches Thamūd**

The litany continues to the second great Arabian tribe: *wa fī Thamūda idh qīla lahum tamattaʿū ḥattā ḥīn* — "and in Thamūd [We left a sign], when it was said unto them, 'Enjoy yourselves for a time.'" The opening again carries the verb of verse 37 — "and in Thamūd We left a sign" — and the sign is the tribe's story: Thamūd, the people of the prophet Ṣāliḥ, who carved their dwellings into the rock of al-Ḥijr, to whom God sent the she-camel as a sign, and who hamstrung her in defiance. The source commentary points to the fuller accounts — 7:73–79, 11:61–68, 26:141–58, and 54:23–31 — and the sūrah, as with ʿĀd, selects a single moment: the word spoken to them after their crime, the ironic grant of respite, *"enjoy yourselves for a time."*

**The She-Camel and the Crime That Preceded the Word**

The word "enjoy yourselves" was spoken to Thamūd *after* their defining crime, and the sūrah assumes its hearer knows the story the source commentary supplies: God sent Thamūd a she-camel as a sign, a test of their obedience, and they hamstrung her, killing the very sign God had set among them. The Qur'an records the command and the crime:

> *"And to Thamūd [We sent] their brother Ṣāliḥ. He said, 'O my people, worship God; you have no god other than He. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is the she-camel of God, a sign unto you. So leave her to graze upon God's earth, and touch her not with harm, lest a painful punishment seize you.'"* (Qur'an 7:73)

> *"But they hamstrung her, and so became regretful."* (Qur'an 26:157)

It was after this hamstringing that the word of the verse was spoken — *"enjoy yourselves in your dwellings for three days; that is a promise not to be belied"* (11:65) — the grant of a final, numbered respite before the punishment. The sūrah's "for a time" (*ḥattā ḥīn*) is that numbered period, and the word "enjoy" is its bitter irony.

**The Irony of "Enjoy": The Word Turned Against Its Hearers**

The verb *tamattaʿū* — "enjoy yourselves" — is the verse's whole point, and the source commentary unfolds its irony with care. The word is, in the Qur'an, sometimes a genuine grant — the invitation to enjoy God's lawful provision, as in *"Eat and drink of God's provision"* (2:60) — but here, spoken to a people already condemned, it is the most terrible of ironies: they are told to enjoy themselves *because* the enjoyment is about to end, and the respite is the prelude to the seizure. The Qur'an's doctrine of this world is compressed into the single word: the life of this world is *"naught but the enjoyment of delusion"* (3:185; 57:20), and the enjoyment of the condemned is the sharpest form of that delusion. The source commentary points to the fullest statement of the irony in Sūrah al-Mursalāt, where the guilty are told in the Hereafter:

> *"Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; truly you are guilty."* (Qur'an 77:46)

The "enjoy" of this world and the "enjoy" of the Hereafter are one irony: the condemned are granted their pleasure as the final proof of their guilt, and the pleasure is revealed, at the end, to have been the bait of the trap.

**The Theology of the Respite**

The verse also teaches the Qur'an's doctrine of the *imlāʾ* — the granting of respite, the letting-out of the rope. The condemned are not always struck at the moment of their crime; often they are *granted time* — "enjoy yourselves for a time" — and the grant is itself a test and a mercy: a test, because the respite shows whether the sinner will turn back; a mercy, because the door of repentance remains open until the final moment. The Qur'an states the doctrine explicitly:

> *"And let not those who disbelieve think that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin, and theirs is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

Thamūd's three days were that respite in miniature: time given after the crime, and the time only confirmed what the crime had already shown — that they would not turn back. The sūrah's Makkan hearers, who enjoyed their own respite from the judgment, are being shown the meaning of the delay: it is not neglect but patience, and the patience has a fixed end.

**For the Modern Reader: The Danger of the Uninterrupted Good**

The modern reader lives in the midst of the respite — the time in which enjoyment continues and judgment delays — and the verse's warning is addressed precisely to that condition. The danger of the granted time is that it is mistaken for immunity: the longer the rope, the more the condemned feels safe, until the rope's end arrives. The verse's lesson is the counterweight: every day of uninterrupted enjoyment is a day of the *ḥattā ḥīn* — "until a time" — a day drawn from a numbered account, and the wise one is he who reads the respite as an opportunity for return, not as a promise of safety. The next verse will show the end of Thamūd's three days, and the thunderbolt will make the irony of "enjoy yourselves" complete.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:44

> **Then they insolently defied the Command of their Lord, and the thunderbolt seized them as they looked on.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crime and the Seizure in One Verse**

The verse joins Thamūd's final crime to its punishment: *fa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim fa-akhadhat-humu al-ṣāʿiqatu wa-hum yanẓurūn* — "then they insolently defied the Command of their Lord, and the thunderbolt seized them as they looked on." The verb *ʿataw* is from the root *ʿ-t-w*, which names insolence, arrogance, the overstepping that refuses to be bound — the Qur'an's word for the defiant pride that answers a command with contempt. The object of their defiance is named with the sūrah's most important possessive: *amr rabbihim* — "the Command of *their Lord*" — the same Lord who sent the she-camel, the same Lord whose command the whole sūrah has been showing as the order that governs the winds and the stars and the seas. And the punishment is named with precision: *al-ṣāʿiqah*, the thunderbolt — the lightning-blast, the cry, the overwhelming stroke — which *seized them as they looked on*, the phrase *wa-hum yanẓurūn* painting the final terrible detail: they saw it coming, and the seeing did not save them.

**The Many Names of One Destruction**

The source commentary addresses a question the attentive reader will raise: the Qur'an describes Thamūd's destruction in several different terms, and the sūrah itself chooses the thunderbolt. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf it is an *earthquake*:

> *"So the earthquake seized them, and morning found them lying lifeless in their abode."* (Qur'an 7:78)

In Sūrah Hūd it is the *Cry*:

> *"And the Cry seized those who did wrong, and morning found them lying lifeless in their homes."* (Qur'an 11:67)

In Sūrah Fuṣṣilat it is again the thunderbolt:

> *"And as for Thamūd, We guided them, but they preferred blindness over guidance; so the thunderbolt of a humiliating punishment seized them."* (Qur'an 41:17)

And in Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah the destruction is named with a single encompassing word:

> *"As for Thamūd, they were destroyed by the overwhelming (al-ṭāghiyah)."* (Qur'an 69:5)

The source commentary resolves the apparent contradiction with the classical scholars' characteristic wisdom: these are not contradictory accounts but *different aspects of one overwhelming event* — the thunderbolt's flash, the earthquake's shaking, the cry's sound, all of them facets of the single *ṭāghiyah*, the overwhelming, that befell them. A great destruction is seldom one thing; it is a flash and a sound and a shaking at once, and each verse names the facet that its context requires.

**"As They Looked On": The Irony of the Open Eyes**

The phrase *wa-hum yanẓurūn* — "as they looked on" — is the verse's sharpest stroke, and it completes the irony the previous verse began. Thamūd were granted three days of enjoyment; they knew the deadline; they had been told the punishment would come; and when it came, they were *looking* — wide awake, watching, perhaps having gathered to see what would happen — and the looking was useless. The phrase is the Qur'an's way of saying that knowledge of the punishment's approach is not protection against it: the ones who saw it coming were not saved by the seeing, because seeing without believing is the worst blindness of all. The word *yanẓurūn* is the verb of the eye's glance, not the insight's recognition — the same distinction the sūrah drew in verse 21's *tubṣirūn* — and the verse thus closes the story of Thamūd with the sūrah's constant lesson: the eye that looks without seeing the sign is the eye that watches its own destruction without averting it.

**The Defiance and Its Root**

The verse's first half deserves equal weight, for it names the *cause* of the destruction with the same precision the previous verse named the crime of ʿĀd. Thamūd's sin was not ignorance but *defiance* — *ʿataw*, the insolent overstepping of a known command. They had been guided; the she-camel had been sent as a proof; the command had been plain — and they answered it not with doubt but with contempt, hamstringing the sign and challenging the warning. The Qur'an's doctrine of judgment is here at its most exact: the punishment falls not on the mistaken but on the *defiant*, on those who knew the command and set themselves against it. The word *ʿataw* is the sūrah's diagnosis of the denier's final state — not the confusion of verse 8 but the hardened defiance of verse 44 — and the thunderbolt is its answer.

**For the Modern Reader: The Deadline We All See**

The modern reader, no less than Thamūd, lives under a deadline that he knows and ignores — the certainty of death, the certainty of the accounting, the certainty that the respite of "enjoy yourselves" has a fixed end. The verse's warning is that the *knowing* is not the saving: the ones who "looked on" were the ones who were destroyed, and the looking was the proof that they had seen the sign and refused it. The lesson is the sūrah's throughout: it is not enough to watch the warning approach; the only safety is in turning before it arrives. The next verse will show the aftermath — the people who could not rise, who could not help one another — and the catalogue of the judged will turn toward its oldest entry, the people of Noah.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:45

> **So they were not able to rise; nor could they help one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Aftermath: The Inability to Rise or Help**

The verse completes the picture of Thamūd's end: *fa-mā istaṭāʿū min qiyāmin wa-mā kānū muntaṣirīn* — "so they were not able to rise; nor could they help one another." The first clause, *mā istaṭāʿū min qiyāmin*, is a negated phrase of total inability — "they had no power of any rising" — and the second, *mā kānū muntaṣirīn* — "nor were they able to help themselves, nor to be helped" — closes the account with the same totality. The source commentary supplies the picture the verse compresses: the people of Thamūd were unable to flee, unable to rise from the place where the seizure struck them, because of the force with which it came — the same picture as Sūrah al-Aʿrāf:

> *"So the earthquake seized them, and morning found them lying lifeless in their abode."* (Qur'an 7:78)

The two clauses are the Qur'an's final word on every destroyed people: the judged are struck *down* — they cannot rise — and they are struck *alone* — no ally, no god, no host can help them. The two negations are the sum of the denier's end.

**The Grammar of Total Powerlessness**

The verse's grammar does its own work, and the commentators draw it out. The first clause uses *istaṭāʿū* — the tenth form of *ṭāʿa*, "to be able, to have the power" — negated with *mā*, and the construction with *min* ("of rising") is the Arabic idiom of the *partial negation extended to totality*: "they had not even *some* of the ability to rise," which is the strongest way Arabic has of saying "they had none at all." The second clause, *wa-mā kānū muntaṣirīn*, uses the past of *kāna* — "they were not" — with the participle *muntaṣirīn*, "ones who help themselves or seek victory," so that the negation covers their whole condition: not only did they fail to be helped, they *were not* the kind who could be — the power of self-help was struck from them in the same stroke that struck them down. The verse's grammar is the grammar of the final fact: when the judgment falls, the judged have no standing and no support.

**The Collapse of Every Rukn**

The verse is the sūrah's conclusion to the theme it began with Pharaoh's *rukn*. Pharaoh leaned on his court and hosts, and the sea swallowed them; Thamūd leaned on their carved dwellings, their strength, their defiance, and the thunderbolt left them unable to rise or to help one another. The two negations of this verse are the sūrah's answer to every false support: the *rukn* — whatever a man leans on against God — is revealed, at the moment of judgment, to be no support at all. The Qur'an states the principle in its most general form:

> *"And they have taken gods besides God, that they might be a might for them. Nay! They will deny their worship, and they will be against them as adversaries."* (Qur'an 19:81–82)

The gods, the hosts, the towers, the wealth — all of it is present until the moment of the seizure, and absent from that moment on. The verse's two negations are the epitaph of every *rukn*: it cannot raise the fallen, and it cannot be raised to help them.

**The Sūrah's Catalogue Approaching Its End**

With Thamūd's aftermath, the sūrah's catalogue of the judged is nearly complete: the cities of Lot, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd — and one more, the oldest of all, still to come. The chain has been building a cumulative case, and this verse is its repeated refrain: each people ends in the same two negations — unable to rise, unable to be helped. The repetition is the argument: the judgment is not a single accident of history but a *law*, and the law's operation is identical in every age and against every strength. The sūrah's Makkan hearers, who trusted their own *rukn* — their wealth, their numbers, their idols — are being shown, in four swift histories, exactly what that trust is worth. The next verse will reach back before all of them, to Noah, and the catalogue will be complete.

**For the Modern Reader: The Two Negations and the One Support**

The modern reader may test the verse against his own supports — the systems, the networks, the insurances, the powers that promise to raise the fallen and help the helpless — and find that the verse's two negations are the truth about them all at the final hour. The Qur'an's lesson is not that one should abandon every worldly support but that one should know what they are: instruments, not guarantees, and no substitute for the one Support that does not fall. The verse's two negations are the negative image of the sūrah's central command, which will be spoken in a few verses: *flee unto God* — the only rising and the only help that the seizure cannot strike down. The next verse turns to Noah, and then the sūrah will turn, at last, from the judged to the Judge.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:46

> **As for the people of Noah before, truly they were an iniquitous people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Catalogue Reaches Its Oldest Entry**

The chain of precedents closes, as it should, with the first of all the judgments: *wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn* — "and as for the people of Noah before, truly they were an iniquitous people." The phrase *min qablu* — "before" — marks Noah's people as the *earliest* of the sūrah's catalogue, the ancestors of every destruction that followed, the people whose story stands at the head of the Qur'an's history of judgment. The sūrah's order is now revealed as a deliberate descent through time: from Abraham and Lot, to Moses and Pharaoh, to ʿĀd and Thamūd, and finally back to Noah, who *preceded* all of them — the first prophet sent to a people who defied him, the first nation drowned for its defiance. The word *fāsiqīn* — "iniquitous" — is the root *f-s-q*, which names the one who *steps out* of the path of obedience, who breaks through the bounds God has set, the same word the Qur'an uses for Iblīs's refusal to bow — *"he was of the jinn, and he stepped out (fasaqa) of the command of his Lord"* (18:50). Noah's people were *fāsiqūn*: they had stepped out, and the flood was their reaping.

**The Story the Verse Compresses**

The source commentary points to the fuller accounts of Noah for the reader who wishes the complete story — 11:25–48, 23:23–30, 26:105–21, and the whole of Sūrah Nūḥ (71) — and the sūrah, true to its method, compresses the story to its single essential fact: the people of Noah were iniquitous, and they were destroyed. The fuller accounts tell of Noah's long warning — nine hundred and fifty years of calling his people to God — and of their refusal, their mockery, and their demand that he bring the punishment he promised. The Qur'an records their taunt:

> *"They said, 'O Noah, thou hast disputed with us, and multiplied thy disputation with us. So bring us that which thou dost promise us, if thou art among the truthful.'"* (Qur'an 11:32)

The same taunt as the sūrah's own verse 12 — "when is the Day of Judgment?" — and the same answer: the flood. The sūrah's compression is the Qur'an's pedagogy: the hearer who knows the story needs only the word *fāsiqīn* to feel the whole of it, and the hearer who does not know it is sent to the fuller accounts.

**Why Noah Is Placed Last: The Architecture of the Catalogue**

The placement of Noah *after* ʿĀd and Thamūd, though he preceded them in time, is a deliberate stroke of the sūrah's design, and the source commentary notes it: Noah, who preceded all the aforementioned tribes and peoples, is mentioned last, as if the sūrah were drilling down through the ages to the very root of the judgment's history. The effect is cumulative and climactic: each story has been a sign, and the final sign is the *first* — the one that proves the judgment is not a recent invention but the oldest law of history, older than Egypt and Arabia, reaching back to the world's first drowned nation. The catalogue thus ends where the history of judgment begins, and the sūrah's hearer is made to feel the full weight of the precedent: the destruction of the deniers is as old as humanity's first defiance, and it has never once failed to follow.

**The Word "Iniquitous" and the Root of Every Judgment**

The choice of *fāsiqīn* as the verse's single descriptive word deserves its full weight. The sūrah has used a different word for each judged people — *mujrimīn* (the guilty) for Lot's people, *mulīm* (the blameworthy) for Pharaoh, the *ʿataw* (insolence) of Thamūd — and for Noah's people it chooses *fāsiqīn*, the *steppers-out*. The word gathers the meaning of all the others into its root: every sin, at bottom, is a *fisq*, a stepping out of the path, a breaking of the bounds, and the flood is the answer to the stepping-out. The word is also the Qur'an's link between the judgments of history and the judgment of the Hereafter, for the same root describes those who will be asked in the next life about the prophets they defied. The sūrah's catalogue of words for sin — crime, blame, insolence, iniquity — is itself a theology: the denier's condition has many names, but one essence, and one end.

**For the Modern Reader: The First Judgment and the Last**

The modern reader may read the verse as the Qur'an's summary of its own argument: the judgment of the deniers is not a threat but a *record*. The people of Noah were the first, and the catalogue that follows them — Lot, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd — is the proof that the law of judgment has operated without exception from the beginning of history to its latest entries. The sūrah has now finished its histories, and it will turn from the judged to the Judge — from the ruins to the heavens and the earth that declare Him, and to the command that the histories were assembled to enforce: *flee unto God*. The catalogue of the *fāsiqūn* is complete, and the sūrah's next movement will crown it with the signs of the Creator's power and the summons to the one refuge that never fails.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:47

> **And the sky We established with might; truly We make vast!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return to the Signs: The Sky Established**

The sūrah, having finished its histories, returns to the signs with which it began: *wa'l-samāʾa banaynāhā bi-aydin wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn* — "and the sky We established with might; truly We make vast!" The verb *banaynā* — "We built" — is the Qur'an's word for the creation of the heavens as an act of *construction*, of raising a structure; the same root *b-n-y* gives *bināʾ*, a building, and the Qur'an repeatedly describes the sky as a *bināʾ* raised without visible pillars:

> *"God it is Who raised the heavens without pillars that you see."* (Qur'an 13:2)

The phrase *bi-aydin* — "with might" — is the verse's first key: the word *ayd* (the *yad* of strength, the "hand" of power) names the strength by which the vast structure was raised, and the commentators read it as the might that no material, no pillar, no support could supply — the heavens stand by God's power alone. The verse thus opens the sūrah's final movement by re-establishing the premise the oaths first swore: the sky is a *built* thing, and its builder is mighty.

**"Truly We Make Vast": The Expansive Provision**

The verse's second half, *wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn* — "and truly We are the vast-makers," "We make vast" — has been the subject of the sūrah's most famous and most discussed interpretation. The word *mūsiʿūn* is the active participle of *awsaʿa*, "to make wide, to expand, to enlarge," and the classical commentators read it in two ways. The first, recorded by al-Ālūsī and al-Shawkānī, is that it describes God's *power*: He is the One who makes vast, who expands the heavens in their unimaginable extent, the One whose creation is a continuous act of enlargement — and many modern readers have seen in this verse an anticipation of the expanding universe that later cosmology would describe. The second reading, equally classical, takes it as God's *generosity*: He makes vast His provision, He enlarges His bounty to the creatures, He is the One who spreads abundance so wide that no creature is excluded. Both readings are true to the root and to the context, and the source commentary preserves both: the *mūsiʿ* is the One whose creation is vast and whose giving is vast — the sky itself is the standing image of both, an expanse that no eye can compass and a provision that no account can exhaust.

**The Contrast With the Destroyed Nations**

The verse is placed after the histories with a deliberate purpose: the sūrah has just shown the *narrowing* — the destruction of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh, the cities of Lot, each a people whose world was *closed in* upon them by judgment — and now it turns to the *vastness* of the Creator's works. The contrast is the sūrah's quiet argument: the same God who makes vast, who expands the sky and spreads the provision, is the God who narrows the denier's world to a drowned sea or a thunderstruck plain. The *mūsiʿūn* and the *muḍayyiqūn* are one God, and the sūrah's hearer is made to feel the difference between the two faces of His power: the vastness of His mercy and the narrowness of His judgment. The verse thus functions as the hinge of the sūrah's final section — the return from the ruins to the Builder, from the narrowing of the judged to the vastness of the Judge.

**The Sky as a Sign of Sustaining Power**

The verse also resumes the sūrah's opening theology of the sky, and the connection is exact. The sūrah began with the winds that scatter and the clouds that bear, and swore by them that the promise is true; it then declared, in verse 22, that *"in the sky is your provision and that which you were promised."* Now the sky itself is named as a *built* thing, raised with might and expanded by the Expander, so that the provision and the promise both rest on the same foundation — the sustaining power of the One who holds the sky up and spreads it wide. The believer is thus given the sūrah's full cosmology: the sky is not a void but a *structure*, not a given but a *gift*, and the One who raised it with might is the One who will lower it at the Hour and raise the dead from beneath it.

**For the Modern Reader: The Vastness and the Builder**

The modern reader, who has seen the heavens through the telescope's eye — the billions of galaxies, the expanding fabric of space, the vastness that dwarfs every human measure — may find in this verse the Qur'an's invitation to see that vastness not as a rebuttal of the Creator but as His signature. The word *mūsiʿūn* — "We make vast" — has, for the modern reader, a resonance the classical commentators could not have known but which the word itself always contained: the creation is an *ongoing* expansion, and its expansion is the work of the Expander. And the word *bi-aydin* — "with might" — is the answer to the modern tendency to see the universe as self-sustaining: the structure stands by a power, and the power is the sūrah's constant subject. The next verse will descend from the sky to the earth, and the image of the cradle will complete the picture of the two great signs.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:48

> **And the earth We laid out—what excellent spreaders!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earth Spread as a Cradle**

The descent from sky to earth is completed: *wa'l-arḍa farashnāhā fa-niʿma al-māhidūn* — "and the earth We laid out — what excellent spreaders!" The verb *farashnā* — "We spread, We laid out" — is the root of *firāsh*, the bed, the carpet, the thing spread for rest, and the Qur'an uses the image of the bed for the earth repeatedly:

> *"And God has made the earth for you a spread (bisāṭan)."* (Qur'an 71:19)

> *"Have We not made the earth a cradle (mihādan)?"* (Qur'an 78:6)

The earth is thus described as a thing *prepared* — spread flat, softened, made habitable — not a random rock but a *bed* laid for the creatures who sleep upon it. And the verse's second half, *fa-niʿma al-māhidūn* — "what excellent spreaders!" — is the Qur'an's rare and beautiful form of self-praise: the plural *al-māhidūn* is the plural of majesty, referring to God Himself, and the exclamation *niʿma* — "how excellent!" — is the praise of the work through the praise of its Worker.

**The Word Māhidūn and Its Family**

The word *māhidūn* — "spreaders, preparers" — is the active participle of *mahada*, "to spread, to make smooth, to prepare," and the source commentary draws out its family, for the same root gives *mahd*, the cradle — the prepared place where an infant is laid — and the Qur'an links the earth to the cradle explicitly:

> *"He it is Who made the earth a cradle (mahdan) for you, and made paths for you therein, that haply you may be guided."* (Qur'an 43:10)

> *"Did We not make the earth a receptacle, for the living and the dead?"* (Qur'an 77:25–26)

The image is tender and precise: the earth is not merely a place but a *preparation*, a cradle prepared for the human race before its arrival, spread by a Worker who prepared it well. The exclamation *fa-niʿma al-māhidūn* — "what excellent spreaders!" — is the Qur'an's verdict on that preparation: the earth, with its soil for the seed, its paths for the traveler, its beds for the dead and the living, is a work of the finest craftsmanship, and the praise belongs to its Maker.

**The Plural of Majesty and the Praise of the Worker**

The grammar of the verse deserves a pause, for the source commentary notes it explicitly. The sentence is in the first-person plural throughout — *farashnāhā*, "We spread it" — and the praise *al-māhidūn* is a plural participle applied to God, the "plural of majesty" by which Arabic honors a singular agent of supreme dignity. The effect is the verse's own: the earth's excellence is so great that its Maker is praised in the plural of majesty, as a king is addressed in the plural, and the exclamation "what excellent spreaders!" is the Qur'an's way of teaching the believer to praise the Maker *through* the work — to see the earth's habitability and say, of its Maker, *niʿma al-māhidūn*. The verse is thus a model of the believer's posture toward creation: not to worship the work but to praise the Worker through it, and to find in the earth's excellence the signature of His excellence.

**The Wisdom and Generosity in the Spreading**

The source commentary, citing al-Ālūsī and al-Shawkānī, explains the two things the verse's praise implies: the *wisdom* behind the manner in which the earth was created, and the *generosity* in doing so. The earth's spreading is wise because it is exactly fitted to its inhabitants — the soil to the seed, the slope to the water's flow, the firmness to the builder's foundation — and generous because it was prepared *for* the creatures, laid out for their benefit without their earning it or even asking. The verse thus joins the two names the sūrah has been weaving together: the wisdom of the Maker who designs, and the generosity of the Provider who gives. The earth is the standing evidence of both — a gift so well made that its recipients forget it is a gift, and so the sūrah's next verse will call them to *remember*.

**For the Modern Reader: The Bed We Forgot to Notice**

The modern reader, who lives on floors and in cities and forgets that beneath every structure lies the spread earth, is invited by this verse to recover the wonder the sūrah assumes: the ground itself is a *preparation*, a cradle, a bed laid for the human race by a Worker who prepared it well. The verse's praise — *what excellent spreaders!* — is the answer to the modern habit of seeing the earth as mere matter: the habitability of the planet, the soil that feeds, the water that flows, the firmness that holds — all of it is the work of the *māhidūn*, and the work is excellent. The next verse will name the pairs of creation, and the sūrah's final movement will rise from the spread earth to the command that all of it was preparing: *remember*.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:49

> **And of all things We created pairs, that haply you may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of Pairs in Creation**

The sūrah's catalogue of signs rises to its most general statement: *wa-min kulli shayʾin khalaqnā zawjayni laʿallakum tadhakkarūn* — "and of all things We created pairs, that haply you may remember." The word *zawj* is the pair, the mate, the counterpart — the root gives *zawj* for husband and wife, for the two of any species, and for any thing that exists in counterpart with another. The verse declares a universal law: *of everything* — *min kulli shayʾ* — God created *pairs*, so that the whole of creation is structured by duality. The source commentary records the two great readings of this duality. The first, reported by al-Ālūsī, is the most literal: all *animals* are created male and female, the pairing of the sexes by which every species continues. The second is the widest: creation is made up of *opposites* — night and day, heaven and earth, distress and happiness, life and death, the visible and the hidden — so that every thing in the universe is known by its counterpart, and the very structure of existence is the pairing of complementaries.

**The Sign of the Pair: From Biology to Cosmology**

The verse's range is deliberately comprehensive, and the two readings are not rivals but levels of one truth. At the level of the living, the pair is the male and the female — the Qur'an's constant sign, stated with emphasis elsewhere:

> *"And that He created the two pairs, the male and the female."* (Qur'an 53:45)

> *"Glory be to Him Who created the pairs, all of them, of what the earth produces, and of themselves, and of what they know not."* (Qur'an 36:36)

At the level of the cosmos, the pair is every opposite — the Qur'an's other constant, the pairing of night and day, sun and moon, land and sea, which it recites as signs of the Creator's oneness. The verse's *min kulli shayʾ* — "of everything" — deliberately refuses to be limited to either level: the law of the pair runs through the whole of creation, from the atom to the species to the stars, and the one who sees it has seen the signature of the One who is *without* a pair — the single God, of whom the Qur'an says *"there is nothing like unto Him"* (42:11), the Unpaired whose creation is all pairs.

**"That Haply You May Remember": The Purpose of the Sign**

The verse's final clause, *laʿallakum tadhakkarūn* — "that haply you may remember" — states the purpose of the whole catalogue of signs, and the word deserves its full weight. *Tadhakkarūn* is from *dhakara*, "to remember, to call to mind," and the root is the root of *dhikr* — remembrance, the Qur'an's master-word for the act of the believing heart. The signs were not created for their own sake but *so that you may remember* — remember the Creator, remember the covenant, remember the judgment the sūrah has been announcing. Al-Ālūsī, al-Rāzī, and al-Shawkānī all explain the clause in this sense: creation is for reflection, and the reflection is meant to lead to the recognition that God is the Creator. The word *laʿallā* — "haply, that perhaps" — is the Qur'an's hopeful particle, leaving the door open to the hearer's will: the signs are set, the pairs are paired, and the remembrance is offered — but it must be *taken*. The verse thus joins the sūrah's constant theme: the evidence is complete, and the only missing element is the act of remembering.

**The Pair as an Argument for Oneness**

There is a subtle logic in the verse that the commentators draw out: the *pair* is itself an argument for the *One*. Every pair is two, and every two needs a cause that is not itself paired — for if the cause of the pair were itself a pair, the question would simply be pushed back one step, and so on without end. The existence of pairs therefore points, in the end, to the Unpaired, the single Creator whose oneness is the ground of every duality. The Qur'an makes the argument by juxtaposition throughout: the creation is all pairs, and the Creator is all One, and the believer's creed is precisely this — *"He begets not, nor is He begotten"* (112:3) — the refusal of the pair to the divine, the confession that the Unpaired is the source of every pair. The verse's *zawjayni* is thus not only a sign of the Creator's power but of His oneness: the world of two is the proof of the One.

**For the Modern Reader: The Duality Everywhere**

The modern reader, whose science has mapped the pair to its foundations — the positive and negative charge, the matter and antimatter, the male and female, the particle and antiparticle — finds in this verse the Qur'an's invitation to see that duality not as the world's ultimate fact but as its *signature*. The pair is everywhere, and its everywhere-ness is the sign: a creation structured by complementarity points to a Creator above complementarity, the One of whom the believer says, with the sūrah's own emphasis, *remember*. The verse's purpose is the purpose of the whole sūrah: to turn the eye from the many to the One, and the heart from heedlessness to remembrance. The next verse will turn the remembrance into a command, and the sūrah's great summons — *flee unto God* — will rise from the paired creation to the unpaired refuge.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:50

> **So flee unto God. Truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Command: Flee Unto God**

The sūrah's argument, built through oaths and signs and histories, now discharges into its central command: *fa-firrū ilā Allāh* — "so flee unto God" — followed by the Prophet's self-description: *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn* — "truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him." The particle *fa-* ("so, therefore") ties the command to everything before it: because the promise is true, because the judgment shall fall, because the judged peoples lie in ruins, because the creation is all signs and pairs — *therefore* flee unto God. The verb *firrū* is the imperative of *farra*, "to flee," and it is chosen with the Qur'an's exactness: the sūrah has described the deniers as *turning away* (51:9, 39), and now the believer is commanded to the opposite motion — not to stand, not to stroll, but to *flee*, with the urgency of one who runs from what is falling toward what holds firm. The command is the sūrah's summation, and the whole of the chapter has been its preamble.

**What It Means to Flee Unto God**

The commentators unfold the command's meaning with a richness that the verse's brevity conceals. Al-Ṭabarī records the primary reading: flee from the punishment of God to His mercy, through faith in Him, following His command, and working in obedience to Him. Al-Shawkānī adds the other directions of the flight: flee from obeying Satan to obeying God, flee from ignorance to knowledge. And Ibn ʿAbbās, as the source commentary reports, explains the verse with the concision of the first generations:

> *"Flee from sins and take shelter with God through repentance."*

The flight is thus not a movement of the body but of the heart and the will — a turning of the whole self from sin to obedience, from heedlessness to remembrance, from everything that perishes to the One who does not. The command is addressed in the plural, *firrū* — "flee, all of you" — for it is the summons of the whole community, the one command that gathers every other command into itself.

**The Source Commentary's Deep Reading: Al-Qushayrī's Analysis**

The source commentary preserves the profound analysis of al-Qushayrī, the great Sufi commentator, who explains *why* the flight must be to God and to nothing else:

> *"The human being is in one of two states: either the state of coveting something or the state of dreading something, either the state of hope or the state of fear, either the state of attracting benefit or the state of repelling harm. So his fleeing must be to God, for the One who benefits and the One who harms is God."*

The analysis is a complete psychology of the human condition: every moment of every life is spent either seeking a benefit or fleeing a harm, hoping or fearing, grasping or recoiling — and the only object that can truly satisfy either motion is God, for He alone is the source of benefit and harm. Al-Qushayrī then extends the flight to its full spiritual depth:

> *"It is incumbent upon the servant to flee from ignorance to knowledge, from caprice to reverence, from doubt to certainty, and from Satan to God. It is incumbent upon the servant to flee from his actions that are a trial to his actions that are sufficient; and from characterizing Him in terms of God's Wrath to characterizing Him in terms of His Mercy; and from His Self, as when He says, 'God warns you of Himself' (3:28), to His Self, as when He says, 'So flee unto God.'"*

The flight is thus from the self to the Self — from the fear of God's justice to the hope of His mercy, from the dread that He warns of to the refuge that He is — and the whole spiritual path is comprehended in the single verb *firrū*.

**Why the Flight Is to God Alone**

The sūrah's placement of the command is deliberate, and the source commentary notes the logic: the command to flee unto God is placed *after* the creation of all things in pairs (verse 49) to emphasize that one cannot flee to anything *other than* God, since all things perish, save His Face:

> *"All things perish, save His Face."* (Qur'an 28:88)

Every created thing is a *zawj* — a paired, dependent, perishable thing — and a thing that perishes cannot be a refuge from what is falling. The sūrah's histories have proved the point: the *rukn* of Pharaoh, the towers of ʿĀd, the carved dwellings of Thamūd — every refuge other than God collapsed at the moment of judgment. The command *flee unto God* is thus the logical conclusion of the whole chapter: since every other refuge is a ruin, flee to the one refuge that is not. The Prophet's own prayer, recorded in the source commentary, is the command's purest expression:

> *"O God! I seek refuge in Thy Contentment from Thine Anger, and in Thy Pardon from Thy Punishment. I seek refuge in Thee from Thee."* (Muslim 486, from ʿĀʾishah)

The flight is to God — and from God as Judge to God as Refuge, from His wrath to His mercy, from Himself to Himself.

**"A Clear Warner Unto You From Him"**

The verse's second half identifies the speaker and his office: *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn* — "truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him." The word *nadhīr* — "warner" — is the Qur'an's name for the Prophet's office, the one who warns of what is coming before it comes, and the word *mubīn* — "clear, manifest" — is the same adjective the sūrah applied to Moses' authority (51:38): the warning is not veiled or ambiguous but plain, addressed to every hearer in the hearer's own tongue. The preposition *minhu* — "from Him" — is the verse's theological keystone: the warner does not warn on his own behalf but *from God*, as God's messenger, and the flight he commands is not to himself but to the One who sent him. The Prophet is thus the command's herald, not its object, and the sūrah's next verse will complete the summons by naming what the flight must flee *from* — the setting up of other gods.

**For the Modern Reader: The One Motion That Never Fails**

The modern reader, who flees from anxiety to distraction, from emptiness to entertainment, from the fear of death to the forgetting of death — who spends his life, in al-Qushayrī's terms, seeking benefits and fleeing harms — is offered by this verse the single motion that never fails: *flee unto God*. The command is not a denial of the world's real dangers but the recognition that every other refuge is temporary and every other flight is circular; the only flight that leads out is the flight to the One who does not perish. And the verse's urgency is its mercy: the command is given in the imperative because the situation is urgent, and the warner is *clear* because the way is plain. The next verse will guard the flight's destination by forbidding the one thing that would cancel it — the taking of another god — and the sūrah's great summons will be complete.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:51

> **And do not set up another god along with God. Truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Command: The Prohibition That Guards the First**

The command to flee is now guarded by its necessary complement: *wa-lā tajʿalū maʿa Allāhi ilāhan ākhar* — "and do not set up another god along with God" — followed by the same self-description as the previous verse: *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn* — "truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him." The two commands are one: the flight to God is completed only when it is a flight *from* every other god, and the prohibition of the false god is the negative form of the affirmation of the true one. The verb *tajʿalū* — "do not *make*, do not *set up*" — is precise: a false god is never a real god but a *made* one, a thing *set up* by human hands or hearts, and the prohibition is against the *setting up* — the act of granting to a creature the worship that belongs to the Creator. The phrase *ilāhan ākhar* — "another god" — carries the Qur'an's constant denial: there is no "another," for God has no second, and the very phrase is a contradiction the believer is forbidden to enact.

**The Central Theme of the Qur'an**

The source commentary notes that this prohibition is among the most predominant themes of the entire Qur'an, and the cross-references it supplies are the proof: the Qur'an forbids the setting up of equals to God:

> *"So set not up equals unto God, while you know."* (Qur'an 2:22)

And the ascribing of partners:

> *"And worship God, and ascribe no partners unto Him."* (Qur'an 4:36)

> *"Say: He has only forbidden indecencies, the outward and the inward, and sin, and insolent wrongdoing, and that you ascribe unto God that for which He has sent down no authority."* (Qur'an 7:33)

The prohibition is the first of the divine commands in every scripture and the last word of every prophet, for the whole of religion hangs upon it: the oneness of God is the root, and every virtue and every law is a branch. The sūrah's placement of the prohibition immediately after the command to flee is its own proof of this priority: one cannot flee to God while carrying another god, and the flight is tested by the renunciation.

**The Source Commentary's Deep Reading: Al-Ghazzālī on Hidden Idolatry**

The source commentary preserves the profound extension of the prohibition by al-Ghazzālī, from his *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*:

> *"One who does not see God in everything sees something other than Him. And if there is something other than God to which one gives attention, this attention involves an element of hidden idolatry (al-shirk al-khafī). Rather, pure monotheism (al-tawḥīd al-khāliṣ) consists in seeing only God in everything."*

The analysis opens the verse to its deepest reach: idolatry is not only the worship of the carved stone but the *attention* given to anything as though it were independent of God — the reliance on a cause as though it caused by itself, the trust in a creature as though it could benefit or harm, the love of a thing as though it were ultimate. The *shirk al-khafī*, the hidden idolatry, is the subtle form of the disease, and pure monotheism is its cure — the seeing of God in everything, the recognition that no cause, no creature, no good is anything but His. The verse's prohibition is thus the widest and the deepest of the sūrah's commands: it forbids not only the open idol but the hidden one, and it calls the believer to the purity of the heart that sees only God.

**The Logic of the Prohibition**

The prohibition's logic is the logic of the sūrah's whole argument, and the commentators state it plainly: the setting up of another god is the *one* act that cancels the flight to God, for it is the flight's opposite — a turning to something other than the refuge. The sūrah has shown, in its histories, exactly what the false god is worth: Pharaoh's gods did not hold back the sea, ʿĀd's gods did not stop the wind, Thamūd's gods did not deflect the thunderbolt. The prohibition is thus not an arbitrary rule but a mercy: it forbids the believer from leaning on what the sūrah has proved to be a ruin, and it directs the heart to the only support that holds. The verse's second half — "truly I am a clear warner unto you from Him" — seals the logic: the warner who commands the flight and forbids the false god warns *from Him*, and the warning is clear, so that no hearer can claim he was not told.

**The Two Commands and the Creed They Compress**

Read together, verses 50 and 51 are the Islamic creed in its most compact form: *flee unto God, and set up no other god with Him* — the affirmation and the negation, the flight and the renunciation, the *lā ilāha illā Allāh* (there is no god but God) rendered as two imperatives. The sūrah has traveled from the oaths to the histories to the signs, and all of it has led to these two commands, which are themselves the whole of the religion in miniature. The Prophet's self-description, repeated in both verses, frames them as his office: the *nadhīr mubīn* — the clear warner — whose entire message is this flight and this renunciation. The next verse will comfort the warner himself, by showing that his accusers are not original but are repeating, word for word, the accusation of every age.

**For the Modern Reader: The Hidden Idols of the Age**

The modern reader, who may think idolatry a thing of the past, is addressed by al-Ghazzālī's analysis at the deepest level: the age's idols are not statues but statuses, not carved stones but the *attention* given to money, reputation, desire, and power as though they were ultimate. The verse's prohibition, in its widest reach, is the call to dethrone every one of them — to see in each a creature, and in each a gift or a test from the one God, and to let no attention rest on anything as though it were itself the source. The flight to God is incomplete while any idol remains; the renunciation is the flight's other half; and the sūrah's two commands are, for the modern reader as for the Makkan, the whole of the way.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:52

> **Likewise, there came no messenger unto those before them, but that they said, “A sorcerer or one possessed.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Comfort of the Repeated Accusation**

The sūrah turns to comfort the Prophet with the weight of all history: *kadhālika mā atā alladhīna min qablihim min rasūlin illā qālū sāḥirun aw majnūn* — "likewise, there came no messenger unto those before them, but that they said, 'A sorcerer or one possessed.'" The word *kadhālika* — "likewise, even so" — points back to the accusation of Pharaoh in verse 39, and the verse's logic is the sūrah's great consolation: the Makkans who call Muhammad ﷺ "a sorcerer or one possessed" are not original; they are quoting Pharaoh, who said the same of Moses, and the same words have been the stock-in-trade of every people against every prophet. The accusation is not evidence but *tradition* — the oldest smear in history, passed down from one generation of deniers to the next — and its very age is its refutation. The Prophet is being told: you are not being accused because of anything in you; you are being accused because you are a messenger, and this is what every messenger has been called.

**The Parallel With Moses, and the Twofold Reading**

The source commentary notes that the verse parallels the manner in which Pharaoh and his people sought to discredit Moses in verse 39, and it records the two readings the verse supports. The first reading is *consolation*: the Prophet is shown that his plight is the same as that of every previous messenger, so that the accusation loses its power to wound — if Moses was called a sorcerer and was God's messenger, then the accusation against Muhammad proves nothing. The second reading is *warning*: the verse tells the Quraysh that their fate will be the fate of Pharaoh and his followers if they do not heed the Prophet — the accusation is the first step on the road that ended in the sea. Both readings are true, and the sūrah intends both: the verse is comfort to the warner and warning to the warned, the same words serving the mercy of the one and the doom of the other.

**The Accusation in the Makkan Record**

The source commentary catalogues the accusation's appearances in the Makkan sūrahs, and the catalogue is itself the argument. The Quraysh called the Prophet a sorcerer:

> *"And they say, 'What! A mortal who claims that when we have died and become dust and bones, we shall be brought forth?'"* (Qur'an 23:82)

They called him possessed:

> *"And they say, 'He is but a man possessed, so wait on him for a time.'"* (Qur'an 23:25)

And they said of his message:

> *"And they say, 'A jumble of dreams! Nay, he has fabricated it! Nay, he is a poet!'"* (Qur'an 21:5)

The source commentary collects the verses that liken the Prophet's plight to that of the previous prophets — 15:11, 34:34, 36:30, 43:7 and 23 — each one a repetition of this sūrah's verse: the accusation is the uniform, unvarying response of denial to truth, and its uniformity is the proof that the truth is one.

**Why the Deniers Always Say the Same Thing**

The verse's deeper point is a psychology of denial that the sūrah has been building throughout: the denier, unable to answer the messenger's proof, has no resource but the label, and the label is always the same because the denier's position is always the same. The "sorcerer or one possessed" is the refuge of the man who has seen the sign and will not accept it — the miracle dismissed as magic, the message dismissed as madness — and the dismissal is as old as Pharaoh because the refusal is as old as Pharaoh. The sūrah's histories have shown the accusation in action — Pharaoh against Moses, every people against its warner — and now the verse generalizes the pattern into a law: *there came no messenger but that they said it*. The Prophet is thus placed in the company of all the messengers, and the accusation is revealed as the badge of the office rather than the refutation of it.

**The Comfort for Every Subsequent Warner**

The verse's consolation was not exhausted in Makkah; it is the standing comfort of every believer who is smeared for the truth in any age. The one who is called a fool, a fanatic, a deceiver, for holding to what is true is hearing the modern echo of *sāḥirun aw majnūn*, and the sūrah's answer is the same for every age: the accusation is not about you, it is about the truth, and the truth has been accused since the first warner opened his mouth. The verse thus performs, for every generation, the same double office it performed for the Prophet: it takes the sting from the smear, and it turns the smear back upon the smearing — for if the accusation is the oldest in history, then the accused stands in the oldest and most honored company in history.

**For the Modern Reader: Recognizing the Pattern**

The modern reader may test the verse against his own time and find the pattern intact: the truth's bearers are still called by the age's equivalents of "sorcerer or possessed" — the labels change with the fashion, the mechanism does not. The verse's gift is the recognition of the pattern: to see, behind every dismissal of the truth, the same old refusal, and to know that the dismissed stand with Moses and Muhammad and every messenger, while the dismissers stand with Pharaoh. The next verse will turn from the accusation to the accusers' solidarity in it, and the sūrah's question — *do they exhort one another to it?* — will expose the last refuge of the denier.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:53

> **Do they exhort one another to it? Nay, they are a rebellious people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of Inherited Denial**

The sūrah interrogates the pattern it has just described: *a-tawāṣaw bihi* — "do they exhort one another to it?" — followed by the answer that reverses the question: *bal hum qawmun ṭāghūn* — "nay, they are a rebellious people." The verb *tawāṣaw* is the sixth form of *waṣā*, "to enjoin, to exhort," and the form *tawāṣaw* is the *mutual* — "they exhort *one another*" — so that the question asks: did these people, generation after generation, hand down the accusation "sorcerer or possessed" as a *bequest*, an inherited counsel, a tradition passed from fathers to sons? The source commentary records the two readings of the pronoun "it." Al-Rāzī reads "it" as the *accusation* — do they exhort one another to say that the Prophet is a sorcerer or one possessed? — while al-Ālūsī and al-Baghawī read it as the *denial* — do they exhort one another to deny the Prophet and the revelation? Both readings converge: the question is whether the denial is a deliberate, mutual, inherited counsel.

**The Two Ways of Reading the Question**

The source commentary notes that most commentators interpret the verse as an *expression of wonder* — an exclamation that these people would, in fact, exhort one another to the denial of God and of revelation, as though disbelief were a cause to be championed and passed down. But it also records the alternative reading as a *negation*: "they do not exhort one another to it" — in which case "it" refers to *belief*, and the verse becomes a statement of their failure: they exhort one another to no good, they bequeath no faith, and their mutual counsel is the counsel of the heedless. The two readings, far from canceling, complete each other: the deniers *do* exhort one another to denial, and *do not* exhort one another to belief — the one statement is the mirror of the other, and the sūrah's question carries both at once. The rhetoric is the Qur'an's own: a single question that opens in two directions, each leading to the same verdict.

**"Nay, They Are a Rebellious People": The Verdict**

The verse's answer, *bal hum qawmun ṭāghūn*, is the sūrah's diagnosis stated in its final, clinical form: *ṭāghūn* — "a rebellious people," the active participle of *ṭaghā*, "to overflow, to transgress, to rebel." The root *ṭ-g-y* is the root of *ṭāghūt* — the false god, the idol, the tyrant — and of *ṭughyān* — the overflowing rebellion — and it names the denier's essential condition: not error but *rebellion*, the overflow of the self beyond every bound, the refusal to be governed. The sūrah has used a word for each stage of the denier's descent — the *kharrāṣūn* who conjecture (51:10), the *sāhūn* who are heedless (51:11), the *musrifūn* who exceed (51:34), the *fāsiqūn* who step out (51:46) — and now it crowns the list with *ṭāghūn*, the rebellious, the ones whose denial is not a mistake but a defiance. The word answers the question: they do not *exhort* one another to this as men pass down a tradition; they are *driven* to it by the rebellion that is their nature, and their mutual counsel is the counsel of the rebellious.

**The Inheritance of Disbelief and the Inheritance of Faith**

The verse's question, read with its answer, draws a contrast the sūrah has been building between two inheritances. The deniers *exhort one another* to denial — the accusation, the refusal, the heedlessness, passed down as a bequest — while the believers, as the Qur'an teaches everywhere, *exhort one another* to the truth and to patience:

> *"By the declining day, truly man is in loss, save those who believe, and do righteous deeds, and exhort one another to truth, and exhort one another to patience."* (Qur'an 103:1–3)

The same verb, *tawāṣaw*, governs both inheritances: the one community bequeaths denial, the other bequeaths truth, and the difference between the two bequests is the difference between the two ends the sūrah has set before its hearers. The verse's question — *do they exhort one another to it?* — is thus a question about the direction of a whole society's mutual counsel, and the answer is the sūrah's verdict on the society that bequeaths denial.

**For the Modern Reader: What We Bequeath**

The modern reader may hear the verse as a question addressed to his own generation: what do *we* exhort one another to? The sūrah's diagnosis is that every community is defined by its *mutual counsel* — the things it urges upon its own, the inheritance it passes down — and the only question is whether the counsel is the counsel of denial or the counsel of truth. The *ṭāghūn* are the ones whose counsel is rebellion; the believers are the ones whose counsel is truth and patience; and the verse's wonder — that a people could exhort one another *to* denial — is the Qur'an's standing amazement at the human capacity to bequeath one's own ruin. The next verse will turn from the deniers to the Prophet, and the command that follows — *so turn away from them* — will mark the boundary between the two inheritances.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:54

> **So turn away from them, for thou shalt not be blamed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Boundary the Prophet Is Commanded to Draw**

The sūrah now addresses the Prophet directly with a command that defines the limits of his office: *fa-tawalla ʿanhum fa-mā anta bi-malūm* — "so turn away from them, for thou shalt not be blamed." The verb *tawalla* is the same verb the sūrah used of Pharaoh's turning away (51:39) and of the denier's turning from the truth (51:9), but here its direction is reversed and its meaning transformed: the Prophet is commanded to *turn away* from the deniers — not in the denier's spirit of refusal, but in the spirit of one who has delivered his message and is released from the burden of their response. The source commentary states the meaning precisely: once the Prophet has delivered the message, he is not responsible for whether the disbelievers follow it, nor for the punishment that will afflict them if they do not; his responsibility is to continue reminding the believers who benefit from the reminder. The verse is the Qur'an's great statement of the boundary between the warner's duty and the warned's accountability, and it is addressed as much to the Prophet's exhausted heart as to his mission.

**"Thou Shalt Not Be Blamed": The Release of the Warner**

The verse's second half, *fa-mā anta bi-malūm* — "for thou shalt not be blamed" — is the sūrah's tender absolution of its messenger. The word *malūm* is the passive participle of *lāma*, "to blame," the same root the sūrah used for Pharaoh in verse 40 — *mulīm*, "blameworthy" — and the contrast is deliberate and complete: Pharaoh was blameworthy and was seized; the Prophet, having delivered the message, is *not* to be blamed, for the blame of the deniers' refusal rests on the deniers alone. The absolution is the Qur'an's constant comfort to the Prophet, repeated wherever the weight of rejection pressed on him:

> *"Then, haply, thou wouldst kill thyself with grief over their footsteps, if they believe not in this account."* (Qur'an 18:6)

> *"So let not thy soul be consumed with sorrow over them."* (Qur'an 35:8)

The Prophet, who carried the burden of his people's salvation as though it were his own, is here released: the message is delivered, the proof is complete, and the refusal of the refusers is no longer his to carry. The warner warns; the warned chooses; and the blame falls where the choice was made.

**The Theological Boundary: Guidance and Free Will**

The verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement of the boundary that defines its whole theology of guidance. The Prophet's office is to *deliver* — not to *compel*; the message is offered, not imposed; and the acceptance or refusal of the message is the hearer's own act, for which the hearer, not the warner, is answerable. The Qur'an states the boundary in its most famous form:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion; rectitude has become clear from error."* (Qur'an 2:256)

And again, in the Prophet's own commission:

> *"Remind, then, for thou art but a reminder; thou art not a warden over them."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

The verse's *turn away* is thus not a withdrawal of care but a recognition of the boundary: the warner cannot do the warned's choosing for him, and the attempt to do so would be to overstep the very office God assigned. The Prophet is *not blamed* for the refusal because the refusal is not his act — it is the refuser's, and the refuser's alone.

**What "Turning Away" Means and What It Does Not Mean**

The command must be read with precision, for it has been misread. The *tawalla* here is not the abandonment of the deniers to their fate without further effort — the next verse will immediately command the Prophet to *remind* — but the release of the *emotional* entanglement with their refusal: the Prophet is to stop carrying the weight of their denial as though it were his failure. The source commentary is explicit: he is to continue reminding the believers who benefit, and the turning away is the turning of the heart from the *blame* of the deniers' choice, not from the *mercy* of continuing to warn. The distinction is the verse's whole subtlety: the warner's duty continues, but the warner's *guilt* ends — the message is delivered, the proof is complete, and the response is in the hands of the hearers and their Lord.

**For the Modern Reader: The Boundaries of Our Own Responsibility**

The modern reader, who carries the refusals of others as though they were his own failures, may find in this verse the boundary that sets him free: the caller to truth delivers, and the delivery is his duty's end; the acceptance or refusal is the hearer's, and the blame for refusal is not the caller's to carry. The verse does not license indifference — the reminder continues — but it releases the caller from the impossible burden of making others choose, and it returns to each soul the full weight and full freedom of its own choice. The next verse will turn the release into a continuing commission — *and remind* — and the boundary of the previous verse will be joined to the ongoing duty of the next.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:55

> **And remind, for truly the Reminder benefits the believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Continuing Commission: Remind**

The release of the previous verse is immediately joined to the duty that continues: *wa-dhakkir fa-inna al-dhikrā tanfaʿu al-muʾminīn* — "and remind, for truly the Reminder benefits the believers." The verb *dhakkir* is the imperative of *dhakkara*, "to remind," from the root *dh-k-r* — the same root as *dhikr*, remembrance, the Qur'an's master-word for the act of the believing heart, and as *tadhakkarū* in verse 49, the remembering for which the signs were created. The command thus closes a circle the sūrah has been drawing: the signs of creation were made *that you may remember* (51:49), and now the Prophet is commanded to be the instrument of that remembering — to *remind*, to bring back to the heart what it has forgotten. The noun *al-dhikrā* — "the reminder, the remembrance" — is, as the source commentary notes, a reference to the Qur'an itself, so that the command is the command to preach the Book, to keep reciting its verses and reviving its message in the hearts that have drifted from it.

**Why the Reminder Benefits the Believers**

The verse's reason — *fa-inna al-dhikrā tanfaʿu al-muʾminīn* — "for truly the Reminder benefits the believers" — is the sūrah's answer to the discouragement that the previous verses might have produced. If the deniers will not listen, why keep preaching? Because the preaching is not for them alone: the Reminder *benefits the believers*, and the believers are the soil in which the reminder grows. The source commentary, citing al-Ālūsī and others, explains the benefit precisely: the Reminder increases the believers' spiritual insight (*baṣīrah*), gives them strength in certainty and knowledge, deepens their faith, and renews their resolve. The believer, like every human being, forgets — the sūrah has made heedlessness the central human disease — and the Reminder is the medicine that the forgetting requires. The command to remind is thus not a hopeless gesture toward the deaf but a vital service to the hearing, and the Prophet is told to preach for the sake of those who benefit, not to be discouraged by those who do not.

**The Audience of the Reminder: Believers, or Believers and Deniers Alike?**

The source commentary records the two readings of the verse's scope. The first takes the reminder as aimed at the *believers* to the exclusion of the deniers — the Prophet has been told to turn away from the deniers, and now he is told to continue reminding the believers, for they are the ones who benefit. The second takes the reminder as aimed at *both* — the believers and the deniers together — for the denier of today may be the believer of tomorrow, and the reminder that does not benefit the one may yet reach the other. Both readings are true to the Qur'an's practice: the Prophet preached to all, but he was sustained by the knowledge that the believers were the ones in whom the preaching bore fruit, and the verse's reason — *it benefits the believers* — is the ground of the preacher's perseverance in either case. The Reminder is never wasted, because it is the food of every heart that has even a seed of faith.

**The Psychology of Repetition and the Necessity of Reminding**

The verse rests on a profound psychology that the modern reader will recognize at once: human beings forget even what they know, and the forgetting is not cured by a single hearing but by *repetition* — the constant, patient re-presentation of the truth until it sinks from the ear to the heart. The Qur'an's own method is built on this psychology: it repeats its stories and its commands and its signs, not because it has nothing new to say but because the human heart is a vessel that leaks, and the Reminder is the repeated filling. The believer needs the reminder as the plant needs water — not once but continuously — and the sūrah's command to the Prophet is the commission to keep the water flowing. The Prophet ﷺ himself described the believer's need for the Reminder when he said:

> *"Faith wears out in the heart of any one of you just as a garment wears out, so ask God to renew the faith in your hearts."* (al-Ṭabarānī, al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr; and similar in al-Ḥākim)

The verse is that renewal commissioned: the Reminder is the perpetual renewer of the heart's faith.

**For the Modern Reader: The Reminder We All Need**

The modern reader, no less than the Makkan believer, lives in a state of constant forgetting — the world's noise covering the heart's memory, the daily rush burying the eternal — and the verse's command is the standing answer: *remind*. The reader is both the reminded and the reminder — the one who needs the Reminder daily, and the one who is called to carry it to others, beginning with the self. The verse's reason is the preacher's strength and the believer's hope: the Reminder is never useless, for wherever there is a believing heart, there the Reminder finds its soil and bears its fruit. The next verse will state the purpose of the whole creation, and the sūrah's great declaration — *I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me* — will rise as the summit toward which every reminder has been pointing.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:56

> **I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summit of the Sūrah: The Purpose of Creation**

The sūrah rises to its most famous declaration, spoken in God's own voice: *wa-mā khalaqtu al-jinna wa'l-insa illā li-yaʿbudūn* — "I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me." The verse is one of the Qur'an's great statements of purpose, and it gathers the whole sūrah into a single sentence. Every oath, every sign, every history, every command has been pointing here: the winds scatter, the clouds bear, the ships course, the angels apportion, the earth is spread, the sky is built, the pairs are paired — and the *reason* for it all, as far as the two responsible orders of creation are concerned, is worship. The verse names the two creatures to whom it is addressed — *al-jinn* and *al-ins*, the hidden and the human, the two species endowed with will and reason — and it declares that God's purpose in creating them was a single thing: that they worship Him.

**The Word "Worship" and Its Full Meaning**

The word *li-yaʿbudūn* — "to worship" — is from the root *ʿ-b-d*, whose primary sense is *servitude*: the *ʿabd* is the servant, the slave, and *ʿibādah* is the service, the devotion, the worship by which the servant acknowledges and serves his Lord. The word is the Qur'an's master-term for the whole of the religious life, and the source commentary draws out its range through the classical scholars. Ibn Kathīr explains the verse's connection to what precedes it: the reminder (verse 55) and the command to flee (verse 50) are both expressions of this purpose, for the reminder is the call *to* the worship, and everything else in which one seeks refuge distracts from it. And Ibn ʿAbbās, as the source commentary records, interpreted the verse with the profound concision of the first generation:

> *"Save to worship Me" means "save to know Me."*

The two glosses are one: worship is not mere ritual but the whole life of the servant turned toward his Lord — the knowledge of God expressed in obedience, the obedience rooted in the knowledge of God. The *ʿibādah* the verse names is thus the widest and deepest of human activities: every act performed in obedience to God and for His sake — prayer and fasting, justice and mercy, the honesty of the marketplace and the tenderness of the home — is worship, and the purpose of creation is that the whole of life be lived as this service.

**The Freedom of the Worship: Al-Zamakhsharī's Argument**

The source commentary preserves al-Zamakhsharī's careful analysis of the verse's theology, and it is essential to understanding the verse's meaning. Al-Zamakhsharī argues that the verse points to the *purpose* for which human beings were created, even if most of them do not fulfill it: God created them to worship Him *by choosing* to worship freely, not by being constrained to do so, because He created them as contingent, willed beings. Had God wanted their worship by way of *coercion* and *compulsion*, that worship would be found among all of them — but the worship God willed is the worship of the free, the willing surrender of a creature who could have refused. The verse is thus the Qur'an's great statement of the dignity of free will: the purpose of creation is worship, and the worship is meaningful precisely because it is chosen. The angels worship by nature, without choice; the jinn and mankind worship by *will*, and the will is what gives the worship its worth.

**The Ḥadīth Qudsī and the Divine Self-Sufficiency**

The source commentary adjoins to the verse the reports that illuminate its meaning, and they are worth quoting in full. The first is the ḥadīth qudsī transmitted by Ibn Kathīr:

> *"O Son of Adam! Dedicate yourself to worshipping Me (tafarrugh), and I will fill your chest with riches and make your poverty dissipate. Otherwise, I will fill your chest with distractions and will not make your poverty dissipate."*

The report teaches the verse's practical meaning: the heart that is given to worship is a heart filled — with contentment, with certainty, with the wealth that no poverty can touch — while the heart that is given to distraction is filled with the endless, unsatisfiable wants that no wealth can quench. And Ibn Kathīr also transmits the saying reported in one of the earlier scriptures:

> *"Son of Adam, I created you to worship Me, so play not! I have taken on the burden of your provision, so tire yourself not! If you seek Me, you will find Me. If you find Me, you have found everything. If I pass you by, everything has passed you by. And I am more beloved to you than everything."*

The saying joins the verse to the sūrah's earlier teaching about provision (51:22): God has taken the burden of provision upon Himself, and the creature's burden is the worship — so the creature who tires himself for provision while neglecting worship has reversed the entire arrangement.

**The "Hidden Treasure" and the Purpose of Knowledge**

The source commentary records the final, famous extension of the verse's meaning, which al-Ālūsī cites and which the tradition widely repeats, while noting that its chain of transmission cannot be fully authenticated — the ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"I was a hidden treasure, and I loved to be known; so I created creation in order to be known."*

Whether or not the report's chain is sound, its meaning, as al-Ālūsī and the scholars note, is the meaning of Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss — *worship Me* means *know Me* — and the source commentary cites Ibn ʿArabī's *Futūḥāt* to the same effect: God created the cosmos only for knowledge of Him. The verse, read in this light, is the sūrah's deepest statement of purpose: creation is not a random event but a *self-revelation*, the act by which the hidden treasure was made known, and the worship of the creature is the knowledge of the Creator returning to its source.

**The Verse as the Answer to the Sūrah's Whole Argument**

Read in its place, the verse is the answer to everything the sūrah has said. The deniers asked "when is the Day of Judgment?" — the answer is that the question is the wrong one, for the question that matters is *why were you created*. The sūrah has shown the signs and the histories and the commands, and all of them converge on this single declaration: the purpose of the two responsible species is worship, and every other purpose is a forgetting of the one that matters. The verse is also the ground of the sūrah's central command — *flee unto God* — for the flight to God is simply the return to the purpose of one's creation, the recovery of the worship for which one was made. The sūrah has come full circle: from the oaths to the purpose, from the winds to the worship, and the next verses will seal the purpose by declaring the Creator's self-sufficiency.

**For the Modern Reader: The Answer to the Question of Purpose**

The modern reader, who asks the question of purpose with an urgency no age has exceeded — *why am I here, what is life for?* — finds in this verse the Qur'an's answer, given with the absolute clarity of the divine voice: you were created to worship God. The answer is not a restriction but a release: the creature who lives for the worship lives *for* something, and everything else — work, love, rest, ambition — takes its proper place as the material of the worship or the field of its exercise. The verse's freedom is its greatest gift: the worship is *chosen*, and the choosing is the dignity; the purpose is *given*, and the giving is the mercy. The next verse will declare what the Creator does *not* need from His creatures, and the sūrah's portrait of the self-sufficient Lord will complete the picture of the purpose.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:57

> **I desire no provision from them; nor do I desire that they should feed Me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Declaration of Divine Self-Sufficiency**

The verse follows the purpose of creation with the Creator's declaration of independence: *mā urīdu minhum min rizqin wa-mā urīdu an yuṭʿimūn* — "I desire no provision from them; nor do I desire that they should feed Me." The verb *urīdu* — "I desire, I want" — is the verb of *will* and *want*, and its double negation is the verse's whole point: God wants nothing from His creatures, asks nothing of them, needs nothing from them — neither *provision* (*rizq*) nor *feeding* (*iṭʿām*). The two words are chosen deliberately: *rizq* is the word the sūrah used in verse 22 for the *creatures'* provision, the daily sustenance God sends down from the sky, and *yuṭʿimūn* is the feeding, the supplying of food — so that the verse says, in effect: the provision you receive from Me, and the food you eat, I neither receive from you nor need from you. The verse is the Qur'an's most direct statement of the Creator's freedom from every need, and it is the necessary complement to the previous verse: the worship God wills is not a service He requires but a gift He offers, and the creature's worship benefits no one but the creature.

**The Cross-Reference: God Is the Self-Sufficient**

The source commentary supplies the verse's companion in the Qur'an, and the cross-reference is exact:

> *"O mankind, you are needful of God, and God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised."* (Qur'an 35:15)

The two verses state the same truth from opposite directions: the creatures are the *needy* — needful of God in every breath, every morsel, every moment — and God is the *Self-Sufficient* (*al-Ghanī*), the One who needs nothing and is praised in Himself. The word *ghanī* — "rich, self-sufficient" — is the root of the verse's whole theology: God's wealth is His essence, not an acquired abundance, and His freedom from need is absolute. The sūrah's earlier verse (51:22) declared that the creatures' provision is *in the sky* — descending from God — and this verse completes the circle: the Provider is not provided for, the Feeder is not fed, and the worship of the creatures adds nothing to His fullness. The relation between Creator and creature is thus, at its root, a one-way flow of grace: everything descends from Him, nothing ascends to Him but the creature's own gratitude, which itself is His gift.

**Why the Declaration Follows the Purpose**

The verse's placement after verse 56 is a piece of the sūrah's exact logic, and the commentators draw it out. The previous verse declared that God created jinn and mankind *to worship Him* — a declaration that a careless reader might misread as though God *needed* the worship, as though the Creator were somehow dependent on His creatures' service. This verse corrects the misreading before it can form: the worship is not a need but a *purpose*, and the purpose is not for God's benefit but for the creatures' — the worship is the creature's completion, not the Creator's provision. The sūrah thus guards its theology at every step: God's will that He be worshipped is the will of a generous Lord, not a needy one, and the declaration of self-sufficiency protects the doctrine of the purpose from the corruption of imagining a dependent God. The two verses together are the Qur'an's complete teaching: the creature is made *for* worship, and God is *beyond* need.

**The Psychology of Worshiping a Self-Sufficient God**

The verse's teaching has a profound effect on the psychology of worship, and it is worth drawing out. If God needed the worship, then the worshiper would be doing God a favor, and the worship would be a burden the creature bore for the Creator's sake. But because God is self-sufficient, the relation is reversed: the worship is the *creature's* need, and the burden it lifts is the creature's own. The believer who understands this verse worships with a double freedom — free from the anxiety of *owing* God something He lacks (He lacks nothing), and free from the pride of *supplying* God something He wants (He wants nothing). The worship becomes what it was meant to be: not a tax paid to a needy deity but the natural act of a creature returning to its source, the gratitude of a beggar to the One who filled his hands. The verse's negations — *no provision, no feeding* — are thus the foundations of the believer's peace: God needs nothing, and so the creature's worship is pure gift, pure gratitude, pure return.

**For the Modern Reader: The Liberation of the Needy**

The modern reader, who lives in a world of transaction — where every relation is measured by what each party gets — may find in this verse the liberation of the divine relation: God is the one party who needs nothing from the other, and so the relation with Him is the only relation in which one can never be exploited, never be used, never be drained. The verse's declaration is the ground of the believer's confidence: the Lord who wants nothing from me cannot be disappointed by my poverty, cannot be enriched by my wealth, and loves me not for what I give Him but for what I am in His sight. The next verse will complete the declaration with the divine names — the Provider, the Possessor of Strength, the Firm — and the sūrah's portrait of the self-sufficient Lord will be finished.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:58

> **Truly God is the Provider, the Possessor of Strength, the Firm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Names That Seal the Declaration**

The verse seals the declaration of self-sufficiency with three divine names: *inna Allāha huwa al-razzāqu dhū al-quwwati al-matīn* — "truly God is the Provider, the Possessor of Strength, the Firm." The emphatic opening — *inna Allāha huwa* — "truly God, He it is" — is the Qur'an's strongest form of affirmation, and the three names that follow are chosen with the sūrah's exact logic. *Al-Razzāq* — the Provider — is the intensive form of *rāziq*, the One who provides abundantly, continuously, to every creature; it is the same root as the *rizq* of verse 22 and verse 57, so that the name gathers the sūrah's whole teaching on provision: the creatures' provision descends from this Provider, and the Provider is provided for by none. *Dhū al-quwwah* — the Possessor of Strength — names the power that the sūrah's opening oaths and histories displayed: the might that raised the sky (51:47), that scattered the winds, that drowned Pharaoh and seized Thamūd. And *al-matīn* — the Firm — is the name of unshakable solidity, the strength that never weakens, the firmness that never yields; the root *m-t-n* gives *matn*, the firm, solid part of a thing, the ground that does not give way. The three names together are the sūrah's summary of its whole theology: God is the Provider, so the creature need not fear want; God is the Possessor of Strength, so the denier cannot resist His judgment; God is the Firm, so the believer's refuge never fails.

**The Provider: The Name That Answers the Deniers' Error**

The first name, *al-Razzāq*, is placed first with deliberate emphasis, for it answers the central error the sūrah has been combating. The Makkan deniers sought their provision from their idols, feared poverty, and worshiped what they hoped would feed them — and the sūrah's answer is that the Provider is God alone, the One who *"in the sky is your provision"* (51:22), the One who desires no provision from His creatures (51:57). The name *al-Razzāq* is the believer's complete security: the provision of every creature — the sustenance of the sparrow, the water of the whale, the daily bread of every human being — is in the hand of this Provider, and no power can give what He withholds or withhold what He gives. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to rest in this name, and the tradition records his description of its completeness:

> *"If you were to rely upon God with the reliance He deserves, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning hungry and return in the evening full."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; Ibn Mājah 4164; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The name *al-Razzāq* is that reliance given its object.

**The Possessor of Strength and the Firm: The Names of Judgment and Refuge**

The second and third names, *dhū al-quwwati al-matīn* — "the Possessor of Strength, the Firm" — are the sūrah's answer to the deniers' *rukn*, the false supports on which they leaned. The sūrah has shown every false support collapse — Pharaoh's hosts, ʿĀd's towers, Thamūd's dwellings — and the two names now declare the one support that does not collapse: the strength of God is the only strength, and His firmness is the only ground. The pairing is precise: *quwwah* is the active power, the strength that acts; *matīn* is the enduring solidity, the firmness that remains — and the believer's refuge in God is thus a refuge in both: the power that can help, and the firmness that will not fail. The sūrah's central command — *flee unto God* (51:50) — is here given its full ground: flee to the Possessor of Strength, for every other strength is weakness; flee to the Firm, for every other ground is shifting sand. The three names together are the complete answer to the sūrah's whole argument: the Provider whom the deniers should have served, the Strength they should have feared, and the Firmness they should have trusted.

**The Names as the Structure of the Believer's Trust**

Read together, the three names give the believer the complete structure of trust, and the sūrah's order is its own lesson. *Al-Razzāq* answers the fear of *want* — the anxiety that tomorrow's provision will fail — with the certainty of the Provider. *Dhū al-quwwah* answers the fear of *harm* — the anxiety that no power can protect against the world's dangers — with the certainty of the Possessor of Strength. *Al-matīn* answers the fear of *loss* — the anxiety that the ground itself will give way — with the certainty of the Firm. The believer who holds these three names has an answer to the three great fears of the human heart, and the answer is the sūrah's throughout: God is the Provider, the Strong, the Firm, and the one who flees to Him has fled to the only refuge that holds. The verse is thus not a mere list of divine names but the sūrah's final, compressed theology of trust.

**For the Modern Reader: The Names in the Age of Anxiety**

The modern reader, who lives in an age of unprecedented material security and unprecedented anxiety — who fears want despite abundance, harm despite protection, and loss despite insurance — may find in these three names the answer the age has lost. The Provider frees the believer from the terror of scarcity; the Possessor of Strength frees him from the terror of powerlessness; the Firm frees him from the terror of instability. The verse's three names are the sūrah's parting gift before its final warning: the believer is left with the God who provides, who strengthens, and who does not fail, and the next verse will turn from the believer's refuge to the denier's reckoning.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:59

> **And surely for those who do wrong are sins like unto the sins of their companions; so let them not seek to hasten.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of the Inherited Sin**

The sūrah returns to the deniers with its final warning: *fa-inna li'lladhīna ẓalamū dhanūban mithla dhanūbi aṣḥābihim fa-lā yastaʿjilūn* — "and surely for those who do wrong are sins like unto the sins of their companions; so let them not seek to hasten." The particle *fa-* ("and surely, so") ties the verse to the divine names just declared: because God is the Provider, the Strong, the Firm, *therefore* the wrongdoers will not escape their reckoning. The word *ẓalamū* — "those who do wrong" — is the root of *ẓulm*, injustice, darkness, the placing of a thing in its wrong place, and it names the denier's condition at its root: the one who wrongs his own soul by denying its Lord, and wrongs the truth by refusing it. And the verse's central declaration — *dhanūbun mithla dhanūbi aṣḥābihim* — "sins like unto the sins of their companions" — is the sūrah's law of the inherited sin: the wrongdoers of the present will carry a load of sin like the load of their companions, the wrongdoers of the past who preceded them in denial.

**The Meaning of "Their Companions"**

The source commentary unfolds the phrase's meaning in its two dimensions. The first is the *historical*: "their companions" are the fellow wrongdoers from generations past — the peoples the sūrah has just recounted, the people of Noah and ʿĀd and Thamūd and Lot and Pharaoh, whose sins the present deniers have inherited and whose punishment they will share. The verse thus says: the Quraysh who deny Muhammad ﷺ will be forced to endure a punishment similar to that endured by the peoples who rejected the prophets before them, for their sin is the same sin and its wage is the same wage. The second dimension is the *social*: "their companions" are the like-minded of one's own time — the friends, the contemporaries, the crowd with whom one shares one's denial — and the verse warns that following the people or customs of one's own time blindly will not suffice as an excuse on the Day of Judgment. Both dimensions converge on the sūrah's constant lesson: sin is not made safe by its companions, and the company of the sinful is no shelter from the reckoning.

**The Word "Sins" and the Load It Names**

The word *dhanūb* deserves its full weight. It is the plural of *dhanb*, "a sin, a guilt, a load," and the root's imagery is of the *tail* — the part that follows behind — so that a *dhanb* is what a person drags behind him, the load of his deeds that follows him to the reckoning. The verse's *dhanūbun mithla dhanūbi aṣḥābihim* thus pictures the wrongdoers of the present dragging behind them the same load the wrongdoers of the past dragged — the same guilt, the same consequence, the same dragging to the same end. The Qur'an uses the same imagery in its doctrine of the Hereafter:

> *"And no bearer of burden shall bear the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164)

The verse's load is each soul's own — inherited in *kind* from the companions, but owned in *full* by the carrier — and the "like unto" of the verse is the Qur'an's precise way of saying that the punishment fits the crime, and the crime of the present is the crime of the past, so the punishment will be its like.

**"Let Them Not Seek to Hasten": The Return to the Scoffer's Taunt**

The verse's closing clause, *fa-lā yastaʿjilūn* — "so let them not seek to hasten" — returns the sūrah to the taunt with which it opened its argument against the deniers. The source commentary notes the connection explicitly: the hastening here relates to the trial that they sought to hasten in verse 14 — *"this is what you sought to hasten"* — and in verse 12, the mockery, *"when is the Day of Judgment?"* The sūrah has now come full circle: it began with the deniers demanding the judgment's hastening, it showed them the histories of those who made the same demand and received it, and it closes by telling them, in effect, that the demand is already answered — the load is already theirs, the wage is already fixed, and the hastening they mockingly seek is the last thing they should want. The prohibition *lā yastaʿjilūn* is thus a warning wrapped in the sūrah's deepest irony: the ones who hurry the judgment are the ones who will least be able to bear it when it hurries back to them.

**The Justice of the Corresponding Punishment**

The verse is the sūrah's final statement of its doctrine of *correspondence*, the principle that has governed every judgment the sūrah has recounted. The wrongdoers of the present will receive *like* what the wrongdoers of the past received, because the sin is the same and the Judge is the same and the justice is the same. The doctrine is the sūrah's answer to the denier's hope that he might escape — that his sin, because it is his own time's and his own companions', might somehow be exempt from the law that has governed every sin before it. The verse answers: the law has no exceptions, and the load is the load, whoever drags it. The sūrah's histories were not told as ancient curiosities but as the *precedents* of the present, and this verse makes the precedent explicit: what fell on the companions will fall on the companions, and the only difference is the name and the day.

**For the Modern Reader: The Company We Keep**

The modern reader may hear in this verse the warning that is also its mercy: the sin one inherits from one's time and one's company is still one's own, and the crowd's approval is no shield against the reckoning. The verse invites the reader to ask whose companion he is — the companions of denial, dragging the load of the past into the present, or the companions of faith, whose mutual counsel is truth and patience (103:3). And the verse's closing prohibition is the sūrah's final word of restraint to the impatient: do not seek to hasten what you cannot bear — the judgment is certain, and the certain thing is best met with the flight to God, not the demand for its hurrying. The next verse, the sūrah's last, will pronounce the woe upon the deniers, and the chapter's long argument will close with the Day it promised at its opening.

## Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt 51:60

> **Woe unto those who disbelieve on account of the Day that they are promised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Final Word: Woe**

The sūrah closes with a single, ringing verdict: *fa-waylun li'lladhīna kafarū min yawmihimu alladhī yūʿadūn* — "so woe unto those who disbelieve on account of the Day that they are promised." The word *wayl* is the Qur'an's most emphatic word of warning — a word of woe, of destruction, of grief, used throughout the Book to open its most severe condemnations, as in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn, *"Woe unto those who give short measure"* (83:1), and Sūrah al-Humazah, *"Woe unto every slanderer, backbiter"* (104:1). The particle *fa-* ("so, therefore") ties the woe to everything before it: because the promise is true (51:5), because the judgment shall fall (51:6), because the wrongdoers have inherited the sins of their companions (51:59), *therefore* woe unto the disbelievers. The sūrah that opened with oaths of certainty closes with the consequence of that certainty, and the arc from the first verse to the last is the sūrah's whole argument in miniature: the promise sworn by the winds is the Day whose denial brings the woe.

**"On Account of the Day That They Are Promised"**

The verse's phrase, *min yawmihimi alladhī yūʿadūn* — "on account of their Day, the one they are promised" — is the sūrah's final naming of its central subject, and the source commentary identifies it precisely: the Day is the Day of Judgment, as promised in verses 5–6. The word *yūʿadūn* — "they are promised" — is the same passive of *waʿada*, "to promise," with which the sūrah's fifth verse declared *"what you are promised is true,"* so that the sūrah ends with the very word with which its argument began. The Day is *theirs* — *yawmihim* — the possessive pronoun carrying the same doctrine of correspondence the sūrah has taught throughout: the Day is not an alien event imposed from outside but *their* Day, the Day their deeds have prepared, the promise their denial has made into a threat. And the preposition *min* — "on account of, from, because of" — makes the Day the *source* of the woe: the woe is not an arbitrary punishment but the direct consequence of the Day, the Day whose reality they denied becoming the reality that overwhelms them.

**The Arc of the Sūrah Completed**

The verse completes the sūrah's architecture, and it is worth pausing to see the whole. The sūrah opened with oaths — the scatterers, the bearers, the coursers, the apportioners — sworn to establish that the promise is true and the judgment shall fall. It diagnosed the deniers — their differing claims, their turned hearts, their conjecture, their stupor, their taunting demand for the Day's date. It set against them the reverent — their gardens and springs, their sleepless nights, their dawn forgiveness, their wealth's due. It laid out the signs — on the earth, in the soul, in the sky, in the pairs of creation. It recounted the histories — Abraham's guests, the cities of Lot, Moses and Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Noah — each a precedent of the judgment. It issued the commands — flee unto God, set up no other god — and comforted the warner and declared the purpose of creation and the self-sufficiency of the Creator. And now, with a single word — *wayl* — it closes the whole on the note toward which it has been moving from its first oath: the Day is real, and woe to those who disbelieve it. The verse is the sūrah's period, and the period is a warning.

**Why the Sūrah Ends With Woe and Not With Mercy**

A careful reader may ask why a chapter that declared God "the Compassionate, the Merciful" in its opening line ends with the word *woe*. The answer is the sūrah's own, and it is a theology in itself: the mercy is the *invitation*, and the woe is the *consequence of its refusal*. The sūrah has been, from first to last, an act of mercy — the oaths, the signs, the histories, the commands, the reminder, all of it offered so that the hearer might flee to God and be saved. The woe is not the opposite of that mercy but its final urgency: the warning is the mercy's last form, and the one who is warned and still refuses has chosen the woe. The Qur'an's warnings are never given for their own sake but always as the *completion* of the invitation, and the sūrah's last word is the invitation's last chance: hear the woe now, while hearing can still save, and flee before the woe arrives. The woe of this verse is, in the deepest sense, a mercy in the imperative — the final call of a Lord who would rather be fled to than fled from.

**The Day That Both Promises Meet**

There is a final subtlety in the verse's word *yūʿadūn* — "they are promised" — that closes the sūrah's whole argument. The sūrah has used the language of *promise* for both the believers and the deniers: the believers are promised gardens and springs, and the deniers are promised the Day of woe. The same word, *waʿd*, the same Promise, the same Promiser — and the difference is only in the receiving. The source commentary's cross-reference to verses 5–6 makes the point: the promise that is *true* and the judgment that will *fall* are one event, and the event is mercy to the one who believed it and woe to the one who denied it. The sūrah thus ends where it began, with the single fact that governed it throughout: the promise is true, and the Day will come — and the only question the sūrah leaves its hearer is the question it has asked from the first verse: will the Day find him among the reverent in their gardens, or among the heedless in their woe?

**For the Modern Reader: The Last Word and the Open Door**

The modern reader, closing the sūrah, is left with its last word ringing — *wayl* — and with the door the sūrah has left open throughout: the command *flee unto God* (51:50) still stands, the Reminder still benefits the believer (51:55), and the woe is still only a warning, not yet a sentence. The sūrah's final verse is thus not the slamming of a door but the sounding of a bell — the last, clearest call of a chapter that has spent sixty verses summoning its hearer to the only refuge that holds. The Day is promised; the woe is warned; the flight is open. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its first oath to its last woe has been given everything the sūrah has to give — and the choice that remains is the reader's own.
