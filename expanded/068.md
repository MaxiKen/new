# Sūrah al-Qalam (The Pen)

## Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

> *In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.*

Sūrah al-Qalam is the sixty-eighth chapter of the Qur'an. Its opening was revealed into a hostile environment in which the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ was called a poet, a soothsayer, and a man overcome by madness. The sūrah answers those accusations not with a merely defensive argument but with a moral and intellectual portrait: revelation is linked to the pen, the Messenger’s life displays extraordinary character, and the opponents’ own conduct exposes the weakness of their claims. It then moves from public controversy to the very ordinary moral test of wealth, food, and the rights of the poor. The story of the owners of the garden makes a private intention visible: people who plan to exclude the needy discover that their harvest was never fully under their control.

Many scholars class most of the sūrah among the early Makkan revelations, while reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah differ over the dating of some passages. Those reports should be handled with care: they illuminate early reception, but do not undo the literary coherence of the received sūrah. Whatever the date assigned to a passage, its clear progression is: the truthfulness and character of the Messenger; the anatomy of arrogant refusal; a lived parable of greed; the certainty of moral distinction before God; and a closing command to be patient. The final verses return to the original accusation of madness and reverse it. What the opponents call madness is a universal reminder.

This commentary preserves each verse as its own unit. Where a verse depends closely on its neighbours, the connection is explained without merging the verses into a single commentary. Qur'anic translations are rendered in clear English while remaining close to the supplied source. The Qur'an’s direct words, prophetic reports, and any direct scholarly citations are presented as blockquotes; explanatory discussion follows in ordinary paragraphs.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:1

> **Nūn. By the pen and by what they inscribe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An oath that dignifies knowledge**

The sūrah opens with an isolated Arabic letter, **Nūn**, followed immediately by a Divine oath: *wa-l-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūn*. God swears by the pen and by what is written through it. An oath in the Qur'an is not an attempt to persuade because the Speaker lacks credibility; it is a solemn act that directs the hearer’s attention to a sign whose significance will illuminate what follows. Here, the pen stands at the beginning of a defence of prophethood. The Messenger ﷺ is accused of irrationality, yet the reply begins with the instrument of recording, learning, deliberation, law, memory, and accountable testimony. Revelation does not ask human beings to abandon thought. It restores thought to its proper relation with the One who gave it.

The meaning of the disconnected letters (*al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*) is a matter on which the early scholars did not claim a single decisive explanation. In their treatments of such letters, al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī preserve transmitted possibilities while also reflecting the early caution against claiming certainty where revelation has not specified a meaning. A widespread and reverent approach, associated with many early exegetes, is to say that their fullest intended meaning belongs to God. This is not intellectual defeat. It is a lesson in the limits of interpretation: the Qur'an is made of the letters its first hearers knew, but its source and total depths are not mastered by human beings. Other observations offered by commentators—such as the Arabic use of *nūn* for a fish, or associations with an inkwell—remain possible linguistic reflections rather than grounds for a doctrine. The safe interpretive centre is that the letter opens a divine discourse whose certainty does not depend on our solving every literary feature.

**The pen before human self-sufficiency**

The Qur'an elsewhere joins the pen to the origin of human learning:

> **Read in the name of your Lord who created—created the human being from a clinging substance. Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught the human being what he did not know.** (Qur'an 96:1–5)

The sequence is striking. Human beings are created needy and taught what they did not know. A pen can preserve insight beyond the lifespan of its writer, transmit justice through records, and allow a community to examine evidence instead of relying only on memory or power. Yet its very usefulness exposes human dependence. No document writes itself; no archive makes itself true; and no intelligence creates the reality it describes. The Qur'an honours literacy without making literacy an idol.

Classical tafsīr commonly distinguishes levels in the phrase *what they inscribe*. It can include the writing of angels who record deeds, the writing through which human beings preserve knowledge and transactions, and the primordial writing of the decree known to God. The first two meanings are especially tangible in the Qur'an’s moral imagination:

> **When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left, not a word does he utter except that with him is an observer ready to record.** (Qur'an 50:17–18)

> **And the record will be placed, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it. They will say: What is with this record, leaving neither small nor great except that it has counted it?** (Qur'an 18:49)

The point is not that God needs clerks in order to learn what happened. His knowledge is complete. The written record manifests justice to creatures: nothing has vanished into obscurity, and no influential person can rewrite the moral past when the final account is opened.

**Decree, agency, and a written world**

A well-known report connects the pen to divine decree. It should be read alongside—not against—the Qur'an’s repeated assignment of human choices to human beings:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: God wrote the decrees of the creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Belief in *qadar* means that history is not outside God’s knowledge, will, and wisdom. It does not turn a person’s theft, lie, generosity, or repentance into something for which that person bears no responsibility. The Qur'an calls people to choose, warns them against choices, praises obedience, and judges deeds; those forms of address would be empty if moral agency were imaginary. A useful distinction is between God’s all-encompassing knowledge of an act and a servant’s willing performance of that act. A teacher who accurately knows that an unprepared student will fail did not cause the student’s refusal to study. The comparison is limited—God’s knowledge is perfect and eternal—but it helps prevent the mistaken leap from foreknowledge to coercion.

The pen therefore gives both consolation and sobriety. A believer need not imagine that an unexpected loss has escaped the Lord’s knowledge; nor may a believer hide a voluntary wrong behind the phrase, “It was written.” The first response breeds trust, the second evades repentance.

**From parchment to the digital record**

The verse also speaks powerfully in a world where writing is constant but reflection is scarce. A signature can transfer property, a message can injure a reputation, a medical record can save a life, and a fabricated image can spread falsehood to thousands in minutes. The moral status of a pen is not in its material form. A keyboard, camera, code repository, legal brief, research paper, school notebook, and private message all belong to the broad human reality of inscription. The question is not only, “Can this be written?” but, “Can I stand before God with what I am putting into the record?”

The opening oath is thus not a celebration of information alone. It is a call to truthful knowledge, responsible communication, and humility before revelation. The next verse names the immediate issue: the very man through whom this measured, morally searching Book is delivered is not possessed or deranged.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:2

> **By the grace of your Lord, you are not mad.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The accusation answered by a life**

The Arabic expression *mā anta bi-niʿmati rabbika bi-majnūn* is emphatic in more than one way. It begins with a direct negation, addresses the Prophet ﷺ personally, and grounds the negation in the favour of his Lord. The charge of *junūn* did not merely mean modern clinical illness. It was a hostile claim that his speech and conduct were controlled by a hidden spirit, and therefore that the Qur'an could be dismissed without facing its message. Similar accusations appear elsewhere:

> **They say: O you upon whom the Reminder has been sent down, you are surely mad. Why do you not bring us the angels, if you are among the truthful?** (Qur'an 15:6–7)

The Qur'an does not answer this slander by inviting the opponents to admire an abstract slogan. It directs them to the Prophet’s actual integrity, consistency, endurance, mercy, and freedom from the self-serving habits of false claimants. The following verses mention his unbroken reward and magnificent character; the wider Qur'an reminds his people that they had known him for decades before revelation. His message was difficult for their interests, but his life made the theory of madness morally and historically implausible.

**Grace is the ground of prophetic steadiness**

The phrase *bi-niʿmati rabbika*—by the blessing or grace of your Lord—has an important theological delicacy. It honours the Prophet ﷺ without presenting prophethood as a personal achievement independent of God. His soundness, revelation, patience, and moral excellence are all gifts from the Lord who chose him. At the same time, the verse does not erase the Prophet’s human effort. He endured mockery, taught, prayed, made judgements, forgave, and persevered. Islamic faith avoids two opposite errors: treating a prophet as divine, and treating divine favour as though it had no effect on a human life. God’s grace elevates and protects; the servant responds with perfect worship and responsibility.

The word *niʿmah* also quietly overturns the opponent’s scale of value. They may have imagined that wealth, clan strength, and public acclaim proved sanity and honour. The Qur'an identifies the decisive blessing differently: to be addressed by one’s Lord, entrusted with revelation, and kept upon truth. This is why a person can be socially isolated yet spiritually sound, or socially celebrated yet deeply disordered.

**Mental health is not a weapon of ridicule**

This verse must not be misused to stigmatize people who live with mental illness. The Qur'an refutes a specific malicious allegation against a truthful Messenger; it does not turn illness into a moral insult. Muslim ethics requires compassion, practical treatment, protection of dignity, and careful speech for everyone who is unwell. The Prophet ﷺ made room for vulnerable people and taught a principle of mercy rather than mockery:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: God has not sent down a disease except that He has also sent down for it a cure.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)

Seeking medical care, counselling, medication when appropriate, family support, and spiritual comfort are not competitors. A believer can pray and seek professional treatment; trust in God is not refusal to use the means God has made available. What the verse condemns is the cynical use of a label to avoid listening to truth or to humiliate someone whose message threatens one’s pride.

**How truth is often pathologized**

There is a recurring social pattern behind the accusation. When a reforming voice exposes a profitable injustice, those benefiting from it may not answer the evidence. They may call the voice unstable, extreme, naïve, dangerous, or obsessed. Labels are rhetorically efficient because they invite an audience to stop thinking. The Qur'an teaches the believer to notice that tactic without responding with equal contempt. The Prophet ﷺ was instructed to remain patient and was forbidden from trading falsehood for falsehood.

That lesson applies inside religious communities as well. A person should not diagnose another person’s inward state from disagreement, unusual temperament, or a difficult question. Nor should concern for someone’s well-being be dismissed whenever it is sincerely raised. The difference lies in truthfulness, evidence, compassion, and purpose. In this verse, the accusation is exposed because it was a slander deployed against revelation, not because the Qur'an gives permission to make casual judgments about mental health.

**A Lord who knows His servant**

The possessive formulation identifying God as the Messenger’s Lord carries consolation. Public voices can distort a person’s motives, but God knows the reality of His servant. This does not mean every believer will be vindicated immediately in public opinion. It means the deepest verdict is not controlled by a crowd, a hostile headline, or a loud family dispute. For the Prophet ﷺ, the verdict comes directly from the Lord of the worlds. For those who follow him, it is an invitation to seek moral soundness before God rather than to build a life around escaping every accusation.

The next verse adds that the cost of carrying revelation is not overlooked. The Lord who calls His Messenger sound also promises him a reward that cannot be exhausted.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:3

> **And indeed, for you is a reward never cut off.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An inexhaustible wage for an unpurchasable mission**

The phrase *wa-inna laka la-ajran ghayra mamnūn* gives the Prophet ﷺ assurance after the rejection named in the previous verse. *Ajr* is a recompense or wage; *mam­nūn* can mean cut off, diminished, held over someone as a reproach, or tainted by an obligation. The verse therefore promises more than a large reward. It promises a reward that does not run out, is not reduced by the passage of time, and is not accompanied by humiliating reminders of favour. God’s generosity is unlike a manipulative human gift that is later used to control its recipient.

This matters because prophetic work looks, at the surface level, costly. The Messenger ﷺ loses ease, faces boycotts and insults, carries the anxieties of his community, and refuses offers of worldly compromise. The Qur'an does not pretend that truthfulness is always socially profitable in the short term. Rather, it places the cost inside a horizon wider than a lifetime. The Lord who sees every rejected invitation, every patient reply, and every night of prayer gives a reward that no opponent can cancel.

> **Surely those who believe and do righteous deeds will have a reward never cut off.** (Qur'an 41:8)

The repetition of this description elsewhere shows that the Prophet ﷺ is not promised a kind of divine justice alien to his followers. His rank is unique, but the principle is shared: sincere faith and righteous action are never lost with God.

**Why the reward belongs to God alone**

The opening of this sūrah ties its Messenger to the pen, not to a commercial contract. Later verses ask whether he demands payment. He does not. His task cannot be bought by the powerful because its reward has already been secured by the One whose wealth has no limit. This freedom is morally essential. A messenger who needs an audience’s money, votes, or praise can be pressured to alter the message; a messenger whose reward is with God can tell a wealthy opponent that wealth will not make falsehood true.

The Qur'an places the same declaration on the tongues of earlier prophets:

> **I ask of you no reward for it. My reward is only with the Lord of the worlds.** (Qur'an 26:109)

The point is not that teachers, workers, translators, or caregivers may never be paid for lawful labour. Islamic law recognizes ordinary contracts and the needs of those who serve communities. The point is that revelation itself is not a commodity, and a prophet does not adjust the truth in order to secure private gain. The verse is a protection both for the audience and for the bearer of the message.

**Continuity beyond the visible result**

The description of the reward as uninterrupted corrects a narrow way of evaluating religious work. People often measure worth by visible outcome: how many applauded, how quickly an injustice disappeared, whether a family member changed, or whether a difficult conversation ended well. The prophetic measure includes faithful effort even when the outcome remains hidden. Noah preached for generations; prophets were opposed by their own people; the final Messenger ﷺ saw many reject him during the Makkan period. Their success was never defined by a market metric.

This does not excuse carelessness. A teacher should prepare, a parent should listen, and a caller to God should use wisdom. But it releases the servant from the fantasy that only immediate measurable success counts. A seed planted in another person’s conscience may grow years later, after the planter has died. God’s record is more complete than a human report.

**No favour held over the recipient**

One shade of *ghayra mamnūn* is especially tender: God’s reward is not a gift repeatedly held over the servant’s head. Human beings rightly thank one another, but generosity can be corrupted when a giver says, in effect, “You owe me your dignity because I once helped you.” The Qur'an teaches that even human charity should avoid this poison:

> **O you who believe, do not nullify your charities by reminders of generosity or injury.** (Qur'an 2:264)

If people are warned not to wound recipients by counting their gifts, then how much more perfect is the Lord’s giving. He knows every shortcoming of His servant, yet His promised reward is not grudging, unstable, or humiliating. This helps a believer understand worship as grateful response, not a negotiation with a resentful deity.

**The enduring reward of good influence**

The Prophet ﷺ also taught that a person’s benefit can continue after death:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: When a human being dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, or a righteous child who prays for him.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This hadith gives a limited human analogy for the verse. A book that teaches truth, a well that serves travellers, a child formed by faithful care, or a quiet act that inspires another good act can outlive its first moment. The Prophet’s ﷺ reward is unsurpassed because his guidance reaches every later believer who learns, prays, gives, and reforms through the message he delivered. Yet the hadith also invites ordinary people to ask what kind of trace they are leaving.

The reward promised in this verse leads naturally to the reason the Messenger is such a trustworthy bearer of guidance: his inner and outward conduct are described next as magnificent character.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:4

> **And indeed, you are surely upon tremendous character.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Character as the lived proof of revelation**

This concise verse, *wa-innaka laʿalā khuluqin ʿaẓīm*, is among the Qur'an’s clearest descriptions of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ. The preposition *ʿalā* carries the image of being firmly established upon something: he is not occasionally capable of good manners, but stands upon a lofty moral foundation. *Khuluq* means inward disposition or character. It is related in sound and root-pattern to *khalq*, created form. The pairing is meaningful: a beautiful outward form is a gift, but a beautiful inward form is cultivated through worship, discipline, truthfulness, and divine aid.

The verse comes after the charge of madness. A single strange utterance can be misrepresented; a lifetime of balanced moral conduct is much harder to explain away. His enemies could disagree with his message, but they knew his trustworthiness, restraint, courage, and concern for people. The Qur'an makes his character part of the evidence that revelation had not produced disorder in him. It had refined and displayed the finest human servitude to God.

When ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet’s wife and a major transmitter of his life, was asked about his character, she directed the questioner to the Qur'an. Ibn Kathīr prominently cites the report when explaining this verse, because it makes the exegetical claim concrete through the witness of someone who knew the Prophet’s ﷺ domestic and public conduct. The report is given below:

> **His character was the Qur'an.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This does not mean that he merely recited scripture beautifully. It means the Qur'an’s commands, prohibitions, patience, justice, mercy, courage, modesty, and reverence became visible in how he spoke, forgave, led, ate, judged, and treated the weak.

**Greatness without self-display**

The adjective *ʿaẓīm* means tremendous, immense, or great. In ordinary speech, greatness can suggest status, domination, or the ability to make others small. Qur'anic moral greatness is different. It includes the ability to restrain anger when revenge is available, to speak truth when silence is profitable, to honour a poor person in front of the rich, and to remain gentle without becoming morally weak. It is strength governed by worship.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that moral strength is not the same as physical force:

> **The strong person is not the one who overcomes people by wrestling. Rather, the strong person is the one who controls himself when angry.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This prophetic definition exposes a common mistake. An intimidating manager, a cutting debater, or a person who wins every household argument may appear powerful while being ruled by anger. Conversely, the person who pauses, verifies, speaks fairly, and refuses to humiliate another person may look less dramatic while possessing the deeper strength of self-command.

**Ethics are not an optional ornament**

Some people divide religion into “belief” and “manners,” as if belief were serious while character were merely decorative. This verse rejects that division. The greatest messenger’s character is made part of the Qur'an itself. A creed that produces persistent cruelty, dishonesty, arrogance, or contempt has not been allowed to reach the places in a person where it is meant to work. Good character does not replace prayer, justice, or sound belief; it is one of the principal ways those commitments become real in human relationships.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **Nothing is heavier on the believer’s scale on the Day of Resurrection than good character.** (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī; graded sound)

The “scale” language guards against sentimental morality. Character is not simply a private preference for niceness. It has weight before God because it affects the dignity, safety, trust, and spiritual condition of other people. A truthful shopkeeper, a patient spouse, a fair supervisor, and a neighbour who does not spread suspicion may never be publicly famous, but their conduct creates a social world in which people can breathe.

**Formation rather than temperament alone**

No one should read the verse and conclude that good character is reserved for those born naturally calm or charming. Temperament matters, but *khuluq* is also trained. The Qur'an and Sunnah teach practices that reshape disposition: guarding the tongue, giving secretly, keeping promises, visiting the sick, reconciling conflict, reflecting before reacting, asking forgiveness, and remembering God at the moment of temptation. Repetition builds a moral habit just as repeated physical exercise builds bodily capacity.

A person who is naturally blunt may need to train gentleness; a conflict-avoidant person may need to train honest courage; a generous person may need to train boundaries that prevent resentment. The aim is not a uniform personality. It is that each personality become obedient to God and beneficial to people. The Prophet ﷺ gave a comprehensive moral standard:

> **The most beloved of you to me and the nearest of you to me in sitting on the Day of Resurrection are those of you with the best character.** (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī; graded sound)

**The Qur'an as an ethical mirror**

This reported description of his character also changes how believers read. A reader should ask not only, “What information have I acquired?” but, “What is this verse training in my response to family, enemies, money, anxiety, and power?” Sūrah al-Qalam itself provides the test: do we resist compromise in truth, refuse gossip, protect the poor, receive loss without despair, and endure public hostility with patient trust? The chapter will identify ugly traits one by one so that the reader does not use admiration for prophetic character as a substitute for examining personal conduct.

The next two verses turn the opponents’ insult back toward the future. Time and final judgment will show which party was truly afflicted and which was guided.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:5

> **So you will see, and they will see,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A verdict deferred, not abandoned**

The short phrase *fa-satubṣiru wa-yubṣirūn* shifts the sūrah from accusation to outcome. The future particle *sa-* gives the assurance an explicitly future horizon. The Prophet ﷺ and his opponents were looking at the same society but drawing opposite conclusions. One side considered revelation a mercy and idolatry a ruin; the other treated revelation as madness and inherited custom as sanity. The verse does not demand that every disagreement be settled by a loud argument in the present moment. It declares that reality will disclose what polemic tries to hide.

The doubled verb is from the root *b-ṣ-r*, connected to sight, perception, and insight. Both parties will “see,” but not necessarily in the same way or at the same time. Some opponents would see the truth in the course of worldly events: the steadiness of the Prophet ﷺ, the protection and spread of the message, the collapse of false claims when tested, or their own later repentance. Others would see only when the veil of the Hereafter is removed. The Qur'an frequently uses sight in this wider sense:

> **You were certainly heedless of this; so We removed from you your covering, and today your sight is sharp.** (Qur'an 50:22)

That final sharpness is terrifying precisely because it arrives after the period in which a person could still choose, repair, and return.

**Patience is not passivity**

A deferred verdict can sound like a call to do nothing. It is not. The Messenger ﷺ continued to recite, teach, answer questions, protect the vulnerable, and endure harm. This future declaration liberates him from the destructive urge to force recognition on people who are not ready to recognize truth. It does not relieve him of the duty to convey it beautifully. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the division clear:

> **So remind; you are only a reminder. You are not over them a controller.** (Qur'an 88:21–22)

A believer may therefore work hard while refusing to confuse effort with control. A parent can offer counsel, a teacher can prepare carefully, and a community can establish just institutions, but none can compel the heart to receive guidance. This is neither fatalism nor indifference. It is moral work joined to humility about outcomes.

**When public opinion feels final**

The verse is especially healing for anyone who has been misrepresented. In the moment of insult, a false narrative can appear to have won simply because it is repeated. A workplace rumor, a distorted social-media post, or a family’s settled prejudice can make truth seem powerless. The Qur'an does not promise that every reputation will be repaired immediately in this world. It gives a deeper assurance: public opinion is not the court of final appeal.

That assurance must not become an excuse for refusing correction. A person who has caused harm should not tell every critic, “You will see.” The Prophet ﷺ was vindicated because his message and conduct were true. The verse calls for patient confidence when one is standing upon truth, not defensive self-certainty whenever one is challenged. The practical question is: have I honestly examined the evidence, sought counsel from trustworthy people, and remained willing to repent if shown wrong? Patience and self-review belong together.

**Sight that becomes moral knowledge**

The verse prepares for the next question: who, exactly, is afflicted? It exposes the weakness of judging truth by immediate comfort, numbers, or social status. The wealthy Makkan opponent may have looked successful; the persecuted believer may have looked defeated. Yet worldly visibility is not the same as moral clarity. A person can see the road, know its danger, and still drive toward the cliff because pride has made turning around unbearable.

This is why the Qur'an does not merely promise an immediate defeat for opponents. It says both sides will see. The faithful too will see the full wisdom behind trials they could not understand. A believer who suffered for a principle will see its hidden fruit; a person who was spared an apparent success will see what it would have cost. The verse expands hope beyond revenge. The final unveiling belongs to a Lord who knows not only public actions but inner intentions and unrecorded wounds.

**Living without the need to win every moment**

There is profound psychological discipline in this brief future tense. Much wrongdoing begins in the panic to be seen as right now. People exaggerate, shame opponents, manipulate evidence, or compromise convictions because they cannot tolerate temporary misunderstanding. This verse gives a different kind of courage: live truthfully enough that time, consequence, and God’s judgment can be trusted to reveal what argument alone cannot settle.

Such trust should make speech calmer, not colder. It allows a person to say what is true without needing to crush another person. It allows continued invitation rather than resentment. And it puts the present dispute in its proper place: the One who will make the truth visible has not lost sight of it now.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:6

> **which of you is the afflicted.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The insult is turned into a question of reality**

Verse 6 completes the sentence begun in the previous verse: both sides will see *bi-ayyikumu al-maftūn*. The expression has been explained in more than one closely related way: which of you has been afflicted with madness, in which of you is the affliction, or which of you has been subjected to the decisive test. The Arabic root *f-t-n* originally evokes exposing metal to fire so that its quality is revealed. From that root come ideas of testing, temptation, upheaval, and being tried. The opponents had confidently placed the label of madness on the Messenger ﷺ. The Qur'an answers that the real test of soundness is not a crowd’s insult but the final exposure of what a person loved, followed, and became.

There is irony here, but it is not cheap mockery. The Qur'an does not answer the insult with a counter-insult. It makes a moral and eschatological claim: the party that rejects the clear signs of God, calls truth insanity, and follows arrogance will discover the affliction of its own condition. A person may look composed while pursuing a path that destroys judgment from within. The Qur'an gives a general image of that reversal:

> **Is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him a light by which he walks among people like one who is in darknesses from which he cannot emerge?** (Qur'an 6:122)

The question does not erase the opponent’s humanity. It identifies a spiritual condition whose danger is deeper than public embarrassment.

**Fitnah is exposure as well as temptation**

Modern readers often use *fitnah* only for a scandal or conflict. In Qur'anic language, it is broader. Wealth can be *fitnah*, suffering can be *fitnah*, authority can be *fitnah*, and an alluring falsehood can be *fitnah*. The issue is not simply whether an event feels pleasant or painful. It is whether the event reveals a heart that turns toward God or away from Him.

> **Every soul will taste death, and We test you with evil and with good as a trial; and to Us you will be returned.** (Qur'an 21:35)

This verse prevents a simplistic reading of Sūrah al-Qalam. The Prophet ﷺ was harmed and opposed, but hardship did not prove that he was false. His persecutors enjoyed status and resources, but ease did not prove that they were right. Both adversity and prosperity are tests. The later garden parable will make this point concrete: abundance itself can become the fire in which greed is exposed.

**Do not use the verse as a licence to diagnose souls**

Since God promises to reveal who was truly afflicted, believers should be especially cautious about pretending to possess that verdict now. It is easy to read a verse about opponents of revelation and use it to declare every personal rival spiritually diseased. That reverses the verse’s moral discipline. The opponents’ error was false confidence about the Prophet ﷺ; the reader must not imitate their confidence about others.

Islamic ethics permits moral judgments about public actions—lying is wrong, oppression is wrong, exploitation is wrong—but leaves hidden final states to God. A person may have committed serious wrong and still repent sincerely. Another may appear outwardly pious while concealing injustice. The Qur'an trains believers to combine clarity about deeds with humility about ultimate destinies.

**The psychology of projection**

Calling a truthful person irrational can be an act of projection. A group whose interests depend on an unjust arrangement feels threatened by anyone who names the injustice. Rather than reconsider the arrangement, it may accuse the critic of instability. The mechanism is familiar today: a person raises evidence of harm, and the response shifts from the evidence to the person’s tone, personality, or supposed motives. Sometimes tone does need correction; often it is used as an escape route from a serious claim.

The Qur'anic response is neither to deny the value of mental well-being nor to encourage suspicion. It invites examination of fruits. Does the message call to truthfulness, worship of the Creator, protection of the weak, responsibility, and repentance? Does the bearer’s conduct display integrity? Does the opposing claim rest on evidence or only on a dismissive label? This is a more humane and rational way to test a message than social mockery.

**The final test changes present priorities**

A person who remembers that ultimate sight is coming begins to ask different questions. Instead of “Who looks successful today?” one asks, “What is this success doing to my gratitude and justice?” Instead of “Who won the argument?” one asks, “Who followed what is true after it became clear?” The shift is not escapism; it makes present choices more responsible because every choice becomes part of the test.

Verse 6 therefore creates the moral space for verse 7. If human perception is limited and accusations can be reversed, then the decisive knowledge of straying and guidance belongs to the Lord, not to the loudest party.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:7

> **Surely your Lord knows best who has strayed from His way, and He knows best who are rightly guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lord alone has the complete map**

The verse answers the contest of claims in verses 5–6 with a principle of divine knowledge. *Inna rabbaka huwa aʿlamu* means that the Lord knows best—not merely better than one opponent, but with knowledge no creature can match—who has gone astray from His path and who is rightly guided. The two descriptions are deliberately parallel. Guidance and deviation are not finally decided by pedigree, wealth, eloquence, majority, or a person’s ability to defend himself in public. They are known perfectly by the Lord who knows revelation, circumstances, motives, opportunities, wounds, secrets, and outcomes.

The possessive phrase that locates the path in God is important. Guidance is not a private invention in which every desire receives the name of truth. It is relation to the path God has shown: worship of Him alone, truthful response to His messengers, justice, mercy, repentance, and obedience. At the same time, the verse avoids making humans the owners of the path. It is *His* way, so no group may turn its customs, party, ethnicity, or personal preference into the ultimate measure of everyone else.

A near-parallel occurs in another Makkan passage:

> **Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His way, and He knows best the guided.** (Qur'an 6:117)

The repeated formulation gives the Messenger ﷺ a stable basis for patience. He must convey the message with clarity; he is not asked to penetrate every heart or to carry the burden of producing guidance by himself.

**Knowledge is not compulsion**

This verse raises a necessary question: if God knows who will stray and who will be guided, are human choices meaningful? The Qur'an refuses the conclusion that divine knowledge makes responsibility unreal. It speaks repeatedly to people as agents who hear, choose, turn, refuse, repent, and answer for what they did. For example:

> **Whoever wills—let him believe; and whoever wills—let him disbelieve.** (Qur'an 18:29)

The verse is not a celebration of disbelief; its context warns of consequences. It shows, however, that human willing is treated as real. God’s eternal knowledge of an action is not the same as forcing that action. He knows the servant’s freely chosen response more fully than the servant knows it, but the servant still intends, acts, and bears the moral meaning of the act.

Classical Sunni discussions of *qadar* preserve both sides: nothing escapes God’s knowledge and will, and people are accountable for their acquired deeds. Reducing everything to compulsion would make command, prohibition, praise, blame, and repentance incoherent. Reducing everything to independent human control would deny the creature’s need for God and the comprehensive sovereignty of the Creator. The Qur'an’s language is more demanding than either reduction. It requires reliance on God and serious moral effort at the same time.

**Why appearances cannot settle guidance**

Human beings see fragments. We may see a person’s final public speech but not decades of private struggle. We may see someone leave an obvious sin but not the arrogance that simply moved into a new form. We may see an opponent and assume permanent enmity, while God knows that repentance will arrive before death. In the Prophet’s own life, people who once persecuted the Muslims later became devoted believers. The verse protects hope by refusing to freeze anyone inside a current public label.

It also protects justice. Not every prosperous person is approved by God, and not every afflicted person is abandoned. The garden owners later in this sūrah are a powerful case: their property is a test, not a certificate of their goodness. The Prophet ﷺ is attacked and economically pressured, yet he is the recipient of revelation and divine reward. Earthly conditions are morally significant because of how people respond to them, not because they announce a final verdict by themselves.

**Guidance needs both humility and effort**

Because guidance is from God, believers ask for it constantly. The central daily prayer of Sūrah al-Fātiḥah is not a request for bare information but for being kept, increased, and made steadfast upon the straight path:

> **Guide us to the straight path—the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour.** (Qur'an 1:6–7)

This prayer is made by people who already believe because guidance has layers. One needs to recognize truth, love it, prefer it over desire, act upon it, and remain firm until death. Knowledge without humility can become an argument for pride. Emotion without knowledge can become zeal without direction. The guided person continually asks God to unite insight, intention, and action.

The practical implication is modest but profound: do not claim purity for yourself, do not despair of someone else’s return, and do not stop seeking the means of guidance. Read revelation, pray, ask questions, keep good company, repair wrongs quickly, and seek forgiveness when conscience signals deviation. A traveler on a dangerous road does not claim to own the road; he watches its signs and asks for help when the weather changes.

**A consolation that preserves moral seriousness**

For the Prophet ﷺ, this verse means that the opponents’ accusations do not define reality. For the believer, it means that God’s knowledge is a consolation without being a sedative. One should not become relaxed about sin because God knows; one should become vigilant because God knows. The next verse translates that knowledge into conduct: the Messenger must not obey the deniers, even if their pressure is intense and their claims are loud.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:8

> **So do not obey the deniers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A short command after a long assurance**

After affirming that the Lord knows who is guided and who has strayed, the sūrah gives the Prophet ﷺ a direct practical command: *fa-lā tuṭiʿ al-mukadhdhibīn*—do not obey those who deny. The connection matters. Divine knowledge is not offered as an abstract doctrine; it equips faithful conduct under pressure. Because God knows the real status of the competing claims, the Messenger ﷺ need not make the deniers’ approval the measure of wisdom.

The word *mukadhdhibīn* is stronger than simple disagreement. It describes people who declare the truth to be false, especially after its signs have been presented. The command does not tell the Prophet ﷺ to hate every individual or to refuse ordinary human fairness. It tells him not to obey denial where obedience would mean distorting revelation, abandoning his mission, or accepting a false moral standard.

**Obedience is not the same as kindness**

A crucial Qur'anic distinction prevents this verse from being misused as a charter for hostility. Islam does not command a believer to obey another person in disobedience to God, but it does command justice, good conduct, and fulfilment of rights. The Qur'an states:

> **God does not forbid you, regarding those who did not fight you because of religion and did not expel you from your homes, from being kind to them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, God loves those who act justly.** (Qur'an 60:8)

A Muslim may work alongside, trade with, help, visit, and speak graciously to a person who does not share the faith. The Prophet ﷺ dealt honourably with neighbours and visitors of different convictions. What is prohibited here is yielding the truth itself—allowing external pressure to make revelation say what it does not say or to make prohibited conduct appear acceptable merely because influential people demand it.

This distinction applies beyond formal religious conflict. A young person may love parents deeply while respectfully refusing a request to lie. An employee may honour a supervisor while declining to falsify records. A citizen may cooperate for public good without approving oppression. Moral independence is not rebellion for its own sake; it is fidelity to a higher trust.

**The quiet mechanics of pressure**

Obedience to falsehood rarely begins with an open demand to abandon everything. It often arrives as small adjustments: avoid one teaching so no one is offended; make one dishonest exception because the client matters; stay silent about one injustice because the room is powerful; recast one clear obligation as merely personal preference. The next verse identifies the desired endpoint as compromise. Verse 8 stops the process at its first step.

This is why the command is addressed first to the Prophet ﷺ, whose character has just been praised. Even the most principled person needs divine instruction when pressure becomes socially complex. Good character alone does not tell a person every boundary; revelation gives the criterion. Conversely, insistence on a boundary without character can become vanity or cruelty. In this sūrah, firmness is framed by the Prophet’s tremendous character, not by contempt for others.

**No obedience in disobedience**

The Prophet ﷺ gave a concise rule for conflicts of authority:

> **There is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator.** (Musnad Aḥmad; also reported with sound meaning in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This maxim does not authorize chaos. It recognizes that every human authority—family, employer, scholar, ruler, group, or online community—has limits. A believer obeys lawful and beneficial direction, but God’s command cannot be overwritten by social rank. The same person who refuses a sinful order should still speak respectfully, avoid unnecessary harm, and accept lawful consequences where necessary. The Prophet’s model joins courage to discipline.

**What fidelity looks like now**

In contemporary life, verse 8 challenges both obvious and subtle forms of conformity. It can mean refusing to participate in financial deception even when it is normalized. It can mean not joining a pile-on of mockery to secure belonging. It can mean preserving the truthfulness of religious teaching while explaining it with wisdom to people who find it difficult. It can mean refusing to make worship, modesty, racial justice, family responsibility, or sexual ethics into a bargaining chip for approval.

The believer need not be abrasive to be firm. A bridge stands because its supports are anchored, not because it attacks the river below. Likewise, a person grounded in revelation can listen carefully, acknowledge shared concerns, and communicate graciously without surrendering the source of moral direction. Verse 9 will name the particular temptation: an apparently smooth compromise that asks truth to become soft at its centre.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:9

> **They wish that you would compromise, so that they too would compromise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The oil-slick image of *mudāhanah***

The key verb in this verse is *tud'hinu*, from a root associated with oil and softening. In this context, it does not praise gentleness. It describes *mudāhanah*: smoothing over a matter by sacrificing a principle, treating a serious truth as though it were negotiable in order to purchase peace, status, or advantage. The deniers do not simply want a peaceful public coexistence with the Prophet ﷺ. They want revelation to be softened at the point where it judges their idolatry, injustice, and moral autonomy. If he yields there, they will respond with a reciprocal softening—not toward truth, but toward a mutual arrangement in which no one challenges what matters.

This explains why verse 8 comes immediately before it. The pressure to obey is often presented as a reasonable request for harmony. But harmony built by concealing truth is fragile. It resembles painting over a crack in a wall: the surface looks calmer, while the structural weakness remains and grows beneath it.

> **Say: O disbelievers, I do not worship what you worship, nor are you worshippers of what I worship. For you is your religion, and for me is mine.** (Qur'an 109:1–2, 6)

Sūrah al-Kāfirūn is not an instruction to insult or persecute. It establishes the boundary that worship of God alone cannot be traded for a rotational or blended arrangement with idolatry.

**Gentleness is not compromise**

It is essential not to confuse *mudāhanah* with *mudārāh*, wise and gentle treatment of people. The Prophet ﷺ was gentle, patient, and forgiving. He chose kind words, accepted gifts, visited the sick, and made allowances for weakness. None of that required him to say that falsehood was true or that injustice was harmless. Good communication lowers unnecessary barriers to truth; corrupt compromise lowers the truth itself.

The Qur'an commands the Prophet ﷺ to speak in a way that is best:

> **And say to My servants that they should speak that which is best. Surely Satan sows discord among them.** (Qur'an 17:53)

The combination is a reliable ethical test. If a person’s “firmness” makes him enjoy humiliating people, he may be serving ego rather than truth. If a person’s “kindness” requires him to praise what God has forbidden, he may be serving approval rather than mercy. The Qur'anic middle path is lucid conviction delivered with justice and good speech.

**Compromise often begins with a false symmetry**

The verse’s reciprocal-compromise clause exposes a common negotiation tactic. A powerful party frames its demand as mutual moderation: “If you stop saying that our practice is wrong, we will stop criticizing you.” But not all differences are symmetric. There is a difference between asking someone to speak respectfully and asking someone to deny the core of revelation. There is a difference between sharing civic space and sharing worship. There is a difference between revising a human habit and altering a divine command.

The sūrah’s opponents benefited from a religious economy of idols, inherited prestige, and social hierarchy. A monotheistic message that made every human accountable before one Lord threatened that arrangement. Their desired compromise was therefore not neutral diplomacy. It asked the Messenger ﷺ to protect the structures that revelation came to reform.

**Boundaries can make relationships more honest**

People often fear that clear religious boundaries make coexistence impossible. In fact, honest boundaries can make coexistence more stable. A relationship built on pretending not to believe what one believes will eventually resent its own falseness. A relationship in which people know where they differ but commit to justice, kindness, and peaceful conduct has a more truthful foundation.

In a family, workplace, or plural society, a believer can say, “I respect you and will treat you fairly; I cannot endorse this action or participate in it.” Such a statement may be difficult, but it avoids the two corrosive alternatives of contempt and dishonesty. It recognizes the other person’s dignity without surrendering worship and conscience.

**The personal bargains beneath public slogans**

Not every *mudāhanah* involves public theology. A person can compromise by accepting a promotion built on fraud, hiding abuse because it would embarrass a group, or altering a religious practice to gain online acceptance. The temptation often feels minor because it promises quick relief from awkwardness. Verse 9 asks whether the relief is purchased by blurring what one knows is true.

The remedy is not rigid performance. It is a heart that values God’s pleasure above the unstable applause of people. That heart can apologize for a harsh tone, learn from better evidence, and adjust a mistaken opinion—none of those are compromises with falsehood. It simply refuses to sell the foundation. The sūrah now turns from the pressure of a proposed bargain to a vivid moral profile of the kind of person whose influence must not be followed.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:10

> **And do not obey every habitual swearer, contemptible in character—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A profile begins with speech that has lost its weight**

Verse 10 begins a sustained description of a corrupt influencer: *wa-lā tuṭiʿ kulla ḥallāfin mahīn*. The instruction repeats the command not to obey, but it now adds a profile that helps readers recognize how moral corruption acquires social influence. *Ḥallāf* is an intensive form: not merely someone who takes an oath when a serious matter requires it, but a person given to incessant swearing. *Mahīn* conveys lowness, contemptibility, or a character made cheap by its habits.

The first trait is telling. An oath invokes God’s name as a witness. When a person repeatedly swears to make every casual claim sound convincing, the oath is emptied of its gravity. This often signals a deeper problem: truth no longer has enough force by itself, so the speaker tries to manufacture credibility through religious language, emotional pressure, or exaggerated certainty. The Qur'an does not condemn careful testimony; it condemns the manipulation of sacred language and the normalization of false assurance.

> **And do not make God, by your oaths, an obstacle to your being righteous, reverent, and reconciling between people.** (Qur'an 2:224)

> **God will not take you to account for unconsidered oaths, but He will take you to account for what your hearts have earned.** (Qur'an 2:225)

These verses show that oaths have moral seriousness. They are not conversational decorations or tools for trapping others.

**Why constant swearing weakens trust**

Truthful people sometimes need to swear an oath in court, reconciliation, or a solemn promise. The Qur'an itself regulates oaths rather than forbidding them entirely. But the habitual swearer turns a remedy for exceptional situations into a substitute for integrity. If every sentence arrives with “I swear to God,” listeners eventually ask why ordinary honesty is not enough. The speaker’s very insistence becomes evidence of unreliability.

The pattern remains familiar. In a sales pitch, a family dispute, a political speech, or an online debate, someone may use religious language to block questions rather than to tell the truth. The person may swear repeatedly, demand instant trust, and accuse anyone who asks for evidence of disrespect. Verse 10 teaches that piety does not require gullibility. Respect for God’s name should make a believer more careful about claims, not easier to manipulate.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against an oath used to take what belongs to another:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: Whoever takes the right of a Muslim by his oath, God has made the Fire obligatory for him and forbidden Paradise for him.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The hadith concerns a grave legal wrong, but its moral direction reaches further. An oath cannot transform falsehood into truth or theft into entitlement. It only adds sacrilege to injustice.

**Contemptible is an ethical judgment, not a social rank**

*Mahīn* should not be misunderstood as an insult aimed at poverty, disability, low social station, or an unpopular personality. Sūrah al-Qalam will repeatedly expose wealthy people whose status does not make them honourable. The “lowness” here is moral: a person lowers himself by degrading language, violating trusts, and using God’s name as a tool of deception. A poor person who is honest may be far more honoured before God than a celebrated person who lies easily.

This is an important corrective to societies that confuse confidence with credibility. Loud certainty, expensive clothing, credentials, or a powerful family can make someone appear authoritative. The verse asks a different question: does this person’s speech become more truthful when no one is watching and when truth costs him something? Moral authority grows from fidelity, not performance.

**Refusing influence without becoming harsh**

The prohibition concerns influence. It does not require the believer to return every lie with an insult. If a colleague habitually exaggerates, one can verify facts without mocking him. If a family member uses oaths to pressure others, one can decline the pressure while remaining respectful. If a public figure treats sacred language as branding, one can refuse to amplify the manipulation.

This requires inner freedom. People often obey a corrupt voice because they fear conflict, need access, or want the security of belonging. The sūrah asks the Prophet ﷺ—and through him, every reader—to decide that no amount of pressure will make false speech a moral compass. The following verse shows how the same corrupt personality uses speech not only to deceive but to break bonds between people.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:11

> **a persistent slanderer, going about with malicious gossip;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Speech that travels in order to wound**

The Qur'an continues the profile with *hammāzin mashshā'in bi-namīm*. *Hammāz* is an intensive form connected to belittling, fault-finding, and slander, whether by spoken words, facial gestures, or insinuation. *Mashshā'* pictures someone who walks repeatedly from person to person. *Namīm* is malicious tale-bearing: carrying words between people to spoil trust, ignite hostility, or elevate oneself through another person’s embarrassment. The verse does not describe an accidental mistake in reporting information. It describes a habit of movement and speech organized around division.

The Qur'an elsewhere gives a sharp warning about the slanderer:

> **Woe to every scorner and mocker.** (Qur'an 104:1)

Mockery can be overt; malicious gossip is often quieter. It works by offering the listener a feeling of privileged access: “I should not say this, but…” The listener is invited to feel informed, loyal, or superior. Meanwhile, the absent person loses the ability to clarify, relationships become guarded, and a community spends its energy managing suspicion instead of doing good.

**Backbiting, advice, and necessary reporting**

Islamic ethics distinguishes several things that are often confused. Backbiting (*ghībah*) is mentioning about a person, in his absence, something he would dislike, even if it is true. Slander (*buhtān*) is saying what is false. *Namīmah* is carrying speech in a way that corrupts relations. All are serious harms. The Qur'an gives an unforgettable image for backbiting:

> **And do not backbite one another. Would one of you love to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would detest it.** (Qur'an 49:12)

Yet not every mention of someone’s fault is forbidden. A victim may report abuse, a witness may give testimony, a doctor may need relevant information, a person seeking counsel may describe a problem, and a community may warn against clear harm. The difference lies in necessity, truth, proportion, intention, and restraint. Is the information being shared with someone able to address a genuine need? Is it verified? Is it limited to what is needed? Or is it being carried for the pleasure of exposure and division?

**A prophetic warning about social sabotage**

The Prophet ﷺ warned clearly about tale-bearing:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: No tale-bearer will enter Paradise.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The warning does not mean that repentance is closed or that a Muslim should pronounce a final verdict on an individual. It reveals the gravity of a sin that many people treat as entertainment. A tongue can create injuries that outlast a physical wound because it changes what people believe about one another. The severity of the warning matches the seriousness of breaking families, workplaces, and communities from within.

In another authentic report, the Prophet ﷺ passed two graves and explained that one of the occupants had been punished for going about with malicious gossip. The point is not morbid curiosity. It is that sins dismissed as “only words” are still deeds placed into a record.

**The modern speed of *namīmah***

The traveller of the verse once walked between tents and gatherings. Today a screenshot, clipped recording, forwarded voice note, or anonymous post can travel across continents before the original speaker knows it exists. Technology changes the scale, not the moral structure. Context can be stripped away, a private mistake can be converted into public spectacle, and people can participate in *namīmah* simply by forwarding what they have not verified.

The Qur'an offers a practical brake:

> **O you who believe, if a defiantly disobedient person comes to you with news, verify it, lest you harm people in ignorance and then become regretful for what you have done.** (Qur'an 49:6)

Verification is not suspicion for its own sake. It is mercy for everyone involved: the accused, the hearer, and even the bearer of news, who may himself be mistaken or manipulative.

**Repairing a tongue-trained habit**

Someone who recognizes this habit in himself should not despair. Repair begins by stopping the transmission, deleting or correcting what can be corrected, seeking forgiveness from God, and apologizing to the person harmed when doing so will genuinely heal rather than reopen danger. It also involves replacing the reward of gossip with the reward of protection: speak well of an absent person, refuse baited conversations, and change the subject without self-righteousness.

A reliable personal test is to ask, “Would I say this if the person were in the room, and is there a legitimate purpose in saying it at all?” That question does not solve every complex case, but it slows the rush toward harm. Verse 12 now adds another dimension: corrupt character does not merely speak against people; it actively obstructs good from reaching them.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:12

> **a hinderer of good, a transgressor, steeped in sin;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Evil that blocks the good of others**

The phrase *mannāʿin lil-khayri muʿtadin athīm* deepens the moral profile. *Mannāʿ* is an intensive form: someone who repeatedly withholds or obstructs *khayr*, goodness. Exegetes have explained this both as extreme miserliness and as blocking people from faith or righteous action. The two meanings fit together. A person can hinder material good by refusing the poor their due, and hinder moral good by discouraging prayer, truthfulness, education, reconciliation, or access to guidance. Corruption is not only doing wrong personally; it is building barriers that keep good from circulating.

The word *khayr* is deliberately broad. It includes wealth when wealth is used beneficially, but it also includes revelation, justice, sound advice, mercy, and public benefit. The later garden story will portray this trait in action: people plan to keep the needy away from a harvest that God made possible. The sūrah therefore joins theology to economics. A person cannot claim reverence for the Creator while treating the Creator’s bounty as a private fortress against those in need.

> **And in their wealth there was a known right for the one who asks and the one deprived.** (Qur'an 70:24–25)

The Qur'an’s wording is stronger than a mere appeal to occasional generosity. It presents a recognized right, which changes how the giver thinks. The poor person is not simply an interruption of wealth; the wealthy person is a trustee of a share that must not be wrongfully withheld.

**Transgression crosses limits set for human good**

*Muʿtadin* comes from the root of crossing a boundary. *Athīm* describes someone deeply involved in sin, someone for whom wrongdoing has become a practiced pattern rather than a rare fall followed by repentance. Together, the words show that obstruction of good is not a harmless personality quirk. It grows from a refusal to respect the limits God has set for truth, property, dignity, and relationship.

There is a difference between falling into sin and becoming settled in it. Every human being errs. The danger named here is not that someone needs forgiveness, but that he converts wrongdoing into an identity and recruits others into it. He discourages the generous, mocks the conscientious, blocks reform, and calls restraint weakness. The spiritual harm is social: a community eventually makes goodness feel embarrassing and exploitation normal.

> **And do not transgress. Indeed, God does not love the transgressors.** (Qur'an 2:190)

Although that verse occurs in a context of conflict, its moral principle is broad. Even when one has power, grievance, or a seemingly good cause, there are limits one may not cross.

**Miserliness is a false theory of security**

The hinderer of good often imagines that withholding is rational self-protection. “If I share, there will not be enough.” “If I support this reform, I may lose influence.” “If I acknowledge another person’s right, my own position will weaken.” Sūrah al-Qalam answers this theory through the garden owners: the provisions one tries to preserve through injustice can disappear altogether.

This does not mean a believer must give without thought or neglect legitimate responsibilities. Islam recognizes family duties, debts, planning, and stewardship. The sin is not prudent budgeting; it is allowing fear and possessiveness to make a person deny an established right or prevent good that one is able to facilitate. Generosity needs wisdom, and wisdom must not be a decorative name for greed.

**Obstruction can be institutional**

The verse also speaks to systems, not only individuals. A policy can make it needlessly difficult for the poor to access food, medicine, education, or legal protection. A culture can ridicule people for seeking knowledge or reporting abuse. An organization can reward those who hide wrongdoing and punish those who speak responsibly. In each case, “hinderer of good” becomes a structural description. The Qur'anic reader should ask not only, “Do I personally give?” but also, “Do my decisions make it easier or harder for good to reach others?”

This question belongs in homes as much as governments. A parent who mocks a child’s desire to learn, a spouse who prevents charitable spending out of sheer control, or a friend group that derides every moral aspiration can all become small barriers against *khayr*. Conversely, a person who connects someone to a job, protects a reputation, shares useful knowledge, or makes repentance feel possible becomes a channel through which good travels.

**The antidote: make good easier**

The Prophet ﷺ taught a simple social principle:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: Make things easy and do not make them difficult; give glad tidings and do not drive people away.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This is not an instruction to erase obligations. It means that a teacher, leader, parent, or caller to God should not place needless obstacles in the path of what God has made good. Clarity, encouragement, fair access, and practical help are forms of worship.

Verse 12 asks readers to notice whether their influence releases good or chokes it. Verse 13 moves to the harsh temper that often protects a life built on obstruction.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:13

> **coarse, and beyond that, marked by disgrace—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Harshness joined to a settled moral mark**

The verse uses two unusually forceful terms: *ʿutullin baʿda dhālika zanīm*. *ʿutull* conveys roughness, harshness, and a cruel or overbearing manner. It is not strength in the noble sense of courage or resilience; it is a force that pushes through people without regard for their dignity. The phrase *baʿda dhālika*—“beyond that” or “after all of that”—suggests that the preceding traits are already grave, and that an additional, conspicuous moral defect completes the portrait.

The word *zanīm* has been explained in several ways in classical Arabic and tafsīr. It can evoke a person marked or attached to a group in a way that makes a trait conspicuous; some old reports use it in a harsh genealogical sense. Those reports must not be turned into a license for contempt toward anyone because of birth, family, adoption, or ancestry. The Qur'an’s own moral principle is unequivocal:

> **No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another.** (Qur'an 6:164)

A human being is not guilty because of origins outside his choice. In this context, the enduring lesson of *zanīm* is that persistent vice can become so visible that it functions like a mark: a person becomes known not by lineage but by the trail of harm left through arrogance, slander, greed, and cruelty.

**The difference between strength and brutality**

The sūrah’s description is a needed correction to cultures that praise harshness as leadership. A manager who humiliates staff, a parent who frightens children into silence, or a public figure who enjoys crushing opponents may acquire obedience, but not moral authority. Fear can produce compliance for a time; it cannot produce trust, inward reform, or a healthy community.

The Prophet ﷺ was sent with extraordinary courage, yet his courage was inseparable from mercy. God addresses him:

> **It is by mercy from God that you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you.** (Qur'an 3:159)

This verse does not say that gentleness means lack of standards. It appears in a passage about leadership after a painful military setback. The Prophet ﷺ still taught, corrected, consulted, and made decisions. The difference is between firm principle and a heart that enjoys making people small.

**A character becomes visible through repetition**

No single angry moment makes someone the person described here. Human beings may speak sharply under stress, then regret it and repair the damage. The Qur'an’s sequence describes repeated habits: habitual oath-taking, travelling with gossip, blocking good, transgression, entrenched sin, and coarse conduct. Repetition gives moral acts a social identity. People learn whether another person’s apology is followed by change or whether cruelty returns as soon as it is convenient.

This has a practical implication for self-examination. Rather than asking only, “Did I mean well?” one should ask, “What pattern do my words and decisions create around me?” If several people independently fear one’s reactions, if one is always at the centre of broken trust, or if compassion feels like weakness, these may be warning signs worth bringing before God in repentance and before wise people in honest counsel.

**Do not weaponize Qur'anic descriptions**

The force of this verse can tempt readers to paste its labels onto political opponents, relatives, or members of another school of thought. That is spiritually dangerous. The Qur'an reveals a moral anatomy so that the reader may avoid it, not so that the reader can enjoy the prestige of condemning others. A person may need to name abuse, fraud, or cruelty plainly; that is different from delighting in stripping another person of dignity.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against abusive language:

> **The believer is not one who taunts, curses, uses obscene speech, or is foul-mouthed.** (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī; graded sound)

The believer therefore learns from the verse to set boundaries around destructive influence while keeping his own tongue clean. He can say, “This conduct is unjust and must stop,” without turning correction into personal degradation.

**The mark that repentance can erase**

A moral mark is serious, but it is not necessarily permanent. Islam’s language of repentance means that even habits deeply engraved into a life can be confronted. Repair requires more than regret: it requires stopping harm, seeking forgiveness, making restitution where possible, and replacing an old pattern with a new one. A harsh person may need to learn listening; a gossip may need to practise silence; a miser may need structured generosity. The fact that a trait has become visible makes deliberate retraining more urgent, not less possible.

The next verse uncovers one source of the arrogance described here: a person mistakes wealth and children for proof that he deserves deference, even when his character tells another story.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:14

> **because he possesses wealth and children.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Possessions mistaken for a moral credential**

This brief clause exposes a motive beneath the preceding moral ugliness: *an kāna dhā mālin wa-banīn*—because he has wealth and sons. The syntax is read in more than one connected way. It may explain why the person is given undue influence despite his vices, or it may lead into the next verse: because he has wealth and children, he reacts to revelation with arrogant dismissal. In either reading, the Qur'an identifies a dangerous mental error: treating material abundance and family strength as evidence that one is right, untouchable, or entitled to define moral reality.

In the tribal setting of the first hearers, wealth and sons meant security, political reach, defenders, inheritance, and public prestige. The verse does not deny that money and family can be genuine blessings. It denies that they certify a heart. A person may possess both and still be a habitual liar, a gossip, a miser, and a transgressor. Another person may lack both and be honoured before God through faith, integrity, and patience.

> **Wealth and children are the adornment of the worldly life, but enduring righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward and better in hope.** (Qur'an 18:46)

“Adornment” is not the same as ultimate substance. An adornment can be beautiful and useful, but it is not what a house stands on. The Qur'an asks human beings to enjoy blessings gratefully without making them the foundation of identity or judgment.

**A test, not an entitlement**

The Qur'an repeatedly calls wealth and children a test. A test is not necessarily a punishment; it is an entrusted circumstance that reveals what a person does with access, fear, love, and power.

> **Your wealth and your children are only a trial, and with God is a tremendous reward.** (Qur'an 64:15)

For some, wealth becomes a means of feeding families, supporting learning, relieving debt, protecting the vulnerable, and building public good. For others, it becomes a wall against criticism and a reason to deny others’ rights. Children can deepen mercy, responsibility, and gratitude; they can also become a pretext for greed: “I must keep everything for my own household.” The owners of the garden will make precisely this excuse in the narrative ahead.

The verse therefore does not ask a parent to love children less. It asks a parent to love God more truthfully, so that love for children does not justify injustice toward other people’s children. Provision is ultimately from God, not from a closed fist.

**The social bias of visible success**

Human societies often lend moral authority to wealth before examining character. A rich donor may be excused for abuse because he funds visible projects. A prominent family may be protected from accountability because its name opens doors. A successful entrepreneur may be treated as wise in every field merely because he accumulated money in one. Verse 14 calls this a failure of judgment.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against measuring people by their outward assets alone:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: God does not look at your forms or your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

This does not mean that outward actions are irrelevant. Deeds matter profoundly. It means that God’s measure penetrates the social display around a deed. A large public gift can conceal pride; a small private act can carry great sincerity. A family name can carry honourable history, but it cannot inherit righteousness into every descendant.

**Why arrogance is so tempting to the secure**

Material security can create an illusion of self-sufficiency. When food, housing, influence, and social protection are reliable, a person may forget the many conditions he did not create: health, rainfall, stable markets, parents, workers, social peace, and the passing of time. The Qur'an diagnoses that illusion elsewhere:

> **No! Truly the human being transgresses because he sees himself self-sufficient.** (Qur'an 96:6–7)

The wording is exact: not simply because he *is* self-sufficient, but because he *sees himself* that way. The error is perceptual before it becomes behavioural. Gratitude corrects sight by naming gifts as gifts, rights as rights, and human power as temporary.

**A practical audit of blessing**

A believer can turn verse 14 into a gentle audit. Has increased income made prayer easier or less urgent? Has family concern made one more generous or more fearful of every request? Does a position of influence make one more willing to hear correction or more skilled at avoiding it? These questions are not invitations to guilt over blessings. They are ways of keeping blessings in their proper role.

The antidote is not romanticizing poverty or neglecting one’s family. It is stewardship: earn lawfully, plan responsibly, pay what is due, teach children gratitude, welcome correction, and remember that neither account balances nor descendants will answer for a corrupt heart. The following verse shows how privilege can make a person dismiss divine signs rather than receive them.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:15

> **When Our signs are recited to him, he says: Tales of the former peoples.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A label used to avoid listening**

The phrase *idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīr al-awwalīn* portrays an encounter with revelation. God’s signs are not merely mentioned; they are recited to him, placed before his hearing in a form that asks for thought, moral response, and humility. His answer is not an argument that addresses their language, coherence, ethical force, or claim to revelation. He dismisses them as *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*, tales or legends of ancient peoples.

The Qur'an records this response in several places because it is a recurring strategy of denial. A person may call a message “just stories” not after serious examination but as a way of ensuring that it never has to become personally relevant. The label creates distance: old things are imagined to be irrelevant, and narrative is imagined to be opposed to truth. The person can then keep wealth, habits, and social rank intact without facing what the recited signs demand.

> **And when it is said to them, What has your Lord sent down? They say: Tales of the former peoples.** (Qur'an 16:24)

The repetition shows that the issue is not historical material itself. The Qur'an openly tells accounts of earlier prophets and communities. The issue is the dishonest use of “story” as a verdict before one has listened for truth.

**Narrative can carry evidence and warning**

A story need not be fiction, and a true account need not be morally inert. Human beings learn through narrative because it allows actions to unfold, consequences to become visible, and hidden motives to be tested. Sūrah al-Qalam itself will immediately tell the account of garden owners. The story is not a detour from argument; it is moral reasoning embodied in events. Greed makes a plan, the plan meets a reality outside human control, loss exposes the plan, and repentance becomes possible.

The Qur'an describes its accounts as material for reflection:

> **In their stories there is surely a lesson for people of understanding. It was not a fabricated narration, but a confirmation of what came before it, a detailed explanation of all things, and guidance and mercy for people who believe.** (Qur'an 12:111)

This does not require a reader to abandon questions about context, language, or interpretation. It does require intellectual honesty. One should not use the existence of narrative form as an excuse to evade a text’s claim or moral challenge.

**The ego’s defence against unwelcome truth**

The response in the verse is psychologically recognizable. A person who benefits from an unjust system may not be able to refute a moral critique, but he can make it sound unsophisticated: “That is outdated,” “That is myth,” “That is merely emotional,” or “That is not serious enough for modern people.” Such phrases may sometimes signal a real question; they may also be a shield against reflection. The difference appears in what happens next. Does the speaker listen, ask, compare evidence, and allow the possibility of correction? Or does he use a fashionable dismissal to prevent the conversation from starting?

The Qur'an calls people to use reason, but it repeatedly exposes reason when it is enlisted only to protect desire. A person can have great technical skill and still lack the moral courage to let evidence disturb a preferred life. The prior verse has already shown how wealth and children can reinforce this defence: privilege supplies both the incentive and the confidence to treat a divine warning as beneath one’s attention.

**Reading inherited material with reverence and intelligence**

For Muslims, this verse also cautions against a shallow reaction in the opposite direction. Respect for revelation does not mean repeating every historical claim one encounters without verification or treating every old interpretation as equally certain. The Qur'an itself asks its audience to listen, reflect, compare signs, and seek knowledge. What must be rejected is the arrogant premise that age by itself disproves truth or that contemporary fashion by itself proves it.

A serious reader asks: What does the text actually say? What is the Arabic wording? How did reliable early scholarship understand it? How does this passage relate to the Qur'an’s broader moral vision? What evidence supports a report attributed to the Prophet ﷺ? These questions honour revelation because they refuse both careless rejection and careless appropriation.

**The signs remain an address**

“Our signs” are called *āyāt*, a word that can mean verses, signs, and indicators. The same word links the recited Qur'an to signs in the world and within the human self. When a person reads this verse, the question is not only whether an ancient opponent dismissed ancient revelation. It is whether the reader hears divine instruction as a living address or turns it into distant material that makes no claim on conduct.

The warning is gentle but severe: contempt for a message can be a way of protecting a life one does not wish to reform. Verse 16 answers the arrogance of that dismissal with an image of a mark no wealth or rhetorical label can conceal.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:16

> **We shall mark him upon the snout.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A visible mark against manufactured honour**

The verse is brief and arresting: *sanasimuhu ʿalā al-khurṭūm*. *Nusim* means to mark or brand, while *khurṭūm* refers literally to a snout, a term often used for an animal’s protruding nose rather than the dignified word normally used for a human face. The imagery answers the person who inflated himself through wealth, children, oaths, and social power. He acted as if no one could expose his real character. God declares that disgrace will be made unmistakably visible.

The purpose is not to invite believers to mock someone’s physical appearance. The verse’s moral force lies precisely in the contrast between a face used for arrogant display and a moral reality that God makes known. A human being may curate reputation, hide cruelty behind generosity, or use status to silence testimony. The final account cannot be managed in that way.

> **The guilty will be known by their marks, and they will be seized by the forelocks and the feet.** (Qur'an 55:41)

The Qur'an uses bodily imagery to teach that inner deeds are not ultimately private. What a person repeatedly chooses becomes manifest before the One who knows every secret.

**Worldly reports and the lasting warning**

Classical commentators report different identifications for the opponent described in verses 10–16; some connect the passage with a leading Makkan adversary and reports of a facial wound suffered in conflict. Such reports may illuminate how early Muslims connected the warning to their history, but the Qur'an does not name an individual in the verse itself. Its wording is wider and more enduring than a single biography. Every person who uses status to make vice respectable should hear the warning.

A careful reader should also resist treating a worldly injury as proof that a particular person was condemned by God. Human suffering can be punishment, consequence, trial, expiation, or a reality whose full wisdom is unknown to observers. The Qur'an’s final judgment is certain; people are not authorized to turn every hardship in another person’s life into a confident verdict about the unseen.

**The face and the moral record**

The “snout” is a fitting image because the face is central to public recognition. People seek honour through being seen: admired photographs, titles, followers, expensive signs of rank, or the fear their face produces in others. Verse 16 says that the public self cannot finally outrun the moral record. The person who turns away contemptuously from God’s signs will be marked by the very reality he tried to conceal.

This theme does not eliminate mercy. Islam’s teaching on repentance means that a person who abandons arrogance and returns to God is not trapped by a past identity. A brand in the verse is a threat for persistent, unrepentant corruption, not a command to freeze every sinner forever in his worst moment. The door of return remains open while life remains.

**Humiliation versus accountable justice**

Modern readers may feel the severity of the image, and rightly so. The Qur'an is not indifferent to the humiliation of the arrogant because arrogance humiliates others first. The person described has manipulated God’s name, spread harm, prevented good, crossed limits, and dismissed revelation. Divine justice gives moral weight to injuries powerful people often escape in the present world.

Yet believers must not turn this justice into private vengeance. The Qur'an does not authorize someone who feels insulted to “brand” opponents publicly, expose their faults, or celebrate their misfortune. The final judgment belongs to God, who knows facts, motives, repentance, and context. The reader’s task is to remove the described traits from his own conduct and to protect people from their harm through lawful, just means.

**The mark of a different kind of life**

There is an implied alternative. If vice leaves a mark, so does repentance and integrity. A truthful person becomes known as someone whose word can be trusted; a generous person becomes a refuge; a humble person makes correction possible; a worshipper’s face is not made sacred by appearance alone, but the person’s conduct gradually becomes recognizable. The Qur'an does not reduce human worth to reputation, but it insists that character has consequences that become visible.

The sūrah now shifts from a portrait of a proud individual to a story in which an entire household discovers how quickly presumed security can be lost. The garden parable gives narrative form to the truth this verse has announced: ownership without gratitude and justice is not secure ownership at all.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:17

> **Indeed, We tested them as We tested the owners of the garden, when they swore that they would surely harvest it in the morning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From a single arrogant man to a collective test**

With *innā balawnāhum kamā balawnā aṣḥāba al-jannah*, the sūrah turns from direct warning to a parable-like account. *Balawnā* comes from a root of testing and trying. The pronoun “them” has been understood broadly as the deniers addressed by the sūrah: they are being tested through the Prophet’s message, their prosperity, and the moral choices open before them. Their case is compared to the owners of a garden, whose abundance became a test of gratitude and social responsibility.

The word *jannah* literally denotes a garden whose dense growth conceals the ground. In Qur'anic usage it can refer to a worldly orchard or the Garden of the Hereafter; here it is a productive cultivated property. The transition is deliberate. The prior passage warned against a person who possessed wealth and children. The garden owners will reveal what such possession can do when people begin to think of provision as exclusively their own.

Some tafsīr reports give details about the owners’ location, religion, and family history. The Qur'an itself leaves those details unspecified. That restraint is instructive: the moral truth does not depend on identifying the exact orchard. The story can belong to any household, business, farm, inheritance, or institution whose members inherit a blessing and then decide that those with less have no claim upon it.

**The test is not only deprivation**

People often use “test” as though it means suffering alone. This verse teaches that abundance is also a test—and often a more concealed one. When something is scarce, need is obvious. When it is abundant, the heart may begin to say, “I earned this; I control it; I can decide who deserves it.” The garden is a gift, but it becomes an examination of whether its owners recognize the Giver and the rights attached to His provision.

> **And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient.** (Qur'an 2:155)

> **Know that your wealth and your children are a trial, and that with God is a tremendous reward.** (Qur'an 8:28)

The first verse names forms of loss. The second names forms of possession. Together they establish a balanced Qur'anic view: neither loss nor abundance by itself tells us whether God loves or hates a person. The moral question is how the person responds.

**A morning plan made in false confidence**

The owners “swore” (*a qsamū*) that they would harvest early. The oath gives their plan a hard, self-assured edge. They are not simply scheduling agricultural work; the next verses show that they want to harvest before poor people can arrive to claim their due. Dawn is chosen for secrecy. The plan depends on coordinating desire, timing, and concealment.

This is why the story is so psychologically sharp. Major injustices are rarely announced as, “We intend to oppress.” They are often framed as efficiency, prudent planning, protection of one’s family, or the right to control private property. The brothers’ decision might sound reasonable if the poor are removed from the picture. The Qur'an restores the missing moral fact: provision comes from God, and other people’s need places a claim upon the conscience of the owner.

**Property, stewardship, and the poor**

Islam recognizes ownership, inheritance, work, and the right to benefit from lawful property. The garden owners are not condemned because they cultivate a field or hope for a harvest. Their wrong lies in an intention to exclude the needy from a bounty whose social obligations had been recognized. The Qur'an elsewhere commands:

> **Eat of its fruit when it bears fruit, and give its due on the day of its harvest; and do not be excessive. Indeed, He does not love the excessive.** (Qur'an 6:141)

This “due” can include the legal obligations clarified in Islamic law as well as the broader moral duty not to close the heart to a visible need. The verse gives a practical temporal detail: generosity should not always be postponed until the convenient future. Harvest day is the day when abundance is visible and the temptation to claim it all is strongest.

**The opening question for every owner**

This verse asks modern readers: what garden have we inherited or acquired? It may be income, education, professional access, a family business, land, citizenship, health, time, or a platform. Each blessing can be planned around in two ways. We can ask how to maximize private retention, or how to meet obligations while preserving the trust God has placed in our hands.

The story does not make planning sinful. The error begins when planning is severed from remembrance, gratitude, and justice. Verse 18 focuses that diagnosis in a remarkably compact phrase: they made no exception.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:18

> **And they made no exception.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A plan with no space for God or for the needy**

The Arabic phrase *wa-lā yastathnūn* is concise enough to carry more than one closely connected meaning. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī record the major early explanations: that the owners did not say *in shāʾ Allāh*—if God wills—when they resolved to harvest, and that they made no exception from the harvest for the poor. The two readings reinforce rather than cancel each other. A heart that imagines it can guarantee tomorrow’s outcome often becomes the same heart that imagines it can reserve every benefit for itself.

To say *in shāʾ Allāh* is not a verbal charm that guarantees success. It is an acknowledgement of reality: human beings make plans, but no plan controls weather, health, time, markets, life, or the final outcome. The Qur'an teaches the Prophet ﷺ:

> **And never say of anything, I will surely do that tomorrow, except by saying: If God wills. And remember your Lord when you forget.** (Qur'an 18:23–24)

This is not an instruction to avoid commitments. A Muslim should make appointments, sign contracts, prepare for work, and keep promises. The phrase trains planners to join resolve with humility. “I will do this, if God wills” means: I will take the means seriously, but I do not pretend to own the future.

**The missing exception in a budget of greed**

The other reading adds a social dimension. The owners did not set aside any portion for the poor. Their plan was total: all of it for us, none for them. This is the logic of greed at its clearest. It refuses not only generosity beyond obligation but even the recognition that another human being has a rightful claim on one’s abundance.

The Qur'an does not ask people to wait for perfect emotional warmth before fulfilling rights. A person may feel anxious about finances and still pay what is due. A business may need to plan carefully and still treat workers, customers, and vulnerable neighbours fairly. Ethical obligation is precisely what keeps fear from becoming an excuse for total self-enclosure.

> **And those in whose wealth is a known right—for the beggar and the deprived.** (Qur'an 70:24–25)

The “deprived” person may not ask openly. He may be ashamed, isolated, ill, or invisible to ordinary charity systems. The garden owners’ whispered plan will attempt to ensure that no needy person reaches them at all. The Qur'an makes the absent poor morally present before they arrive.

**Planning versus the illusion of control**

There is a healthy form of planning and an unhealthy one. Healthy planning says: “I will plant, save, prepare, insure where lawful, consult, and fulfil obligations, knowing that results are with God.” Unhealthy planning says: “If I arrange every detail cleverly enough, I can make life answer only to my desire.” The difference is not whether a spreadsheet exists. It is whether the spreadsheet has room for divine dependence, human rights, and the possibility of correction.

This distinction is liberating. If outcomes rested entirely on human control, every unexpected event would prove personal failure and every successful outcome would feed pride. Recognizing God’s will allows responsibility without omnipotence. It teaches one to work diligently, adapt when a door closes, and receive success with gratitude rather than self-worship.

**An exception that saves the whole plan**

The verse suggests that what looked like an “exception”—a portion left for the poor or a humble recognition of God’s will—was actually the moral condition that could have protected the whole undertaking. Greed sees charity as subtraction. Revelation teaches that wrongful withholding can hollow out the blessing of what remains. This does not reduce generosity to a financial technique for obtaining more; it restores the truth that property has a moral purpose beyond private consumption.

A household can apply this by building giving into its planning rather than treating it as an afterthought. A professional can reserve time for service. A business can factor fair wages and honest dealings into its model rather than call them unexpected burdens. An individual can say *in shāʾ Allāh* with consciousness, then let the phrase produce better preparation and more humility, not vague delay.

Verse 19 shows how quickly a plan that left no room for God encounters an event utterly outside its control.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:19

> **Then a visitation from your Lord came upon it while they slept,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The night reveals the limit of ownership**

The garden owners have made a secret, tightly organized plan. Before they can act, *ṭāʾifun min rabbika*—a visitation, passing calamity, or enveloping scourge from their Lord—comes upon the garden while they sleep. The word *ṭāʾif* carries the sense of something that goes around or descends upon a place. The verse does not specify its physical form. It may be understood as a destructive fire, storm, or other divinely sent calamity. The Qur'an leaves the mechanism unelaborated because the moral meaning does not depend on identifying it. In one night, the owners discover that a garden is never merely what human planning says it is.

Sleep is central to the image. While awake, people often feel competent: they calculate, lock doors, schedule work, and congratulate themselves on foresight. In sleep, even the most powerful person relinquishes control. He cannot watch his property, defend his plans, or guarantee the next morning. The verse turns this ordinary human vulnerability into spiritual instruction. The owners imagined that dawn belonged to their plan; the night belonged to the Lord who had given them the garden.

> **It is God who takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die during their sleep. Then He keeps those for which He has decreed death and releases the others until an appointed term.** (Qur'an 39:42)

Sleep is not death, but the Qur'an presents it as a daily reminder that life and capacity are received anew, not possessed without limit.

**A revealed judgment, not a licence for easy explanations**

This verse explicitly frames the destruction in this particular story as a visitation “from your Lord.” Because revelation supplies that interpretation, the story can teach a moral causal link between injustice and the loss of blessing. It does **not** give ordinary people permission to declare that every fire, illness, crop failure, market crash, or personal tragedy is a punishment for a specific sin. The Qur'an also describes hardship as a test, a consequence of natural conditions, an expiation, a means of raising rank, or a wisdom hidden from human observers.

The difference is crucial for compassion. When someone suffers loss, the believer should first offer help, grief, practical support, and prayer—not speculative accusations. The garden story is a mirror for the reader’s own intentions, not a weapon for explaining away another person’s pain.

**The Lord’s action and human responsibility**

Islamic belief affirms that nothing occurs outside God’s power and knowledge. It also affirms that human intentions are morally real. The owners chose to deny the poor their share; the calamity did not make that choice good or erase its responsibility. Divine decree and human agency meet without becoming identical. The story gives no comfort to the one who says, “Since God controls outcomes, my plan cannot be blamed.” It is precisely their plan that the Qur'an exposes.

This balance matters in everyday life. A company may suffer a loss through events beyond its control, but that does not absolve it from prior fraud. A family may face illness, but illness does not prove guilt. A person may encounter an unexpected obstacle, then use it as an occasion to review whether he has neglected an obligation. Humble self-examination is different from a verdict against oneself or others.

**Provision has conditions beyond effort**

The verse challenges the modern illusion that effort and outcome stand in a simple one-to-one relation. Work matters. Agriculture requires planting, irrigation, skill, labour, and timing. Yet rain, soil, seed vitality, health, safety, and countless other conditions lie beyond a grower’s command. In modern economies, the same truth appears through supply chains, employment, health, legal stability, and relationships. Diligence is a duty; self-congratulation is a mistake.

A believer therefore says neither “planning is useless” nor “planning guarantees success.” He takes lawful means and recognizes God as the giver of results. This recognition should generate gratitude in prosperity and resilience in loss. It should also make a person less likely to deny the rights of people who lack what he has, since the difference between abundance and need can be altered overnight.

**The hidden mercy of interruption**

A calamity can be a warning before a greater ruin. The owners will not respond perfectly at first, but their loss eventually opens a path toward confession, glorification of God, and hope. This possibility prevents the story from becoming a simple tale of destruction. A painful interruption can expose an unjust direction while repentance remains possible.

The verse thus asks an unsettling but merciful question: if God interrupted our plans tonight, what intention would be uncovered? Would our arrangements show gratitude and justice, or would they show that we had built success by excluding people to whom we owed good? Verse 20 lets the owners see the answer in the transformed condition of their garden.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:20

> **so that it became like a harvested field, stripped bare.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From promised abundance to blankness**

The Arabic expression *fa-aṣbaḥat ka-l-ṣarīm* conveys a shocking transformation. *Ṣarīm* has been explained as a field stripped by harvesting, a garden burned until black, or something cut off from its fruit. The variations converge in the image of total loss: what had been dense, fertile, and expected to yield has become bare and unusable. The owners intended to be the ones who stripped the trees for private gain; by morning, the garden itself has been stripped before they arrive.

The verse’s economy mirrors the speed of the event. There is no extended description of flames, weather, or mourning. The reader is taken directly from the secret plan to the emptied result. This sharpness makes the moral point difficult to evade: possession is contingent. A person can spend years building something and still discover that the conditions sustaining it were never fully in his hand.

> **Have you considered what you reap? Is it you who make it grow, or are We the Grower? If We willed, We could make it dry debris, and you would remain in wonder, saying: Surely we are burdened with loss; rather, we have been deprived.** (Qur'an 56:63–67)

This cross-reference echoes the owners’ later words almost exactly. It gives agricultural language a universal reach. The question applies not only to orchards but to every harvest of human labour.

**A loss that corrects the imagination**

The owners did not deny that they had worked the land. Their error was to confuse participation with authorship. Human beings cultivate, but they do not create the life within a seed; they arrange means, but they do not command every condition. The garden’s sudden barrenness corrects an imagination in which wealth is the permanent extension of the owner’s will.

This correction should not make people indifferent to conservation, farming skill, insurance, disaster preparation, or justice in economic systems. The Qur'an does not praise neglect. It teaches that even excellent preparation cannot make a human being sovereign. A farmer can use sound agricultural methods and still pray for rain; a surgeon can use every skill and still recognize the limits of medicine; a business owner can plan carefully and still know that profit is not a proof of moral independence.

**Barakah is more than numerical increase**

The story raises the idea of *barakah*, blessing. Blessing is not identical with having the largest visible quantity. A smaller income spent lawfully, shared fairly, and received with gratitude may sustain a household with peace and benefit. A larger income gained through exploitation may breed fear, conflict, legal trouble, spiritual numbness, and harm. This is not a formula by which observers can calculate whose life is blessed; only God fully knows. It is a moral orientation: the goodness of a provision includes how it is acquired, how it is used, whom it benefits, and what it does to the heart.

The garden becomes *ṣarīm* because it was treated as an object of total private control. Its emptiness dramatizes what greed does internally even before visible loss arrives. A miser may retain the fruit while losing gratitude, trust, tenderness, and the ability to enjoy what he has. In that sense, a garden can be stripped while still looking green.

**Do not romanticize loss**

There is a danger in reading this verse too quickly: one might romanticize deprivation or speak as though every loss automatically improves a person. Loss hurts. Crops feed families; jobs support children; a disaster can devastate people who did no wrong. The Qur'an does not celebrate pain as an aesthetic spectacle. In this particular revealed story, loss becomes a moral wake-up call because it confronts a deliberate plan to deny the poor.

A compassionate response to real loss is therefore not “You must have needed this lesson.” It is help, solidarity, fair policy, prayer, and practical restoration. The garden owners’ later repentance does not erase the severity of the loss, but it shows that hardship can become an opening when received with truthfulness.

**What can be stripped from a life**

Not every *ṣarīm* is material. A person may wake up to discover that a relationship has been emptied by years of gossip, that a reputation has been lost through one deception, that spiritual joy has dried up after persistent sin, or that an institution trusted by many has been hollowed out by leaders who refused accountability. The preceding verses have described those destructive habits. Verse 20 makes their end visible.

The remedy begins not with panic but with recognition. The owners do not yet recognize fully what happened; first they will think they have lost their way. That initial confusion is psychologically honest. When the expected world disappears, people often need time before they can name the deeper loss.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:21

> **Then they called out to one another at daybreak,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The same morning, a different reality**

The verse returns to the owners’ perspective: *fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīn*—they called to one another in the morning. The scene is deliberately ordinary. The men wake, coordinate, and prepare for work. They do not yet know that the object of their plan has been transformed. The contrast between their confidence and the reality already present in the garden gives the verse a quiet irony. Human beings often act from yesterday’s assumptions while conditions have already changed beyond their awareness.

Their calling to one another also shows that wrongdoing can be collaborative. No single person has to carry the whole plan for an unjust decision to proceed. One sibling proposes, another agrees, a third remains silent, and the group’s shared momentum makes the plan feel inevitable. The Qur'an will later identify one member as more moderate and morally awake, but at this moment the household moves as a coordinated unit toward exclusion.

**Early rising is morally neutral**

Dawn is usually associated in Islamic life with prayer, renewal, and productive effort. There is nothing wrong with rising early to harvest. The moral significance lies in what the early effort serves. The same discipline that can support a family, feed a community, or make room for worship can also be harnessed to greed. The Qur'an does not teach that a schedule is virtuous by itself; it asks what intention animates it.

> **By the dawn, and by ten nights.** (Qur'an 89:1–2)

The Qur'an elsewhere gives dawn sacred resonance, but that resonance makes this story sharper rather than softer. A time suited to remembrance is being used to conceal a plan from the poor. The readers are invited to ask whether their own “productive” habits are connected to gratitude and justice or merely to acquisition.

**Collective momentum can silence conscience**

A group decision often reduces personal resistance. Each participant can tell himself that the others must know best, that the plan is normal because everyone agreed, or that speaking up would make him disloyal. This is one reason unjust systems can persist even when no individual actor feels fully responsible. The garden story interrupts this diffusion of responsibility. Every owner participated in the intention, and each will later face its meaning.

The lesson applies to boardrooms, family inheritances, online groups, and friendships. When a group begins planning how to deny someone a right, conceal a problem, or benefit from another’s vulnerability, the first moral act may be to interrupt the mutual call: “We should not proceed this way.” Silence is not always neutrality; in a coordinated injustice, it can become a contribution.

**A call can gather people for good or evil**

The Qur'an often uses calling language for invitation—to faith, prayer, forgiveness, or accountability. This verse shows the darker possibility: people can call one another toward a shared wrong. The social power of invitation is morally flexible. A friend’s message can draw someone to prayer, study, service, and reconciliation; it can also draw someone into mockery, secrecy, and exploitation.

The believer should therefore examine the small calls he sends and answers. What kind of gathering does a message create? Does it make people more generous, truthful, and attentive to God? Or does it create a protected circle in which the absent poor, the silenced victim, or the inconvenient truth is excluded?

**God’s knowledge precedes human discovery**

The most sobering feature of the verse is that the garden’s destruction occurs before their morning call. The owners’ lack of knowledge does not change reality. This mirrors the broader sūrah: opponents may call the Prophet ﷺ mad while God has already declared his soundness; they may treat wealth as security while it is already a test; they may assume the Hereafter is distant while its judgment is certain. Human confidence cannot edit what God knows.

This should produce humility rather than paralysis. We do not know every hidden condition, so we plan responsibly, seek counsel, pray for guidance, and remain ready to revise when truth appears. Verse 22 gives the content of the owners’ early command, revealing how purpose turns ordinary work into an act of intended exclusion.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:22

> **Go early to your crop, if you are going to harvest.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Urgency in the service of a hidden intention**

The owners’ command is simple: *an ighdū ʿalā ḥarthikum in kuntum ṣārimīn*—go early to your tilth or crop if you intend to harvest. *Ḥarth* can refer to cultivated land, what is sown, or the resulting crop. The conditional phrase gives the instruction a tone of practical efficiency: if you really mean to harvest, act now. On its own, this is the language of responsible labour. Its moral darkness becomes clear only because the following verses uncover the intention governing it.

The Qur'an’s moral teaching is subtle here. It does not condemn organization, early work, agricultural skill, or the desire to gather a lawful harvest. It condemns an intention that turns those neutral capacities into tools for depriving others. This protects readers from a false spirituality that imagines good intentions make planning unnecessary, and from a false pragmatism that imagines efficiency excuses any end.

**Ends shape the meaning of means**

A knife can prepare food or injure a person; a private meeting can protect dignity or coordinate deception; an early start can serve a public good or evade those with a rightful claim. Means have a moral character partly because of the ends they serve and the constraints observed along the way. The garden owners’ dawn labour is not righteous simply because it is disciplined.

The Qur'an gives a broader standard:

> **Help one another in righteousness and reverence, and do not help one another in sin and transgression.** (Qur'an 5:2)

Their collective instruction is a small instance of cooperation in sin. Each participant makes the plan easier for the others. The verse asks every reader to consider not only what work he does, but what collective outcome his work is helping make possible.

**Work belongs inside gratitude**

Islam values labour. The Qur'an speaks of trade, cultivation, travel, craftsmanship, and provision without treating the material world as spiritually irrelevant. What it rejects is work detached from gratitude and moral limits. A person can work long hours for a family, build something useful, and seek a lawful livelihood as an act of worship. But when work becomes a reason to ignore prayer, break trusts, underpay labourers, pollute without concern, or shut out the needy, the activity has left its ethical frame.

The owners’ statement anticipates a common rationalization: “We worked for this.” Work matters, but it does not erase the roles of God’s provision, other people’s labour, public conditions, or the rights attached to wealth. The Qur'an’s view of property is neither contempt for effort nor worship of effort. It is stewardship.

**The harvest that includes the unseen neighbour**

An ethical harvest asks who has been made invisible. In a farm, it may be the poor who customarily glean. In a company, it may be the worker whose contribution is hidden behind a final product. In a household, it may be the caregiver whose labour is assumed. In a city, it may be the people displaced by a profitable development. The verse does not supply a one-size-fits-all policy, but it forces the moral question that efficient systems prefer not to ask.

This is why the Qur'an repeatedly ties provision to giving. A believer should not wait until a harvest is secure to remember the poor; the very anticipation of gain should awaken responsibility. Plans that include justice are not less intelligent than plans of exclusion. They are more realistic about the One who gives and the people whom God has placed within our moral field.

**A question behind every ambitious plan**

The verse is especially relevant when ambition is praised without qualification. “Go early” can be a wise discipline, but toward what? “Harvest” can be a lawful aim, but at whose expense? A person may set career goals, savings goals, and business goals while also setting moral commitments: no fraud, no exploitation, fair treatment, structured charity, time for worship, and a willingness to revise a profitable plan when it violates a right.

The owners will soon walk toward their garden convinced they have acted decisively. Their resolve will not save them because it was directed toward a corrupt end. The next verse lets the reader overhear the secrecy that they thought would keep that end hidden.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:23

> **So they set out, whispering to one another,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Secrecy as a sign of a troubled intention**

The verse says, *fa-nṭalaqū wa-hum yatakhāfatūn*: they set out while lowering their voices and whispering among themselves. The verb suggests reciprocal quiet speech. The brothers are not merely walking to work; they are coordinating a plan they do not want the poor to hear. Their whispers create a small closed world in which each reassures the other that exclusion is sensible, necessary, and possible.

The Qur'an does not declare all privacy sinful. Families need confidential conversations, professionals must protect entrusted information, and a person may give charity secretly to preserve another person’s dignity. The moral issue is the purpose of the concealment. Here the secrecy exists to prevent people from reaching a share that the owners know they would otherwise seek. The lowered voices are evidence that conscience already recognizes a problem before the loss occurs.

> **O you who believe, when you confer secretly, do not confer in sin, transgression, and disobedience to the Messenger; rather confer in righteousness and reverence.** (Qur'an 58:9)

This Qur'anic distinction is exact. Secret consultation can be good or evil depending on whether it protects a right or organizes harm.

**How groups make wrongdoing feel normal**

Whispering has a social effect. It turns a wrong idea into a shared project. Each participant can borrow courage from the others and tell himself that collective agreement makes the decision reasonable. The group may not need to state the ugly goal in full because everyone understands it. This is one reason moral failure often accelerates in private channels: the absence of the affected person removes a source of human reality, while mutual reassurance removes the discomfort that might have stopped the plan.

In modern life, the whisper may be an encrypted chat, a private meeting, a document not shared with those affected, or a series of comments made after someone leaves the room. Privacy itself is not the problem. The question is whether a person would be ashamed for the relevant stakeholders to know the plan. If a decision must be hidden from those whose rights it damages, secrecy is not neutral; it is part of the damage.

**The absent poor remain morally present**

The owners whisper because the poor are not physically present on the path with them. The Qur'an makes their absence morally important. The person in need need not stand before us for his right to exist. A person’s claim does not disappear because we have arranged our schedule to avoid seeing him.

This has broad relevance. Consumers can benefit from labour conditions they never see. Citizens can support policies whose burdens fall on people in distant neighbourhoods. A family can make an inheritance decision without inviting the person whose vulnerability should affect it. The Qur'an trains moral imagination: before making an advantageous decision, ask who will be absent from the room but still affected by it.

**The difference between discretion and concealment**

A helpful test is the fruit of silence. Discretion protects dignity, safety, and trust. Concealment protects wrongdoing from accountability. A doctor who keeps a patient’s information private acts ethically; an employer who hides safety violations acts corruptly. A person who conceals another’s forgiven mistake may be merciful; a person who hides evidence of abuse is not. The garden owners’ whispering falls clearly into the second category because its aim is to evade the poor.

The Prophet ﷺ praised concealment when it protects others from needless exposure, but his teaching never turns confidentiality into a shelter for injustice. Sound character requires enough wisdom to distinguish the two. That wisdom is especially needed in communities where loyalty language can make people confuse silence about harm with piety.

**A private conversation before God**

The verse also reminds believers that no conversation is actually hidden from God. The Qur'an says:

> **Do they think that We do not hear their secrets and their private counsels? Yes indeed; and Our messengers are with them writing.** (Qur'an 43:80)

This is not meant to produce paranoia. It is meant to produce integrity: the person should try to be the same moral self in the private room as in the public statement. If a plan cannot survive honest daylight, it needs examination before it becomes action.

The brothers’ whispers will become explicit in the next verse. What they say confirms that the poor were not forgotten by accident; they were deliberately treated as people to be kept outside the gate.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:24

> **Let no needy person enter it upon you today.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The poor are treated as an intrusion**

The whisper now becomes direct intention: *an lā yadkhulannahā al-yawma ʿalaykum miskīn*—let no needy person enter it upon you today. The phrase does not simply say that the brothers failed to find the poor. It depicts them actively organizing the harvest around their exclusion. The word *miskīn* refers to a person in need, often someone whose resources are insufficient and whose condition deserves active social concern. In the brothers’ private logic, that person becomes an obstacle to profit.

This is the moral heart of the story. The owners do not deny the garden’s fertility; they deny the relational duties that fertility creates. They see the poor as a threat to the quantity they can retain rather than as human beings whose need invokes compassion and a recognized right. The Qur'an exposes how easily possession can narrow vision: a person begins by saying “my harvest,” then arrives at “no needy person may come near.”

> **Have you seen the one who denies the Judgment? That is the one who repulses the orphan and does not urge the feeding of the needy.** (Qur'an 107:1–3)

The Qur'an links care for vulnerable people to belief in accountability. This is not because every poor person is morally superior and every wealthy person morally corrupt. It is because a person’s response to visible need reveals whether he really believes that provision, power, and life are answerable to God.

**From charity as favour to rights as trust**

The passage challenges an attitude that treats all help as a discretionary favour from the wealthy. Islamic teachings include voluntary charity, but they also speak of obligations: zakāh, debts, fair wages, inheritance shares, family support, and the due of those deprived. The earlier command concerning harvest gives the moral framework:

> **Eat of its fruit when it bears fruit, and give its due on the day of its harvest.** (Qur'an 6:141)

A due is not the same as a gift offered only when the giver feels generous. When a person recognizes another’s due, he gives without demanding gratitude as the price of dignity. This does not eliminate the beauty of voluntary kindness; it protects the poor from being made to depend entirely on the moods of the comfortable.

The Qur'an’s concern also reaches the person who does not ask. Some people are excluded from help because they are too proud, traumatized, isolated, or unaware of available support. A community that waits only for visible requests may congratulate itself on generosity while leaving many deprived. The brothers’ strategy depends precisely on invisibility: harvest before anyone can come.

**A moral failure can hide inside efficiency**

The brothers might have defended their plan as practical. They may have had expenses, dependants, fears about the future, or a desire to manage their land without interruption. Ethical failure often wears the language of efficiency because efficiency is easier to defend than cruelty. But an efficient process that systematically excludes vulnerable people is not neutral. It is a well-organized injustice.

This insight applies to more than almsgiving. A service designed so that only the well-connected can access it, a workplace schedule that makes worship or family care impossible for the least powerful, a legal process too costly for the poor, or a public space made hostile to those without money can all embody the attitude of this verse. The planner must ask who is being silently prevented from entering.

**No humiliation in giving**

The Qur'an does not merely command giving; it protects the recipient’s dignity. A person should not remind others of help, expose their need publicly, or make assistance a means of control. The giver is also a recipient—of God’s provision, health, opportunity, and time. This recognition dissolves the superiority that makes a needy person look like an invader.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **The upper hand is better than the lower hand, and begin with those for whose support you are responsible.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The report encourages lawful work, responsible support of dependants, and generosity. It does not demean the person receiving help. Need can come to anyone through illness, displacement, disability, unemployment, caregiving, or crisis. A community faithful to this verse makes asking safer and giving humbler.

**The gate we build around the heart**

The garden gate in the story is also an inner image. People can build gates around time, attention, sympathy, and resources. “Do not let anyone interrupt my comfort today” may be the private version of the brothers’ plan. Healthy boundaries are necessary; no person can meet every request alone. But boundaries become a moral wall when they are designed to keep compassion from ever making a claim.

A practical response is to decide in advance what portion of one’s income, time, skill, or influence will remain open to good. That is not naivety. It is a way of making sure fear does not secretly author the whole plan. Verse 25 shows the brothers moving forward with complete confidence in their ability to enforce the exclusion they have devised.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:25

> **And they went early, determined and fully able.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Confidence without moral sight**

The verse concludes the owners’ advance: *wa-ghadaw ʿalā ḥardin qādirīn*. The expression carries several shades recognized by commentators. *Ḥard* can convey firm determination, a settled purpose, or a restrictive intention; *qādirīn* means capable, powerful, or convinced of one’s ability. Some interpreters understand the phrase as “they went early, able to prevent” the poor from reaching the harvest. Others stress their confidence that they had power to carry out their design. Both readings fit the narrative. They are resolute, organized, and sure of themselves—and completely unaware that the garden has already been taken from their control.

The Qur'an does not mock competence itself. Determination, planning, and capacity are valuable qualities when directed toward good. The tragedy is that the brothers’ virtues of coordination and resolve are serving greed. Moral excellence cannot be assessed by energy alone. A person can be highly disciplined in a destructive project; efficiency has no conscience unless a conscience governs it.

**Power is real, but never absolute**

The word *qādirīn* lets the reader feel their confidence before it collapses. Human beings do possess limited power. They can wake early, make contracts, lock gates, plant crops, move resources, and influence other people. Denying this would be false humility. The Qur'an’s correction is that creaturely power remains contingent. It operates inside conditions God creates and can alter.

> **And you do not will unless God wills—the Lord of the worlds.** (Qur'an 81:29)

This verse does not erase human willing; it places it within God’s greater will. The owners genuinely intended their plan. Their ability was real but bounded. The following verse will show them discovering the boundary in the most concrete way possible.

**The danger of competence without compassion**

Modern institutions often reward the qualities represented by this verse: speed, strategic discipline, execution, control of access, and measurable outcomes. Those qualities can serve immense good when linked to justice. They can also produce sophisticated exclusion. An organization may become exceptionally capable at maximizing revenue while becoming incapable of seeing those harmed by its methods. A person may become excellent at protecting a personal schedule while losing the ability to respond to another’s need.

This is why ethical formation must keep pace with technical formation. Teaching someone how to build, manage, persuade, or optimize without teaching truthfulness, accountability, and mercy can magnify harm. The garden owners do not lack intelligence; they lack the moral sight that tells intelligence what it is for.

**Determination must answer to the destination**

There is a common motivational slogan that says determination is everything. The Qur'an asks a prior question: determined toward what? A ship can have a powerful engine and still be moving in the wrong direction. The speed then increases danger rather than success. Likewise, resolve becomes spiritually admirable only when its object is lawful and just.

The believer’s version of determination joins effort to *taqwā*. Taqwā is often translated as God-consciousness or reverent awareness. It is like moving carefully along a road where a hidden danger may lie ahead: attention does not stop the traveler from moving, but it keeps movement from becoming reckless. A person with taqwā plans, works, and uses skill; he also asks whether his plan violates a right, feeds vanity, or depends on an injustice he would not defend before God.

**What the brothers fail to ask**

They do not ask whether God wills the plan. They do not ask what is due to the poor. They do not ask whether their father’s inherited practice contained wisdom. They do not ask what loss of blessing might follow from withholding. They ask only whether they can get there early enough. Their questions are tactical, not moral.

This is a warning for every ambitious person. Before pressing ahead, one should ask: Is this lawful? Whom will it affect? Am I concealing the plan because it is unjust? Have I consulted people who are not invested in my desired answer? Have I left room for God to redirect me? These questions may slow a plan, but they protect it from becoming a well-executed disaster.

The next verse records the instant in which confidence meets reality. Their first reaction is telling: they do not immediately recognize moral deprivation; they think they must have taken the wrong path.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:26

> **But when they saw it, they said: Surely we have lost our way.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The mind’s first refuge: it cannot be our garden**

When the owners see the ruined garden, their first words are *innā la-ḍāllūn*—surely we are lost, astray, or on the wrong path. The immediate meaning is practical: this cannot be the orchard we know; perhaps we have taken the wrong road in the dark. The word *ḍāll* also carries the wider Qur'anic sense of being astray. That resonance allows the verse to work on two levels. They think they have lost geographical direction, while the story has exposed a deeper moral direction they had already lost.

This first reaction is psychologically credible. When reality contradicts a strongly expected outcome, the mind often reaches first for an external mistake. The address is wrong, the report is false, the market will recover tomorrow, someone else caused the problem. Sometimes such explanations are correct. The Qur'an does not teach people to blame themselves irrationally for every loss. But in this revealed story, the first explanation proves inadequate. The owners must move from confusion to recognition.

> **Have you considered what you reap? Is it you who make it grow, or are We the Grower? If We willed, We could make it dry debris, and you would remain in wonder, saying: Surely we are burdened with loss.** (Qur'an 56:63–66)

The nearly identical language confirms that stunned disorientation is a human response to the collapse of presumed security.

**Being lost can be a moment before return**

The word “lost” need not be the end of the story. It can be the first honest interruption of denial. A person cannot repent from a path he still calls perfect. The owners’ shock breaks the closed confidence of their secret plan. For the first time, they are forced to see that the future did not obey their intention.

In spiritual life, a similar moment may occur when a person recognizes that a habit has cost more than expected: a lie has broken trust, greed has hollowed out a family, anger has isolated him, or a career pursuit has left worship and conscience neglected. The initial reaction may be denial or bewilderment. The mercy lies in allowing the shock to become inquiry rather than immediately rebuilding the same false explanation.

**Do not turn every hardship into a private verdict**

The interpretation of the garden’s ruin comes from the Qur'anic narrative itself. Readers should not generalize by saying that anyone who loses money, health, or property has obviously “lost the way” morally. Job loss can afflict a righteous person; illness can come to a child; disasters can strike without allowing human observers to know their unseen meaning. The Prophet ﷺ endured severe hardship while being the most beloved servant of God.

The practical lesson is instead self-examination without cruelty. When a loss occurs, a believer can ask, “Is there an obligation I have neglected? Is there an injustice I need to repair? What can this teach me?” He should make those questions about himself before applying them to others, and he should not ignore practical causes, professional advice, or the duty to seek help.

**Wrong maps and moral maps**

The garden owners thought they had a map to the orchard. They did. What they lacked was a moral map of ownership. Their property was not a sealed private world; it was part of a relationship with God and neighbours. The sūrah uses their physical disorientation to reveal that moral reality. A person can be very accurate in technical navigation and still be lost in the purposes for which he is travelling.

This distinction matters in highly capable societies. We can map genomes, markets, roads, and planets while remaining uncertain about what human life is for, what we owe one another, and how power should be used. Technical knowledge is precious, but it does not replace revelation’s moral guidance. The pen in verse 1 dignified knowledge; this verse reminds us that knowledge must include knowledge of the path.

**The beginning of a truthful diagnosis**

Their first statement is incomplete, but it is better than pretending the garden is still intact. Honest recognition of rupture creates the possibility of a fuller diagnosis. In the next verse they revise their conclusion: this is not merely the wrong place; we have been deprived. That movement from confusion to acknowledgement prepares the way for the more complete repentance that follows.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:27

> **Rather, we have been deprived.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From mistaken direction to real deprivation**

The owners correct their first reaction with *bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn*—rather, we are deprived. *Bal* overturns the earlier explanation. They are not merely travellers who took a wrong turn; they have lost the harvest itself. *Maḥrūm* comes from a root associated with being prevented, withheld from, or deprived of something one hoped to receive. The word carries more weight than ordinary financial loss. It names the condition of discovering that access to a blessing has been blocked.

The irony is exact. The men had planned to deprive needy people of their share. Now they name themselves deprived. The Qur'an does not present this as a childish reversal for its own sake. It reveals a moral law: when people build security by refusing legitimate claims, they may lose the very blessing they attempted to monopolize. Their plan was designed to make the poor *maḥrūm*; their outcome teaches them the vulnerability of being *maḥrūm* themselves.

> **And in their wealth there was a known right for the one who asks and the one deprived.** (Qur'an 51:19)

The Qur'an uses the same family of meaning to make the invisible poor morally visible. The garden owners had treated deprivation as someone else’s problem. Their loss forces them to experience the word from the other side.

**Loss of fruit and loss of illusion**

The most immediate deprivation is material: the garden no longer yields the crop they expected. But the story also records the loss of an illusion. They believed that inheritance, effort, secrecy, and an early start gave them mastery over the result. The destroyed orchard removes that illusion in one night. This secondary loss is painful, yet it can become merciful if it opens the door to truth.

Not every form of deprivation is material. A person may have money but be deprived of contentment, trusted relationships, restful sleep, moral clarity, or the ability to enjoy what he owns without fear. A community may have resources but be deprived of justice because it hoards access among a few. The Qur'an asks readers to recognize these less visible losses before they become irreversible.

**The difference between entitlement and gratitude**

The word “deprived” can expose entitlement. If a person assumes that a crop, career, relationship, or health outcome is owed to him simply because he worked for it, loss feels like an incomprehensible theft. Islam teaches that effort has dignity and that people have real rights against injustice. It also teaches that no creature has an independent claim upon God that makes every desired result obligatory. Whatever reaches us is a gift and a trust; whatever leaves us remains within the wisdom of the One who owns all things.

This does not make grief improper. The owners’ shock is understandable. A believer may mourn a loss, seek restitution from those who wronged him, and work to rebuild. Gratitude is not emotional numbness. It is the refusal to turn pain into a claim that God has acted without wisdom or that other people’s needs are now irrelevant.

**Deprivation can reveal shared vulnerability**

The story’s ethical power lies in empathy produced through reversal. People who have never faced need can imagine that poverty reflects laziness, bad choices, or distance from their own world. A sudden loss can reveal how many supports were previously invisible. This realization should not produce shame toward those in need; it should produce solidarity. The owners’ suffering is meaningful only if it leads them to recognize the injustice of making others suffer intentionally.

In contemporary terms, a person who has experienced unemployment may become more attentive to fair hiring. Someone who has faced illness may become more compassionate toward patients. A family touched by debt may become more careful not to judge another household’s hardship. Hardship does not automatically generate compassion, but it can soften the false confidence that blocks it.

**Do not confuse lack with divine rejection**

The Qur'an’s use of deprivation never permits the conclusion that every poor person is rejected by God. The Messenger ﷺ and many of the most honoured believers lived with material scarcity. The criterion is not possession but faith, justice, patience, gratitude, and response to truth. The garden owners are deprived after a deliberate wrong because the narrative reveals that relation; the poor they hoped to exclude are not morally diminished by their need.

This distinction protects both humility and social justice. The wealthy person should not read abundance as a divine endorsement; the poor person should not read hardship as a verdict of worthlessness. Each person is tested differently, and each can turn toward God.

**Recognition before repentance**

Verse 27 is not yet the owners’ final repentance. They have named their loss but have not fully named their own wrongdoing. This sequence is realistic. People often see consequences before they see causes. The next verse introduces the most balanced among them, who guides the group from mere loss toward remembrance of God and moral understanding.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:28

> **The most moderate among them said: Did I not tell you, why do you not glorify God?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The voice of balance within a compromised group**

The verse calls one brother *awsaṭuhum*, often translated “the most moderate among them.” Al-Ṭabarī records early explanations that connect the word with the most just and soundest member of the group. From the root associated with the middle, *awsaṭ* can indicate balance, fairness, soundness, or the best and most just member of a group. It does not mean a person who splits every moral difference down the middle. In this story, he is moderate because he recognizes the excess of greed and calls his companions back to God.

His question—*a-lam aqul lakum lawlā tusabbiḥūn*—means, “Did I not tell you: why do you not glorify [God]?” The wording suggests he had previously warned them. He was not magically wiser only after the disaster; he had tried to interrupt the plan before it matured. His presence shows that collective wrongdoing is not always unanimous. Sometimes one person sees the moral danger but is ignored, overruled, or silenced by a group’s momentum.

**What glorification corrects**

To glorify God, *tasbīḥ*, is to declare Him far above defect, injustice, need, or limitation. In this context, commentators relate the call to gratitude, remembrance, saying “if God wills,” and acknowledging that the garden belongs ultimately to God. The exact practice need not be reduced to one formula. The core is that glorification corrects the owners’ false self-centering. They planned as though their will were decisive and the poor had no claim; *tasbīḥ* restores the Creator to the centre of the picture.

> **So glorify with the praise of your Lord and be among those who prostrate.** (Qur'an 15:98)

Glorification is not an escape from moral duty into private devotional sound. It is a discipline that makes moral duty intelligible. If God is the giver of provision and the judge of conduct, then wealth cannot be treated as a private proof of superiority and the needy cannot be treated as irrelevant.

**Moderation is not moral dilution**

It is important to connect this verse with verse 9. There, the sūrah rejected compromise that dilutes truth. Here, it praises the “moderate” brother. The two ideas are not in tension. Qur'anic moderation is not the habit of making every truth less demanding so that everyone feels comfortable. It is balanced justice: neither greed nor waste, neither harshness nor surrender to falsehood, neither despair nor presumption.

A bridge has a middle not because it is indecisive about its destination, but because balance lets it carry weight without collapsing. Similarly, the *awsaṭ* brother is morally centred precisely because he has a clear direction. He calls the group to remember God at the moment when they are being pulled toward possessiveness.

**The responsibility of the one who sees first**

This brother’s example speaks to people who recognize a wrong plan while others appear united around it. One should not assume that speaking up will always change the group. The Qur'an does not say that his warning prevented the loss. It honours the warning itself and allows it to become a source of later moral clarity. Silence may preserve temporary comfort, but it leaves the group without a witness to another way.

How one speaks matters. The brother asks a question rather than beginning with humiliation. His words point the others toward God rather than merely displaying his own superiority. This is a model for correction: name the moral issue, recall the higher principle, give people room to recognize it, and avoid making their repentance harder through contempt.

**A conscience needs cultivation before crisis**

The brother could speak at the moment of loss because he had apparently cultivated a different moral instinct before the loss. This is why daily remembrance, charity, honest companionship, and regular self-examination matter. In a crisis, people rarely invent a conscience from nothing. They draw upon habits already formed.

For families and organizations, the verse recommends giving moral dissent a protected place. If every difficult question is treated as disloyalty, the group loses its *awsaṭ* voice and may discover truth only after damage is done. Leaders should want someone who can say, “Did we remember God? Did we consider who is harmed? Is this decision just?”

**The question that opens repentance**

The brother’s question does not accuse God of taking the garden; it directs the group inward. This is the turning point. Their next response begins with glorification and an admission of wrongdoing. The verse shows that moral correction can be both firm and hopeful: it tells the truth about a failure while still inviting a way back.

---

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:29

> **They said: Glory be to our Lord! Truly, we were wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Repentance begins by clearing God of blame**

The owners answer with *subḥāna rabbinā innā kunnā ẓālimīn*—glory be to our Lord; indeed, we were wrongdoers. Their first word is not a complaint against fate or an accusation that the punishment was excessive. *Subḥān* declares God far above injustice and defect. By beginning this way, they correct the moral inversion at the heart of their earlier plan. They no longer act as though they are independent owners whose loss needs no explanation; they recognize the Lord’s perfection and their own wrongdoing.

The second part is equally essential: *innā kunnā ẓālimīn*, “we were wrongdoers.” *Ẓulm* is placing something where it does not belong, violating a right, or acting unjustly. Their wrong included intending to withhold the poor’s share and behaving as though provision could be detached from accountability. The plural confession matters. Each participant speaks inside a shared “we,” not as a spectator blaming the others.

> **Whatever good reaches you is from God, and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself.** (Qur'an 4:79)

This verse does not mean that every pain can be traced by human beings to a particular personal sin. In the garden narrative, however, the owners are given knowledge of their wrong by revelation, and they respond without blaming their Lord.

**Confession is not self-destruction**

To admit wrongdoing is not to say that a person has no dignity or no future. It is to stop defending what has damaged the self and others. Many people avoid repentance because they fear that acknowledging wrong will annihilate their identity. The Qur'an presents the opposite possibility: truthful confession is the beginning of moral restoration. As long as a person says, “I did nothing,” he remains chained to the action. When he says, “I was wrong,” he can ask forgiveness, repair harm, and learn a new way to live.

The owners’ language contains neither theatrical despair nor self-congratulation. They do not praise themselves for recognizing the error; they do not minimize it by calling it a misunderstanding. Their sentence joins reverence and responsibility. That pairing is the grammar of genuine repentance.

**Tawḥīd reshapes accountability**

Their glorification also reveals the connection between belief in God’s oneness and ethics. If God is the true Lord and giver, then a human being cannot make his own desire the final authority over wealth. If God is perfectly just, then the believer cannot call injustice “success” merely because it produces a private benefit. *Tawḥīd* is not only a proposition about metaphysics. It reorganizes how a person sees ownership, power, other people, and the self.

This is why the Qur'an often joins remembrance to justice. A heart that forgets God easily makes itself the measure of all things. A heart that glorifies God remembers that there are limits it did not invent and rights it may not erase.

**The elements of a living repentance**

Islamic teachers commonly describe repentance through elements visible in this verse and its setting: recognizing the wrong, feeling remorse, ceasing the wrongful course, resolving not to return, and restoring another person’s right where possible. The brothers cannot undo the destroyed garden, but their language shows recognition and regret. Had the crop still stood, repentance would require opening it to the needy rather than merely uttering pious words.

This protects repentance from becoming verbal performance. If someone has lied, he should correct the lie when possible. If he has taken money, he should return it. If he has spread gossip, he should halt its spread and repair what he can. Circumstances differ, and repairing harm should not create a greater danger, but a sincere heart asks what responsibility follows its confession.

**No delayed repentance should be despised**

The brothers did not listen before their loss. Their repentance comes after an event that forced them to see. This should make readers grateful for any awakening rather than cynical about its timing. Human beings often understand only after consequences arrive. The door of mercy remains precious because God receives those who return, even after they have wasted opportunities.

At the same time, the story warns against postponement. It is better to recognize the right of the poor before a garden is destroyed than to confess after it. The wisest reader is not the one who can describe repentance beautifully after failure, but the one who lets this verse interrupt greed before it becomes action.

**From confession to the social wound**

The next verse records the group turning toward one another in reproach. Their confession before God does not immediately erase the interpersonal consequences of their plan. True repentance must also reckon with the ways wrongdoing has been shared, encouraged, and excused among people.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:30

> **Then they turned to one another, blaming one another.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The human reflex to distribute blame**

After their confession, the owners *aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatalāwamūn*—turn toward one another, reproaching each other. The verb evokes reciprocal blame. A shared plan has collapsed, and the people who once reinforced one another’s confidence now revisit who proposed it, who pushed hardest, who stayed silent, and who ignored the warning. The verse is psychologically exact: wrongdoing often feels communal while it is profitable, then becomes individualized when consequences arrive.

Their blame is not entirely false. People do influence one another. Someone may have urged greed, mocked the moderate brother, or supplied the rationalization that made exclusion feel necessary. Communities should not erase such influence. But the Qur'an will not allow the brothers to use one another’s participation as a total escape from personal responsibility. They had just said, “We were wrongdoers.” Each person must retain that first-person moral truth even while recognizing shared causes.

**Accountability is different from scapegoating**

There is a constructive form of examining responsibility. A family, organization, or society after a failure needs to know what decisions caused harm, what warnings were ignored, and what structures must change. That is not forbidden blame; it is accountability. The destructive form is scapegoating: locating all fault in a convenient person so that everyone else can preserve a flattering self-image.

The distinction can be tested by the goal. Does the conversation aim to repair, tell the truth, protect those harmed, and change future conduct? Or does it aim to humiliate someone else while preserving one’s own innocence? The owners’ later collective confession suggests that their reproach begins moving toward shared recognition rather than ending in permanent evasion.

> **They will all come forth before God, and the weak will say to those who were arrogant: We were your followers; can you then avail us anything against God’s punishment? They will say: Had God guided us, we would have guided you.** (Qur'an 14:21)

This scene of the Hereafter shows that following influential people does not erase responsibility. Influence is real, but each person still answers for the choice to follow.

**The social life of a bad decision**

The garden story teaches that many moral failures are not solitary. A selfish family rule, a fraudulent corporate practice, a culture of gossip, or a harmful institutional policy can become normal because many people benefit in different ways. One person writes the policy, another enforces it, another stays silent, another enjoys the gain, and another says the consequences were unforeseeable. When harm emerges, each may point to another link in the chain.

The Qur'an neither accepts the fiction that one person always bears every burden nor the fiction that shared participation means no one bears any. It asks for a mature moral account: name leaders’ greater influence, name individual choices, hear those harmed, and create repair proportionate to each person’s role.

**How repentance changes a blame conversation**

The brothers’ prior glorification matters. Without it, their mutual reproach might simply be an angry scramble for moral superiority. With it, there is at least the possibility that blame becomes a step toward sober accountability. Repentance changes the question from “How can I escape the consequences?” to “What did I do, what did we do, and how can we return rightly to God?”

In personal life, this can transform a household conflict. Instead of listing the other person’s faults first, each person begins with his own contribution. That does not require a victim of abuse to accept blame for abuse, nor does it ask people to ignore power differences. It means that those who have participated in a shared wrong should not use another participant’s faults as a curtain over their own.

**The warning for bystanders**

One of the hidden lessons of the verse is for the person who did not originate a wrong but went along because resistance seemed inconvenient. The garden owners show that passive cooperation has consequences. If a group is moving toward injustice, “I did not make the plan” may be true but insufficient. The moderate brother had warned them; the others apparently chose not to heed him.

This does not mean that every person always has equal ability to resist. Power, safety, dependency, and knowledge vary. Islamic justice considers those realities. Still, where a person can interrupt harm, ask a question, refuse participation, or support a better path, moral courage matters.

The next verse gives the owners a more comprehensive lament. Their mutual blame does not remain a tool of denial; it gives way to an admission that their conduct was rebellion against the limits God had set.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:31

> **They said: Woe to us! Truly, we were rebellious transgressors.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A deeper name for the wrong**

The owners’ lament, *yā waylanā innā kunnā ṭāghīn*, intensifies their confession. *Waylanā* is an exclamation of anguish, regret, and recognition of ruin: “Woe to us.” *Ṭāghīn* comes from *ṭughyān*, crossing or overflowing a limit. It describes more than a simple mistake in judgment. The brothers now see that they had exceeded the moral bounds set by their Lord. They were not merely unfortunate managers of a crop; they had become rebels against the obligations attached to blessing.

This language develops their earlier admission that they were *ẓālimīn*, wrongdoers. Wrongdoing names the violation of a right; transgression names the crossing of a boundary. Both belong to their act. They intended to deny vulnerable people their due and behaved as if their own need, fear, and property claims were the final limit of moral concern. The ruined garden has made the hidden rebellion visible.

> **And whoever transgresses the limits of God has certainly wronged himself.** (Qur'an 65:1)

The Qur'an’s language is precise: the transgressor may harm many people, but he also wrongs himself. He damages the very moral and spiritual life he imagines he is protecting through selfishness.

**Regret becomes meaningful when it tells the truth**

Not every expression of sorrow is repentance. People can regret being caught while still defending the wrong that brought consequences. The owners’ words point beyond that shallow regret because they name themselves as transgressors. They do not say only, “What bad luck,” or, “Why did this happen to us?” They connect their pain to a moral failure.

This does not mean a person must remain trapped in self-condemnation. The word *wayl* is severe, but the Qur'an narrates it on a path that continues toward hope in the next verse. True remorse has two sides: it refuses to beautify sin, and it refuses to deny God’s mercy. Despair can become another form of self-centering if it says that one’s failure is greater than the Creator’s ability to forgive and reform.

The Prophet ﷺ summarized the inner core of repentance in a concise report:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: Remorse is repentance.** (Sunan Ibn Mājah; graded sound by a number of hadith scholars)

Remorse here is not mere sadness. It is a moral grief that turns the will away from the wrong and toward repair.

**Crossing limits often feels like normal life**

*Ṭughyān* rarely announces itself with a dramatic declaration. It may appear in a small decision to keep what should be shared, a habit of silencing criticism, a refusal to pay someone fairly, or a repeated insistence that personal advantage outranks every claim of conscience. Because each step is explainable, people may not notice that they have crossed a line until consequences expose it.

This is why religious limits can be merciful. They are not arbitrary fences designed to frustrate human life. They work like guardrails near a dangerous road. A driver may feel constrained by the barrier, but its purpose is not to diminish travel; it is to prevent a moment of speed or distraction from becoming catastrophe. The owners’ inherited practice of leaving something for the poor was such a guardrail. By removing it, they mistook liberation from obligation for freedom.

**Lament should lead to repair**

The brothers cannot recover the lost harvest by crying out. Their next moral task is not to perform grief but to change direction. If they receive another garden, it must be held differently. If they have harmed relationships, they must rebuild trust. If the poor were excluded, they must become people who include them. The Qur'an’s account is useful only if it turns regret into action.

A believer facing a smaller version of this moment can ask: What limit did I cross? Whose right did I ignore? What must I stop now? What restitution is possible? Who can help me remain accountable? These questions make *waylanā* a beginning rather than an endpoint.

**The communal form of confession**

Again the verse says “we.” The brothers do not hide behind the group, but neither does one person claim all the guilt while the rest silently preserve themselves. Shared confession can be morally powerful when it is sincere. A family, institution, or community that has harmed others may need to say, “We were wrong,” rather than issue vague language about unfortunate events. Such speech must be followed by concrete change, but without truthful speech, change often remains impossible.

The next verse shows that their regret is not despair. They turn their attention from the destroyed garden to the Lord whose provision is not exhausted by one loss.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:32

> **Perhaps our Lord will give us something better in its place. Truly, we turn our longing to our Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hope after the truth has been faced**

The owners now say, *ʿasā rabbunā an yubdilanā khayran minhā innā ilā rabbinā rāghibūn*: perhaps our Lord will replace it for us with something better; truly, to our Lord we turn in longing. The movement is spiritually important. They do not demand compensation as if God owes them a new garden. They say “perhaps,” a word of hope joined to humility. They do not set their longing on the crop alone; they turn it toward their Lord.

*Yubdilanā* means to substitute or replace. What they ask for may be a better orchard in this life, but the phrase is open enough to include a better outcome in the Hereafter and, more deeply, a better relation to wealth itself. A garden regained with the same greedy heart would not be an unqualified good. The true replacement includes gratitude, justice, and the ability to use provision without being owned by it.

> **Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Surely God forgives all sins. Indeed, He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.** (Qur'an 39:53)

Hope is not denial of sin. In the Qur'an, the servants addressed in this verse are precisely those who have transgressed. The door of mercy opens after truth is admitted, not after wrongdoing is renamed as harmless.

**Longing directed to the right object**

The word *rāghibūn* expresses earnest desire, inclination, and hope. Every human being is a seeker; the question is where desire finally points. The brothers had previously directed desire toward a private harvest, imagining that its full possession would secure them. Now they direct desire toward the Lord who gives and takes. This is a reordering of the heart, not a simple change of economic strategy.

A believer may ask God for material restoration. There is no virtue in pretending that food, housing, health, or work do not matter. But the believer asks in a way that recognizes God’s wisdom and the moral purpose of provision. “Give me something better” can mean: grant what is beneficial, protect me from returning to the vice that harmed me, and make whatever You give a means of nearness rather than arrogance.

**Mercy does not make rights optional**

The hope in this verse cannot be used to bypass the poor whom the owners intended to exclude. If God grants a new opportunity, that opportunity will carry renewed obligations. Repentance that asks for a better future while refusing to repair the social wrong of the past is incomplete. The Qur'an’s mercy is not a private emotional comfort detached from justice; it is a force that enables a person to become more just.

This is especially relevant when someone has harmed another person financially. Asking God for forgiveness is necessary, but where a debt, stolen right, unpaid wage, or withheld support can be restored, restoration is part of returning to God. The details vary and sometimes direct contact could cause further harm, so wise counsel may be needed. The principle remains: God’s mercy should make the repentant person more attentive to human rights, not less.

**The reports of a better garden**

Some classical accounts report that the brothers were later given a better garden. The Qur'an does not state this outcome explicitly, so it should not be treated as the central certainty of the passage. The certain lesson is in their changed orientation: they recognize their Lord, name their transgression, and hope in His replacement. Whether restoration comes immediately, gradually, differently than expected, or only in the Hereafter, the Lord’s generosity is not limited by the loss that has occurred.

This restraint matters for readers who repent but do not receive a visible worldly replacement. A person may leave an unlawful income and face a difficult transition. Someone may repair a relationship that is not restored. A family may learn generosity after loss but never regain the same material ease. Their repentance is not invalid because the worldly ending differs from the garden story’s reported details. God’s “better” is known by His wisdom, not by our narrow timetable.

**Hope guards against two opposite errors**

One error after failure is despair: “I have ruined everything, so change is pointless.” The other is presumption: “God is merciful, so there is no urgency to change.” The brothers’ sentence avoids both. “Perhaps” keeps them humble; “we turn our longing to our Lord” keeps them active in hope. Healthy hope moves the hands as well as the heart. It leads to prayer, restitution, altered habits, and patient effort.

This is how a person can respond when an old “garden” is lost—whether a job, an opportunity, a reputation, or a plan. Grieve honestly. Examine the self without cruelty. Repair what is possible. Ask God for what is better. Then make sure that the next blessing is not organized around the same injustice that ruined the first.

Verse 33 widens the story from a single household to a general warning. This worldly loss is real, but it is not the greatest consequence people should fear.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:33

> **Such is the punishment; and surely the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if only they knew.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A worldly warning before the final account**

The verse generalizes the garden story: *kadhālika al-ʿadhābu wa-la-ʿadhābu al-ākhirati akbaru law kānū yaʿlamūn*—such is the punishment, and the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if only they knew. The demonstrative “such” points back to the destroyed garden as an example of a worldly consequence. It then directs attention forward. However severe the loss of a crop may be, it is not comparable to the final consequence of persistent injustice and denial before God.

This is not a threat designed to make people morbid. It restores proportion. Human beings often organize life around avoiding immediate losses: money, reputation, comfort, access, and status. They can become willing to commit grave wrongs in order to protect those things. The verse reverses the calculation. A temporary gain purchased through rebellion may conceal a far greater loss; a temporary warning can be mercy if it awakens a person before the final account.

> **And the punishment of the Hereafter is more severe and more lasting.** (Qur'an 20:127)

The Hereafter matters not because this world is worthless, but because this world is incomplete. Many injustices are never fully repaired in human courts; many hidden sacrifices are never publicly honoured. Final judgment ensures that moral reality is not exhausted by visible outcomes.

**Knowledge that changes what is feared**

The closing phrase, “if only they knew,” does not imply lack of raw information alone. The garden owners had knowledge of farming, property, and the poor who came at harvest. The deeper missing knowledge was lived recognition—knowledge that reshapes priorities. A person may know intellectually that death and accountability are certain while living as if no action will ever be weighed.

Qur'anic knowledge therefore includes moral awakening. It is like knowing a road contains a dangerous curve: the knowledge has not truly reached its purpose until the driver slows down. The greater punishment is not mentioned so that readers can feel superior to others; it is mentioned so that they take seriously the danger of allowing sin to become a settled path.

**Worldly hardship is not a universal sentence**

This verse must be read with the caution already taught by the story. The Qur'an identifies this particular loss as punishment. It does not teach believers to interpret every hardship in every life that way. Prophets were tested intensely, children suffer without personal guilt, and righteous people may lose wealth while the unjust prosper for a time. To point at an afflicted person and declare, “This is God’s punishment,” is often ignorance joined to cruelty.

The safe application is inward: when warned by revelation or faced with the consequences of one’s own choices, take the opportunity for repentance seriously. Outwardly, offer mercy, assistance, and justice to those who suffer. God alone knows the hidden interpretation of another person’s trial.

**Justice, mercy, and the possibility of return**

The verse’s warning belongs within a sūrah that has just narrated the owners’ movement toward repentance. The Hereafter’s punishment is greater for those who persist in denial and rebellion; the door of return is still open for the one who turns to God. Divine justice does not trivialize evil, and divine mercy does not trivialize repentance. Both truths protect human dignity. Victims are not told that their injury is inconsequential, and wrongdoers are not told that they are beyond reform.

This balance is vital in social ethics. Communities should not use mercy language to cover abuse or evade restitution. Nor should they speak as though one grave failure makes transformation impossible. Accountability, protection of the harmed, repentance, and meaningful repair are not rivals.

**A present life lived under a future horizon**

Belief in the Hereafter alters practical decisions. A person who expects only immediate consequences may ask, “Can I get away with this?” A person who expects to stand before God asks, “Can I carry this into the record that will be opened?” That second question affects contracts, private messages, treatment of employees, family conflict, charity, and secret intentions. It does not make life joyless. It makes joy safer because it disconnects happiness from wrongdoing.

The garden owners’ loss is a miniature judgment: a private scheme is exposed, the expected reward disappears, and the participants must name what they did. The final judgment will be more complete, more just, and more enduring. Wisdom is to learn from the miniature before one faces the whole.

**The turn from warning to promise**

Verse 33 could leave the reader only with fear, but the next verse immediately names the contrasting destination of the reverent. The Qur'an’s method is not to imprison the heart in terror. It places warning beside hope so that people can choose a path of vigilance, trust, and righteous action.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:34

> **Surely, for the reverent there are Gardens of bliss with their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A garden unlike the garden that vanished**

After the garden of this world is stripped bare, the sūrah speaks of *jannāti al-naʿīm*—Gardens of bliss—for the *muttaqīn*, the reverent or God-conscious. The contrast is deliberate. The owners’ orchard was vulnerable to loss, mixed with anxiety, and morally endangered by greed. The Gardens promised here are not insecure possessions that invite hoarding. They are the pure gift of God, given to those who live with *taqwā*.

*Naʿīm* means ease, delight, and unspoiled blessing. Worldly enjoyment is real, but it is mixed: every meal can be followed by hunger, every possession by fear of loss, every physical strength by decline, and every pleasure by questions of conscience. The bliss of the Hereafter is not mixed in that way. The Qur'an describes its security:

> **They will have whatever they wish with their Lord. That is the reward of those who do good.** (Qur'an 39:34)

The promise corrects the garden owners’ fearful logic. One does not secure eternal happiness by clinging unjustly to temporary fruit. Eternal happiness is received from the Lord through faith, reverence, and the mercy He grants.

**Taqwā: alert care on a moral road**

The *muttaqīn* are those who possess *taqwā*, a word rooted in protection or guarding. It is often rendered fear of God, but “reverent awareness” better captures its active quality. Taqwā is like walking or driving attentively on a road where real dangers exist: awareness makes the traveler protect himself from avoidable harm. It includes fear of wrongdoing’s consequences, but also love of God, hope in His mercy, gratitude for His gifts, and a desire to remain within the path He has shown.

The garden story gives taqwā a practical shape. It is not a vague inward feeling. It appears when a person remembers God while planning, says *in shāʾ Allāh* with consciousness, protects the poor’s due, resists group greed, confesses wrong without evasion, and hopes in God after loss. Taqwā is not flawless success; it is a life that returns to God rather than defending transgression.

> **And whoever is reverent toward God—He will make for him a way out and provide for him from where he does not expect.** (Qur'an 65:2–3)

This promise should not be reduced to a guarantee of immediate wealth. Its point is that obedience is never a foolish bargain, even when its worldly outcome is not visible on our preferred schedule.

**With their Lord: the highest security**

The phrase *ʿinda rabbihim*—with their Lord—gives the promise its deepest meaning. Paradise is not merely a place of beautiful things. Its honour lies in its nearness to the Lord who created, guided, forgave, and rewarded. The pronoun “their” is intimate: the Lord who knew their hidden restraint and unseen sacrifices has not forgotten them.

This does not suggest physical proximity in a creaturely sense. It expresses divine honour, care, and a station of acceptance beyond worldly measure. People may be ignored, poor, misunderstood, or deprived of recognition in this life. “With their Lord” assures them that no faithful act has slipped outside the true court of value.

**Hope that strengthens justice now**

Promise of the Hereafter is sometimes misunderstood as a reason to neglect justice in the present. The sūrah teaches the opposite. The promise comes after a narrative about the poor’s rights. Belief in Gardens of bliss should make a person less greedy, more honest, and more willing to share, because he no longer treats temporary possession as his only security. A heart convinced that God can give without limit has less reason to defend every coin through injustice.

This does not produce irresponsible spending. The reverent plan, work, save lawfully, and support dependants. They simply do not make material preservation their god. Their hope frees them to give, forgive, and resist corrupt influence.

**The transition to a demand for moral coherence**

Verse 34 prepares the sūrah’s next series of questions. If God has promised the reverent a distinct destination, how could the submitters and the guilty be treated as morally identical? The questions that follow are not arbitrary assertions of privilege. They appeal to the basic coherence of divine justice: actions, faith, intentions, and responses to truth matter.

For the reader, the verse keeps fear from becoming despair. The garden story warns that blessing can be lost through greed. The promise assures that no act of taqwā—however unnoticed or costly—is wasted with the Lord.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:35

> **Should We then treat those who submit as We treat the guilty?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Moral distinction is part of justice**

The verse asks a rhetorical question: *a-fa-najʿalu al-muslimīna ka-l-mujrimīn*—should We make the submitters like the guilty? *Muslimīn* are those who submit themselves to God; *mujrimīn* are those committed to crime, sin, or moral violation. The question does not make a claim of tribal privilege. It challenges the idea that faithfulness and rebellion, justice and exploitation, repentance and arrogant persistence will finally be treated as if they carried exactly the same moral meaning.

This follows naturally from the garden story. The owners wanted the world arranged so that their greed had no consequence and the poor had no claim. Verse 34 promised the reverent Gardens with their Lord. Verse 35 now asks whether divine justice could simply flatten all these differences. If the one who guards rights, gives, repents, and submits to truth received the same final outcome as the one who mocks revelation, withholds rights, and persists in harm, moral language itself would lose its seriousness.

> **Or shall We make those who believe and do righteous deeds like those who spread corruption in the earth? Or shall We make the reverent like the wicked?** (Qur'an 38:28)

The Qur'an’s answer is not that human beings become entitled to demand Paradise by their own achievement. It is that God is just and does not treat devotion and corruption as identical in the final reckoning.

**Submission is an orientation, not a label**

The word *muslim* in this verse should be heard in its basic Qur'anic sense: one who submits to God. It is connected to *islām*, surrendering oneself in peace and obedience to the Creator. A name, ancestry, or community affiliation by itself cannot guarantee the reality of this submission. The sūrah has just shown how easily wealth and social identity can become a substitute for moral substance.

A submitter is someone who, when God’s truth becomes clear, seeks to align desire and action with it. That alignment may involve struggle, failure, and repentance. It is not a claim of personal flawlessness. By contrast, *ijrām* is not simply making a mistake. It is a pattern of treating God’s limits and other people’s rights as expendable. The contrast is ethical and spiritual, not ethnic or economic.

**Equality of human dignity and difference of moral outcome**

The verse requires a careful distinction. All people possess human dignity as creatures of God, and believers are commanded to act justly even toward those with whom they differ. This verse does not authorize contempt, collective punishment, or discrimination. It speaks about the final moral significance of how people respond to truth and conduct themselves—not about whether one may deny another person food, safety, legal fairness, or basic respect.

At the same time, equal human dignity does not mean every action is equally good or every final outcome is morally interchangeable. A court that treated theft and honest stewardship identically would not be just. A teacher who marked every answer correct regardless of its content would not be merciful to learners. Divine justice is more perfect than human analogy: it knows intentions, opportunities, pressures, repentance, hidden good, and hidden harm. That perfect knowledge makes real distinction possible without human cruelty.

**Mercy remains at the centre**

Some might hear the verse as though believers deserve salvation purely because they bear a label. The Prophet ﷺ corrected this assumption:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: None of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone. They said: Not even you, Messenger of God? He said: Not even me, unless God covers me with His mercy.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Deeds matter; the Qur'an is full of commands, warnings, and promises. But a servant enters Paradise through God’s mercy, not through a claim that he has placed God in his debt. This makes righteous action an expression of grateful submission rather than a currency used to boast over others.

**A corrective to moral relativism and moral arrogance**

Verse 35 challenges two opposite errors. Moral relativism says that there is no meaningful difference between truthfulness and exploitation, worship and arrogance, mercy and cruelty, except individual preference. Moral arrogance says that I can identify myself as righteous, condemn everyone else, and stop examining my own conduct. The Qur'an rejects both. It insists that moral difference is real, while also locating final judgment with God.

The practical result is humility with principle. A believer can say that oppression is wrong and honesty is right without pretending to know every soul’s final destination. He can pursue justice without self-worship. He can hope for God’s mercy while fearing to become like the garden owners who assumed that a privileged position made them safe.

**The question asks for an answer in conduct**

The rhetorical question demands more than agreement. If a person believes God distinguishes the submitter from the guilty, he must let that belief shape daily choices. A private lie matters. A withheld wage matters. A patient act of worship matters. A hidden charity matters. A sincere repentance matters. Nothing is insignificant simply because human observers do not notice it.

The next verse presses the opponents’ logic further: what is wrong with their judgment that they expect no moral distinction at all?

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:36

> **What is the matter with you? How do you judge?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A challenge to a broken moral calculation**

The two questions are terse and urgent: *mā lakum kayfa taḥkumūn*. “What is the matter with you?” is not an invitation to insult the audience; it is an exposure of an irrational moral conclusion. “How do you judge?” asks them to account for the method by which they reached it. They have implied that worldly privilege will continue into the Hereafter and that the faithful can be treated like the guilty. The Qur'an asks them to show the reasoning behind that claim.

This is a notable feature of Qur'anic discourse. It does not ask people to abandon judgment; it asks them to judge responsibly. It questions the source, standards, and hidden interests behind a conclusion. The opponents have evidence of the Messenger’s character, the recited signs, the moral incoherence of their idolatry, and the warning of history. Yet they want their desire to function as a verdict. Verse 36 interrupts that move.

> **Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god? Will you then be a guardian over him?** (Qur'an 25:43)

When desire becomes the judge, a person may still use intellectual language, but the verdict was chosen before the evidence was considered.

**Judgment needs a standard outside appetite**

Every person makes judgments: what is fair, who should be trusted, how money should be used, whether an action is harmful, what a family owes its members, how a society should treat the vulnerable. The question is not whether to judge, but by what standard. If the standard is merely power, then the powerful will always define justice in their favour. If it is merely personal pleasure, then other people’s rights become negotiable whenever they are inconvenient.

Revelation gives a standard that confronts both private appetite and group custom. It tells the rich that wealth is a trust, the strong that power has limits, the weak that their dignity is real, and every person that he will return to God. This is why revelation can feel demanding: it does not simply bless what a society already prefers.

**Self-critique is part of sound reasoning**

“How do you judge?” is also a question believers should direct toward themselves. It is easy to identify bias in a rival and ignore it in one’s own family, school, political group, or religious circle. A sound moral judgment requires asking: What evidence would make me revise this view? Whose experience is missing from the decision? Do I benefit from the conclusion? Have I treated a respected person’s claim as proof simply because it came from him? Have I allowed anger to choose the answer?

This is not scepticism about all truth. It is disciplined humility. The Qur'an gives clear moral teachings, but applying them to complex situations requires verification, knowledge, consultation, and justice. Certainty about a principle should not become carelessness about facts.

> **O you who believe, be persistently standing firm for God, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people cause you to be unjust. Be just; that is nearer to reverence.** (Qur'an 5:8)

The command to be just applies even when emotion strongly favours a different conclusion.

**A question that unmasks entitlement**

The opponents’ error is not simply an intellectual mistake. They want a universe in which their position is secure regardless of their response to God. They judge as though status gives them a permanent exemption. Verse 36 punctures that entitlement. A person cannot manufacture a final privilege by asserting it loudly, and a group cannot turn inherited prestige into a binding promise from God.

This matters in religious communities too. Someone may assume that birth into a Muslim family, public religious speech, a scholarly title, charitable visibility, or political influence places him beyond accountability. The Qur'an asks: how do you judge? What revelation says that your conduct no longer matters? The garden story has already answered through experience: wealth does not make a person immune to moral consequence.

**Constructive disagreement and intellectual humility**

The verse models a form of disagreement that is more serious than insult. It does not respond to false claims merely by calling opponents foolish. It asks them to expose the warrant for their belief. This is useful in modern conversation. Rather than only saying, “You are wrong,” one can ask, “What principle leads you to that conclusion? Would you accept it if the roles were reversed? What evidence supports it? What do you make of the harm it causes?”

Such questions may not always persuade, but they clarify whether a dispute is about evidence, values, self-interest, or an unwillingness to be accountable. They also discipline the questioner, who must be ready to answer the same questions honestly.

Verses 37 and 38 now make the demand more concrete. If the opponents’ hoped-for outcome is not grounded in sound judgment, do they possess a revealed book that actually promises it?

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:37

> **Or do you have a book in which you study,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A claim to ultimate privilege requires evidence**

The sūrah asks: *am lakum kitābun fīhi tadrusūn*—or do you have a book in which you study? The rhetorical question moves from moral intuition to evidence. The opponents expect that they will be rewarded as they please despite their denial of revelation. If that expectation rests on more than desire, where is the revealed warrant? Do they possess a book from God that they can actually study and that supports their claim?

*Kitāb* can mean a written book, scripture, decree, or authoritative record. *Tadrusūn* comes from the root of study and repeated reading. The Qur'an does not reject learning; it demands serious learning rather than inherited assertion. A claim about God, the Hereafter, salvation, or moral law is not made true by confidence, ancestry, or repetition. It needs a reliable source and responsible interpretation.

> **Or have We sent down to them an authority, and it speaks of what they associate with Him?** (Qur'an 30:35)

The question exposes a central issue in idolatry: the claim of divine authorization had no divine evidence behind it.

**Study can seek truth or seek permission**

The verse’s use of “study” is especially searching. It is possible to have a book, quote a book, and even spend years studying a book while treating it as a mirror for prior desire. The reader comes not to learn what God says but to find a phrase that can justify what he already wants. The next verse will expose this temptation as the expectation of finding “whatever you choose.”

True study requires humility. It asks what the text means in its language and context, how it relates to the wider revelation, what reliable scholarship has transmitted, and how it corrects the reader. This can be demanding because a real book sometimes says what we did not wish to hear. But that difficulty is evidence that the reader is encountering an authority beyond the self rather than merely using words to decorate preference.

**The Qur'an invites evidence, not blind imitation**

The Qur'an frequently challenges people who follow inherited belief without proof:

> **And when it is said to them, Follow what God has sent down, they say: Rather, we follow what we found our fathers upon. Even though their fathers understood nothing and were not guided?** (Qur'an 2:170)

This is not contempt for parents or tradition as such. Human beings learn through teachers, families, and inherited communities. The problem is making inheritance immune to examination when revelation and reason call it into question. A tradition can carry wisdom; it can also carry an unexamined error. The believer honours predecessors by seeking truth carefully, not by repeating them blindly.

**Authority in an age of fragments**

The verse has special relevance in an age of searchable fragments. A person can find a sentence from scripture, law, a scholar, or a news source that appears to support nearly any conclusion. The ability to retrieve words quickly is not the same as study. Study requires context, language, reliability, competing evidence, and moral humility. It also requires admitting when one does not know.

For Muslims, this means neither dismissing scholarship nor outsourcing conscience to an isolated quotation. A responsible reader consults qualified learning, reads the Qur'an as a coherent whole, distinguishes definitive texts from disputed interpretations, and refuses to use God’s words to win a personal argument. This is slower than slogan-making, but it is closer to *tadrusūn* in its serious sense.

**The burden of proof follows the claim**

The opponents have claimed an outcome in the Hereafter that contradicts the moral logic of the sūrah. Verse 37 asks them to produce their source. This is a principle of intellectual justice: the stronger the claim, the stronger the evidence required. To say that God will grant salvation, privilege, or exemption is not a minor personal opinion; it attributes something to the Lord of the worlds.

A believer should apply the same caution to himself. Before claiming that God approves a particular ambition, condemns a specific person, or guarantees a result, ask: What is the textual basis? Is it clear? Am I confusing a general principle with a certain prediction about an individual? Humility before the unseen protects religion from becoming a vehicle for personal certainty.

The next verse names the claim that no genuine revelation could authorize: a book in which every outcome is simply whatever its readers happen to choose.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:38

> **that in it you shall surely have whatever you choose?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The fantasy of a scripture that obeys the reader**

Verse 38 completes the question: *inna lakum fīhi lamā takhayyarūn*—that in it you will surely have whatever you choose or select. The wording exposes the opponents’ wish for a book whose contents are not an authority over them but a guarantee of their preferences. They want revelation to promise them exactly the outcome they have already decided they deserve.

The Qur'an’s criticism is not aimed at choosing among lawful options. Human beings legitimately choose work, food, clothing, residence, study, and many other matters. The problem is making personal choice the source of ultimate truth and assuming that divine judgment must ratify it. A text that never corrects its reader is not functioning as revelation; it is functioning as a projection screen.

> **Have you seen the one who takes his desire as his god, and God leaves him astray upon knowledge, seals his hearing and heart, and places a covering over his sight?** (Qur'an 45:23)

The verse describes a grave condition: knowledge may be present, yet desire is allowed to govern what knowledge is permitted to mean.

**Choice is real; sovereignty is not ours**

Islam recognizes human choice and responsibility. People make decisions and are addressed as agents. But human choice is not sovereignty over the moral order. A person may choose an action, but cannot choose to make injustice just, falsehood true, or a rejected obligation optional. The same person who insists on unlimited autonomy in one area usually expects moral limits from others when their choices cause him harm. This inconsistency reveals why a standard beyond private preference is necessary.

The Qur'an’s vision is not that every desire is evil. Desire for food, companionship, beauty, safety, knowledge, and meaningful work is part of human creation. The question is whether desire is educated by revelation and justice or allowed to rule them. The garden owners desired security for their families; that desire became corrupt when it justified excluding the poor.

**Mercy is promised, but not on our terms**

The opponents’ error should not lead readers to despair of divine mercy. God promises forgiveness, accepts repentance, and rewards faithfulness. But His promises are revealed by Him, not written by our wishes. A servant may hope intensely for Paradise and ask God for it, while also recognizing that entrance is through God’s mercy and not an entitlement imposed upon Him.

> **And race toward forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the reverent.** (Qur'an 3:133)

This is a real promise that gives hope and direction. It differs completely from a claim that a person will receive “whatever he chooses” regardless of what he believes, does, or repents from.

**The ethics of interpretation**

The verse warns readers of sacred texts against selection without submission. It is tempting to gather only passages that comfort us, ignore passages that challenge our habits, or quote one line without the qualifications that surround it. It is equally tempting to select only warnings for people we dislike and promises for ourselves. Both are forms of making the book serve the reader.

A more faithful approach asks: What does the Qur'an say in full? Where does it comfort, where does it warn, where does it leave room for scholarly difference, and where does it demand a change in me? The reader should feel addressed, not merely affirmed. This is not a burden imposed by an arbitrary text; it is the condition of learning from any real teacher.

**Consumer culture and spiritual entitlement**

Modern consumer culture trains people to expect customizable outcomes. A product can be tailored, a feed can be filtered, and a service can be designed around preference. These conveniences can quietly shape spiritual expectations: perhaps faith too should deliver only what I want, validate every identity I choose, and never call for restraint. Verse 38 exposes the danger. God is not a product and revelation is not a customer-service agreement.

Freedom becomes deeper, not smaller, when a person is freed from enslavement to every passing preference. The Qur'an offers a path toward a good that is greater than what the self happens to select in a moment of fear or appetite. Verse 39 sharpens the challenge: perhaps, the opponents imagine, they have secured a binding promise from God Himself. The sūrah asks them to produce it.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:39

> **Or do you have binding oaths upon Us until the Day of Resurrection that you will surely have whatever you decide?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Can a creature bind the Creator without His word?**

The question becomes still more pointed: *am lakum aymānun ʿalaynā bālighatun ilā yawmi al-qiyāmah inna lakum lamā taḥkumūn*—do you possess binding oaths from Us, reaching to the Day of Resurrection, that whatever you decide will be yours? *Aymān* can mean solemn oaths or covenants. *Bālighah* conveys an oath that is complete, effective, and reaching its stated end. The opponents behave as though they hold a signed guarantee from God that their preferred judgment about themselves will prevail forever. The Qur'an asks where such a guarantee could have come from.

The language does not deny that God makes promises. The Qur'an is full of His truthful promises to the faithful, the repentant, the patient, and the just. What it denies is the human fantasy of making God answerable to an agreement He never made. No wealth, lineage, reputation, ritual display, or group identity can compel the Lord to approve what He has forbidden.

> **Our Lord, and give us what You promised us through Your messengers, and do not disgrace us on the Day of Resurrection. Surely, You do not fail in the promise.** (Qur'an 3:194)

The believers’ prayer is humble because it rests on a promise God has actually revealed. It does not invent terms and then present them to God as a claim.

**The difference between hope and presumption**

Hope in God is a virtue. A believer asks for forgiveness, Paradise, protection, provision, and guidance because God has made His mercy known. Presumption is different. It says, “God must give me what I want because I have decided I deserve it,” even while refusing the path God has shown. The garden owners displayed a worldly version of presumption; the opponents in this passage display an eschatological one.

This distinction is spiritually liberating. If a person’s relationship with God were a negotiation in which God owed results according to human demands, every unanswered prayer would destroy trust. But a servant asks a wise and merciful Lord, takes the means, accepts that outcomes may differ from immediate desire, and remains certain that no sincere prayer or righteous effort is wasted. Humility is not uncertainty about God’s goodness; it is certainty that His wisdom exceeds ours.

**Promises in revelation are morally shaped**

Qur'anic promises are not arbitrary favours to a privileged class. They are tied to faith, repentance, justice, patience, charity, and God’s mercy. This does not turn salvation into a mechanical wage. It shows that the moral order has coherence. God does not promise the same final response to arrogance and repentance, exploitation and repair, or denial and submission.

The verse therefore corrects religious entitlement in every era. A person may imagine that being born into a believing community secures him regardless of conduct. Another may use a religious title to insulate himself from correction. Another may confuse a strong emotional experience with a permanent guarantee. The Qur'an asks each: what binding promise do you possess from God for the exemption you claim?

**The Day that exposes empty contracts**

“Until the Day of Resurrection” gives the claim its full scale. An unauthorized promise may sound convincing in a short life because wealth and influence can protect a person from immediate consequence. The Day of Resurrection is the point at which every false contract is exposed. No lawyer, patron, algorithm, or family name can produce a hidden covenant that God did not grant.

This is not a call to anxious scrupulosity. The Qur'an repeatedly names God as Forgiving and Merciful. It is a call to base hope on what is true: His revealed mercy, sincere turning, and humble obedience, rather than invented exemptions.

**When spirituality becomes a demand for control**

People sometimes turn religious language into a way of demanding certainty about personal outcomes: “If I do this act, God must give me that result by this date.” Worship can then become a transaction, and disappointment can turn into resentment. Verse 39 restores the servant’s posture. We ask, strive, and hope; we do not dictate to the Lord of the worlds.

This posture actually strengthens action. A person who knows he cannot control every outcome is freed to act sincerely rather than manipulate every result. He can give without demanding immediate return, apologize without demanding instant forgiveness, and pray without treating God as an instrument for private plans.

**The claim must identify its guarantor**

The next verse moves from the alleged covenant to the person willing to stand behind it. If anyone claims such a guarantee, who among them will take responsibility for the assertion? The Qur'an’s demand for accountability turns vague confidence into a question that cannot be evaded.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:40

> **Ask them which of them will guarantee that.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Put a name beside the claim**

The verse commands the Prophet ﷺ: *salhum ayyuhum bi-dhālika zaʿīm*—ask them which of them is a guarantor for that claim. A *zaʿīm* is one who undertakes responsibility, stands surety, or vouches for something. The command is concise, but its logic is powerful. The opponents have made an immense assertion about God’s final judgment. Rather than allowing it to float as a collective confidence, the Qur'an asks: who will personally stand behind it? Who will attest that God has made this promise, and on what evidence?

False certainty often survives by remaining anonymous. “Everyone knows,” “our people have always believed,” “they say,” or “it will work out” can create an atmosphere of assurance without anyone taking responsibility for facts. Verse 40 breaks that atmosphere. A claim about the unseen must have a warrant; if it does not, rhetorical confidence is not a substitute.

> **Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.** (Qur'an 2:111)

This recurring Qur'anic demand is not hostile to faith. It distinguishes revealed faith, which rests on divine signs and trustworthy transmission, from assertions made to protect desire or status.

**Authority should bear the weight of its influence**

The verse also speaks to leadership. People who influence others—parents, scholars, teachers, executives, public officials, and content creators—should be especially careful with claims that shape moral decisions. It is easy to make a bold statement and let ordinary followers bear the consequences if it proves false. A *zaʿīm* is someone whose name is attached to the guarantee.

This does not mean every person must have expertise in every field before acting. It means that people should distinguish what they know, what they infer, what they have heard, and what they do not know. Intellectual humility is not weakness. It is a form of trustworthiness. In religious matters particularly, a person should not promise divine approval, declare a specific unseen outcome, or pronounce on another person’s final fate without clear evidence.

**Evidence is more than loud confidence**

The sūrah’s opponents likely possessed social rank and rhetorical power. Those things can make a claim feel credible, but feeling credible is not being true. The demand for a guarantor shifts attention from charisma to responsibility. Who can show the book? Who can show the covenant? Who can account for the conclusion?

A similar discipline helps in ordinary life. Before forwarding a claim, ask who sourced it. Before accepting financial advice, ask what evidence supports it and who benefits. Before joining an accusation, ask who has verified the facts. Before following a religious ruling online, ask whether the speaker has accurately represented the sources and the limits of disagreement. Verification protects people from both deception and the humiliation of later regret.

**Guaranteeing what no human can control**

There is a further irony: the opponents want someone to guarantee an outcome that only God can know and decree. Human beings can guarantee limited things in lawful contracts—payment, delivery, testimony within their knowledge—but no one can guarantee divine favour independently of revelation. The most sincere believer hopes and fears; he does not write his own certificate of salvation.

The Prophet ﷺ himself, despite his unique rank, taught his community not to rely on lineage or claims. This humility is part of why his message is trustworthy. A false spiritual leader often promises followers special immunity; a true messenger directs people toward God’s mercy, obedience, and accountability.

**Public responsibility and private integrity**

Verse 40 is also a personal question. What claims do we make that we would not be willing to explain carefully? Do we say “God wants this” when we mean “I want this”? Do we use certainty to end a difficult discussion? Do we let a group’s confidence replace our own obligation to think and verify?

A mature believer can say, “I believe this based on these texts,” “I am not certain,” or “I need to learn more.” Such speech does not weaken faith; it protects it from becoming a mask for ego. It also makes room for correction, which is indispensable to moral growth.

The next verse presents another possible source of supposed support: perhaps the opponents have partners—deities, patrons, or allies—who can substantiate their claim. The Qur'an asks them to bring those partners forward.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:41

> **Or do they have partners? Then let them bring their partners, if they are truthful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No partner can share God’s final authority**

The third challenge asks: *am lahum shurakāʾu fa-lyaʾtū bi-shurakāʾihim in kānū ṣādiqīn*—do they have partners? Then let them bring their partners if they are truthful. *Shurakāʾ* means partners. In the immediate Makkan setting, it includes the idols and claimed intercessors to whom the opponents attributed a share in divine authority. It can also carry the broader sense of human allies, authorities, or supposed guarantors invoked to support an indefensible claim.

The challenge is not merely rhetorical theatre. If those beings truly share in God’s judgment, they should be able to appear with evidence, answer for the claim, and exercise the authority attributed to them. They cannot. The Qur'an repeatedly exposes the contradiction of assigning divine powers to beings that cannot create, know the unseen, respond independently, or protect even themselves.

> **Say: Have you considered your partners whom you call upon besides God? Show me what they created of the earth, or do they have a share in the heavens? Or have We given them a Book so that they stand upon a clear proof from it?** (Qur'an 35:40)

The questions are logical. Creation, ownership, knowledge, and final judgment belong to the true Lord. A claimed partner must show some real basis for the authority assigned to it.

**Intercession is not an independent power**

The Qur'an’s critique of partners does not deny that God may honour servants by permitting intercession on the Day of Judgment. It denies the idea that anyone can compel God, act independently of Him, or become a private route around moral accountability. Every honoured servant remains a servant; every intercession is by God’s permission.

> **Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?** (Qur'an 2:255)

This principle protects pure monotheism and ethical seriousness. A person cannot exploit attachment to a revered figure, family, institution, or spiritual leader as a license to persist in injustice. Hope in God’s mercy and love for His righteous servants should make one more obedient, not more presumptuous.

**The modern partners of moral judgment**

Idolatry is not only a matter of statues. A person can give practical ultimacy to money, reputation, ideology, nation, tribe, professional status, or a charismatic leader. These things become “partners” in a functional sense when their approval is allowed to override what one knows God requires. The person may never speak a formal theological statement, yet his decisions reveal whom he fears, obeys, and trusts most.

For example, someone may know that a business practice is unjust but say, “The market demands it.” Another may know that a group narrative is false but say, “My side needs it.” Another may excuse abuse because a respected leader is involved. Verse 41 asks whether any of these forces can stand before God and guarantee the exemption being claimed. None can.

**Truth must be able to bear scrutiny**

The demand that purported partners be produced is a demand for visibility. Claims that depend on vague spiritual power or anonymous social backing fear scrutiny. The Qur'an does not fear scrutiny because it calls people to signs, reflection, and evidence. The believer need not be threatened by sincere questions. Truth can be examined; falsehood depends on keeping its supports indistinct.

This does not mean that every questioner has good intentions or that every complex matter can be resolved in a single discussion. It means that faith should not require the suppression of responsible inquiry. The Qur'an’s own questions model a reasoned challenge to claims that shape how people worship and live.

**Companionship that leads toward God**

The verse also suggests a positive question: whom do we bring as companions in our moral lives? Friends, teachers, and communities influence what we normalize. Good companionship does not become a partner with God; it helps a person remember God and correct himself. Bad companionship encourages the illusion that group loyalty can shield one from truth.

The Qur'an depicts the collapse of false alliances on the Last Day:

> **It will be said: Call your partners. Then they will call them, but they will not answer them, and they will see the punishment—if only they had been guided.** (Qur'an 28:64)

The warning is not meant to isolate the believer. It is meant to make him choose relationships and influences that can survive the test of accountability.

Verses 35–41 have dismantled the opponents’ claim through moral coherence, revealed evidence, alleged covenants, human guarantors, and supposed partners. The next scene moves to the Day on which all invented supports disappear and the truth of worship becomes impossible to evade.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:42

> **On the Day when the shank is laid bare and they are called to prostrate, but are unable,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Day when evasions can no longer stand**

The verse moves from rhetorical challenge to the scene of final disclosure: *yawma yukshafu ʿan sāqin wa-yudʿawna ilā al-sujūdi fa-lā yastaṭīʿūn*—the Day when the shank is laid bare and they are called to prostrate but cannot. In classical Arabic, uncovering the shank is an idiom for an overwhelming matter becoming severe and fully exposed, like a person gathering his garment to meet a crisis. On this reading, the verse announces the gravity of the Day of Judgment: every concealed reality will be uncovered and every imagined source of safety will fail.

The image is deliberately more intense than a generic statement that judgment will be difficult. The people addressed have treated worship as beneath them and assumed that their status will secure them. On that Day, they are summoned to the act that embodied creaturely humility in this world—prostration—and discover that the time for voluntary response has passed.

> **The Day when secrets will be put to the test, and he will have neither strength nor helper.** (Qur'an 86:9–10)

The verse’s severity is moral rather than arbitrary. A life spent refusing truth does not simply disappear when the consequences become visible. The Hereafter reveals what the choices of this life have formed.

**How scholars have approached the wording**

This passage has been discussed carefully in Islamic theology because a rigorously authenticated prophetic report uses related wording. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī preserve more than one line of explanation, including the established Arabic idiom of overwhelming severity. Some scholars emphasize that idiomatic sense; others, particularly scholars in the early traditionalist approach and later writers such as Ibn Taymiyyah, affirm the wording found in the Qur'an and authentic hadith concerning God in a manner befitting His majesty, without asking how, imagining a bodily likeness, or comparing the Creator to creation. Both approaches begin from the Qur'an’s foundational protection of divine transcendence:

> **There is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.** (Qur'an 42:11)

A responsible reader should not turn a theological discussion into mockery or a test of tribal identity. The shared certainty is the reality of the Day of Judgment, God’s absolute uniqueness, and the warning against hypocritical or arrogant worship. Where scholars explain the precise wording differently, believers preserve reverence, avoid speculative imagery, and do not claim knowledge God has not given.

A report in the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections describes the consequence of this call:

> **The Prophet ﷺ said: Then every believing man and believing woman will prostrate, while there will remain one who used to prostrate in the world for show and reputation. He will try to prostrate, but his back will become one rigid piece.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The report must be heard within the rule of divine incomparability just cited. Its ethical thrust is unmistakable: an outward act emptied of sincere worship cannot be converted into truth at the moment when truth becomes unavoidable.

**Prostration as the body’s truthful theology**

*Sujūd* is more than a physical posture. In prayer, the forehead—the part of the body associated with human dignity and self-presentation—is placed upon the earth before the Creator. The body enacts the truth that the mind and heart are meant to know: I am not self-made, self-sustaining, or the final judge of reality. This is why prostration can be a source of profound spiritual freedom. It liberates a person from prostrating inwardly to status, fear, wealth, audience approval, or desire.

For the arrogant, however, prostration appears humiliating because they have made self-exaltation their refuge. The verse shows the tragic reversal: what they refused as a voluntary act of dignity before God becomes an impossible act when the final court appears. The inability is not portrayed as a random physical punishment detached from the person’s history. It is the outward manifestation of an inward stiffness cultivated through refusal.

**The difference between inability and incapacity**

This verse must never be directed against people whose bodies prevent them from making ordinary prostration. Islamic law is merciful toward illness, disability, injury, age, and other real limitations. A person prays standing if able, sitting if unable, lying down if necessary, and according to the capacity God has given. The inability here is an eschatological consequence of insincere or rejected worship, not a condemnation of bodily limitation.

The distinction matters deeply. Physical capacity is not a measure of a person’s worth before God. A person who cannot bow may possess more humility than someone who performs every movement for public display. The Qur'an judges hearts and deeds with complete knowledge of each person’s circumstances.

**The habits that make response possible**

The verse asks readers to see worldly worship as preparation, not ritual performance to be postponed until fear makes it convenient. A person who returns to prostration daily is training the self in a reality that no one can escape: God is Lord, and I am His servant. This training does not make someone superior; it makes repentance more accessible because the heart is accustomed to lowering itself before truth.

In a culture that often equates freedom with refusing every claim upon the self, *sujūd* presents another vision. Freedom from God’s worship can become slavery to many smaller masters. Prostration to God alone is a refusal to let any created thing possess ultimate authority. Verse 43 now describes the visible state of those called to this act after they refused it while strength and opportunity were available.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:43

> **their eyes humbled, abasement covering them; for they had been called to prostrate while they were sound.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Humiliation after refused humility**

The verse completes the scene: *khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillah*—their eyes are lowered in humiliation and abasement covers them. Their inward pride has become outwardly visible. The Qur'an often speaks of eyes because the eye expresses the direction of the heart: confidence, shame, fear, arrogance, attentiveness, or submission. Here the eyes are not peacefully lowered in reverence; they are lowered under an abasement the person can no longer evade.

The final clause supplies the moral history behind the scene: they were called to prostration *wa-hum sālimūn*, while they were sound, safe, or physically capable. They had opportunity. The issue is not that they lacked knowledge, bodily ability, or time; it is that they rejected the call while the door of voluntary response remained open. The verse therefore joins God’s justice to human responsibility. A consequence becomes meaningful because it relates to a real history of choices.

> **And on the Day of Resurrection you will see those who lied about God, their faces darkened. Is there not in Hell a dwelling for the arrogant?** (Qur'an 39:60)

The Qur'an’s imagery of faces and eyes is not a fascination with humiliation. It teaches that inner moral realities, often hidden by worldly status, will become manifest in the final court.

**What the call to prostrate includes**

Many commentators connect the worldly call in this verse to the daily prayer and its prostration. That connection is natural: prayer is the regular embodied summons to acknowledge God’s lordship. The broader meaning includes responding to revelation with humility rather than treating God’s command as an insult to self-rule. A person may perform the motions of prayer while refusing its moral call in business, family, speech, and private conduct. Conversely, sincere prayer should gradually soften the heart toward justice and repentance.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the special nearness of prostration:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: The nearest a servant is to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so increase supplication.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

Nearness here should be understood in a manner befitting God, not as physical distance. Its practical meaning is that the posture of deepest humility is a privileged place for prayer, dependence, and honest need. The person who bows willingly in this world is not diminished; he is given an opening for intimate appeal to the One who can answer.

**Riyāʾ: when worship faces the audience**

The authentic report connected to verse 42 warns specifically about someone who prostrated “for show and reputation.” This is *riyāʾ*, performing an act of worship so that people will see and praise it. It is dangerous because it can make an outwardly religious act serve the very ego it is supposed to discipline. The person appears to bow before God while inwardly seeking an audience.

The Qur'an warns:

> **So woe to those who pray—those who are heedless of their prayer, those who make a show, and withhold small kindnesses.** (Qur'an 107:4–7)

This does not require people to abandon public good for fear of being seen. Congregational worship, charitable leadership, teaching, and social service may necessarily be visible. The question is whom the heart is seeking. A believer can ask God for sincerity, conceal voluntary acts where appropriate, accept that others may see necessary acts, and refuse to make praise the fuel of worship.

**Healthy bodies and entrusted opportunities**

The clause describing their sound condition is a call to gratitude. Health, mobility, time, mental clarity, and access to a place of worship are all conditions that can be taken for granted until they change. The verse does not threaten the ill; it asks the well to recognize the opportunities they possess. A healthy person may postpone prayer, repair, charity, or reconciliation with the vague promise of “later.” The Qur'an interrupts that illusion. Later is not owned.

At the same time, a person who becomes ill should not read the loss of capacity as proof of divine rejection. Islamic law accommodates incapacity, and God judges each servant according to what was genuinely available to him. The verse is addressed to a chosen refusal, not to involuntary limitation.

**Humility protects rather than degrades**

Modern pride sometimes treats humility as low self-esteem or weakness. Qur'anic humility is neither. It is truthful self-placement: the servant recognizes his dependence on God and, therefore, becomes less enslaved to other people’s approval. A humble person can apologize, learn, resist injustice, and acknowledge limits because ego does not have to be defended at every moment.

The final abasement of the verse is the consequence of refusing that truthful humility until reality makes it inescapable. The Qur'an’s warning is merciful precisely because it is given now, while prostration, repentance, and return are still possible.

**Eyes trained to see before the veil is removed**

The contrast between verses 42 and 43 suggests that worship trains perception. A person who bows before God learns to see worldly power as temporary and human need as morally significant. A person who repeatedly refuses may become unable to see beyond self-interest. The eyes of the Hereafter then reveal the blindness that had been cultivated in this life.

The sūrah moves next to those who deny the Qur'an itself and appear to flourish. The warning is that delayed consequence must not be mistaken for approval.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:44

> **So leave Me with those who deny this discourse. We shall draw them on gradually from where they do not know.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A divine threat that needs no human enforcement**

The command *fa-dharnī wa-man yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā al-ḥadīth* directs the Messenger ﷺ to leave the deniers of the revealed discourse to God. The phrase identifying the discourse refers to the Qur'an, the message that opponents had called tales of the former peoples. God does not ask the Prophet ﷺ to defeat the deniers through rage, coercion, or private retaliation. He tells him to leave their final matter to the One whose knowledge and power are complete.

This is both warning and consolation. The Prophet ﷺ has a duty to convey, clarify, remain patient, and act justly. He does not have the burden of executing final judgment against everyone who refuses. The phrase gives moral space between being opposed and becoming consumed by vengeance. God sees the lie, the mockery, the harm, and the hidden motive; His judgment does not require the servant’s desperation.

**The frightening ease of *istidrāj***

The following phrase is *sanastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn*—We shall draw them on step by step from where they do not know. *Istidrāj* evokes being led gradually, degree by degree. The danger is not necessarily immediate disaster. A person may be given time, wealth, influence, applause, and opportunities while becoming more deeply settled in sin. He can misread the continued ease as proof that God approves of him, when it is instead part of a delayed reckoning.

> **Do they think that, because We provide them with wealth and children, We hasten for them good things? Rather, they do not perceive.** (Qur'an 23:55–56)

The wording deliberately echoes the earlier discussion of wealth and children in this sūrah. Worldly increase is not a reliable certificate of divine favour. It can be a gift received with gratitude, a test requiring responsibility, or, for the persistently unjust, a form of gradual exposure.

**Respite is not neglect**

The Prophet ﷺ explained this principle with a grave image:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: Indeed, God gives respite to the wrongdoer, but when He takes him, He does not let him escape. Then he recited: Such is the seizure of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong. Indeed, His seizure is painful and severe.** (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim; Qur'an 11:102)

Respite can be mercy because it gives time to repent. It becomes terrifying only when a person spends the additional time multiplying wrong, interpreting every delayed consequence as permission. The difference is in the response. A believer who recognizes a blessing thanks God, asks whether rights are being fulfilled, and fears becoming careless. A wrongdoer who treats blessing as immunity may be walking deeper into *istidrāj* without realizing it.

**Why observers must be cautious**

The concept of gradual leading-on is easy to weaponize. Someone may look at a wealthy rival, a public sinner, or a successful institution and declare, “Their success is surely *istidrāj*.” Such claims concern the unseen and should be avoided. The Qur'an tells us the principle so that we fear for ourselves, not so that we gain a satisfying explanation for other people’s lives.

Likewise, a believer facing hardship should not assume that the prosperous are necessarily condemned or that he himself is necessarily rejected. The prophets endured hardship; righteous people can have abundance; unjust people may repent; and God alone knows the inward state. The practical use is self-scrutiny: does ease make me more grateful and just, or more heedless and entitled?

**The moral danger of uninterrupted success**

Failure often forces reflection. Success can remove the very signals that would prompt it. An unethical business may grow; a dishonest speaker may gain followers; a selfish person may receive admiration; an oppressive system may function efficiently for years. The absence of visible collapse can numb conscience. This is why Sūrah al-Qalam repeatedly uncouples outcome from moral approval. The garden owners lose quickly; others may be led on gradually. Both patterns leave God’s justice intact.

A wise person therefore builds voluntary interruptions into success: prayer, charity, trusted criticism, regular audits, accountability, remembrance of death, and contact with people who cannot advance his interests. These practices keep prosperity from becoming a sealed echo chamber.

**Leave the final account to God, pursue present justice**

This instruction to leave the deniers’ final matter to God does not mean believers ignore present harm. They must protect victims, pursue lawful justice, correct falsehood, and establish fair boundaries. It means they do so without pretending to possess the final divine verdict or needing to avenge every insult personally. The Prophet ﷺ’s patience was active moral leadership, not surrender to abuse.

Verse 45 adds that the Divine plan has a firmness human schemes cannot outmanoeuvre. The delay that confuses the denier is not weakness, indecision, or forgetfulness.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:45

> **And I will grant them respite; surely My plan is firm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Delay belongs to a firm wisdom**

The verse continues the warning: *wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn*—I will grant them respite; surely My plan is firm. *Umlī* means to extend time or allow a period to continue. *Matīn* means firm, strong, or unbreakable. The deniers may interpret delay as weakness in the warning, but God declares that delay itself is contained within a plan they cannot weaken or escape.

The term *kayd* can be rendered plan, arrangement, or measured counter-plan. When human beings plot, their plotting often involves ignorance, injustice, manipulation, or harm. God is not described by those creaturely defects. His “plan” is perfect knowledge and just governance: He gives opportunity, exposes intentions, allows freedom to show its chosen direction, and brings consequences at the time He knows is wise. It is not a trick born of need; the Creator needs no deception to overcome a creature.

> **They planned, and God planned; and God is the best of planners.** (Qur'an 3:54)

The verse does not praise human treachery. It announces that no unjust scheme can place God in a position of helplessness. The moral universe is not finally controlled by the cleverness of those who exploit it.

**Why immediate consequence is not always justice**

A reader may ask why God grants respite at all. If oppression is wrong, why not stop it instantly? The Qur'an does not give a single simplistic answer to every case, but it repeatedly shows that delay can serve several wise purposes: it leaves room for repentance, allows evidence and consequences to become clear, distinguishes sincere return from coerced compliance, and unfolds a history in which moral choices are truly chosen.

Immediate punishment for every wrong would also erase the ordinary human world in which people learn, struggle, forgive, reform, and exercise responsibility. This does not make suffering caused by injustice acceptable; believers are commanded to resist harm through lawful and ethical means. It means that the final completion of justice belongs to a horizon beyond the immediate moment.

> **And if God were to take people to task for what they earned, He would not leave upon the earth any creature, but He delays them until an appointed term.** (Qur'an 35:45)

The verse produces humility. Each person lives by divine forbearance more than he realizes.

**Respite can be a mercy or a danger**

The same additional time can become a mercy for one person and a danger for another. A person who has sinned may use it to repent, reconcile, return rights, and change direction. Another may take it as proof that nothing matters and grow more entrenched. The outward circumstance—continued health, income, status, or opportunity—does not announce which meaning it has. The inner response reveals it.

This is why believers should receive ease with vigilance. Rather than saying, “Nothing bad happened, so my choices must be approved,” one asks, “Has this ease made me more grateful, generous, and attentive to God?” A blessing received with arrogance can become a test; a blessing received with gratitude becomes a means of good.

**Firmness without panic**

God’s plan being firm should not make a believer passive or fatalistic. It gives courage to act without despair. An oppressed person may use lawful means, seek allies, document wrong, and pursue justice while trusting that ultimate outcomes are not controlled by the oppressor. A reformer may continue honest work even when corrupt systems appear unshakeable. A person tempted by a profitable sin may remember that apparent escape is not actual escape.

At the personal level, the verse can quiet the panic that leads people to unethical shortcuts. If provision, reputation, and future are in God’s knowledge, there is no need to secure them through deception. This does not promise a painless life. It promises that no pain, injustice, or hidden act falls outside a firm divine governance.

**Do not imitate the very trait being condemned**

The verse must not be read as permission for believers to plot harm against others under the name of religious strategy. God’s perfection cannot be used to justify human manipulation. The Prophet ﷺ taught honesty, fulfilment of trusts, mercy, and open justice. A believer responds to wrongdoing through truth, law, patience, and appropriate protection, not by claiming a divine right to deceive.

This distinction is especially important when people defend unethical conduct by saying their cause is good. A good end does not convert betrayal, false accusation, or cruelty into piety. God’s firm plan belongs to God; human beings remain bound by His commands.

**The next questions remove common excuses**

Having warned that delayed consequence is not approval, the sūrah returns to questions that reveal the irrationality of denial. Is the Prophet ﷺ asking them for money? Do they have access to the unseen? The answer to both is no. Their resistance cannot be explained by financial burden or superior knowledge; it is a refusal to accept a message that challenges their self-rule.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:46

> **Or do you ask them for a payment, so that they are weighed down by debt?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The message does not burden them with a price**

The rhetorical question is *am tasʾaluhum ajran fa-hum min maghramin muthqalūn*—do you ask them for a payment, so that they are burdened by a debt? *Ajr* is a wage, recompense, or payment. *Maghram* is a liability or debt one must pay; *muthqalūn* means weighed down. The sūrah asks the opponents to consider whether the Prophet’s ﷺ call is financially exploiting them. Is he demanding a fee that leaves them oppressed by obligation? The obvious answer is no.

This is part of the Qur'an’s repeated defence of prophetic sincerity. A messenger brings a message that challenges wealth, status, inherited custom, and selfishness, yet he does not ask the people to fund a private empire in return. The lack of financial motive does not by itself prove every claim true, but it removes a common explanation for why someone might fabricate a message. The Messenger ﷺ is not enriching himself through the people he asks to worship God.

> **Or do you ask them for a reward? But the reward of your Lord is better, and He is the best of providers.** (Qur'an 23:72)

The verse returns to the principle of 68:3: the Prophet’s reward is with God. That freedom enables him to speak what powerful people do not want to hear.

**Why people still feel burdened by revelation**

The opponents may not owe a financial debt, but they do feel the moral weight of the message. Revelation asks them to abandon idols, stop exploiting the weak, answer for their conduct, and recognize that wealth does not make them sovereign. It is easier to accuse the Messenger ﷺ of creating a burden than to admit that the burden is conscience responding to truth.

Moral obligation can feel heavy because it restrains desires that have become familiar. Paying a debt, returning property, telling the truth after a lie, reconciling after pride, or giving the poor their due may all cost something. But these costs are not arbitrary burdens imposed for the pleasure of restriction. They restore rights, protect trust, and free the person from the deeper weight of injustice.

The Qur'an describes the Messenger ﷺ as one who relieves burdens rather than multiplying them:

> **He enjoins upon them what is right and forbids them what is wrong, makes lawful for them the good things and forbids for them the harmful things, and lifts from them their burden and the shackles that were upon them.** (Qur'an 7:157)

Guidance can require discipline while still being liberation from destructive forms of bondage.

**Religion must not become a commercial trap**

This verse gives an enduring ethical criterion for religious leadership. The Qur'an does not forbid lawful compensation for ordinary labour. Teachers, translators, caretakers, and workers have needs and may be paid fairly. It does warn against turning access to God, forgiveness, truth, or religious status into a private revenue scheme. A caller who makes people financially dependent on his personal approval risks reproducing the very pattern the prophets rejected.

Communities should support those who serve them with fairness and dignity, while also keeping financial dealings transparent. Leaders should avoid exploiting guilt, fear, or promises of guaranteed spiritual outcomes in order to extract money. Donors should give sincerely rather than purchasing control. The Messenger’s example is a standard of moral independence, not a denial that human service has material needs.

**Debt as a moral image**

The word *maghram* also makes the listener feel the difference between divine command and exploitative debt. An unjust creditor can burden someone until every choice is narrowed by fear. The Prophet’s ﷺ message is not such a creditor. It calls people to worship the One who owns them already and whose commands are rooted in wisdom, mercy, and justice. The sūrah has shown this in the garden story: what initially felt like a “loss” to the brothers—sharing with the poor—was actually a protection against a much greater deprivation.

This does not make every religious instruction easy. Honest faith can demand sacrifice. But the sacrifice is not payment to a human messenger; it is a return to the right order of life. A person who gives up fraud loses an unlawful gain but gains a conscience no longer divided against itself. A person who prays makes time, but gains recurring return to the source of meaning.

**A question for every messenger and listener**

The verse asks leaders whether they are making religion a burden for personal benefit. It asks listeners whether they are treating a moral challenge as “too costly” merely because it disrupts a profitable habit. It also reminds every believer that truth should be offered without manipulation. Persuasion may be earnest, but it must not become coercion through money, fear, or false promises.

The next verse removes another imagined excuse. If the opponents reject the Prophet ﷺ because they believe they possess superior hidden knowledge, let them show what access to the unseen they actually have.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:47

> **Or do they possess the unseen, so that they write it down?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No private access to the hidden record**

The final rhetorical question in this sequence is *am ʿindahumu al-ghaybu fa-hum yaktubūn*—do they possess the unseen, so that they write it down? *Al-ghayb* is what lies beyond ordinary human perception and independent access: the final future, hidden intentions, the realities of the Hereafter, and the full decree known to God. The verse asks whether the deniers have somehow obtained this unseen knowledge and are therefore recording authoritative information about who will be saved, punished, rewarded, or vindicated. They have not.

The image of writing returns the reader to the pen at the beginning of the sūrah. God swore by the pen and what is inscribed; angels record deeds; divine decree encompasses creation. The opponents, however, do not possess the Preserved Record or a secret book that authorizes their confident claims. Their assertions about the Hereafter are not revelation. They are wish, habit, and self-interest dressed as certainty.

> **Say: None in the heavens and the earth knows the unseen except God, and they do not perceive when they will be resurrected.** (Qur'an 27:65)

The verse protects a central dimension of monotheism: hidden ultimate knowledge belongs to God alone.

**Revelation is an exception granted by God**

Saying that creatures do not independently possess the unseen does not deny revelation. God may disclose from the unseen what He wills to His messengers. The Qur'an itself makes that distinction:

> **He is the Knower of the unseen, and He does not disclose His unseen to anyone, except a messenger whom He has chosen.** (Qur'an 72:26–27)

The messenger does not own the unseen; he receives what God reveals for the purpose God chooses. This is why the Prophet ﷺ could teach about the Hereafter, prior communities, and matters God informed him of, while also saying that he did not know every hidden matter or control benefit and harm independently. Revelation is received knowledge, not human divinity.

**Writing can give false certainty a false authority**

The verb *yaktubūn*—they write—carries an important rhetorical force. Putting a claim in writing can make it look official. A chart, certificate, prediction, post, policy, or published opinion may acquire an aura of certainty simply because it has been recorded. But writing does not transform conjecture into knowledge. A false forecast remains false in elegant handwriting; an unjust rule remains unjust on official stationery.

This matters today in forms both religious and secular. People may claim precise knowledge of another person’s destiny, the exact date of a future event, the hidden motive of a stranger, or a guaranteed spiritual result for a payment or ritual. Others may treat data models, ideological forecasts, or institutional documents as though they eliminate uncertainty about human beings. Evidence and expertise have real value, but neither should be confused with possession of the unseen.

**Humility about what we cannot know**

Belief in *al-ghayb* does not make Muslims anti-knowledge. It gives knowledge its proper boundaries. We investigate what can be investigated, consult specialists, use evidence, plan for likely outcomes, and learn from experience. We do not pretend that probability is certainty or that access to information makes us masters of destiny. A doctor can offer prognosis without claiming absolute knowledge; an analyst can identify risk without declaring the future fixed; a religious teacher can state a clear text without assigning an individual’s final fate.

This humility is ethically protective. It keeps people from exploiting fear, silences the arrogance of false prophets, and makes room for repentance. If no human possesses the unseen record, no one can lock another person into permanent condemnation or claim absolute immunity for himself.

**The corrective to conspiracy and superstition**

The verse is also a cure for the craving to explain every hidden thing. Uncertainty can be uncomfortable, so people may turn to fortune-telling, sensational theories, spiritual manipulation, or confident speculation. The Qur'an asks the believer to distinguish what God has revealed, what evidence supports, what remains unknown, and what is merely imagined. This is not a colder life; it is a more truthful one.

A person who accepts limits can pray, prepare, seek counsel, and trust God without being driven by every rumor. He can say, “I do not know what tomorrow holds, but I know the Lord of tomorrow.” That is not ignorance; it is faithful proportion.

**From epistemic arrogance to patient obedience**

Verses 46 and 47 together remove two excuses for rejecting the Messenger ﷺ: he is not demanding payment, and they do not possess superior hidden knowledge. The next verse gives him the positive command appropriate to that situation: be patient with your Lord’s judgment, and learn from the story of the companion of the fish without repeating his moment of haste.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:48

> **So be patient for the judgment of your Lord, and do not be like the companion of the fish when he called out while choked with distress.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Patience under a judgment not yet visible**

After exposing the emptiness of the deniers’ claims, the sūrah returns directly to the Prophet ﷺ: *fa-iṣbir li-ḥukmi rabbika*—be patient for, or with, the judgment of your Lord. *Ḥukm* includes decree, decision, command, and judgment. The Prophet ﷺ is being asked to remain steadfast while God’s promise unfolds in a time and manner not controlled by hostile opponents or by his own understandable desire for relief. Patience here is not resignation to falsehood; it is disciplined endurance in carrying revelation, refusing retaliation outside God’s limits, and trusting that the Lord’s justice is not delayed through forgetfulness.

The command comes after verses about divine respite. The Prophet ﷺ sees mockery, accusation, and apparent success among those who reject him. The temptation in such a setting is to demand immediate resolution or to abandon the burden of patient invitation. God tells him to hold his place. The moral force of the earlier verses—that God knows, records, gives respite, and judges—now becomes a personal instruction in *ṣabr*.

> **So be patient. Surely the promise of God is true, and do not let those without certainty make you unsettled.** (Qur'an 30:60)

Patience is therefore an expression of certainty, not emotional numbness. A patient person may grieve, fear, make plans, seek protection, and ask God for relief. What he refuses is to let hardship drive him into betrayal of the mission or despair of the Lord.

**The companion of the fish**

The verse then invokes Jonah, *ṣāḥib al-ḥūt*, the companion of the fish, also called Dhū al-Nūn in the Qur'an. The title is dignified: he is not reduced to his difficult moment, and the sūrah does not name him with contempt. The Qur'an relates that Jonah left his people in a moment of distress and embarked on a laden ship, then was swallowed by a fish. The accounts of his mission elsewhere should be read together with care:

> **And Jonah was surely among the messengers, when he fled to the laden ship. Then he cast lots and was among those overcome. Then the fish swallowed him while he was blameworthy.** (Qur'an 37:139–142)

Prophets are honoured servants chosen to convey God’s revelation. Muslim scholars have discussed the nature of Jonah’s action in ways that preserve that prophetic dignity: he did not betray revelation or call people to falsehood; rather, he left his people before receiving permission to depart, and God corrected him through an extraordinary trial. The Qur'an tells the story not to diminish him but to teach all who carry responsibility that zeal and pain must remain inside obedience to God’s timing.

**The cry from inside constriction**

The phrase *wa-huwa makẓūm* evokes someone overwhelmed, constricted, or choked by grief and distress. Jonah’s response from the darkness is given elsewhere in one of the Qur'an’s most widely recited prayers:

> **And [remember] Dhū al-Nūn, when he went away in anger and thought that We would not constrain him. Then he called out in the darknesses: There is no god except You. Glory be to You. Indeed, I have been among the wrongdoers. So We answered him and saved him from distress. Thus do We save the believers.** (Qur'an 21:87–88)

The prayer is a complete theology in a few words. It begins with *tawḥīd*: no one is worthy of worship but God. It continues with *tasbīḥ*: God is declared free of injustice and defect. It ends with personal responsibility: I have been among the wrongdoers. Jonah does not blame God for the darkness, the sea, the ship, or the fish. He turns toward his Lord with reverence and truthfulness.

**Why Jonah is an example, not a condemnation**

The instruction not to imitate Jonah in this particular matter does not prohibit admiration for his righteousness, nor does it invite followers to speak disrespectfully about a prophet. It means: do not imitate the particular impatience from which Jonah was delivered. Every prophet named in the Qur'an is a model of faith, and each narrated correction teaches a precise human lesson. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ is commanded to endure his opponents’ rejection until God opens the matter, while Jonah’s story shows the danger of leaving a divinely assigned task in a moment of pressure.

The comparison is compassionate. The Messenger ﷺ is not told that his anguish is unreal. He is given the example of another messenger who knew anguish, cried to God, was corrected, and was saved. The lesson for believers is not to suppress emotion but to direct it. When a responsibility becomes heavy, speak to God before making an irreversible exit.

**Patience is active fidelity**

Modern readers sometimes confuse patience with tolerating abuse or refusing to change harmful circumstances. Qur'anic patience is not passive surrender. The Prophet ﷺ migrated when commanded, negotiated, defended the community, sought allies, and took practical precautions. Jonah himself called to God. Patience means remaining faithful to God’s limits while using lawful means. A person can leave a harmful job, report abuse, seek therapy, flee danger, or end an unjust arrangement where Islamic ethics and practical wisdom require it; he should not misuse patience to sanctify harm or silence those at risk.

The relevant warning is against letting frustration become a reason to abandon responsibility, violate a command, or assume that God’s delay means the mission has failed. A parent, teacher, activist, or community leader may feel trapped by slow results. Verse 48 teaches: return to God, seek wise means, and do not let immediate exhaustion author a permanent choice.

**The dignity of calling out**

Jonah’s cry also gives permission for honest prayer. A person does not have to pretend composure before God. Fear, grief, anger, confusion, and exhaustion can all be brought into supplication, provided they are held within reverence and accountability. The Qur'an’s prophets model voices that are truthful without becoming accusatory.

The following verse explains how Jonah’s story turned: a blessing from his Lord reached him. The correction did not end in abandonment; divine mercy met him even in the place of deepest constriction.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:49

> **Had a favour from his Lord not reached him, he would surely have been cast upon the barren shore while blameworthy.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mercy reaches before the final outcome**

The verse is a counterfactual of immense tenderness: *lawlā an tadarakahu niʿmatun min rabbihi la-nubidha bi-l-ʿarāʾi wa-huwa madhmūm*—had a favour from his Lord not reached him, he would have been cast onto the barren shore while blameworthy. *Tadarakahu* means that the blessing caught up with, reached, or overtook him. The image is one of mercy arriving before a deserved consequence reaches its full end. Jonah is not saved because his conduct requires no correction; he is saved because the Lord’s favour encompasses correction and restoration.

*Al-ʿarāʾ* is an exposed, barren shore with little cover or vegetation. *Madhmūm* means blameworthy or reproached. The verse imagines the bleakness of being expelled from the fish into exposure while still carrying unresolved blame. The actual story, however, does not end there. Divine favour has already met the prophet through his glorification, confession, and return.

> **Had he not been among those who glorify God, he would have remained in its belly until the Day they are resurrected. But We cast him upon the open shore while he was ill.** (Qur'an 37:143–145)

The related passage makes the mercy more vivid. Jonah is cast ashore, but not as one abandoned to blame. He is weak, ill, and in need of care, yet he is under God’s preserving attention.

**Grace does not cancel moral truth**

The word *niʿmah*, favour or blessing, appears earlier in the sūrah in God’s refutation of the claim that the Prophet ﷺ is mad through divine grace. Here, grace reaches Jonah after a moment of correction. In both places, divine favour is not a reward that human beings manufacture through self-sufficiency. It is the Lord’s gift, protecting the Messenger ﷺ from false accusation in one case and restoring a prophet after distress in another.

This does not mean grace makes moral action irrelevant. Jonah’s prayer included confession; the Qur'an tells readers that he was among those who glorify God. Divine mercy does not ask us to deny what is wrong. It makes it possible to face what is wrong without being destroyed by despair. A person who thinks he must be flawless before approaching God may remain trapped. The Qur'anic path is to return with truthfulness precisely because God is merciful.

**Counterfactual language teaches gratitude**

The verse’s counterfactual formulation invites contemplation of a mercy that may be invisible in ordinary life. People often recognize only the harm that occurred; they rarely know what worse outcome God prevented, what temptation He interrupted, what relationship He protected, or what consequence He softened. This should produce gratitude without encouraging superstition. We do not invent specific unseen stories about every event. We do recognize, in principle, that life rests continually upon mercies we did not earn or even notice.

The believer therefore looks at deliverance with humility and attributes it to God’s favour rather than self-sufficiency. This does not deny the role of medicine, rescue workers, planning, friends, or personal effort. Those means can themselves be among the forms through which divine favour arrives.

**The exposed shore and the vulnerable self**

The barren shore is a powerful image of what it feels like after a crisis. A person may have survived a mistake, addiction, conflict, illness, or public failure and still feel exposed, weak, and ashamed. The Qur'an does not romanticize that state. Jonah was ill when cast ashore. Survival may be followed by recovery that requires care, time, nourishment, and renewed support.

This teaches communities how to receive people who return. If God restores a servant after error, believers should not make a sport of keeping him permanently in the identity of his worst moment. Accountability and appropriate boundaries can be necessary; so can compassion, privacy, practical support, and room for credible change. The aim is not to erase the wrong but to cooperate with the possibility of restoration.

**Divine favour and human hope**

The verse gives hope to the person who fears he has gone too far. The details of Jonah’s prophetic story are unique, and no one should presume on grace while continuing deliberately in harm. Yet its principle is universal: no darkness is intrinsically beyond the reach of God’s mercy, and no sincere return is invisible to Him. The Qur'an declares:

> **Your Lord has prescribed mercy for Himself: whoever among you does wrong in ignorance and then repents after that and reforms—then surely He is Forgiving, Merciful.** (Qur'an 6:54)

Reform matters. The return is not simply a plea for relief from consequences; it is a movement toward a changed life.

**The distinction between blame and identity**

Jonah could be blameworthy in a particular matter without becoming defined forever by blame. This is a necessary moral distinction. People today often swing between pretending that no one should be criticized and treating a mistake as an unchangeable identity. The Qur'an offers a more truthful path: name the wrong, accept correction, seek forgiveness, and allow divine mercy to restore the servant’s future.

The next verse announces that restoration fully: Jonah’s Lord chooses him and places him among the righteous. The story closes not with shame but with renewed prophetic honour.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:50

> **But his Lord chose him and made him among the righteous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Correction culminates in renewed election**

The verse gives Jonah’s final restoration in two verbs: *fa-ijtabāhu rabbuhu fa-jaʿalahu mina al-ṣāliḥīn*—his Lord chose him and made him among the righteous. *Ijtabā* conveys selecting, drawing close, and choosing for a special purpose. The same Lord who corrected Jonah did not discard him. He chose him anew and affirmed his place among the righteous. This is a decisive answer to the fear that a servant who has stumbled is permanently unusable in the service of God.

The order is meaningful. First comes divine favour, then selection, then placement among the righteous. The restoration is not Jonah’s self-rehabilitation project or a public-relations repair. It is a gift of the Lord whose knowledge includes both the error and the sincerity of the return. God’s correction aims at truth and growth, not humiliation for its own sake.

> **Then his Lord chose him, forgave him, and guided him.** (Qur'an 20:122)

Although this verse speaks of Adam, it expresses a recurring Qur'anic pattern: a servant may err, repent, and be met by divine choosing, forgiveness, and guidance. The pattern does not trivialize error; it displays the breadth of mercy and the possibility of a future not imprisoned by the past.

**Righteousness is restored relationship, not flawless history**

*Ṣāliḥīn* are the righteous, sound, or rightly ordered people. The root *ṣ-l-ḥ* is related to repair and soundness. This is especially apt after Jonah’s ordeal. Righteousness in the Qur'an is not the claim that a human being has never needed correction. It is a condition of being made sound through faith, repentance, obedience, and God’s mercy.

This is good news for believers who feel disqualified by a sincere past failure. Islam does not erase the importance of accountability or restitution, but it refuses a fatalistic identity. A lie can be confessed and corrected; a wrong can be repented from; a broken habit can be retrained; a person who has injured trust can become trustworthy through sustained change. The Lord who made Jonah among the righteous is able to repair what human beings cannot repair by pride alone.

**Do not use mercy to avoid responsibility**

The verse’s hope needs the whole story around it. Jonah did not simply continue on his way and demand that God call him righteous. He called out from distress, glorified God, admitted wrong, and was restored by mercy. The story rejects both despair and presumption. It tells the ashamed servant to return, and it tells the careless servant not to treat divine mercy as permission to postpone return.

For harms involving other people, repentance includes attention to rights. A person who has stolen must return what he can; someone who has spread a falsehood must correct it; someone who has abused power must stop, protect those harmed, and accept appropriate accountability. No individual can declare himself restored simply because he wants righteousness applied to him. The fruit of repentance matters over time.

**A leader can learn from correction**

Jonah’s restoration is also a lesson for leadership. Leaders, teachers, parents, and public servants sometimes fear admitting a mistake because they assume authority depends on appearing incapable of error. The Qur'anic prophetic model is more morally durable. A leader who receives correction, returns to God, repairs what he can, and continues in service can become more trustworthy than one who protects a flawless image through denial.

This does not excuse serious harm or remove the need for safeguards. Some roles require particular qualifications and accountability. It does show that moral authority is not built by never being corrected; it is built by what a person does when correction comes.

**God’s choosing is not human favoritism**

Divine selection can be misunderstood as arbitrary favouritism. In the Qur'an, God’s choosing rests upon perfect knowledge and wisdom. He knows the capacities, trials, sincerity, and future of His servants in a way no human institution can. People should not imitate divine choosing by declaring their own clique uniquely pure or beyond criticism. The correct human response to the verse is gratitude, humility, and hope—not claims of inherited spiritual rank.

For Jonah, chosen prophethood is unique. For ordinary believers, the lesson is to seek to be included among the righteous through faith, obedience, repentance, and mercy from God. The Qur'an regularly teaches believers to pray for that company rather than boast of possessing it.

**The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ receives the lesson**

The immediate addressee of this command is the final Messenger ﷺ. He is told to be patient and not repeat Jonah’s moment of haste. Yet the story ends by honouring Jonah, not by setting prophets against one another. The instruction therefore combines firmness and consolation: endure the present trial, and remember that a prophet who cried out under pressure was received, corrected, and elevated by his Lord.

This is a model for every bearer of a difficult trust. Do not abandon the path because its results are slow. Do not make your pain an excuse to violate God’s limits. If you falter, return quickly and honestly. God’s correction can become part of a deeper righteousness.

The final two verses return to the opponents’ hostile gaze and accusation. The sūrah will answer them one last time: the message they call madness is a reminder for all peoples.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:51

> **Indeed, those who disbelieve would almost make you slip with their eyes when they hear the Reminder, and they say: Surely he is mad.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A gaze charged with hostility**

The verse describes the intensity of the opponents’ reaction: *wa-in yakādu alladhīna kafarū la-yuzliqūnaka bi-abṣārihim lammā samiʿū al-dhikra*. The verb *yuzliqūnaka* can convey making someone slip, strike, or nearly bring down through a fierce look. At the most immediate level, the verse portrays hostile staring: when the deniers hear the Qur'an, their rage is so concentrated that it is as though their eyes could knock the Prophet ﷺ from his footing. They then repeat the accusation already refuted at the sūrah’s opening, again portraying him as mad.

The circular structure is meaningful. Verse 2 declares, by the favour of his Lord, that the Prophet ﷺ is not mad. Verse 51 shows that the accusation persists despite evidence, character, and revelation. Repetition does not make slander true. The opponents’ gaze reveals their agitation, while the Qur'an’s calm return to the charge reveals its emptiness.

> **Your companion has not gone astray, nor has he erred; nor does he speak from his own desire. It is only a revelation revealed.** (Qur'an 53:2–4)

The Qur'an does not make the Prophet ﷺ an untouchable abstraction. It establishes that the message he brings is revelation, not the product of madness or private desire.

**The evil eye and divine decree**

Al-Qurṭubī records classical exegetical reports that connect this verse with the evil eye, a harm associated with envy or an intense gaze; those reports should not be made more certain than the Qur'anic wording itself. The immediate wording can certainly describe hostile looks, and the broader Prophetic teaching affirms that the evil eye is real. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> **The Messenger of God ﷺ said: The evil eye is real.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

In another authentic wording, he said:

> **If anything were to precede the decree, it would be the evil eye.** (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)

The statement does not mean that anything actually escapes or outruns God’s decree. Its rhetorical force is to stress the seriousness of a harm people may dismiss. The evil eye, like every created cause, has no independent power outside God’s will. Belief in it must therefore remain within *tawḥīd*: seek God’s protection, use permitted remedies, and never treat an object, person, formula, or practitioner as possessing autonomous supernatural control.

**Protection without superstition or blame**

The Qur'an teaches believers to seek refuge in God from envy and harm:

> **Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak, from the evil of what He created, from the evil of darkness when it settles, from the evil of those who blow on knots, and from the evil of an envier when he envies.** (Qur'an 113:1–5)

Reciting the protective sūrahs, making authentic supplications, giving blessings when admiring something, and relying on God are spiritually sound practices. At the same time, this belief must not become a culture of suspicion. Not every illness, accident, marital difficulty, or setback should be assigned to envy. Such claims can create fear, accuse innocent people, distract from medical or practical causes, and make vulnerable people susceptible to exploitation.

A Muslim can seek spiritual protection and medical care together. If someone is ill, consult appropriate professionals. If a problem requires legal, financial, or relational action, take those means. Spiritual reliance does not require neglecting ordinary responsibility; ordinary means do not make spiritual reliance unnecessary.

**Envy begins before any imagined effect**

The verse also teaches about the ordinary moral danger of envy. A hostile gaze can reflect pain at another person’s goodness, reputation, stability, or success. The remedy is not hiding every blessing in fear; it is training the heart to ask God for good without resenting His gifts to others. The Qur'an commands:

> **And do not wish for what God has favoured some of you over others. For men is a share of what they earn, and for women is a share of what they earn; and ask God of His bounty.** (Qur'an 4:32)

This transforms comparison into supplication. Rather than wishing a blessing removed from another person, ask the Giver for lawful good and cultivate gratitude for what has already been received.

**Hostility toward the Reminder**

The deniers’ rage is triggered when they hear *al-dhikr*, the Reminder. The Qur'an does not portray them as calmly disagreeing with a set of ideas. The recitation challenges their inherited authority, economic privilege, and moral autonomy. Their accusation of madness becomes a defense against having to hear the challenge. In this sense, the hostile gaze is psychological as well as potentially spiritual: a person can look at truth with such resentment that he is no longer able to evaluate it fairly.

A believer should not answer hostility by returning hostile stares. The Prophet ﷺ was instructed to seek refuge in God, convey the message, and endure. In ordinary life, a person can protect himself through wise boundaries, prayer, lawful precautions, and supportive community without becoming obsessed with enemies or imagined threats.

**The false diagnosis reveals the speaker**

Calling the Prophet ﷺ mad follows the pattern described at the beginning of the sūrah. It attempts to medicalize or dismiss a message rather than meet its content. The Qur'an’s answer is not to ridicule mental illness. It is to let the Messenger’s character, the coherence of revelation, and the moral bankruptcy of his opponents’ conduct demonstrate the falsehood of the charge.

Verse 52 gives the final, serene verdict. The Qur'an is not madness, a private agenda, or a threat to be defeated by a gaze. It is a reminder addressed to all the worlds.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:52

> **Yet it is nothing but a reminder for all the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The final answer is a reminder, not a private possession**

The sūrah closes with *wa-mā huwa illā dhikrun lil-ʿālamīn*—it is nothing but a reminder for all the worlds. The pronoun can be understood primarily as the Qur'an, the Reminder named in the preceding verse. Grammatically, it can also be understood as referring to the Prophet ﷺ as the bearer of reminder. The two meanings are deeply connected: he conveys the Book, and the Book calls human beings back to the truth for which they were created.

*Dhikr* means remembrance, reminder, mention, and recollection. The Qur'an is not merely a storehouse of data about God, history, law, and the future. It reminds the human being of realities he is prone to forget under pressure from desire, fear, habit, and social approval: that God is one, that life is entrusted, that wealth is a test, that the poor have rights, that words are recorded, that repentance remains possible, and that every person will return to the Lord.

> **And you do not ask them for any reward for it. It is nothing but a reminder for all the worlds.** (Qur'an 12:104)

The echo is exact. The message is not sold for a fee, nor is it designed to flatter one tribe or social class. It is an open summons.

**Universality without erasing particular histories**

The final phrase, *al-ʿālamīn*, expresses the universal reach of the Qur'anic reminder. It emerged in a particular language, place, and historical moment, addressing real Makkan opponents and real social injustices. Yet its principles are not confined to that moment. The pen and the moral record, the refusal to compromise truth, the corruption of gossip, the rights of the poor, the danger of wealth, the need for patience, and the promise of final justice speak wherever human beings write, speak, own, fear, and hope.

Universality does not mean cultural uniformity or contempt for peoples. It means that every people stands before the same Creator and can receive guidance without needing to become a copy of another ethnic group. The Qur'an honours human difference while grounding moral accountability in a shared origin and return.

> **We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds.** (Qur'an 21:107)

The Messenger ﷺ is a mercy not because every listener will accept him, but because the message he carries opens a path of truth, justice, forgiveness, and nearness to God for all who receive it.

**Reminder meets the human tendency to forget**

Human beings do not usually need to be told only once that they will die, that lies wound, or that greed is dangerous. The problem is not simply absent information; it is forgetfulness enacted through habit. A person can acknowledge a principle in the morning and betray it by afternoon under the pull of anger, fear, or appetite. *Dhikr* repeatedly returns the heart to its proper centre.

This is why Qur'anic recitation is not a one-time intellectual event. It is repeated in prayer, heard in homes, memorized by children, studied by adults, and returned to in grief and gratitude. Repetition here is not empty redundancy. It is moral formation. A compass is useful not because it reveals north once, but because it can be checked again when the traveller has drifted.

**The sūrah’s own catalogue of remembrance**

Sūrah al-Qalam has enacted what it now calls itself. It reminded the reader of the dignity and responsibility of writing; of the Prophet’s ﷺ sanity, reward, and character; of the danger of letting influential vice shape one’s conduct; of the social claim of the poor; of the contingency of wealth; of repentance after loss; of the distinction between righteous submission and entrenched crime; of the limits of human certainty; of patience; and of God’s protection against hostile forces.

These are not disconnected topics. Together they form a moral world in which God is central, human dignity is protected, and success is not measured by the immediate possession of fruit. The sūrah begins with a pen and ends with a reminder because written and recited revelation creates an accountable people: people who learn, remember, act, repair, and return.

**A reminder does not coerce reception**

The word *dhikr* also explains the Qur'an’s method. A reminder invites attention; it does not erase human responsibility by forcing the heart. The deniers heard the Reminder and called the Messenger ﷺ mad. Others heard and believed. The same recitation becomes guidance for one person and evidence against another depending on how the person receives it. This is why the sūrah combines clear truth with repeated commands to patience rather than coercion.

For believers, the universality of the reminder creates responsibility in communication. The Qur'an should be shared with wisdom, accurate knowledge, mercy, and good character—the very character praised in verse 4. It should never be used as a tool for ethnic superiority, manipulation, or public humiliation. A universal message must be carried in a manner worthy of its Giver.

**The end returns the reader to the beginning**

At the start, opponents call the Prophet ﷺ possessed. At the end, God calls the Qur'an a reminder for all worlds. Between those points, the sūrah has supplied its evidence not merely through abstract claims but through a moral portrait. The Messenger’s character is exalted; his opponents’ profile is exposed; the garden story shows how greed destroys its own security; the Hereafter gives justice its final horizon; Jonah shows that patient return is possible; and hostile accusation cannot alter the nature of revelation.

The closing therefore leaves the reader with a personal question. Will this sūrah be treated as distant material, a set of ancient tales, or will it become *dhikr*—a living reminder that changes how one uses a pen, speaks about others, plans wealth, receives loss, and turns toward God? Its answer is not forced. It is offered, recited, and preserved for every world that hears it.
