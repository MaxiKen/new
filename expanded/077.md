# Sūrah al-Mursalāt (Chapter 77) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter of Those Sent Forth**

Sūrah al-Mursalāt, "Those Sent Forth," is the seventy-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, and it takes its name from the very first phrase of its opening verse: *wal-mursalāti ʿurfā*, "By those sent forth in succession!" The name does not describe the chapter's main subject — the Resurrection — but rather the divine oath with which it begins, exactly as Sūrah al-Ṭūr is named after the mountain mentioned in its first verse and Sūrah al-Najm after the star of its opening oath. Muslim scholarship has also known the chapter as Sūrah al-ʿUrf, after the second word of verse one, which the classical grammarians and commentators understood in several senses: the angels or winds sent one after another *in succession*, like the flowing mane of a horse or the smooth sequence of a caravan, the word *ʿurf* literally suggesting what is known and customary, flowing onward in ordered continuity. Both names point to the same fact: this is a sūrah that begins by swearing — by agencies sent forth upon errands — and everything that follows in its fifty verses is, in one way or another, about the errand of truth to humanity and the day when every errand is brought to its account.

Like the greater part of the Qur'an revealed in Makkah, the chapter belongs to the years before the migration to Madinah, when the central contest between the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his people turned upon two claims: that God raises the dead, and that a day of reckoning is coming in which every denial will meet its consequence. One early commentator of note, Ibn ʿAbbās, and after him al-Qatādah, maintained that its closing passage — and in particular verse 48, with its criticism of those who refuse to bow — was revealed in Madinah, after prayer had become an institution of the new community; the majority of scholars, however, count the whole sūrah as Makkan. In the written order of the Qur'an it stands between Sūrah al-Insān, which closes with the summons "So whosoever wills, let him take a path unto his Lord," and Sūrah al-Nabaʾ, the Great Tiding, which opens with the disbelievers' own question about the news of the Resurrection — a placement that makes al-Mursalāt the hinge between two of the most sustained Resurrection-sūrahs of the final section of the Book.

**A Revelation Received Inside a Cave**

The most celebrated memory of this sūrah's revelation is preserved in an authentic report recorded by Muslim in his Ṣaḥīḥ from Ibn Masʿūd, the Companion whose recitation the Prophet ﷺ praised and whose rendering of the Qur'an the early community treated as a master text. Ibn Masʿūd said:

> "While we were with the Messenger of God ﷺ in a cave [at Minā], 'By those sent forth in succession' was revealed to him. We received it directly from his mouth as soon as it was revealed to him, when suddenly a snake came out of the cave. The Messenger of God ﷺ said, 'Take it and kill it!' We raced to it, but it outstripped us and entered its hole. The Messenger of God ﷺ said, 'It has been saved from your evil, just as you have been saved from its evil.'" (Muslim, from Ibn Masʿūd)

The report is precious for several reasons. It fixes the historical setting of the sūrah: revelation descending not in the sanctuary of the Kaʿbah but in the wilderness of Minā, in a cave, to a man already teaching his companions the words as they came — Ibn Masʿūd "receiving from his mouth" the fresh revelation, the earliest form of Qur'anic transmission being oral and immediate, before any page was written. And the closing sentence of the Prophet ﷺ is itself a small sermon in the language of the sūrah: the snake escaped the Muslims' evil as they escaped its evil — a mutual sparing that the commentators read as a sign of the mercy that surrounds the descent of this very chapter with its warnings. Even in the moment of revelation, the Prophet ﷺ was teaching his companions that the mercy of God pervades the meeting of every creature with every other, a theme to which the sūrah's own contrasts between the mercy of the Garden and the severity of the Fire give cosmic scale.

A second authentic memory connects the sūrah with the prayer of the Prophet ﷺ himself. Umm al-Faḍl Lubābah, the wife of al-ʿAbbās and one of the earliest believing women of Makkah, reported that she heard the Prophet ﷺ recite Sūrah al-Mursalāt aloud in the sunset prayer, the Maghrib; the report is recorded by al-Bukhārī (no. 763) and Muslim (no. 462). The detail tells the reader something important about the sūrah's liturgical life: this dense sequence of oaths, warnings, and refrains was not only a text of private admonition for the disbelieving Quraysh — it was recited publicly, in the daily prayer, to the believing community itself. Its rhythm of "Woe that Day to the deniers!" was heard again and again in the mosque of Madinah at the very hour of sunset, so that the believers too would keep the Day of Division before their eyes as they broke their fast of the day.

**An Architecture Built on a Tenfold Refrain**

The most distinctive structural feature of Sūrah al-Mursalāt is the phrase that rings through it ten times: *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" After the opening oath-cluster (vv. 1–7) and the apocalyptic scene of the heavens dissolving (vv. 8–14), the refrain first strikes at verse 15, and from that moment it beats through the sūrah like a drum at intervals of roughly three to five verses: at verses 15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 40, 45, 47, and 49. Between each stroke of the refrain the sūrah lays down one complete unit of argument or description: the destruction of the ancient peoples (vv. 16–18), the creation of the human being from a drop (vv. 20–23), the making of the earth as a receptacle (vv. 25–27), the horror of the Fire (vv. 29–33), the speechlessness of the guilty (vv. 35–36), the gathering of all generations (vv. 38–39), the challenge to the schemers (v. 39), the bliss of the reverent (vv. 41–44), the brief and ironic enjoyment of the guilty (v. 46), and the refusal to bow (v. 48). Ten warnings, ten denials, and between them a complete course of evidence — this is the sūrah's method of persuasion, closer to the structure of an anthem than of an essay: evidence announced, warning struck, evidence advanced, warning repeated.

The classical commentators meditated at length on the function of this repetition. Al-Ṭabarī read each occurrence as a fresh emphasis of the same dreadful truth — the reality of the Resurrection and the ruin of those who deny it — repetition serving the rhetoric of warning exactly as a judge might repeat a sentence to a defendant who refuses to hear it. Al-Qurṭubī and others noted that each occurrence may also be read contextually, each instance pointing to the specific denial that the immediately preceding verses established: denial of the Resurrection, denial of the lesson of destroyed nations, denial of the power of the Creator, denial of His blessings, denial of the Fire, denial of the Day, denial of God's power to gather and to scheme-proof, denial of the reward of the virtuous, denial of the Source of every enjoyment, and finally denial of the command itself. On this reading the refrain is not ten identical drumbeats but ten different verdicts pronounced over ten different crimes. Both readings are sound, and both will be followed in the verse-by-verse commentary below, in which each of the ten occurrences receives its own full treatment in its own context.

**The Argument of the Sūrah: From the World We Know to the Day We Deny**

Beneath its refrain-structure, the sūrah advances one continuous argument, and it is worth tracing that argument before descending into the verses one by one. The opening oaths (vv. 1–7) establish that God sends forth His errands — winds and angels, messengers and reminders — so that humanity has been reached by a genuine communication: the revelation that excuses or warns. What follows from this is announced in the answer to the oaths: "surely what you are promised will befall" (v. 7). The sūrah then points to the signs that will mark the coming of that promise — stars blotted out, the sky riven, mountains scattered, the messengers gathered to their appointed hour (vv. 8–12) — and names the hour itself: the Day of Division (vv. 13–14). From there the argument moves through three domains in which every human being already possesses the evidence of God's power to do all of this. History: the peoples of old who denied their messengers were destroyed, and the pattern continues (vv. 16–19). The human body: every one of the deniers was himself created from a base fluid, lodged in a secure dwelling for a known measure, determined by an excellent Determiner (vv. 20–23) — the very power that made the embryo from nothing can remake the dead. The earth: the ground beneath every foot is a receptacle for the living and the dead, held firm by mountains, watered by sweet springs (vv. 25–27). Then the sūrah crosses into the Hereafter itself and describes the two destinies: first the sentence of the guilty, driven to the Fire whose shadow gives no shade (vv. 29–40), and then the reward of the reverent, amid shade and springs and fruits such as they desire (vv. 41–45). The final movement turns back to the deniers still living in this world, eating and enjoying "a little" before their guilt overtakes them (v. 46), refusing the command to bow (v. 48), and the sūrah closes with the question that leaves the whole argument in the reader's hands: "So in what discourse after this will they believe?" (v. 50).

The logic is worth pausing on, because the sūrah is doing something more subtle than threatening. It is arguing that the Resurrection is not an arbitrary announcement but the completion of a pattern already visible: the same God who sends forth His messages by ordered agencies, who dissolves the heavens in the apocalyptic descriptions, who destroyed the guilty nations of the past, who builds a human being in the darkness of the womb and spreads the earth as a dwelling, is the God whose word cannot fail. Denial, on this showing, is not intellectual caution; it is a refusal to follow one's own evidence to its conclusion. Every adult human being is a living specimen of the divine power being denied — that is the sūrah's sharpest point, and it is driven home by the refrain after each proof, like a hammer following each stroke of the chisel.

**The Place of the Sūrah Among Its Sisters**

The attentive reader of the Qur'an will notice that al-Mursalāt is one of a family of late-Makkan sūrahs that hammer the same anvil: al-Ṭūr, al-Wāqiʿah, al-Ḥāqqah, al-Maʿārij, al-Nabaʾ, al-Nāziʿāt, al-Takwīr, al-Infiṭār, al-Inshiqāq. What distinguishes al-Mursalāt within this family is its disciplined refrain-form and its tight alternation of world and Hereafter. Its description of the Fire's sparks "like massive tree trunks" (v. 32) and "yellow camels" (v. 33), of the shadow that gives no shade (vv. 30–31), and of the Garden's shade and springs (v. 41) will reappear in expanded form in the longer sūrahs that follow it in revelation; its "Day of Division" (*yawm al-faṣl*) is a name shared with Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt (37:21), al-Dukhān (44:40), and al-Nabaʾ (78:17), and its warning-phrase *wayl* echoes the five woes of Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn and the single woe of Sūrah Ibrāhīm (14:2) and al-Baqarah (2:79). The sūrah is, in effect, a first draft of the Qur'an's full Resurrection-doctrine in miniature: ten warnings, fifty verses, and a complete course of evidence from the winds of this world to the springs of the next.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the fifty verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order: every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through its Arabic vocabulary and grammar, the views of the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī, Fakhr al-Rāzī, al-Baghawī, al-Shawkānī, and others — authenticated Prophetic reports with their sources identified, Qur'anic cross-references quoted at length, and the psychological, ethical, and practical dimensions of the verse's teaching. Where the source commentary treats verses in groups, they are here separated and each given full individual treatment, as the workflow of this project requires; verses that repeat the refrain are each treated independently in their own context, so that the ten occurrences of "Woe that Day to the deniers!" are read ten times, each time freshly, as the sūrah itself recites them. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short or long; each verse receives the depth its content genuinely requires. What follows, then, is the whole of Sūrah al-Mursalāt, verse by verse, from "By those sent forth in succession!" to "So in what discourse after this will they believe?"

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:1

> **By those sent forth in succession!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Oath Swears By, and Why It Opens This Way**

The sūrah begins with a divine oath: *wal-mursalāti ʿurfā*. The particle *wa* is the oath-particle, and the noun it governs is *al-mursalāt*, the feminine plural of *mursalah*, "one sent forth," from the root *r-s-l*, whose meanings cluster around dispatch, release with gentleness, and sending upon a mission. From that same root come *rasūl* (messenger), *risālah* (message), and *irsāl* (sending). The root's core sense is not merely *moving* something but *dispatching it with a purpose and an errand*: what is sent has a sender, a destination, a task, and — when the sent one is a responsible agent — an accountability. The Qur'an opens this chapter, therefore, not by naming a thing but by pointing to a whole category of things that have been *sent*: agencies released into the world upon missions. The second word, *ʿurfā*, is an accusative used adverbially; it comes from the root *ʿ-r-f*, whose basic sense is sequence and flow — the *ʿurf* of a horse being its mane, hair following hair in ordered continuity — and from which also derives *maʿrūf*, the "known" good, because goodness is what flows in the order of things. The classical grammarians gloss *mursalātun ʿurfā* as "sent one after another, in succession," like the steady sequence of a caravan or the waves of the wind following wave on wave. Already in the first breath of the chapter, then, two ideas are joined: mission and order. What God sends does not arrive at random; it comes in sequence, in measure, in the disciplined flow of design.

**Angels, Winds, or Prophets: The Commentators' Range**

The commentators differed — as they often did with the Qur'an's oath-clusters — over the precise referent of "those sent forth," and the range of their readings shows how the verse was understood to gather several realities under one image. Al-Ṭabarī, in his *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān*, allowed that the phrase may refer to the winds, which God sends forth one after another as bearers of rain and mercy, and equally to the angels, whom He sends with His commands, and he saw no need to force the verse to a single referent when its wording could embrace more than one. Al-Qurṭubī likewise recorded both interpretations among the early authorities. Those who read the following verses — "by the storming tempests," "by the spreaders spreading" — as descriptions of wind and weather took "those sent forth" to be the winds that arrive in succession, releasing rain and reviving the earth; this was the reading favored by many of the grammarians and by al-Ṭabarī among the accepted senses. Those who read the cluster as a description of the unseen world took them to be the angels, sent in succession with revelation and command; Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī also recorded the view that they are the prophets and messengers themselves, sent to their peoples one after another across the centuries — a reading that makes the whole sūrah's opening an oath by the unbroken line of divine communication to humanity: messenger after messenger, age after age, each "sent forth in succession" like the *ʿurf* of a mane flowing from the same neck.

A remarkable feature of this first verse is that all three readings are true simultaneously at different levels, and the Qur'an itself supports each. The winds are sent: *"And We send the winds as glad tidings..."* (30:46). The angels are sent: *"He sends down the angels with the Spirit of His command upon whomsoever He wills among His servants..."* (16:2). And the messengers are sent: *"Then We sent Our messengers one after another"* (23:44). The verse, placed at the head of a sūrah about the Resurrection, is an oath by the entire machinery of divine address — the winds that are the world's couriers, the angels that are the unseen's couriers, the prophets that are humanity's couriers — all of them *mursal*, all of them sent, all of them testifying by their very existence that there is a Sender who communicates with His creation.

**Why the Qur'an Swears by Created Things**

The reader may ask why the Qur'an swears by winds and angels rather than simply by God Himself. The classical scholars explained the wisdom of oaths by creatures in several ways. First, such oaths are invitations to *attention and study*: the thing sworn by is placed before the mind's eye as evidence. To swear "by those sent forth" is to say: consider the sending, examine the couriers, and you will find in their order the signature of the Sender. The oath functions like a hand pointing at a constellation before making a promise about the tide: look first, then listen. Second, oaths by creatures magnify the Creator by indirection — the glory of the creature is a borrowed glory, and the oath that exalts the courier secretly exalts the one who commissioned him. Third, as al-Rāzī and others observed, an oath is a bond of seriousness: when God swears by His own works that His promise will befall, He is declaring that the very order of creation is staked upon the truth of the Resurrection — the same power that sends the winds and the angels in ordered sequence will not fail to keep the appointment of the Hour.

**The Psychology of an Oath at the Threshold of a Warning**

There is also a purely human dimension to this opening that the commentators did not miss. When a person in ordinary life swears an oath before making a difficult announcement — "I swear by my life, what I am telling you will happen" — the oath is a concession to the listener's skepticism; it says, in effect, I know you doubt, and I bind myself in your presence so that my words will be taken seriously. The Qur'an's oaths perform the same psychology at a higher level. The Makkan listeners to whom al-Mursalāt was first recited did not doubt that winds blow and that messages pass from hand to hand; these were the most ordinary facts of their desert world. By opening with an oath built from those ordinary facts, the sūrah carries its audience from what it does not doubt to what it does: if the sending is real — and every wind that watered your pastures and every messenger who ever reached your tribe proves it is — then the promise of the One who sends is real as well. The oath is a bridge from the known to the promised, and the sūrah will spend its remaining verses walking its listeners across that bridge.

**Sent Forth in Succession: A Mirror for Every Believer**

There is finally a quiet implication of this verse for the believer in every age. The *mursalāt* are sent upon their errands and return to their Sender; the angels "descend" and ascend; the winds are released and vanish; the prophets delivered and died. Everything in the opening verse is in motion — commissioned, purposeful, and accountable. The verse thereby frames human existence itself: every soul is also "sent forth" into the world for a term, upon an errand that its Lord will review. The tenfold warning of this sūrah is addressed to "the deniers," but the oath with which it opens speaks to everyone who has ever received a message, a breath, a life — asking the only question that matters: the message was sent, and you were reached; what did you do with the errand? The believer who recites this verse in prayer is, in effect, reciting the warrant of his own existence — sent forth in succession from the hand of God, and bound for the day when all that was sent shall be gathered back.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:2

> **By the storming tempests!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Behind "Storming Tempests"**

The second oath of the sūrah swears *wal-ʿāṣifāti ʿaṣfā* — literally, "by those storming with a storming," the verbal noun *ʿaṣf* used for emphasis in the manner of Arabic idiom, like saying "they raged with a raging." The word *ʿāṣifāt* is the feminine plural of *ʿāṣifah*, "storming," from the root *ʿ-ṣ-f*. The classical lexicographers defined *ʿaṣafa* as the violent stirring and churning of the wind, the rushing of a gale that tears and drives; a *ʿāṣifah* is a storm wind, and the Qur'an uses the word elsewhere for the tempest that bursts upon those who are caught in the open: in the story of the people of ʿĀd, destroyed by a wind of destruction, and in the warning that a storm of punishment seizes the heedless like a sudden squall upon a ship. If the previous verse's "sent forth" pictured the couriers in their ordered sequence, this verse pictures them in their force: the same winds that were "sent forth in succession" are here seen in their violent aspect — sweeping, tearing, overturning.

**Winds of Mercy and Winds of Chastisement**

The commentators, reading the verse in the light of the whole Qur'an, saw in this storming both a literal reference and a symbolic one. Al-Ṭabarī explained *al-ʿāṣifāt* as the violent winds that God sends when He wills; others among the early authorities took the verse to refer to the angels who rush with God's command, storming through the heavens in obedience; and al-Qurṭubī gathered both readings, noting that the Qur'an elsewhere describes the angels themselves as those who "extract with violence" (79:1) and "drive on with driving" (79:3), swift executors of the divine will. Beneath the difference of referent lies a single theological point: the forces of nature are not blind. The wind that the Qur'an describes has a moral history — it is sent. Consider how the Qur'an speaks of wind in its two aspects. Wind is mercy: *"He it is Who sends the winds as glad tidings before His Mercy, till when they bear a laden cloud, We drive it unto a dead land and send down water therefrom, and bring forth thereby fruits of every kind..."* (7:57). And wind is chastisement: *"And as for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a furious, roaring wind"* (69:6), and *"We loosed upon them a raging wind in days of ill fortune"* (41:16). The very same element that carries the cloud to the dead land carries destruction when it is commanded to carry destruction. There is no second wind; there is only the command of the Sender.

This is precisely why the sūrah swears by the storming tempests at the head of a chapter about the Resurrection. The Makkan deniers, like people in every age, lived inside the weather: they saw the sky darken, the wind rise, the tent-ropes tear, the caravan scatter. The verse tells them that the power which raises that wind is the power that will raise the dead — and that the same moral seriousness governs both. A universe in which storms are sent is a universe in which warnings are sent; a universe in which the wind obeys its Lord is a universe in which the Hour obeys its Lord. The famous saying of the commentators may be recalled here: the Qur'an points to the ordinary, so that the extraordinary may be believed from the testimony of the ordinary. Every storm that ever flattened a camp was a small, legible signature of the power that will one day "blot out the stars and rive the sky asunder," as this very sūrah will describe in verses 8–9.

**The Prophet's Prayer Upon the Wind**

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ drew the practical consequence of this teaching for his community, and his practice upon the rising of a storm is recorded in the authentic collections. ʿĀʾishah reported that when the sky became dark with wind, the Messenger of God ﷺ would show unease in his face, walking back and forth, and when rain fell he would be relieved. When she asked him about this, he explained that he feared that the wind might be the chastisement of a people who had denied. Muslim records his supplication upon the wind:

> "O God, I ask You for its good, the good of what is in it, and the good of what it has been sent with; and I seek refuge in You from its evil, the evil of what is in it, and the evil of what it has been sent with." (Muslim, from ʿĀʾishah)

The report is a living commentary on this verse. The storm is not merely meteorology; it is *sent*, and because it is sent it may carry mercy or warning, and the believer who stands before it stands before a courier of his Lord. The Prophet's unease was not superstition but the perfectly logical response of a man who had read the Qur'an's own grammar of nature: when the courier arrives, one does not know whether he bears a gift or a summons, so one asks the Sender for the gift and takes refuge from the summons.

**Why Violence Belongs in the Divine Vocabulary**

Modern sensibilities sometimes stumble at scriptures that portray God's power in violent natural imagery. The sūrah's answer is embedded in its structure. The storming tempests are sworn by — made a witness — because destruction itself has a place in the moral order: the destruction of the guilty nations of the past (vv. 16–18 of this very sūrah) is presented as justice, and the Fire described later in the chapter is presented as the consequence of denial. The Qur'an is not delighting in violence; it is insisting that moral reality has teeth — that the same order which rewards the reverent with "shade and springs" (v. 41) must have a corresponding gravity for those who spend their lives denying the Sender of the winds. A world in which cruelty and denial could go on forever without consequence would not be a merciful world; it would be an unjust one, and the storm in this verse is the first intimation that the universe is not indifferent to how its human inhabitants treat their Lord. For the believer, the verse is a call to that reverent fear which the Qur'an calls taqwā — the alertness of one who sees the storm in the distance and secures what he loves before it arrives.

**The Storm as a Figure of the Inner Life**

There is also a psychological reading of the storming tempests that the later commentators and spiritual writers drew from this verse. The human heart has its *ʿāṣifāt* too — storms of anger, gusts of desire, squalls of despair that rise suddenly and tear at resolve. The verse that swears by the storms of the world reminds the reader that his inner weather is also commanded, also observable to its Sender, and also capable of being turned from destruction to mercy. The winds of the soul, like the winds of the earth, take their character from the command they obey: the same passion that can rage destructively can, when reined by revelation, become the driving force of patience, courage, and reform. The classical spiritual commentators loved to note that the very first thing the Qur'an does with the wild forces of the world is not to deny them but to swear by them — to take them into the witness-box of truth. Tamed by the remembrance of God, the storm within becomes the "sent forth" wind that carries clouds of mercy to dead lands; untamed, it becomes the tempest that levels what previous mercies built. Every believer who has wrestled with his own anger knows which storm this verse means — and knows, too, that the same Sender who commands the outer wind commands the inner one, if only the soul will submit its weather to His will.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:3

> **By the spreaders spreading!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Root That Carries the Whole Argument**

The third oath swears *wal-nāshirāti nashran* — "by the spreaders spreading," the verbal noun again reinforcing the verb. The key word is *nāshirāt*, from the root *n-sh-r*, and here the Arabic language itself performs a stroke of theological genius that the reciter of this sūrah should pause to appreciate. The root *n-sh-r* has two faces in the Qur'an. Its first face is *spreading, unfolding, scattering*: the winds spread the clouds across the sky; rain spreads over the thirsty earth; a thing folded is unfolded. Its second face is *reviving, raising after death*: the resurrection of the dead is called *nushūr* in the Qur'an, the "spreading forth" of life after its folding away in death. The classical lexicographers noted the deep intuition at the root of this double usage: death is a folding up, and resurrection is an unfolding — as a garment laid by is shaken out and spread again, as a scroll rolled shut is opened to be read. The Qur'an itself makes the connection explicit in a verse that every commentator on this passage cites:

> "And God is He Who sends the winds, and they raise a cloud; then We drive it unto a dead land and thereby revive the earth after its death. Even so shall be the Resurrection (*nushūr*)." (Qur'an 35:9)

The verse says plainly what the sūrah is swearing by: the spreaders who spread — winds spreading clouds, rain spreading life across the dead land — are living parables of the resurrection itself. The same Power that each spring "unfolds" the dead earth will one day unfold the dead bodies of humanity. The oath of verse 3 is therefore not a decorative nature-image appended to the sūrah; it is the first evidence in a chain of evidence whose conclusion is the Day of Division announced at verse 13.

**Who Are the Spreaders? The Commentators' Readings**

As with the preceding verses, the classical exegetes read "the spreaders" in several complementary ways. Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Jalālayn recorded the reading that they are the winds, which spread and distribute the clouds across the heavens until they release their rain; this is the sense that flows most directly into the resurrection-parable of 35:9. Others, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī in a second recorded view, took them to be the rain itself, which spreads over the face of the earth — or the angels who spread the mercies, blessings, and revelations of God abroad, and who "spread their wings" in the service of the divine command. Al-Ṭabarī, with his characteristic refusal to force a single meaning, noted that the verse does not specify one of these to the exclusion of the others, and that each is true in its own order: the wind spreads the clouds, the rain spreads over the earth, and the angels spread God's revealed books — all of them "spreaders spreading" the mercy of God upon His creation. The unifying image, whatever the referent, is of abundance let loose: nothing in this verse is kept, hoarded, or withheld. The spreaders spread because their Lord is generous, and the whole economy of sky and earth is one of outflow — water given, life released, messages delivered, mercy distributed without invoice.

**The Desert Rain and the Open Hand of God**

To feel the force of this verse, one must stand in the landscape where it was first recited. The Makkan Arabs lived in a land where rain was not an inconvenience but the difference between life and death — where the appearance of clouds on the horizon sent scouts out to read the sky, where a single rainfall could transform a valley of dry wadis into a pasture of green within days, and where the "dead land" was not a metaphor but the actual condition of the ground for most of the year. When the Qur'an said that the wind "spreads" the cloud and the rain "spreads" life upon a land that was dead, its first hearers did not need an explanation; they had watched the whole transaction with their own eyes, season after season. And that is exactly the argumentative strategy of the oath: the Resurrection is not an anomaly but the largest instance of a pattern the Arabs witnessed every year. The earth that was folded in the dryness of summer is spread again in the green of spring; the Qur'an simply asks the listener to raise his eyes from the annual miracle to the final one. Nothing in this verse is abstract; it is a promise written in weather, legible to any shepherd.

**The Unfolding of the Book as a Kind of Spreading**

The *nāshirāt* also bear a second meaning that ties the oath-cluster of verses 1–5 to revelation itself. Just as the winds spread the clouds, the angels "spread" the revealed books — the Qur'an is called a *dhikr* that is "spread" upon the hearts of the prophets, and the metaphor of unfolding governs scripture throughout the Qur'an: the "scrolls of Abraham and Moses" (87:19), the Book that will be spread open on the Day of Judgment (17:13–14: "And We have bound every man's omen upon his neck; and We shall bring forth for him, on the Day of Resurrection, a book he will find spread open"). Human destiny itself is a kind of *nashr*: the record of deeds folded in the breast of time will be unfolded and spread before its owner on the Day when "the messengers are slated" (v. 11 of this sūrah). The verse therefore quietly prepares the reader for the sūrah's later picture of the Day of Division, when everything concealed — belief and denial, good and evil, the folded history of every soul — will be spread out in the open for judgment. The spreaders of this world's clouds are the forecourt of the Spreader of all secrets.

**Learning to Read the World as Open Book**

For the modern reader, this verse carries an invitation that is more urgent, not less, in an age of weather forecasts and satellite images. Science has taught humanity to describe the spreading of clouds and the falling of rain with astonishing precision — and yet description is not the same as reading. The Qur'an's method is to look at the same facts and ask what they *say*: not merely how rain forms, but why the formation of rain should be so regular, so lavish, so geared to the need of living things that the whole system looks like intention; not merely how a dead land revives, but why the revival of dead land should be the exact image the human mind needs for the revival of the dead. The verse invites the reader to move from the mechanism to the meaning, from the how to the who — and then to the when of the final unfolding. Every spring that returns, every rain that falls on a field that was dry, is a "spreader" still going about its errand, still testifying, for those with eyes to read the testimony, that the One who spreads life over the dead earth has given His word about the dead of humanity. The believer's practice of pausing before rain and growth with gratitude is not sentiment; it is literacy — reading, in the language of the *nāshirāt*, the first page of the promise this sūrah was sent to announce.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:4

> **By the discerners discerning!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath That Names the Dividing Line**

The fourth oath swears *fal-fāriqāti farqā* — "by the discerners discerning," the verbal noun *farq* emphasizing the act. The word *fāriqāt* is the feminine plural of *fāriqah*, "one that separates, distinguishes, discriminates," from the root *f-r-q*, whose entire semantic field is division, separation, and the drawing of boundaries: *farq* is the parting of hair, the dawn that splits the night, the separation of truth from falsehood, the sifting of one thing from another. The Qur'an's most famous derivative of this root is *al-Furqān* — the "Criterion" — one of the very names of the Qur'an itself, and the mention of the *fāriqāt* in this oath-cluster is therefore far more than a weather image; it brings the sūrah's opening into the presence of revelation. The same cluster of verses that swore by winds and their spreading now swears by the power that *discerns*, and the classical commentators are nearly unanimous that the primary referent here is the angels, who descend with what distinguishes truth from falsehood, or the revelation itself, which performs that distinguishing among humanity. Al-Qurṭubī explained the discerners as the angels who descend with the command of God, separating truth from falsehood and the lawful from the unlawful; al-Ṭabarī held that the verse may refer simultaneously to the angels, to the Qur'an, and to everything that distinguishes truth from falsehood, since the word's sense is wide enough to embrace every agency of discrimination that God has established.

**The Criterion and the Furqān: Scripture That Divides**

The Qur'an applies the name *Furqān* to itself in verses that make the purpose of revelation explicit: *"Blessed is He Who sent down the Criterion (al-Furqān) upon His servant, that he may be a warner to the worlds"* (25:1), and *"the month of Ramadan, wherein the Qur'an was sent down as a guidance for mankind, and clear proofs of guidance, and the Criterion"* (2:185). The root also appears in the Qur'an's account of the day when truth and falsehood met in open battle at Badr: *"...on the day of discrimination (yawm al-furqān), the day the two hosts met"* (8:41). The word *discernment* in the Qur'an is never merely intellectual; it is the cutting edge of moral reality, the line that God Himself has drawn through human affairs between what is true and what is false, what is commanded and what is forbidden, what leads to life and what leads to ruin. To swear "by the discerners discerning" is to swear by the very existence of that line — to declare, before the sūrah's first warning falls, that reality is not a blur in which every claim is equally true and every conduct equally acceptable. The deniers of Makkah insisted that the distinctions the Prophet drew — between worship of the One and worship of idols, between the Resurrection and the dust, between the Garden and the Fire — were merely his opinions. The oath answers in advance: the distinctions are real, they are drawn by God, and they are enforced by beings sent for that very purpose.

**The Inner Connection to the Day of Division**

The sūrah will announce, at verse 13, that the promised Hour is "the Day of Division" (*yawm al-faṣl*) — and *faṣl* is the near-synonym of *farq*, both meaning the definitive separation of one thing from another. The careful reader should notice that the sūrah has set its own terms at the very beginning: it opens by swearing by "the discerners discerning," and it will unfold, across its fifty verses, into the Day when the discernment becomes final and visible. What the *fāriqāt* do in time — sending revelation that separates truth from falsehood, sending prophets who separate obedience from denial, sending consciences that separate right from wrong — the Day of Division will do in eternity, separating the reverent from the deniers as decisively as the shepherd separates sheep from goats, as decisively as the dawn separates day from night. The connection is not accidental. The whole argument of al-Mursalāt is that the discriminations of this world are rehearsals for the discrimination of the next: the same God who sends discerners now will divide humanity then, and the division will follow the lines that the discerners drew. No one will be able to claim, on that Day, that he never knew where the line was; the fāriqāt drew it, recited it, and swore by it in the opening of this very sūrah.

**Why Distinction Is a Mercy, Not a Burden**

Modern thought often treats divisions — between true and false, lawful and unlawful, right and wrong — with suspicion, as though the drawing of lines were itself the problem. The Qur'an's answer is that distinction is mercy. Consider what the world would be without the discerners: no Criterion to separate the counsel that heals from the counsel that destroys, no law to separate the taking of another's property from honest exchange, no revealed boundary to separate the worship that elevates from the worship that degrades. The child who is never told the difference between the hot surface and the cold is not free; the child is endangered. The adult who is told that every religion, every moral code, every claim about the afterlife is equally true is not liberated; he is abandoned — cut loose in a fog in which no direction can be chosen because no direction can be known. The verse swears by discernment because discernment is the precondition of every meaningful choice. It is precisely *because* human beings are free that they need to know the truth: a freedom exercised in total ignorance is a freedom to stumble. The fāriqāt are the lamps that make freedom walkable — the distinction between truth and falsehood being, as the commentators said, the first service revelation performs for the human mind.

**The Believer as a Discerner in Daily Life**

The verse has a practical edge for the believer that the spiritual commentators developed at length. If God swears by the discerners, and if He sent the Furqān to make His servants discerners too, then the believer is commanded to carry the faculty of discrimination into the ordinary texture of life — to distinguish, as the Qur'an repeatedly says, the pure from the impure (2:219), the good from the bad (3:179), the honest word from the flattering lie. The classical scholars derived from such verses the duty of *naqd* — critical examination — in religion and in conduct: the believer does not swallow every claim whole, does not imitate every fashion, does not let the blur of custom erase the lines of revelation. The same Qur'an that swears by the discerners calls its people "the middle community" (2:143), the community of balance precisely because it is a community of discernment, able to say this is true and that is false, this is commanded and that is forbidden, without apology. In an age of information floods and moral fog, this verse is a standing summons to recover the lost art of discrimination: to test claims against the Furqān, to separate the essential from the trivial, and to live — as the angels of the verse live — on the side of the line where truth is kept and falsehood is refused, until the Day when the same line becomes the boundary of the Garden and the Fire.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:5

> **And by those who bring forth the Reminder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Agent in the Chain of Delivery**

The fifth oath completes the opening cluster: *fal-mulqiyāti dhikrā* — "by those who bring forth the Reminder," or, more literally, "by those who cast the Reminder." The word *mulqiyāt* is the feminine plural of *mulqiyah*, "one who casts or delivers," from the root *l-q-y*, whose sense is the throwing, placing, or delivering of something so that it arrives at its destination: the same root appears in the Qur'an's description of revelation descending upon the heart, *alqā*, "He cast" — as in the verse describing how God taught Adam the names, and in Gabriel's descent with the Qur'an "upon your heart" (26:193–194). The object cast is *al-dhikr*, "the Reminder" — the word the Qur'an most often uses for itself in its Makkan self-descriptions. *Dhikr* comes from the root *dh-k-r*, remembrance, mention, and keeping present; a reminder is precisely that which makes the mind present to what it has neglected, calls back what has slipped away, and keeps in view what the distractions of life push out of sight. The Qur'an calls itself the Reminder in its most famous self-description: *"Truly We sent down the Reminder, and truly We are its Guardian"* (15:9). The angels of this verse are therefore those who cast the Reminder — who deliver revelation — and the commentators understood the verse accordingly: al-Ṭabarī and al-Jalālayn explained the *mulqiyāt* as the angels who descend with the revelation and deliver it to the prophets and messengers among the various human communities, casting the words of God upon their hearts. Ibn Kathīr recorded the same understanding. Where the previous verses swore by the couriers of the natural order — winds, storms, spreaders — this verse swears by the couriers of the revealed order: the angels who carry the Reminder from the Throne to the messengers, and the messengers who carry it from the unseen to humanity.

**A Chain of Transmission Worthy of an Oath**

Look closely at what the opening five verses have assembled. Verse 1 swore by those sent forth; verse 2 by the storming tempests; verse 3 by the spreaders spreading; verse 4 by the discerners discerning; verse 5 by those who deliver the Reminder. Read together, the five oaths describe a complete economy of divine communication: the sending (v. 1), the force with which the sent ones execute their errand (v. 2), the abundance with which they spread their burdens of mercy (v. 3), the precision with which they separate truth from falsehood (v. 4), and finally the delivery itself — the Reminder cast into the waiting hearts of the messengers (v. 5). The sūrah is swearing by the entire chain: God sends; the winds and angels execute; the clouds and mercies spread; the discerners distinguish; and the Reminder arrives — cast, delivered, received. When the Makkan deniers said that Muhammad ﷺ had invented the Qur'an, the oath-cluster answers with a picture of transmission too vast, too ordered, too multi-layered for fabrication: a message carried from the heavens through ranked agencies into the heart of an unlettered man of Makkah, with witnesses at every stage. The Qur'an presses the same point in another sūrah of oaths: *"Nay, I swear by the places of the stars — and truly it is a mighty oath, if you but knew — that this is a noble Qur'an, in a Book hidden, which none touch but the purified: a revelation from the Lord of the worlds"* (56:75–80).

**Gabriel, the Chief of the Deliverers**

The Qur'an identifies the chief of the *mulqiyāt* with unmistakable clarity. The Reminder was delivered by the angel Gabriel, described in the sūrah of the Poets with terms that match this verse's picture of delivery: *"And truly it is a revelation of the Lord of the worlds, which the Trustworthy Spirit brought down upon your heart, that you may be among the warners, in a clear Arabic tongue"* (26:192–195). And in the sūrah of the Enshrouded One the delivery is described as an honorable commission: *"Truly it is the word of a noble messenger, possessed of power, with the Lord of the Throne secure, obeyed there, trustworthy"* (81:19–21). The classical commentators on verse 5 of this sūrah drew the connection explicitly: the *mulqiyāt* are the angels who "cast the Reminder," as Gabriel cast the Qur'an upon the heart of Muhammad ﷺ, and as the angels cast revelation upon the hearts of the prophets before him. The oath therefore stands at the very point where the unseen world touches the visible one: at the moment of delivery, when the word of God enters the heart of a human messenger and becomes the word that human beings recite, memorize, and live. There is, in the entire architecture of Islamic belief, no more sacred juncture than this one, and the sūrah marks it with an oath.

**The Reminder and the Human Constitution**

Why is revelation called a *reminder* rather than a *teaching* or a *message*? The choice of word is deliberate and reveals the Qur'an's anthropology. A teaching conveys what was not known; a reminder calls back what was known and forgotten. The Qur'an's claim is that the truths it announces — that there is one God, that good and evil are not equal, that death is not the end, that the human being will answer for his choices — are not alien information imported into the human mind from outside; they are the native knowledge of the soul itself, written into the original constitution of the human being (*al-fiṭrah*), clouded over by time, distraction, and sin. The Reminder arrives to clear the cloud and wake the memory. The Qur'an says as much: *"And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their progeny, and made them bear witness concerning themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yea, we bear witness'"* (7:172). The deniers of Makkah, on this showing, were not ignorant men; they were forgetful men — men who knew the truth in the marrow of their being and suppressed it at the level of their will. That is why the sūrah can address them as "deniers" rather than as "the uninformed": denial presupposes a truth presented and refused, a Reminder delivered and rejected.

**The Duty of Every Hearer: To Carry What Was Cast**

The verse closes the oath-cluster by placing every reader of the sūrah in the chain. The angels cast the Reminder upon the prophets; the prophets delivered it to their communities; and the communities — that is, every generation of believers until the end of time — are commanded to keep the delivery going: *"Remind, for the Reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55). The mulqiyāt of heaven have their human counterparts, and the classical scholars drew the practical inference: whoever has received the Reminder bears a portion of its delivery. The parent who teaches the Qur'an to a child, the scholar who explains a verse, the ordinary believer who answers honestly when asked about his faith — each performs, in miniature, the work of the angels of this verse, casting the Reminder into the next heart along the chain. And because the Reminder is a *dhikr*, its delivery is not complete at the moment of hearing; it must be kept present, repeated, returned to — which is why the Qur'an is called a Reminder and why the believer is commanded to "remember" (*dhikr*) continually. The oath of verse 5 thus ends where the sūrah will end: the Reminder has been delivered, the deniers have been warned, and the only question left standing is the sūrah's final one — "So in what discourse after this will they believe?" (77:50). What was cast from heaven has arrived; it remains only for the hearer to receive it.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:6

> **to excuse or to warn,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Errands of the Delivered Reminder**

The sixth verse completes the sentence begun in the fifth: the Reminder is delivered *ʿudhran aw nudhrā* — "to excuse or to warn." The word *ʿudhr* is the verbal noun of excusing, from the root *ʿ-dh-r*: to accept or offer an excuse, to clear of blame, to remove the ground of complaint. Its companion *nudhr* is the verbal noun of warning, from the root *n-dh-r*: to caution of danger ahead, the root from which *nadhīr*, "warmer," is formed — the title the Qur'an repeatedly gives to the prophets, who came as *bashīr* (bearer of glad tidings) and *nadhīr* (warmer). The two words together, joined by *aw*, "or," describe the double outcome of a single delivery: the Reminder arrives at every human ear, and its arrival is either an *excuse* — an absolution and a completion of proof that leaves the one who heeds it without blame and the one who rejects it without argument — or a *warning* — an advance notice of the consequence that denial will bring. The classical commentators rendered the pair in exactly these terms. Al-Ṭabarī explained that the revelation brought down by the angels serves as an excuse and clearing of responsibility for those who accept it, and as a warning for those who turn away; Ibn Kathīr understood the two words as the two purposes for which the Reminder was sent: to establish the proof conclusively so that no one might plead ignorance, and to warn of the punishment that follows rejection.

**A Warning Before the Blow: The Logic of Divine Justice**

Underlying the verse is a principle of justice so important that the Qur'an returns to it in every period of revelation: God does not punish before He has warned, and He does not hold people to account for a law they were never given the chance to know. The Qur'an states the principle in its most general form in the sūrah of the Night Journey: *"We do not punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15), and expands it in the sūrah of Women: *"Messengers bearing glad tidings and warning, so that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers"* (4:165). The same logic appears as a memory of the day of judgment in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where the people of the Fire are pictured saying: *"Our Lord, why did You not send us a messenger, that we might have followed Your signs before we were humiliated and disgraced?"* (20:134) — and the sūrah answers that messengers did come. This is the *ʿudhr* in its deepest sense: the Reminder is delivered first, so that when the Day arrives no one can say, "No one told us; we did not know; we were not warned." The verse is, in effect, the Qur'an's own statement of the principle of fair notice, and the sūrah's refrain — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" — derives its moral force entirely from it: the deniers are not people who never heard; they are people who heard and refused. The warning strips denial of its innocence, which is why the refrain can name them so precisely: deniers are, by definition, those to whom the Reminder was delivered as an excuse or a warning and who answered it with rejection.

**Mercy Concealed Inside the Warning**

There is a subtlety in the order of the two words that the commentators did not overlook: the verse says the Reminder comes "to excuse or to warn," and the warning itself is a form of mercy. A warning is a gift — it is the storm signal that lets the sailor reach harbor, the medical notice that lets the patient change course before the disease becomes incurable. If the Reminder had come only as an excuse, the heedless would have no motive to change; if it had come only as a warning, the sincere would have no ground of hope. By joining them, the verse declares that God's address to humanity is never merely punitive: the warning is the sharp edge of the excuse, and the excuse is the soft edge of the warning. The same revelation that frightens the denier with the Fire consoles the believer with the Garden; the same verses that strip the arrogant of their arguments open the door of repentance to the sinner. Al-Zamakhsharī noted the balance: the two words mark the two classes of hearers — those who take the reminder and are thereby excused before their Lord, and those who refuse it and are thereby warned of what refusal brings — while the *aw* ("or") leaves the choice, as all choices must be left, with the hearer himself.

**Why the Human Conscience Needs the Reminder Twice Over**

The pairing of excuse and warning also answers a question in moral psychology: why does the mere existence of a law or a truth not suffice to move people? The Qur'an's answer, embedded in this verse, is that the human being is addressed through two channels that must both be reached. The first is the channel of *proof*: the mind must know that the claim is true and the command is binding — this is the *ʿudhr*, the completion of evidence that leaves no intellectual excuse standing. The second is the channel of *motivation*: the will must be stirred to act on what the mind knows — this is the *nudhr*, the warning that connects present choices to future consequences. Revelation delivers both at once: it argues and it alarms, it demonstrates and it urges. A person can know the truth and still drift, which is why the Reminder is not a treatise but a warning; and a person can be afraid and still be lost, which is why the warning is not a terror but an excusing mercy. The verse thus explains the characteristic sound of the Qur'an itself — its alternation of argument and threat, of evidence and appeal — because the Qur'an is addressing the whole human being, and the whole human being needs both the light that shows the path and the call that moves the feet.

**The Practical Test of the Verse**

Every reader of this sūrah is placed by this verse at the fork it describes. The Reminder has been delivered; the angels of verse 5 have done their work; the Prophet delivered the message; the Qur'an stands in the hand of the reader; and now the verse asks the only question that matters: will this delivery be an *excuse* for you — the completed proof on which you acted, the absolution you carry to your Lord — or a *warning* whose warning you ignored until it became a witness against you? The classical scholars drew the practical inference in the starkest terms: on the Day of Division, the very Reminder that could have saved the deniers will stand as their prosecutor, for the same book that excuses the believer documents the denial of the disbeliever. The verse, placed at the hinge of the oath-cluster, is the sūrah's first announcement that revelation is not a museum piece to be admired but a delivery to be answered — an errand that ends either in the excused who enter the Garden or in the warned who meet the Fire, with the choice recorded in the same breath that recorded the words.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:7

> **surely what you are promised will befall.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Finally Answered**

The seven opening verses of the sūrah have been one long suspended sentence: six oaths — by those sent forth, by the storming tempests, by the spreaders, by the discerners, by the deliverers of the Reminder, and by the double errand of excuse and warning — and only now, in the seventh verse, does the answer arrive: *inna mā tūʿadūna la-wāqiʿ* — "surely what you are promised will befall." In Arabic rhetoric this is the *jawāb al-qasam*, the apodosis of the oath, and the Qur'an has held it in suspense across six verses with a deliberateness that any listener feels: the oaths mount like waves, and the sentence of certainty breaks at last upon the shore. The grammatical structure of the verse is built for maximal emphasis. The particle *inna* announces an emphatic assertion; the verb-form construction *mā tūʿadūna* ("what you are promised," a passive — the promise is made to them, whether they acknowledge its Maker or not) names the content; and the *lām* prefixed to *la-wāqiʿ* is the emphatic lām that seals the whole as the strongest affirmation the language can make. In the idiom of the Qur'an, when God wishes to remove every residue of doubt from a claim, He swears to it, and when He wishes to make even the oath itself carry emphasis, He swears to what the oath is worth: here, the very existence of the couriers and the delivery of the Reminder is made the guarantee that the promise is real. Al-Ṭabarī explained that the verse is the answer to all the preceding oaths: through the angels who bring His messages, God has made known to human beings what will befall them on the Day of Judgment and in the Hereafter — and the verse declares that what He has made known will without fail come to pass.

**The Word That Falls: Wāqiʿ and the Weight of Certainty**

The word *wāqiʿ* is the active participle of the root *w-q-ʿ*, "to fall, to befall, to occur" — and the Qur'an's usage of this root is never casual. It is the root of *al-Wāqiʿah*, "the Befalling," the name of the sūrah that opens with the Hour itself: *"When the Befalling befalls — there is no denying its befalling"* (56:1–2). A thing that is *wāqiʿ* is not merely predicted or possible; it is already falling, already in motion toward its target, like a stone released from the hand — the release has happened, and only the moment of impact remains unknown. This is precisely the verse's intended psychological effect on its first hearers. The Makkan deniers spoke of the Resurrection as a remote or impossible event, a story of the ancients. The verse answers that the event is not remote but falling: it has been released by the same power that released the winds sworn to in the opening verses, and nothing in the universe has the capacity to intercept it. The Qur'an says the same thing in the sūrah of the Cattle: *"Surely that which you are promised is coming, and you cannot escape"* (6:134), and in the sūrah of the Winnowing Winds, whose oath-cluster closely parallels this one: *"By the winnowing winds... surely that which you are promised is true, and surely judgment is coming to pass"* (51:1, 5–6). The promise, in other words, is not one among many possibilities competing in the future; it is the fixed decree of the One who has already demonstrated — through every courier and every Reminder — that His word does not fail.

**A Promise, Not Merely a Threat**

The verse's word choice should be savored: it does not say "surely your punishment will befall" but "surely **what you are promised** will befall." The passive participle leaves the content of the promise open, and the commentators understood this openness as deliberate. What is promised to the deniers includes the punishment their denial earns; but the same promise embraces the Garden for the reverent, the help of God for the believers, and the vindication of the truth against falsehood. In the Qur'an's vocabulary, the "promise" (*waʿd*) of God is the master category of the future: *"God has promised those who believe and perform righteous deeds forgiveness and a great reward"* (5:9), and *"the promise of God is true"* (30:60). The verse thus stands as the sūrah's announcement of its own architecture: everything that follows — the destruction of the guilty of old, the creation of the human being, the Day of Division, the Fire and the Garden — is the unfolding of a single promise made by the Sender to the sent, and the sūrah will alternate between the two faces of that promise exactly as verse 6 alternated between excuse and warning. The believer and the denier recite the same verse in the same sūrah, but they stand on opposite sides of its meaning: for the one it is the guarantee of the reward, for the other the certainty of the reckoning.

**When the Promised Day Seems Late**

Every generation of deniers has drawn confidence from the apparent delay of the promised event, and the Qur'an records their mockery: *"They say: 'When will this promise come, if you are truthful?'"* (67:25). The verse's answer is that delay is not cancellation — the falling stone is no less falling while it is still in the air. The Qur'an teaches that the interval between the release of the promise and its impact is itself a mercy, a span of grace in which the warning of verse 6 can still do its work of turning hearts: *"And God does not grant any soul respite once its term has come; and God is Aware of what you do"* (63:11), and the sūrah of Ibrāhīm gives the reason for the delay: *"God grants respite to no soul when its term arrives"* (63:11, echoing 14:42–43: "Do not suppose that God is heedless of what the wrongdoers do; He only grants them respite until a Day when eyes will stare"). The classical commentators on this verse pointed out the same logic that the sūrah itself will deploy in verse 16–19: the peoples of old were promised, then destroyed when the promise fell due; the Quraysh stood in the same line of promise and would meet the same appointment. The certainty that the verse asserts is not a certainty of date but a certainty of fact — and the sūrah deliberately refuses to give the date, because the promise's power over the soul depends on its being always near: *"The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split"* (54:1).

**The Only Rational Posture Before a Certain Event**

There is a quiet logical challenge in this verse to every listener, and it is worth making explicit because it is the verse's whole point. Human beings live their ordinary lives by promises: the farmer plants because he trusts the promise of the seasons, the merchant extends credit because he trusts the promise of payment, the traveler boards the ship because he trusts the promise of arrival. In each case the trusted promiser has demonstrated reliability, and the trust is called wisdom. The verse invites its hearers to apply the same standard to the greatest promise they have ever received: the promise of the One whose couriers fill the sky, whose Reminder has been delivered with an excuse and a warning, whose past promises to the prophets were fulfilled in the destruction of the guilty and the deliverance of the righteous. To trust the promises of created things while dismissing the promise of the Creator is, on this showing, not skepticism but the most radical inconsistency — a refusal to extend to the most reliable Promiser the confidence routinely extended to the least reliable promisers. The believer who recites "surely what you are promised will befall" is therefore not reciting a wish but registering an inference: the Sender has sent; the couriers have come; the word has been delivered; the promise is falling. The only question that remains — the question the sūrah will spend its remaining verses pressing — is which side of the promise the hearer intends to meet when it lands.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:8

> **So when the stars are blotted out,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn From Oath to Apocalypse**

With verse 8 the sūrah pivots. The oaths are complete; the promise has been affirmed; and now the text does what the Makkan sūrahs do at the climax of their persuasion: it paints the Hour itself. The particle *fa-idhā* — "so when" — opens a sequence of apocalyptic scenes that will run through verse 13, each introduced by the same drumbeat of *idhā*, "when": when the stars are blotted out, when the sky is riven asunder, when the mountains are scattered, when the messengers are slated, for what day are they appointed? The construction is the Qur'an's standard apocalyptic frame — the same frame as the great overture of Sūrah al-Takwīr: *"When the sun is shrouded, when the stars fall, dispersing, when the mountains are set in motion..."* (81:1–3) — and its rhetorical effect is carefully engineered. The *idhā* clauses do not say *if*; they say *when*, converting the entire scene from hypothesis to schedule. The listener is not invited to imagine a possibility but to witness a timetable: these things are not predicted, they are described, as though the Qur'an were reporting events already fixed in the divine decree. And because each scene is painted in the simplest physical terms — light going out, the canopy tearing, the mountains blowing away — the description lands with the force of the familiar: the end of the world is described as the end of the things every listener sees every day.

**The Blotting Out of the Lamps**

The first sign is the extinguishing of the stars: *al-nujūmu ṭumisat* — "the stars are blotted out." The verb *ṭumisat* is from the root *ṭ-m-s*, whose sense is effacement, obliteration, the erasing of a mark or the blinding of an eye — the Qur'an uses the same root for the obliteration of sight visited upon those who refuse to see the truth: *"And We set a barrier before them and a barrier behind them, and We covered them (fa-ṭamasnā), so they do not see"* (36:9). The choice of verb is precise: the stars are not said to fall or to be thrown down; they are said to be *effaced*, as a lamp is snuffed, as writing is erased, as a face is smoothed until no feature remains. The Qur'an describes the stars elsewhere as the lamps of the lowest heaven — *"We adorned the lowest heaven with lamps"* (67:5) — and as the signs by which travelers navigate the night: *"And He it is Who made the stars for you, that you might be guided by them in the darkness of land and sea"* (6:97). Both functions make the blotting a catastrophe of orientation: the lamps of the sky are the oldest clock and compass of the human race, and their extinction is the end of every journey by starlight, the night without navigation, the moment when the heavens themselves can no longer be read.

**Light That Was Borrowed Returns to Its Giver**

Behind the image lies a theological claim of the first importance, which the commentators drew out when they read this verse beside its sisters. The lights of the heaven are not self-sustaining; they shine because their Maker keeps them shining, and the same command that lit them can extinguish them. Sūrah al-Takwīr swears to the same scene with different words: *"When the sun is shrouded, and when the stars fall, dispersing"* (81:1–2); Sūrah al-Infiṭār adds: *"And when the planets are scattered"* (82:2); Sūrah al-Qiyāmah: *"And the moon is eclipsed, and the sun and the moon are joined"* (75:8–9). The Qur'an is consistent: the cosmos is not God; it is His artifact, and artifacts can be dismantled by their Maker. The modern reader, who knows that stars are in fact burning out across the universe by the ordinary physics of their fuel, can appreciate the image from yet another angle: the Qur'an's claim is that the entire visible order — even the most luminous and seemingly eternal objects in the human sky — is contingent, finite, and dependent, held in being at every moment by a will that owes no explanation to its creatures. The extinction of the stars is the most dramatic way imaginable of saying that the universe is a creature, and that the hour of its service as a stage is appointed.

**Why the Scene Begins With Navigation's End**

There is a reason the Qur'an's apocalyptic sequences so often begin with the darkening of the heavens rather than with some purely moral or inward sign. Human beings locate themselves — physically and morally — by reference points above them. The stars are the original symbols of permanence: generation after generation looked up and saw the same constellations, and from their constancy drew the sense that the world itself was stable and would continue as it was. The denial of the Resurrection rested, in the Makkan mind, on exactly this felt permanence: the world has always gone on, the dead stay dead, the order of the sky is unbroken — so how can the Hour come? The sūrah answers at the level of the feeling itself: the first thing the Day does is break the most ancient symbol of permanence. When the reference points of the sky go dark, the argument from permanence collapses with them. The point is psychological as much as cosmological: the denial of the Hereafter has always fed on the apparent eternity of the present order, and the Qur'an's apocalyptic imagery is engineered to break that spell — to make the listener feel the contingency of everything he has mistaken for eternal. The believer who recites this verse is asked to hold the stars loosely, to love the light as a gift rather than to trust it as a god, and to place his ultimate confidence only in the One who can blot out the heavens and still keep His promise.

**Darkness as the Backdrop of the Real Light**

Yet the verse's darkness is not the last word of the sūrah — and the structure of the chapter teaches the reader not to stop at the terror. The stars are blotted out, the sky is riven, the mountains are scattered, all in the service of a Day that is, for the reverent, not a catastrophe but an arrival: the Day of Division when the faithful enter "shade and springs" (77:41) and the promise of verse 7 lands as mercy. The classical commentators noted that the very imagery that terrifies the denier consoles the believer: the dissolving of the world is the clearing of the stage for the world that remains — *"On the Day the earth is changed to other than the earth, and the heavens, and they come forth before God, the One, the Paramount"* (14:48). The believer whose heart is set on the Garden can watch the stars go out without despair, because his light was never in the stars; he can see the universe folded like a garment (21:104) without losing his home, because his home is with the One who folds it. The verse is thus a test of orientation disguised as a description of weather: when the night sky empties, where does the heart look for its bearings? The denier looks around him and finds nothing; the believer looks up through the emptied sky and finds — for the first time without veil — the Lord of the stars.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:9

> **and when the sky is riven asunder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Canopy That Was Never a Ceiling**

The second scene of the apocalypse: *wa-idhā al-samāʾu furijat* — "and when the sky is riven asunder." The verb *furijat* is from the root *f-r-j*, to split, cleave, or open — the Qur'anic word for the sky being torn open, and the root from which comes *farj*, an opening or gap. The Qur'an asks its hearers to consider what the sky has always seemed to be: a roof stretched over the earth, the most reliable ceiling in human experience, the one thing no hand can tear and no age can wear out. The Qur'an itself describes the sky's service in exactly those terms: *"He it is Who made the earth a bed for you and the sky a canopy"* (2:22); *"We made the sky a preserved roof"* (21:32); *"Did We not make the earth a resting place, and the mountains as pegs?"* (78:6–7). The sky is the roof of the human house, and the verse announces that the roof is opened. The Day of Division begins, in the Qur'an's consistent testimony, with the tearing of the heavens: *"The heaven is split asunder thereby; God's promise is ever fulfilled"* (73:18); *"When the heaven is split asunder and turns rosy like red hide"* (55:37); *"When the heaven is rent asunder, hearkening to its Lord — and it is right that it should"* (84:1–2, 5); *"And the heaven is opened and becomes gates"* (78:19). Every one of these verses carries the same implication: the sky's stability was never an independent fact of nature; it was an act of divine preservation, and the Day when preservation is withdrawn will find the sky doing what every servant of God does when its Lord commands — it splits.

**The Rent Sky and the Opening of What Was Hidden**

The commentators read the riven sky in two complementary directions. The first is cosmological: the dissolution of the heavens at the end of time, when the ordered universe of the present world gives way to the new order of the Hereafter. The Qur'an piles image upon image to convey the same event from different angles: the sky "split apart with clouds" on the Day the angels descend — *"On the Day when the heaven is split apart with clouds, and the angels are sent down in descent"* (25:25); the sky grows "frail" — *"And the heaven will split asunder, for it will be frail that Day, and the angels will be at its edges"* (69:16–17); and on the greatest scale of all, the heavens are not merely torn but folded — *"On the Day when We roll up the heaven like the rolling up of scrolls for writings; as We began the first creation, We shall repeat it — a promise binding upon Us. Truly We shall do it"* (21:104). The second direction is moral and revelatory: what the sky conceals, its tearing reveals. The heavens are the veil between the visible world and the unseen — the Throne, the angels, the Preserved Tablet, the very machinery of the divine government of creation. When the veil is rent, the unseen becomes seen: the deniers who spent their lives saying "we do not believe in what we cannot see" will see everything at once — the very sight that will be too late to help them. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators after him noted the justice of the image: in this world the unseen is hidden so that faith may be chosen freely; on that Day the hiding ends because the time of choosing has ended. The sky is torn not as a punishment but as a disclosure — the removal of the last curtain between the creature and the throne of his Creator.

**Why the Roof Must Open for the Verdict to Fall**

There is an inner logic in the Qur'an's insistence that the sky must be riven before the judgment can occur, and it is a logic of moral geography. The present world is a world of veils: the believer and the denier live side by side, indistinguishable from the outside, each carrying his secret — the one his hidden piety, the other his hidden hostility — and the justice of the Hereafter requires that the hidden be made public. The Qur'an describes the Day as the day when secrets are examined: *"On the Day when the secrets are tested"* (86:9); *"He knows the hidden and what is even more hidden"* (20:7). The tearing of the sky is the tearing of the veil of privacy itself: the stage of the world, with its curtains drawn, is replaced by the plain of assembly under an opened heaven, where nothing remains concealed. The classical scholars expressed this by saying that the *ghayb* (the unseen) becomes *shahādah* (the witnessed) on that Day — the entire content of faith becomes the content of sight. For the believer this is the joy of the vision of the Lord promised in the Qur'an: *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). For the denier it is the terror of irrefutable evidence arriving after the closing of the case. The same torn sky is heaven's window for the one and the exposure of the other — which is why the sūrah can follow the scene of the riven heaven with its refrain of woe.

**The Roof That Hears and Obeys: A Lesson in Creaturely Submission**

The sūrah of the Splitting (al-Inshiqāq) adds a detail that casts light back on this verse: *"When the heaven is rent asunder and hearkens to its Lord — and it is right that it should"* (84:1–2). The sky does not split in rebellion or by accident; it "hearkens to its Lord" — it obeys, as it has always obeyed. The Qur'an teaches that the heavens and the earth were addressed by God and answered in submission: *"Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke, and said to it and to the earth: 'Come, willingly or unwillingly.' They said: 'We come willingly'"* (41:11). The heavens, the stars, the mountains, and the trees all bow to their Lord (55:6); only a portion of humanity refuses the submission that the entire universe performs. The tearing of the sky is therefore not a malfunction but the most perfect obedience: the roof that sheltered the drama of human choice is commanded to open, and it opens. The verse carries a quiet rebuke to the deniers of the sūrah, who will be commanded to "bow" in verse 48 and will refuse: the sky bows when its Lord commands it to be torn; the human being will refuse when his Lord commands him to bend. The cosmos obeys the command that ends it; the rebel cannot obey the command that would save him. Between the obedient sky and the defiant denier, the verse leaves no question about which is the wiser creature.

**Living Under an Openable Sky**

For the believer in ordinary life, this verse performs a steadying service against the idolatry of permanence. Every generation has its own version of the unshakeable sky: institutions that will never fall, economies that will never fail, habits of the world that will never change, a status quo that presents itself as eternal. The verse declares that the most permanent-looking thing in human experience — the very vault of heaven — is on notice, and that the God who holds it open today can fold it tomorrow. The practical fruit of this belief is not despair but proportion: the believer who knows the sky is openable cannot mistake the present order for the final one, cannot sell his conscience for a place in an order that is scheduled for dissolution, and cannot treat the goods of a passing world as though they were the goods of the world that remains. The classical spiritual writers loved to say that the believer lives in the world as a traveler who sleeps under a borrowed roof: grateful for its shelter, mindful of its owner, and ready at any moment for the call that will fold the tent and continue the journey — *"The life of this world is but the enjoyment of delusion"* (3:185). When the roof opens, the traveler does not mourn the tent; he looks up — as this sūrah's reverent will look up into "shade and springs" that no sky can be riven to destroy.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:10

> **when the mountains are scattered,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dismantling of the Earth's Anchors**

The third scene of the Day: *wa-idhā al-jibālu nusifat* — "when the mountains are scattered." The verb *nusifat* is from the root *n-s-f*, whose sense is to blow away, to winnow, to disperse a thing until nothing remains of its former shape — the word used for the scattering of dust and chaff by the wind. The choice of verb is the point: the mountains are not said to melt or sink but to be *winnowed*, treated as the chaff is treated on the threshing floor, lifted and carried until no mass remains. The Qur'an's other descriptions of the mountains' end multiply the image from every side: they become like carded wool — *"the mountains become like tufts of colored wool"* (101:5), *"like carded wool"* (70:9); they become heaps of shifting sand — *"the mountains become heaps of shifting sand"* (73:14); they are set in motion and become a mirage — *"the mountains are set in motion and become a mirage"* (78:20); they crumble to dust — *"the mountains crumble into dust"* (56:5–6); they slip away — *"the mountains slip away"* (52:10); they are lifted and crushed in a single crash — *"the earth and the mountains are lifted up and crushed with a single crushing"* (69:14); and the mountains that seemed fixed pass like clouds — *"you see the mountains and suppose them fixed, yet they pass as the clouds pass — the handiwork of God, Who perfected all things"* (27:88). The consistency of the Qur'an's testimony is striking: whatever the image, the mountains — the most massive, stable, immovable objects in human experience — are scheduled for total dissolution.

**The Pegs That Held the Earth**

To understand what the scattering means, one must recall what the Qur'an says the mountains do in the present order of the world. They are the earth's stabilizers and its water-towers: *"He cast onto the earth firm mountains, lest it shake with you, and rivers and paths, that haply you may be guided"* (16:15); *"And We placed upon the earth firm mountains, lest it shake beneath them"* (21:31); *"He created the heavens without pillars you can see, and cast onto the earth firm mountains, lest it shake with you"* (31:10); *"And the mountains as pegs"* (78:7); and later in this very sūrah, verse 27: "And We placed therein firm, soaring mountains and gave you sweet water to drink." Modern geology has its own account of mountains — the Qur'anic image of pegs holding the crust, which early Western readers found puzzling, has been compared by later writers to the role of deep mountain roots in isostatic balance — but the Qur'an's purpose is not geological theory; it is theological meaning. The mountains are the earth's ballast, the visible guarantee that the ground under human feet will stay still, and their scattering announces that the guarantee is withdrawn: the stage of the world, no longer needed for the drama of choice, is dismantled. The same mountains that held the earth steady for the living are winnowed on the Day the dead are raised — the earth no longer needs pegs when it is no longer a house of trial.

**The Most Solid Thing as the Measure of the Hour**

The rhetorical strategy of the verse is to choose the strongest possible example of permanence and annihilate it. Every human argument for the impossibility of the Resurrection rested, in the end, on the perceived permanence of the world: the mountains had stood since the beginning of memory, and the order they anchored had never been disturbed. The Qur'an's apocalyptic method is to attack the argument at its strongest point: if the mountains — the very symbol of "as permanent as a mountain" — are winnowed like chaff, then the permanence on which denial relied was never permanence at all; it was a loan from a Power that can recall it at any instant. The Makkan disbelievers are quoted in another sūrah as daring the Resurrection with precisely this imagery in mind: *"And they say: 'What! When we are lost in the earth, shall we indeed be in a new creation?'"* (32:10) — as though being "lost in the earth" were the end of every trail. The Qur'an's answer is that the earth itself — the great grave of all generations — is not a permanent container; on the Day, it disgorges what it holds: *"the earth casts forth its burdens"* (84:4), *"and when the earth is stretched out, and casts forth what is within it and empties itself"* (84:3–4). The mountain that denies the possibility of resurrection is the same mountain scheduled for scattering; the grave that seems to swallow the dead forever is the same grave scheduled for emptying. The most solid thing in the world is made the witness that nothing solid but God endures.

**A Parable of Pride and Its Leveling**

The commentators also read the scattering of the mountains as a moral image addressed to the human love of grandeur. The Qur'an repeatedly uses the mountain as the figure of the proud — Pharaoh in his towering presumption, the arrogant who "walk upon the earth exultantly" (17:37), those who love to be seen as immovable in their power. The Day of Division levels all of it: the mountains are not merely lowered but removed to nothing, and the plain of judgment is described in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā as perfectly flat: *"He leaves it a level plain, with no curve in it and no rise"* (20:106–107). Human power, like the mountains, presents itself as permanent — dynasties that will never fall, reputations that will never tarnish, structures of dominance that will never be leveled. The scattering of the mountains is the divine announcement that every elevation is temporary: *"We caused the earth to swallow him and his house, and there was no host to help him against God, nor was he among those who could defend themselves"* (28:81). The believer who recites this verse is taught to carry his own eminence lightly, whatever form it takes — wealth, rank, talent, achievement — because the Day that winnows the mountains will not spare the smaller elevations that human vanity builds upon them.

**Between the Scattered Mountains and the Firm Promise**

Finally, the verse belongs to the sūrah's argument in a strictly logical way. Verses 8–10 describe the dissolution of the heavens and the earth — stars blotted out, the sky riven, mountains scattered — and the sequence is not a random collection of terrors but a dismantling in descending order: first the lights above, then the roof, then the floor. Everything that seemed permanent is removed; the visible universe is emptied of every reference point; and only then do the verses ask the question that gives the whole scene its meaning: the messengers are slated — for what day is it appointed? (vv. 11–12) — and the answer comes: for the Day of Division (v. 13). The apocalypse is not an end in itself; it is the demolition of the stage so that the real event can occur — the event for which the messengers were sent and the deniers were warned. The believer's response to the scattering of the mountains is therefore not the despair of one who has lost his world but the confidence of one whose world was never in the mountains: *"Those who were given knowledge beforehand fall upon their faces in prostration"* (17:107) when the truth of the Hour is recited. The mountains scatter; the promise stands; the Day comes; and the sūrah — having dismantled the sky and the earth — will now gather the messengers and ask the question toward which all its oaths have been driving: for what day is all of this appointed?

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:11

> **and when the messengers are slated,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gathering of the Envoys at the End of the Errand**

The fourth scene of the apocalypse marks a startling shift of register: after the stars, the sky, and the mountains — the great objects of the physical universe — the verse turns to *al-rusul*, the messengers: *wa-idhā al-rusulu uqqitat* — "and when the messengers are slated." The verb *uqqitat* is from the root *w-q-t*, "time," and the passive form means "was appointed a time, was fixed to a moment, was scheduled." The classical lexicographers and commentators explained the verb's sense here as the gathering of a thing to its appointed time: the messengers are *uqqitat* — brought to the time fixed for them, assembled at the moment appointed for their witness. Some of the early scholars, including the reading recorded by Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī, understood the word in the additional sense of "postponed" — derived from the same root through the idea of deferring a matter to its time — indicating that God does not punish immediately but grants His creatures respite until the appointed term; while still others read it as "promised," the messengers being those to whom the promise of the Hour was made and for whom the Hour was promised. All the readings converge on a single scene: the messengers, who were sent through the ages of the world "in succession" (as the sūrah's first verse swore), are at the end of the world gathered to their appointment — the one event in all of history that every messenger announced, for which every messenger prepared his people, and before which every messenger will at last stand.

**Why the Messengers Must Be Present at the Judgment**

The presence of the messengers at the Day of Division is one of the Qur'an's most carefully repeated teachings, and it serves several distinct purposes at once. First, the messengers are gathered as *witnesses* — witnesses both for and against their communities. The Qur'an describes the scene: *"And the earth shines with the Light of its Lord, and the Book is set in place, and the prophets and the witnesses are brought"* (39:69). Each messenger will testify that he delivered the message entrusted to him — the *ʿudhr* and *nudhr* of verse 6 — and each community will be confronted with the testimony of the messenger sent to it: *"And on the Day when We raise up in every community a witness over them from among themselves"* (16:89); *"How will it be when We bring a witness from every community, and bring you as a witness against these?"* (4:41). The justice of the Day requires this: no one is condemned on the word of an unseen judge alone; the very messengers whom the deniers rejected in this world are produced as the living records of what was delivered and what was refused. Second, the gathering of the messengers is the vindication of the messengers themselves: the men who were mocked, driven out, and killed by their peoples are raised to the highest station of honor on the very Day their peoples are gathered for judgment — *"God chose Adam, Noah, the house of Abraham, and the house of ʿImrān above the worlds"* (3:33), and the Qur'an promises the messengers the victory that was withheld in their lifetimes: *"God has written: I shall prevail, I and My messengers"* (58:21).

**The Appointment That Every Messenger Kept**

There is a profoundly moving dimension to this verse when it is read against the biography of the prophets as the Qur'an tells it. Every messenger lived under the shadow of an appointment he never saw fulfilled in his lifetime. Noah preached for nearly a thousand years and saw only a handful believe; Abraham was cast into the fire and commanded to leave his son in an empty valley; Moses stood before Pharaoh and was rejected for decades; Muhammad ﷺ himself spent thirteen years in Makkah calling a people who answered with persecution, and when he was asked to curse them he refused, saying he was sent as a mercy, not a curser. The verse announces that all of this deferred justice is gathered to a single appointment: the messengers are *uqqitat* — scheduled, as every prophet knew himself to be scheduled. The word itself is a comfort: the messengers were never abandoned to the verdict of their own ages; they were simply assigned to a later session of the same court. When the sūrah's opening swore "by those sent forth in succession," it described the long relay of the messengers through history; this verse closes the relay — the couriers have all arrived, the last runner has handed over the message, and the whole company stands gathered at the time appointed for the reading of the results.

**The Messengers' Presence as the Deniers' Final Argument**

For the deniers of this sūrah, the presence of the messengers at the Day carries a special sting, and the commentators did not fail to draw it out. In this world, the deniers' case against the messengers seemed plausible: the messengers were mortal men, often poor, often without armies or miracles at hand, and their threats of a coming Day could be dismissed as the ravings of men who had failed in the ordinary contests of life. On the Day, the deniers will see the messengers gathered in honor at the very session where their own accounts are read — and the Qur'an records the deniers' own confession at that sight: *"The guilty will be recognized by their marks, and seized by their forelocks and their feet"* (55:41), and their lament: *"Our Lord, we obeyed our leaders and our elders, and they led us astray from the way"* (33:67). The gathering of the messengers is the visible proof that the messengers were right: the One who sent them kept His word to them, raised them to the station He promised, and convened the Day they announced. The verse is thus the hinge between the cosmic scenes that precede it and the question that follows it: the stars are blotted out, the sky is riven, the mountains are scattered — the physical world is dismantled because its service is over — and the messengers are slated, because the moral world is about to begin. The appointment of the couriers is the signal that the Sender is now receiving His couriers' reports.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:12

> **for what day is it appointed?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Question That Suspends the Answer**

The apocalyptic sequence reaches its summit in a question: *li-ayyi yawmin ujjilat* — "for what day is it appointed?" The verb *ujjilat* returns to the same root as the previous verse's *uqqitat* — *w-q-t*, time, appointment, schedule — so that verse 11 and verse 12 speak the same language: the messengers are "slated," and the question demands to know for what day this slating has been scheduled. The question is rhetorical in form but cosmic in function; the Qur'an does not ask it because the answer is unknown, but because the hearers must be made to feel the weight of the answer before it is pronounced. The commentators noted the dramatic architecture: the scene has been building since verse 8 — the stars blotted out, the sky riven, the mountains scattered, the messengers gathered — and now, at the peak of the crescendo, the sūrah halts all motion and asks the question that gives every preceding scene its meaning. It is the technique of the orator who describes the assembling of a court — the lights dimmed, the hall filled, the judges seated — and then pauses to ask: for what trial is all of this prepared? The pause forces the listener to supply the answer from his own conscience before the sūrah supplies it from revelation, and the double suspense — the deferred answer of the oath in verse 7 and now the deferred answer of the question in verse 12 — keeps the reader leaning forward into the text.

**A Day Beyond Every Calendar**

The question's second function is to detach the Day from every human system of measurement. The Qur'an refuses, throughout its pages, to answer the question of the Hour's date: *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its anchorage? Say: 'Knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none shall unveil it at its time but He. It weighs heavy upon the heavens and the earth; it comes upon you not but suddenly'"* (7:187); *"They ask you about the Hour, when it will come to pass. Say: 'Knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none but He shall unveil it at its time'"* (79:42–44). The question "for what day is it appointed?" is the Qur'an's way of saying that the Day belongs to a calendar that no human hand holds: it is scheduled (*ujjilat*) — fixed, written, appointed — but the schedule is in the keeping of the One who made time itself. The verse thus strikes two notes at once. The first is certainty: the Day is not an accident or a possibility; it is an appointment on a schedule that cannot be changed, and every messenger of God was slated for it. The second is hiddenness: the schedule is not published, and its secrecy is itself a mercy, because a known date would destroy the very moral life the Day is meant to judge — the faithful would wait until the eve of the Hour to repent, and the wicked would sin without restraint until the eve of the Hour to be caught. An announced date would make the world uninhabitable by moral freedom; an unannounced date keeps every generation standing in the posture of the traveler who does not know when the journey's last stage will end.

**The Weight of the Day in the Divine Balance**

The verse's question also carries an emotional charge that the Qur'an places in the mouths of creation itself. When the Day is described in another sūrah, the heavens and the earth feel its gravity: *"They ask you about the Hour, when it will come to pass. Say: 'The knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none shall unveil it at its time but He. It weighs heavy upon the heavens and the earth; it comes not upon you but suddenly'"* (7:187). The Day is heavy even for the angels who serve it: *"The angels stand arrayed in rows on its edges, and eight bear the Throne of your Lord above them on that Day"* (69:17). And the Qur'an gives the measure of its scale in a verse whose imagery this sūrah's question echoes: *"And they do not measure God with His true measure; the whole earth shall be His handful on the Day of Resurrection, and the heavens folded in His right hand. Glory be to Him, exalted above what they associate"* (39:67). The question "for what day is it appointed?" is the Qur'an's invitation to measure the Day by its true measure: not by the small arithmetic of human calendars, but by the scale of the One who folds the heavens — a Day so great that the messengers of God are gathered to it, a Day so grave that the entire physical universe is dismantled to make room for it.

**The Answer Already Known, Yet Still Asking**

The rhetorical question is answered in the very next verse — "For the Day of Division" (77:13) — but the sūrah's placement of the question is itself a lesson in how the Qur'an teaches. The answer comes only after the question has done its work in the listener: the conscience that has been following the oaths of verses 1–7 and the scenes of verses 8–11 is now forced to name the Day for itself, and every listener names it differently according to his state. The believer names it with longing — the Day he has awaited, the Day of meeting his Lord, the Day of the Garden promised to the reverent. The denier names it with dread — the Day he has spent his life refusing to name, the appointment he hoped was fiction. The question is thus a mirror: the same words, "for what day is it appointed?", produce opposite answers in opposite hearts, and the sūrah will spend its remaining verses drawing out precisely that division — the Day of Division in which the question itself will be the boundary. Every recitation of this verse is a small rehearsal of the Day: the reciter is asked, in the privacy of his own conscience, to say what he believes the Day is — and the answer he gives today is the answer he will meet when the messengers are gathered and the question is asked in the full light of the opened heaven.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:13

> **For the Day of Division.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name That Names the Verdict**

The question of verse 12 receives its answer in the briefest and most absolute form the Arabic language allows: *li-yawmi al-faṣl* — "for the Day of Division." The word *al-faṣl* is from the root *f-ṣ-l*, whose sense is decisive separation — the cutting of a thing into its parts, the settling of a dispute by a definitive ruling, the separation of one thing from another such that they do not mingle again. A *faṣl* is both a division and a verdict: the judge who *faṣala* between two claimants does not merely sort them; he ends their contest with a decision from which there is no appeal. The Qur'an uses the word in both registers: the final ruling between the prophets and their peoples is the *faṣl* — *"Our Lord, decide between us and our people in truth, for You are the best of deciders"* (7:89) — and the Day itself is named *yawm al-faṣl*, the Day of Decision and Division, in the sūrah of the Ranks: *"This is the Day of Division, which you used to deny"* (37:21); in the sūrah of Smoke: *"Truly the Day of Division is the appointed time of all of them"* (44:40); and later in this very sūrah: *"This is the Day of Division; We have gathered you and those of old"* (77:38). Al-Zamakhsharī and the commentators after him explained the name with beautiful precision: it is called the Day of Division because on it the believers are divided definitively from the disbelievers, the reverent from the deniers, the people of the Garden from the people of the Fire — a division not of this world's temporary sort, which mixes the two parties in the same streets and the same graves, but the final division, after which the two companies will never meet again.

**The Separation That This World Cannot Perform**

The Qur'an's insistence on the name "Day of Division" answers one of the deepest problems of human experience: the intolerable mixture of good and evil in the present world. Every age has felt the scandal of that mixture — the prophet and the tyrant breathing the same air, the martyr and the murderer buried in the same earth, the sincere believer and the hypocrite praying in the same row. The Qur'an does not deny the mixture; it announces its term: *"God would not leave the believers in the state you are in, until He separates the wicked from the good"* (3:179); and the sūrah of the Spoils says plainly: *"That God may separate the wicked from the good"* (8:37). The Day of Division is the day the long mixture ends — the tares and the wheat, which grew together in the same field through the whole season of the world, are at harvest divided; the Qur'an tells of the two seas that do not mix (55:19–20) and of the field whose harvest separates the crop from the chaff. The present world is the field of mixture precisely because it is the world of choice: if good and evil were visibly separated now, no one would choose freely — the tyrant would be restrained by fear and the believer would be drawn by spectacle, and the moral quality of either choice would be destroyed. The world must be a mixture so that choice can be real; and because choice is real, a Day of final separation must come, so that justice can be real as well.

**The Two Companies on Either Side of the Line**

The sūrah will spend its remaining verses drawing the two sides of the Division, and its refrain marks the boundary at every step. On one side stand the deniers — those who heard the Reminder and refused it, who are driven to the Fire whose shadow gives no shade (vv. 29–31), whose speech is silenced (vv. 35–36), who are gathered with "those of old" who denied before them (v. 38), who "eat and enjoy themselves a little" before their guilt overtakes them (v. 46), and who refuse the command to bow (v. 48). On the other side stand the reverent — those who guarded their covenant with God, who are amid shade and springs (v. 41), who eat fruits such as they desire (v. 42), who are addressed with the greeting of welcome: "Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you used to do" (v. 43). Between the two companies runs the *faṣl* of the Day — and the Qur'an elsewhere describes the very wall of division: *"And between them is a veil, and on the Ramparts are men who know each by his mark"* (7:46). The classical commentators on this verse noted that the Day is called the Day of Division precisely because on it the most intimate associations of this world dissolve: the believer and his disbelieving family member part company forever (66:10 gives the example of the wives of Noah and Lot), the follower and the followed part (the leaders disown their followers on that Day, 2:166–167), and every soul stands alone before its Lord: *"And each of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone"* (19:95).

**Division as the Completion of Mercy and Justice**

There is a subtle point that the commentators drew from the pairing of this name with the rest of the sūrah: the Day of Division is not a cruelty appended to the world but the completion of the world's own logic. The Qur'an teaches that the ultimate end of the reverent is not merely survival but *separation from evil* — the Garden is described as a place from which all falsehood and all harm have been removed: *"They hear therein no idle talk, nor any cause of sin; only the saying: Peace, peace"* (56:25–26). The blessed could not be blessed if the deniers who tormented them in this world were eternally mingled with them; the peace of the Garden requires the division of the Day. Likewise the punishment of the Fire requires division: the deniers could not taste the full consequence of their denial if the believers they scorned shared their company. The *faṣl* is therefore not an arbitrary decree but the necessary consequence of two eternally incompatible ways of being: the way that oriented itself toward God and the way that oriented itself against Him, having become, by their own long choices, as unmixable as light and darkness. The Day of Division merely publishes the division that every soul has been making, day by day, throughout its life: every choice of truth over falsehood was a small division already performed; every act of repentance was a crossing of the line; every refusal of the Reminder was a taking of one's place. The Day of Division is the day the line, so long hidden, is drawn openly — and the sūrah's refrain, "Woe that Day to the deniers!", is the first stroke of the line as it is drawn.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:14

> **And what shall apprise thee of the Day of Division?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Measures the Limits of Human Knowledge**

No sooner has the sūrah named the Day than it steps back and asks the listener to consider the size of what has been named: *wa-mā adrāka mā yawmu al-faṣl* — "And what shall apprise thee of the Day of Division?" The construction *mā adrāka* — literally "what will make you know?" — is one of the Qur'an's most distinctive rhetorical devices. It appears again and again in the Qur'an, always at the threshold of something whose magnitude exceeds ordinary comprehension: *"And what shall apprise thee of the Night of Power?"* (97:2); *"And what shall apprise thee of the Clatterer?"* (101:3); *"And what shall apprise thee of the Inevitable?"* (69:3); *"And what shall apprise thee of the steep pass?"* (90:12); *"And what shall apprise thee of what the piercing star is?"* (86:2). The phrase is a confession of limits framed as a question: it announces, before describing, that no description will exhaust the thing described. The listener is told, in effect: you have heard the name — the Day of Division — but the name is a door, and what stands behind it exceeds everything your experience has prepared you to imagine. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators noted that the question is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself — "thee" — as an acknowledgment that even the messenger who announces the Day has not been given the full measure of its reality; the knowledge of its terrors and its joys in their complete intensity is with God alone.

**The Rhetoric of the Unspeakable**

The purpose of the question is not to frustrate curiosity but to discipline the imagination. The Qur'an knows that the human mind, when confronted with the claim of a Day of absolute division, will attempt to domesticate it — to reduce it to a symbol, a myth, a projection of moral wishes, or a distant event whose terrors can be managed by the imagination. The phrase *mā adrāka* blocks every such reduction in advance. It says: whatever you are now picturing — whatever court you have imagined, whatever separation you have conceived, whatever reward and punishment you have let your mind arrange — is not it. The Day of Division is vaster than the mind that tries to picture it; the Qur'an even describes its scale in terms that strain the imagination on purpose: a Day whose length is fifty thousand years (70:4), a Day when the heavens are folded in the right hand of God (39:67), a Day when every soul finds every good and evil it has done present before it (3:30). The question functions like the warning given to a traveler approaching a mountain range from the plains: the peaks visible on the horizon are real, but they are not the mountains — the range continues beyond the horizon into regions the eye cannot reach. The Day of Division is real in the same way: everything the Qur'an goes on to describe of it is true, and yet the whole of it exceeds the describing.

**Why the Qur'an Insists on Inadequacy**

There is a deep pedagogical reason for the Qur'an's insistence that the Day exceeds description, and the classical scholars articulated it clearly. If the Day could be fully comprehended by the imagination, it could be managed by the imagination — and a managed Hereafter is no Hereafter at all. The deniers of Makkah treated the Resurrection as an idea that could be examined, weighed, and dismissed like any other theory; the Qur'an's answer is that the Resurrection is not a theory but an appointment, and that its reality exceeds the categories in which theories are judged. Moreover, the two audiences of the Day require two different relations to its mystery. For the denier, the inadequacy of description is a warning: the Fire is not a metaphor that can be argued away, not a figure of speech whose heat can be discounted; it is a reality vaster than the words that announce it — *"and what shall apprise thee of the Scorching Fire?"* (104:5). For the believer, the inadequacy of description is a promise: the joy of the Garden is not a projection that will disappoint, not an oasis that will prove to be a mirage; it exceeds every image the Qur'an could paint — *"No soul knows what comfort is hidden for it as a recompense for what it used to do"* (32:17). Both the terror and the bliss are under-described on purpose, so that neither can be exhausted, discounted, or domesticated by the mind that hears of them.

**The Humility That the Question Teaches**

The verse also teaches an intellectual posture that the believer carries into every engagement with the unseen: the posture of acknowledged finitude. *Mā adrāka* is the Qur'an's own admission — placed in the mouth of revelation itself — that the human knower stands before the realities of the Hereafter as a child stands before the ocean: able to see the waves at his feet, able to taste the water, able to know with certainty that the ocean exists and that it is vast — and utterly unable to hold it in his hand. The commentators drew from this a rule of method: when the Qur'an describes the Hereafter, one affirms the description as true without presuming to exhaust it; one believes in the Day of Division without pretending to have measured it; one recites the verses about the Fire and the Garden without the arrogance of the one who claims to know exactly how they will be. The verse is a standing rebuke to two errors at once: the error of the literalist who cramps the Day into the narrow dimensions of his own imagining, and the error of the allegorist who dissolves the Day into a symbol because his imagination cannot contain it. Both errors are answered by the same question: what shall apprise *thee*? — nothing in your experience, nothing in your categories, nothing short of the Day itself will fully apprise you of the Day of Division.

**A Question That Points Beyond Itself**

Finally, the question performs the most important service a question can perform: it points beyond itself. The sūrah does not leave the listener suspended in ignorance; having confessed that the Day exceeds all description, it immediately begins to describe — but now its descriptions carry a different authority. When, in the verses that follow, the sūrah speaks of the Fire's sparks "like massive tree trunks" (v. 32) and the Garden's "shade and springs" (v. 41), the listener who has heard "what shall apprise thee of the Day of Division?" knows how to receive the images: not as exhaustive reports but as true glimpses — revelation's accommodated language, reaching toward a reality that words can point to but cannot contain. The question thus becomes the lens through which the rest of the sūrah must be read: everything that follows is true, and everything that follows is less than the whole truth, and the gap between the telling and the reality is itself part of the message — a gap that will be closed only on the Day itself, when the deniers see what they denied and the reverent enter what they believed, and both discover that the question "what shall apprise thee?" was the Qur'an's way of saying that no one is apprised of the Day of Division until he stands in it.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:15

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain That Gives the Sūrah Its Pulse**

With verse 15 the sūrah sounds for the first time the phrase that will ring through it ten times: *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" The word *wayl* is an Arabic exclamation of calamity — a cry of ruin, a declaration that woe and destruction have come upon someone — and its use here is deliberative: the sūrah is not merely predicting that the deniers will suffer; it is pronouncing the woe over them, as a verdict is pronounced. The *mukadhdhibīn* are those who call false — from the root *k-dh-b*, to declare untrue, to brand as a liar — the intensive form naming those whose denial is a settled habit of the soul: the people who, confronted with the Reminder, the messengers, and the promise, answered "false" so often and so thoroughly that denial became their defining description. The phrase is the first of ten identical strokes, and its placement here is exact: it follows the naming of the Day of Division (v. 13) and the confession that the Day exceeds description (v. 14), and it pronounces over the deniers the consequence of the Day whose imminence the oaths and the apocalypse have established. The sūrah has spent fourteen verses building its case — the couriers sent, the Reminder delivered, the promise affirmed, the world dismantled, the messengers gathered, the Day named — and now, for the first time, the verdict falls: Woe that Day to the deniers.

**Why the Warning Is Repeated Ten Times**

The commentators devoted sustained attention to the fact that this phrase occurs ten times in a sūrah of fifty verses — a density of repetition unmatched in the Qur'an, whose only comparable refrain is the repeated "So which of your Lord's blessings do you two deny?" of Sūrah al-Rahmān. Al-Ṭabarī explained the repetition as deliberate rhetorical emphasis: the warning is struck again and again because the deniers' stubbornness requires an answer of matching insistence — the message is hammered because the audience is hard; each recurrence renews the threat for those who remained unmoved by the previous one, like a creditor who repeats the demand because the debtor will not pay, or a physician who repeats the warning because the patient will not stop the poison. Al-Qurṭubī and others added a second reading: each occurrence belongs to its own context and names a particular denial — the denial of the Resurrection, the denial of the lesson of the destroyed nations, the denial of the Creator's power, the denial of His blessings, the denial of the Fire, the denial of the Day, the denial of the gathering, the denial of the reward, the denial of the Giver of every enjoyment, and the denial of the command itself. On this reading, the tenfold refrain is a ten-count indictment, each count corresponding to the evidence immediately preceding it — and the sūrah's structure becomes a court proceeding in which each exhibit is followed by its verdict. Both readings are sound, and the sūrah itself invites both: the repetition is emphatic because the truth bears repeating, and contextual because each denial is distinct.

**The Woe That Follows the Fair Warning**

The moral force of the refrain depends entirely on what preceded it — and this first occurrence stands at the end of the sūrah's most sustained statement of fair warning. The deniers were not ambushed: the couriers were sent (v. 1), the Reminder was delivered to excuse or to warn (vv. 5–6), the promise was sworn (v. 7), the scene of the Day was painted (vv. 8–13), and only then was the woe pronounced. The *wayl* is thus not the cruelty of a trap but the justice of a summons: it falls upon those who were told, shown, warned, and reminded — and who denied. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same point in the starkest terms when it records the scene of the Fire: *"And the keepers of Hell call out: 'Did no warner come to you?' They say: 'Yes, a warner came to us, but we denied'"* (67:8–9). The refrain's power lies precisely in its fairness: the reader who has followed the sūrah from verse 1 cannot pretend that the deniers were denied knowledge; they were given the full apparatus of evidence, and they used it for denial. The woe is the logical conclusion of the warning refused.

**The Psychology of the Repeated Blow**

There is also a psychology of repetition that the sūrah exploits with deliberate artistry, and the commentators described it in terms of the effect upon the hearer. A single warning can be filed away; a repeated warning, returning at regular intervals through a text, creates an expectation that itself becomes a form of teaching. By the time the listener has heard the refrain a third and fourth time, he has begun to anticipate it — and the anticipation is the lesson: the soul that waits for the refrain is, for that moment, standing in the position of the soul that should wait for the Day. The reciter of the sūrah in prayer, returning to the phrase every few verses, experiences the text as a drumbeat that will not let the heart rest: no sooner has one scene of evidence ended than the woe strikes again, and the pattern imprints the connection — evidence, therefore woe; denial, therefore woe; blessing, therefore woe for those who deny the Giver. The refrain's repetition is the sūrah's way of making the Day of Division present: the listener does not merely hear about a Day; he feels, ten times in a single recitation, the rhythm of a verdict that falls and falls again until the heart either hardens against it completely or opens to it completely — which is, of course, precisely the division the Day itself will make final.

**The First of Ten Gates**

This first occurrence of the refrain stands at the close of the sūrah's overture and opens the sequence of ten. It will be followed by nine more, each after a fresh block of evidence: the destruction of the peoples of old (v. 19), the creation of the human being (v. 24), the provisions of the earth (v. 28), the horrors of the Fire (v. 34), the silence of the guilty (v. 37), the gathering of all generations (v. 40), the reward of the reverent (v. 45), the brief enjoyment of the guilty (v. 47), and the refusal to bow (v. 49). The first occurrence establishes the pattern and names the parties: there is a Day, and there are deniers, and between the Day and the deniers stands this word — *wayl*. Whoever hears the sūrah to its end will hear the word nine more times, but the first hearing is the one that frames all the rest: the deniers are not an abstract class of ancient peoples; they are whoever hears the Reminder and refuses it — and the sūrah's tenfold woe is spoken, as the commentators noted, so that every hearer may examine himself before the Day when the woe will be no longer a refrain to be recited but a sentence to be faced.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:16

> **Did We not destroy those of old,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The History Lesson Opens With a Question**

With verse 16 the sūrah turns from the future to the past — and the turn is deliberate, because the Qur'an's argument for the coming Day leans as heavily on history as on creation. The verse is a question: *a-lam nuhliki al-awwalīn* — "Did We not destroy those of old?" The opening *a-lam* is the interrogative of rebuke and reminder, the question that expects the answer "Yes, indeed" — the rhetorical form of a teacher who asks a question whose answer his students already know, so that the knowledge already lodged in their minds can be converted into evidence. The *al-awwalīn*, "those of old" or "the ancients," are the generations who preceded the Arabs of Makkah: the people of Noah, drowned; ʿĀd, destroyed by the shrieking wind; Thamūd, seized by the Cry; the people of Lot, whose overturned cities lay on the very trade route the Quraysh traveled; the people of Midian; Pharaoh and his hosts, drowned in the sea. The Qur'an returns to their story in sūrah after sūrah with the same question: *"Have they not seen how many a generation We destroyed before them, whom We had established in the earth as We have not established you, and on whom We poured rain in abundance, and made rivers flow beneath them? Then We destroyed them for their sins and brought forth after them another generation"* (6:6); *"How many a city We destroyed while it did wrong, leaving it fallen upon its roofs, and an abandoned well, and a lofty palace!"* (22:45). The question presumes that the listener knows the story — and that is precisely the point: the deniers' own knowledge of the ancients is turned into a witness against them.

**Ruins as Roadside Evidence**

The force of the question in its original setting can hardly be exaggerated, because the evidence it cites was not confined to books; it lay along the roads. The Quraysh were merchants, and their caravans passed twice a year through the very regions where the destroyed peoples had lived: the rock-cut dwellings of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr stood between Makkah and Syria, and the Qur'an points the travelers to them: *"And verily you pass by them in the morning, and by night — will you not reflect?"* (37:137–138). The ruins of the people of Lot were visible on the route to Syria; the desolation of ʿĀd's country was known to the southern caravans; even the name of Thamūd's city was a landmark. The question "Did We not destroy those of old?" therefore meant, in the original setting: look at the stones you pass on your own journeys, look at the empty dwellings whose builders denied their messengers, look at the wells abandoned and the palaces fallen — and answer whether you know of any people who denied the warning of God and survived. The destruction of the ancients was the most public fact available to the first audience of this sūrah: it was written in the landscape, and every caravan was a moving classroom in which the Qur'an's question could be checked against the roadside.

**Why Their End Proves Our Beginning's End**

The logical link between the destruction of the ancients and the promise of the Day of Division deserves to be made explicit, because the sūrah is building a chain. If God destroyed the guilty peoples of the past — after sending them messengers, after warning them, after giving them respite — then three things follow. First, God's word of threat is real: the punishment that the messengers announced to ʿĀd and Thamūd and Pharaoh actually fell, and no power of the destroyed could deflect it. Second, God's dealing with deniers is uniform: the same cause produces the same effect in every age, and the Quraysh who denied Muhammad ﷺ stood in exactly the position of the peoples who denied the messengers before him. Third, and most deeply, the destruction of nations in this world cannot be the whole of the divine justice, because the destroyers and the destroyed are alike dead and the full reckoning of their deeds has not occurred: the tyrant who drowned infants and the prophet who was killed are both in the dust, and if there were no Day of Division, the tyrant would have escaped everything except the death he shared with his victim. The partial justice of history — nations destroyed, empires leveled — points beyond itself to a complete justice that only the gathering of the Day can perform. The Qur'an says as much when it describes the ruined peoples: *"So it is, and if God willed, He would have taken vengeance upon them; but He leaves them to contend, transgressing"* — and records their fate as a sign: *"We destroyed them not but for their sins"* (26:208–209; 28:59). The destruction of the guilty of old is the proof that God's dealing is real; its incompleteness in this world is the proof that a completing Day must come.

**The Ancestors the Deniers Proudly Cited**

There is a sting in the verse's choice of words that the commentators did not miss. The Makkan deniers, like deniers in every age, defended their religion by an appeal to their ancestors: *"We found our fathers upon a way, and we are guided by their traces"* (43:22); *"They say: 'If only the Reminder had been sent down to some great man of the two towns!'"* (43:31). Ancestry was the currency of their pride — the noble lineages of Quraysh, the glories of the fathers, the customs inherited from the grandfathers. The verse meets that pride head-on: the ancestors most worth remembering are not the glorious dead of tribal memory but the destroyed dead of divine history — the generations of old who denied their messengers and were wiped away. The Qur'an repeatedly turns the deniers' own veneration of antiquity against them: *"How many a generation before them did We destroy! Do you perceive any one of them, or hear so much as a whisper?"* (19:98). The past that the deniers treated as a warrant for their religion was in fact a graveyard of religions that had made the same mistake: trusting what the fathers said over what the messengers brought. The question "Did We not destroy those of old?" thus dismantles the argument from ancestry at its root — the fathers followed were themselves the descendants of fathers who were destroyed for following fathers, and the only ancestor worship that history vindicates is the imitation of the messengers who saved their peoples.

**A Question That Expects an Answer of the Heart**

The rhetorical form of the verse leaves the listener no place of refuge. The question is not addressed to the deniers alone; it is addressed through them to every reader of the sūrah in every age, and its answer must be given not in words but in conduct. "Did We not destroy those of old?" — yes; and if yes, then the lesson of their destruction is binding on us: no generation is exempt from the uniform dealing of God; no pride of lineage, no splendor of civilization, no accumulation of power exempts a people that denies its messengers from the fate of the peoples that denied theirs. The believer who recites this verse is asked to carry the ruins of the ancients in his heart as a permanent warning: to read the fall of every empire as a page of the same book, to hear in the silence of every ruined city the echo of the same question, and to answer it — before the Day when the question will be asked not of the peoples of old but of the peoples of the end — with the only answer that has ever saved a generation: belief in the messengers, reverence for the Sender, and the fear that turns history's warnings into wisdom.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:17

> **then make those of later times follow them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sequel to Every Ancient Ruin**

The question begun in verse 16 completes itself in verse 17: *thumma nutbiʿuhumu al-ākhirīn* — "then make those of later times follow them?" God destroyed the ancients, and then He made the later generations follow them — the second half of the verse extends the history lesson from the deep past to the recent past and the present. The word *al-ākhirīn*, "those of later times," is the counterpart of *al-awwalīn*: if the first half of the question cites the peoples of ancient memory, the second half includes every later people that repeated their denial — the generations between the ancients and the Arabs of Makkah, and the Makkans themselves, standing in the same line. The verb *nutbiʿu*, "We make follow," from the root *t-b-ʿ*, "to follow," describes a deliberate act of divine sequence: God does not destroy each guilty people in isolation but lines them up, so to speak, in a procession of doom — one denial follows another through history, and one destruction follows the denial, in a pattern so regular that it can be described as a custom. The commentators read the verse in two complementary ways, both of which are present in the sources. The first reading takes the "later ones" to be the later deniers of earlier times — the peoples who came after ʿĀd and Thamūd and repeated their crime and met their end. The second reading takes them to be the contemporaries of the Prophet himself: the verse threatens that as the ancients were destroyed for denial, so the later deniers — the Quraysh who heard Muhammad ﷺ and refused him — will follow the ancients into the same end, either in this world by the fate that overtook the armies of the deniers at Badr and after, or in the Hereafter by the woe that the refrain pronounces. Al-Qurṭubī noted that some commentators identified "those of later times" in this very sense: the later generations of this community who deny, following the earlier deniers into destruction.

**The Qur'an's Own Witness to the Pattern**

The pattern of one guilty generation following another into ruin is one of the most frequently repeated teachings of the Qur'an, and the sūrah of the Cattle states it in terms almost identical to this verse's: *"Then We destroyed them for their sins and brought forth after them another generation"* (6:6). The sequence is traced explicitly for the peoples of the prophets: after the flood came ʿĀd; after ʿĀd came Thamūd; after Thamūd came the people of Lot and the people of Midian; after them came Pharaoh; and the Qur'an describes the succession in the sūrah of the Spider as a series of lessons: *"And ʿĀd and Thamūd — it has become clear to you from their dwellings. And Satan made their deeds seem fair to them, and turned them from the way, though they were keen-sighted"* (29:38). The deep point of the pattern is that denial is contagious across time: every generation that refuses its own messenger hands on to the next a reinforced habit of refusal — the Qur'an describes the later peoples saying of the earlier: "We found our fathers on a way" — and the divine dealing answers the human imitation: as the later ones imitate the earlier ones in crime, so they follow them in consequence. There is a terrible symmetry in the verse: the deniers of every age imagine themselves free — a fresh people, unbound by the past — and the verse answers that they are the most bound of all: bound by their imitation of the guilty ancients into the procession of their doom.

**The Second Procession: Leaders and Led Into the Fire**

The Qur'an develops the image of "following" into one of the most haunting scenes of the Hereafter — the procession of the led following their leaders into the Fire. In the sūrah of the Heights the two companies are pictured in dialogue: *"And its inhabitants will call out to the inhabitants of the Garden: 'Pour upon us some water, or some of what God has provided!' They will say: 'God has forbidden both to the deniers.'... And every time a community enters, it curses its sister, until, when they have all gathered there, the last of them will say of the first of them: 'Our Lord, these led us astray; give them a double punishment of the Fire'"* (7:44, 38). The sūrah of the Ranks gives the leaders' reply: *"They will say: 'Nay, you were not believers. We had no authority over you — nay, you were a transgressing people. So the Word of our Lord has come due against us; we shall indeed taste it'"* (37:29–31). The verse of al-Mursalāt stands behind both scenes: the "later ones" follow the earlier ones — not because God forces them, but because they chose, in this world, to follow the earlier ones' denial. The double meaning of *nutbiʿu* now appears in its full depth: God makes the later deniers follow the earlier into destruction, and the "making follow" is nothing other than the ratification of a following they had already chosen — they followed their leaders, their fathers, and their idols in this world, and in the next they follow the same leaders into the Fire, in a procession whose order was fixed by their own obedience to false guidance.

**Following the Wrong Procession Is Still Following**

The verse thus confronts every hearer with a question that cuts deeper than the history lesson: which procession are you in? Human beings are followers by nature — no one invents his religion, his morality, or his way of life from nothing; everyone walks in the traces of someone. The only question is whose traces. The Qur'an divides humanity into two processions that run through all of history: the procession of the deniers, in which each generation follows the previous one into ruin — *"And they will say, had we but listened or reflected, we would not be among the inhabitants of the Blaze"* (67:10) — and the procession of the faithful, in which each generation follows the previous one into mercy: the Qur'an prays for those "who came after them saying: 'Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith'" (59:10), and the Prophet ﷺ taught that whoever revives a good sunnah has its reward and the reward of those who act on it until the Day of Resurrection, while whoever establishes an evil way carries its burden and the burdens of those who act on it. The verse's question — "then make those of later times follow them?" — is an invitation to examine the head of the procession one is following: if it is the procession of the deniers, the destination is fixed by the uniform dealing of God; if it is the procession of the messengers, the destination is fixed as well — and the sūrah will describe both destinations before it ends, so that no follower may plead that he did not know where the road he chose was going.

**The Uniform Dealing as a Mercy in Disguise**

There is one further light in which the commentators read the regularity of the pattern, and it softens what might otherwise seem a theology of pure menace: the uniformity of God's dealing is itself a mercy, because it makes the future legible. If God dealt with peoples arbitrarily — destroying one denier and sparing another without reason — no warning could be trusted and no history could teach. Because His dealing is uniform — denial followed by ruin, in every age, without exception — the record of the past becomes a lantern for the future: the later generations are not left to guess the consequence of their choices; they have before them the complete documentation of every people that chose as they are choosing. The verse is thus not a threat hurled blindly at the present but a gift of knowledge: the Quraysh — and every later people — were told in advance, with the full evidence of the ancients and the later ones spread across the landscape of history, that the procession they were joining ended where the earlier processions ended. When the refrain that follows this verse pronounces its woe over the deniers, it pronounces it over people who had been given the rarest of advantages: a preview of the destination, written in the fate of every people that had walked the road before them.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:18

> **Thus do We deal with the guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Law Stated in the Present Tense**

The history lesson closes with a verdict stated as a universal law: *ka-dhālika nafʿalu bil-mujriminīn* — "Thus do We deal with the guilty." The sentence deserves close grammatical attention, because its tense carries its theology. The verb *nafʿalu* — "We do" — is present tense, not past: the verse does not say "Thus did We deal with the guilty of old," which would confine the law to the ancients, but "Thus do We deal with the guilty," which extends it to every age, including the age of the speaker and the age of every reader. The *ka-dhālika*, "thus" or "in like manner," binds the dealing to the pattern just described: the ancients were destroyed for their denial, the later ones followed them, and in this same manner — not in some other manner, not by some changed rule — God deals with the guilty always. The *mujriminīn*, "the guilty," are from the root *j-r-m*, whose primary sense is to cut a thing off, and derivatively to commit a crime, to be a criminal: the *mujrim* is one who has severed himself from the order of truth and transgressed against it deliberately, the word the Qur'an uses for those whose guilt is established and habitual rather than occasional. The verse states, in effect, a law of the moral universe: denial and guilt have a fixed consequence in the divine administration, as fixed as the consequence of sowing tares or drinking poison. The Qur'an announces the same law in the sūrah of Jonah: *"Then when We had saved them, We destroyed those who committed transgressions"* (10:13, in context), and the sūrah of the Smoke: *"How many a generation before them did We destroy, and they cried out, but there was no escape!"* (44:37); and its most universal formulation: *"Such is the custom (sunnah) of God which has passed before; and you will find no alteration in the custom of God"* (33:62; 35:43; 48:23).

**The Deal That Matches the Deed**

The word "deal" (*nafʿalu*) might mislead if it were read as arbitrary treatment — as though God dealt with the guilty by whim. The verse's own context forecloses that reading: the "dealing" described in verses 16–17 was destruction after warning, ruin after messengers, consequence after choice. The Qur'an's doctrine of the divine dealing is one of exact correspondence: *"Is the recompense of goodness anything but goodness?"* (55:60) on the one side, and *"The recompense of an evil is an evil like it"* (42:40) on the other. The *mujrimin* are not victims of a trap; they are contractors who signed, with their own choices, a contract whose terms were published in advance — warned by messengers, reminded by the Reminder, excused or warned by revelation — and the divine dealing is simply the enforcement of the terms. The classical commentators drew out the correspondence by noting that the same *ka-dhālika* construction is used in the Qur'an for the other side of the balance: *"Thus do We rescue the believers"* (10:103). The same "manner" that deals destruction to the guilty deals deliverance to the believers: the divine administration is one law with two applications, and the difference lies not in the dealing but in the people — the guilty who chose transgression meet the consequence of transgression, and the believers who chose faith meet the consequence of faith. The verse's "thus" is the hinge between the two histories of verses 16–17 and the two destinies the sūrah will describe in its later movements.

**The Guilty Who Are Named in the Sūrah**

Within the immediate context of al-Mursalāt, the *mujriminīn* are identified with the *mukadhdhibīn* — the deniers of the refrain. The Qur'an frequently joins the two descriptions: the guilty are those who denied the messengers and the Reminder: *"Thus do We deal with the guilty, for they denied the signs of their Lord"* — the connection is drawn in the sūrah of Yūnus: *"Those who deny Our signs are a people of guilt (mujrimūn)"* (10:17 in sense), and the sūrah of the Poets describes the end of the guilty peoples after the warning: *"So warn! You are only a warner... Woe to the guilty on a painful Day"* — the very pairing of this sūrah's own refrain. Al-Qurṭubī recorded that the "guilty" of this verse was understood by some to refer specifically to the disbelievers of Quraysh — the contemporaries of the Prophet who were being warned that the dealing that destroyed the ancients would deal with them — while others took it as a general description of all who are guilty of denial in any age. Both readings stand: the verse is a threat to the original hearers and a law for everyone after them. And because the law is stated in the present tense, its application is not suspended until the Day of Division alone: the "dealing" of God with the guilty operates in this world as well — in the ruin of nations that deny, in the collapse of orders built on transgression, in the slow corruption that the Qur'an describes: *"Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what the hands of people have earned, that He may let them taste some of what they have done, that haply they might return"* (30:41).

**Why the Law Is Not Suspended for the Powerful**

The verse's quiet defiance of power is one of its most important messages for every age. The *mujrimin* of history were rarely the weak: they were the rulers — Pharaoh with his armies, the chiefs of ʿĀd with their columns, the kings of the overturned cities with their fortresses — men whose guilt was armored by armies and legitimized by custom. The Qur'an repeatedly notes the disparity between the power of the guilty and the ease of their destruction: *"Have they not traveled through the land and seen how the end was of those before them? They were greater than them in strength, and they plowed the earth and built it up more than they built it up; and their messengers came to them with clear proofs. It was not God who wronged them, but themselves they wronged"* (30:9). The verse "Thus do We deal with the guilty" is the divine answer to every concentration of criminal power: the dealing of God does not ask the permission of armies, does not respect the statute of limitations, does not expire with the death of the guilty in this world, and cannot be outmaneuvered by the scheming that verse 39 of this very sūrah will mock. The believer who lives under the rule of the guilty — as the first believers lived under the rule of the Quraysh — is taught by this verse to hold a long view: the present tense of God's dealing covers every age, and the power of the guilty is a lease, not a title.

**The Verse as a Standard of Self-Examination**

Finally, the verse turns its law upon the reader with the same force with which it turned it upon the Quraysh. The category "guilty" is not a foreign nationality; it is a description of conduct, and the conduct it describes — transgression against the commands of God after the Reminder has arrived — is possible for every hearer of the sūrah. The classical scholars drew the practical inference that the believer must examine his own life against the divine dealing: where transgression has been chosen, the law of the verse is already in motion, whether the consequence is visible or deferred; and the only door out of the category of *mujrimīn* is the door that the Qur'an opens in the same breath in which it announces the law — repentance before the dealing falls: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the Mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins"* (39:53). The verse that states the law of the guilty thus becomes, for the believer, not a terror but a navigation instrument: the dealing is uniform, so the course is knowable; the consequence is fixed, so the avoidance is possible; and the mercy is wider than the law — for the same God who "deals with the guilty" is the God who "loves the repentant" (2:222), and the same present tense that covers every age of transgression covers every age of return.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:19

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Stroke of the Refrain Falls on History's Lesson**

The refrain returns for the second time: *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" Its position here is as deliberate as its first occurrence: it closes the sūrah's history lesson, falling immediately after the law of verse 18 — "Thus do We deal with the guilty" — like the gavel that follows the reading of the statute. The first occurrence of the refrain (v. 15) followed the description of the Day itself and warned those who deny the Resurrection. This second occurrence follows the account of the destroyed ancients and the later ones who followed them, and it warns those who deny the lesson of history — those who look at the ruins of the peoples that denied their messengers and say, as the Qur'an records generations saying: *"This is a tale of the ancients"* (6:25; 23:83; 27:68; 46:17), or who treat the destruction of the guilty as ancient mythology with no bearing on the present. Al-Shawkānī recorded a reading reported by some commentators that distinguishes the two occurrences in exactly this way: the first woe (v. 15) warns of the punishment of the Hereafter — the consequence of denying the Resurrection itself — while this second woe (v. 19) warns of the punishment of this world, the fate that overtakes a people that repeats the denial of the destroyed nations and meets their end in the present life. Whether or not one adopts that precise distribution, the context supports its spirit: the first woe named the Day; the second woe names the pattern — and both fall upon the same deniers, who deny the future Day and ignore the past pattern at once.

**The Self-Exemption of Every Later Generation**

The specific blindness that this occurrence of the refrain condemns deserves examination, because it is the most durable blindness in human history: the conviction of every generation that it is exempt from the pattern that destroyed all previous generations. The peoples of old did not believe they would be destroyed — that is precisely why the Qur'an describes their surprise: *"They await but a single Cry that will seize them while they dispute. So they cannot make a bequest, nor can they return to their people"* (36:49–50); *"When the Cry comes to them, they will say: 'This is an ill-omened day for us!'"* (11:64, in the sense of their immediate dread). Each destroyed people imagined itself stronger, more advanced, more secure than the peoples it had read about: ʿĀd prided itself on its might — *"as for ʿĀd, they waxed arrogant in the land without right, and said: 'Who is stronger than us?' Did they not see that God, Who created them, is stronger than them?"* (41:15); Pharaoh said: *"I show you only what I see, and I guide you only to the way of right conduct"* (40:29) — and was drowned with his hosts. The refrain's repetition after the history lesson is the Qur'an's answer to the self-exemption: the woe does not say "Woe to the ancients" — the ancients have already received their woe in this world; it says "Woe **that Day** to the deniers," shifting the weight of the pattern onto the present hearers, the deniers who stand exactly where the ancients stood, hearing exactly what the ancients heard, and imagining exactly what the ancients imagined — that the dealing of God, which never failed against anyone before them, will somehow fail against them.

**Why Denial of History Is Itself a Form of Denial**

The verse's placement teaches that ignoring the past is not a neutral oversight but an active denial — and the Qur'an is precise about the mechanism. The deniers of the Prophet did not deny that the ancients existed or that their ruins stood along the trade routes; they denied the *meaning* of the ruins. They reduced the destruction of the guilty peoples to "the tales of the ancients," to mythology, to the accidents of time — refusing the inference that the Qur'an drew: that the same moral cause (denial of messengers) produced the same effect (destruction) and would produce it again for the same cause. This is why the Qur'an can place the denial of history on the same level as the denial of the Resurrection: both are refusals of inference, refusals to let reality teach its lesson. The sūrah of al-Anʿām describes the same compound refusal: *"And if you see them, their turning away makes you marvel; and if they speak, they listen to your speech — as though they were pieces of wood propped up. They think every cry is against them"* (63:4). And the sūrah of Fāṭir records the same plea against history's evidence: *"They swore by God their most earnest oaths that if a warner came to them they would be better guided than any people; but when a warner came to them, it only increased their aversion"* (35:42). The second woe condemns precisely this: the people who were given the complete record of the past — the documentation of every nation that denied and perished — and answered the record with denial.

**The Two Punishments and the One Mercy**

The distribution of this occurrence of the woe between this world and the next, recorded by al-Shawkānī from earlier authorities, opens a wider perspective on the sūrah's refrain that the commentators developed: the ten woes are not ten announcements of a single event but the full spectrum of the divine dealing. Some of the consequences of denial fall in this world — the destruction of nations, the ruin of orders, the hardening of hearts that the Qur'an calls a punishment in itself: *"We shall turn away their hearts and their eyes, even as they did not believe in it the first time, and We shall leave them in their transgression wandering blindly"* (6:110). Some fall at death — the Qur'an describes the seizure of the guilty at death as a tasting of punishment: *"If you could but see when the wrongdoers are in the throes of death, and the angels stretch out their hands: 'Yield up your souls! Today you shall be recompensed with the punishment of humiliation'"* (6:93). And some fall on the Day of Division — the Fire whose description occupies the middle of this sūrah. The refrain's repetition across the chapter gathers all three horizons into one warning: denial is not a single mistake with a single consequence but a whole trajectory, and the woe accompanies the trajectory at every stage — in the pattern of history, in the life of the denier, and in the Day that the pattern prefigures. The mercy concealed in the repetition is that each woe is still a warning: as long as the refrain is being recited rather than experienced, the door of return stands open — which is why the sūrah, after every woe, continues to speak, and why the deniers, after every warning, are still being addressed rather than abandoned.

**The Reader Who Stands Between the Ruins and the Day**

For the reader of the sūrah, this second occurrence of the refrain fixes the position from which the whole chapter must be read. Behind the reader lie the ruins of the ancients — the complete, documented history of the peoples who denied and were destroyed. Ahead of the reader lies the Day of Division — the complete, promised gathering of all generations for the final dealing. And between the ruins and the Day stands the present moment, the only point in the line where the pattern can still be broken: where the denier can still cease to deny, where the heedless can still take warning from the past before the Day makes the warning moot. The refrain falls at this point in the sūrah as a marker of urgency: the history lesson of verses 16–18 was not given to satisfy curiosity but to convert — the record of the past was placed before the present so that the present would not repeat it. Whoever recites this woe and understands it stands, in that instant, exactly where the Qur'an wants every hearer to stand: with the full evidence of the past behind him, the full warning of the Day before him, and the choice of his own conduct in his hand — invited to be, of all the generations of deniers, the one that listened to the pattern and turned.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:20

> **Did We not create you from a base fluid,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Moves From the Ruins to the Reader's Own Body**

With verse 20 the sūrah performs its most dramatic shift of address: having asked about the destruction of the ancients in the third person — "those of old" — it now turns upon its hearers in the second person: *a-lam nakhluqkum min māʾin mahīn* — "Did We not create **you** from a base fluid?" The rhetorical question is of the same *a-lam* form as verse 16, expecting the answer "Yes indeed" — but now the evidence cited is not the ruins along the trade routes but the reader's own origin, the one fact about the universe that no hearer can examine from outside because he is himself the specimen. The word *māʾ*, "water, fluid," is the Qur'an's general term for the liquid from which life is made — *"We made every living thing from water"* (21:30) — and *mahīn* is from the root *m-h-n*, "to be weak, little, despicable, held in contempt": the fluid is described as base, feeble, and lowly, a word chosen to deflate every pretension of human grandeur. The Qur'an uses the same description elsewhere with the same deflating purpose: *"He created man from a base fluid (sulālah min māʾ mahīn)"* (32:8, in context of the progeny of Adam); *"So let man consider from what he was created: created from a gushing fluid that issues from between the loins and the breastbone"* (86:5–7); *"Did We not create you from a base fluid, then lodge it in a secure dwelling place?"* — the very verses before us. The question is designed to produce a collision: the creature who struts, boasts, and denies his Maker is reminded that his entire biography began as a drop of despised fluid, and that the Power which raised him from that beginning is the Power he denies.

**The Drop as the First Document of the Resurrection**

The argumentative purpose of the verse, however, is not humility for its own sake; it is evidence — and the commentators are unanimous that the creation of the human being from a drop is cited here, as in several other sūrahs, as testimony to God's power to resurrect. The logic is the logic of the beginning: if God created the human being once from a base fluid — from nothing, as it were, or from what is less than nothing in the scale of dignity — then the creation that the deniers call impossible, the re-creation of the dead, is easier than the creation they have already witnessed in themselves. The Qur'an makes the argument explicit in the sūrah of Yā Sīn, where the denier holds up a bone and asks who can give life to decayed bones: *"He says: 'Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?' Say: 'He will give them life Who brought them forth the first time; and He knows every creation'"* (36:78–79); and in the sūrah of Qāf, where the doubt is answered with the same appeal to the first creation: *"What! Were We wearied by the first creation? Nay, but they are in confusion about a new creation"* (50:15). The verse of al-Mursalāt compresses the same argument into a question: every denier is carrying, in his own body, the living proof that the Power he denies has already performed the kind of act he calls impossible — the making of a fully formed, hearing, seeing, thinking human being from a base fluid. To deny the Resurrection while existing as its proof is the inconsistency that the sūrah will name again in verse 23: "Thus do We determine; what excellent determiners!"

**The Double Edge of the Description: Humiliation and Wonder**

The word *mahīn* cuts in two directions, and the Qur'an intends both. The first edge is humiliation: the base fluid is the answer to every form of human arrogance — the pride of lineage (all lineages meet in the same drop), the pride of wealth and power (Pharaoh and the beggar share the same origin), the pride of the denier who sets himself against his Maker. The Qur'an presses this edge when it describes man's ingratitude: *"Has man not seen that We created him from a drop? Yet he is an open adversary!"* (36:77); *"He created man from a drop of fluid, yet behold, he is an open disputant"* (16:4). The commentators marveled at the contrast: the creature who was a drop yesterday disputes with his Creator today — arguing against the very Hand that formed him, as the proverb of the tradition puts it: the clay argues with the potter. The second edge is wonder: that from such a base beginning should come such a creature — hearing, seeing, reasoning, loving, building civilizations, writing poetry, seeking God — is the Qur'an's standing evidence that the Maker's power is not constrained by the meanness of His material. The sūrah of the Believer makes the wonder explicit: *"He created you from dust, then from a drop of fluid, then from a blood clot, then He brings you forth as infants, then you reach your maturity..."* (40:67). The same drop that humbles the proud magnifies the Creator: the baseness of the material and the excellence of the product measure the distance that only divine power can cross.

**The Modern Reader and the Ancient Evidence**

The verse addresses the modern reader with undiminished force, because the science of human origins has only deepened the wonder the Qur'an points to. The base fluid of the verse is now known to contain, in a single ejaculation, hundreds of millions of cells, each carrying the complete genetic library of a human being — the entire specification of eye color, height, temperament, and predisposition compressed into microscopic carriers — of which a single union with a single egg will produce a creature that grows, in nine months, from a speck invisible to the naked eye into an infant with a beating heart, functioning senses, and the rudiments of consciousness. Every step of the process that embryology has mapped — the clinging of the fertilized egg, the formation of the clot-like mass, the differentiation of tissues, the emergence of the limbs and the senses — corresponds, in its broad outlines, to the stages the Qur'an itself describes in the sūrah of the Believers: "Then We made the drop into a clinging form, and the clinging form into a lump, and the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh" (23:14). What the modern reader adds to the ancient argument is not a correction but a magnification: the more precisely the process is known, the more precisely it is seen to be a process of determined, guided, measured transformation — the very "determining" that verse 23 of this sūrah will celebrate. The base fluid remains base; the wonder remains wonder; and the question "Did We not create you from a base fluid?" remains as unanswerable by the modern denier as by the ancient one.

**The Drop That Silences Every Defense**

The rhetorical form of the verse leaves the denier no defense, and it is worth examining why. A question that begins "Did We not...?" can be answered only in two ways: yes or no. To answer "No — You did not create us from a base fluid" is to deny the most certain fact of one's own existence, a denial that would make rational discourse impossible. To answer "Yes" is to concede the entire argument: the Power that made the hearer from a base fluid exists, acted, and is capable of acting again; the denial of the Resurrection collapses because its premise — that such a making is impossible — is refuted by the hearer's own existence. The verse is thus a perfectly constructed trap for denial: it forces the denier to choose between contradicting his own eyes and conceding his own creation, and either choice destroys his case. The Qur'an's deeper purpose, however, is not to trap but to turn: the hearer who answers "Yes" honestly has taken the first step of the return — the recognition of the Creator is already implicit in the recognition of the creation, and from that recognition the rest of the sūrah's argument — the secure dwelling, the known measure, the excellent determining — unfolds as the biography of every reader, written before his birth by the Hand he is invited, at last, to acknowledge.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:21

> **then lodge it in a secure dwelling place**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Drop Is Lodged: Sequence as Signature**

The verse continues the biography of every human being: *fa-jaʿalnāhu fī qarārin makīn* — "then We lodged it in a secure dwelling place." The verb *jaʿalnā*, "We made, We placed, We lodged," is deliberate: the fluid was not merely left to drift; it was *placed*, by an Agent, in a destination. The word *qarār* is from the root *q-r-r*, "to settle, rest, abide," and denotes a place of settling — the Qur'an uses the same word for the earth as a place of settlement (*qarār*, 40:64; 27:61) and for the abode of the Hereafter (*dār al-qarār*, 40:39) — and *makīn* is from the root *m-k-n*, "to be firm, established, secure," the word used for a firmly established place and for the "secure" station of the honored messenger: *"Truly he is possessed of power and secure (makīn) with the Lord of the Throne"* (81:20). Together the two words describe what the commentators, with one voice, identified as the womb: the *qarār makīn* — the secure, settled dwelling — is the uterus, in which the drop of base fluid is lodged and kept. The full verse of the sūrah of the Believers states the sequence in the same terms: *"Then We made the drop into a clinging form... and We placed it in a secure dwelling place until a known measure"* (23:13–14, in sense); and the sūrah of the Pilgrimage adds the detail of the lodging itself: *"We cause what We will to remain in the wombs until a named term"* (22:5). The sequence of the two verses before us — base fluid, then secure dwelling — is the first movement of the argument: the very material that humiliation describes is instantly given the most protected lodging in creation, and the contrast between the meanness of the drop and the honor of its lodging measures the generosity of the Lodger.

**The Most Protected Place in the Natural World**

The description of the womb as a *qarār makīn* — a secure, firm dwelling — is one of those Qur'anic phrases whose accuracy modern science has only underlined. The womb is, in a precise sense, the most protected environment a human being will ever inhabit: the embryo floats in its amniotic sea, cushioned against shock; its temperature is held constant by the mother's body; its oxygen and nourishment are delivered through the placenta by a filter that admits what is needed and excludes what is harmful; it is shielded from the world's light, sound, and weather; and the mother's body expends enormous resources to maintain the environment at exactly the conditions the growing life requires. The classical commentators, reading the phrase in their own terms, extracted the same meaning: al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr explained the "secure dwelling place" as the womb, which God made firm and secure for the drop — protected from disturbance, held in place, nourished in darkness. The Qur'an describes the layered protection elsewhere: *"He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in threefold darkness"* (39:6) — the darkness of the belly, the darkness of the womb, the darkness of the membrane — and the sūrah of the Bee traces the human entry into the world from the same protected beginning: *"And God brought you forth from your mothers' wombs knowing nothing, and gave you hearing and sight and hearts, that haply you might give thanks"* (16:78). Every human being, including every denier of the Resurrection, began his existence in this secure dwelling — protected before he could deserve protection, nourished before he could ask for nourishment, kept by a care that preceded his consciousness of any caregiver.

**The Lodging That No One Arranged for Himself**

The verse's argumentative edge lies in what it implies about the Lodger. The drop could not lodge itself: it had no will, no knowledge, no power to choose its destination; it was carried to the womb and settled there by the same Power that created it. The embryo does not arrange its own protection: it cannot ask for the womb, cannot design the placenta, cannot choose the temperature of its sea; all of it is arranged for it, and the arranging is the act of God — the *jaʿalnā* of the verse, the divine "We placed." The commentators drew the inference that the verse is a lesson in dependence: the human being's first experience of existence was total dependence on a care he could not see, could not earn, and could not even perceive — and the whole of life is a continuation of that first lesson. The same Hand that lodged the drop in the womb lodges the soul in the body, the body in the world, and the world in its orbit; the creature that learned to trust before it could think is invited to trust again after it can. The Qur'an makes the same point when it traces the human being's whole career of care: *"God is He Who made the earth a resting place for you and the sky a canopy, and shaped you and shaped you well, and provided you with good things"* (40:64). The secure dwelling of the womb is the first installment of a care that never stops — and the denier who denies the Caregiver is denying the Hand that has been holding him since before he was born.

**The Womb as the First World and Its Exit as the First Resurrection**

The deepest service of the womb-image to this sūrah's argument, however, is the analogy it sets up — an analogy that the classical commentators and spiritual writers drew with great beauty. The fetus lives in a world whose entire horizon is the womb: it knows no sky, no earth, no parents, no sun; it cannot imagine a life outside the darkness, the warmth, and the muffled sounds that are its whole universe; and if it could reason, the claim that it would one day pass through a narrow passage into a vast world of light, air, speech, and motion — a world where it would walk, eat, see, and know — would sound to it as improbable as the Resurrection sounds to the deniers. Yet the passage happens: at the known measure, every fetus is expelled from the secure dwelling into a world it could not have imagined, and the first act of the newborn is the cry of one who has entered a new creation. The Qur'an alludes to the analogy when it describes the stages: *"We cause what We will to remain in the wombs until a named term, then We bring you forth as infants"* (22:5) — and the sūrah of Luqmān links the two exits: *"Your creation and your resurrection are but as a single soul"* (31:28). The believer who reflects on this verse understands the Hereafter as the second birth: the womb of this world, with all its darkness and its care, will open at the known measure, and the creature that could not imagine the Garden will find itself — as the newborn finds itself in the world — inside a creation vaster than anything its former dwelling could have taught it to conceive: the *dār al-qarār*, the "secure abode" of the Hereafter (40:39), which the same root *qarār* names as the womb's ultimate counterpart.

**The Lesson of the First Home**

For the believer, the verse carries a lesson in gratitude and trust that the commentators never tired of drawing. The secure dwelling was given, not earned: no fetus qualifies for the womb by merit; the protection, nourishment, and darkness of the first home are pure gift, preceding any act of the creature. Gratitude for existence itself — the thanks of the creature for having been lodged, kept, and brought forth — is therefore the most fundamental of human duties, the response that the Qur'an calls *shukr*: *"And We have enjoined upon man concerning his parents — his mother carried him in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is in two years — Give thanks to Me and to your parents; to Me is the journey's end"* (31:14). And trust follows gratitude: the God who lodged the drop securely will lodge the soul securely; the Hand that held the fetus through the darkness of the womb will hold the believer through the darkness of the grave; the Power that brought the infant forth from the first secure dwelling will bring the dead forth from the second. The verse that seemed to be about embryology is thus, in the full architecture of the sūrah, a verse about the Hereafter: the secure dwelling of the womb is the first chapter of every human biography, written by the same Hand that will write the last — and the denier who denies the final chapter is denying the Author whose pen has never once failed in the chapters already written.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:22

> **for a known measure?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Measure That Bounds the Dwelling**

The biography of the drop continues to its appointed horizon: *ilā qadarin maʿlūm* — "for a known measure," or more exactly "until a known measure." The word *qadar* is from the root *q-d-r*, "to measure, to determine, to decree," and it carries the double sense of a quantity and a term: the measure of the drop's development and the term of its stay. The *qadar* is *maʿlūm* — "known" — and the commentators asked: known to whom? The answer that unfolds from the whole Qur'an is that the measure is known to God — fixed in His knowledge and decree before the drop exists — and recorded by the angels appointed to the wombs; it is not known to the creature, whose consciousness of the term begins only with its end. The sūrah of the Pilgrimage states the matter with precision: *"We cause what We will to remain in the wombs until a named term"* (22:5), and the sūrah of the Cattle traces the same fixed measure through the whole of life: *"God knows what every female bears, and what the wombs shrink and swell; everything with Him has a measure"* (13:8). The gestation of the human being is not an open-ended process or a matter of chance: it is bounded by a known measure — the classical scholars noting the fixed course of the months of pregnancy, the sūrah of the Sand Dunes giving the fullest term: *"His gestation and weaning is thirty months"* (46:15). The drop remains in the secure dwelling neither a moment less nor a moment more than the measure decreed; it is brought forth "when its term arrives," as every soul is brought forth from the world when its term arrives.

**The Angel, the Spirit, and the Four Words**

The Qur'anic and Prophetic teaching on the "known measure" of the womb reaches its fullest expression in one of the most celebrated authenticated reports of the Sunnah, and no commentary on this verse can pass it by. Ibn Masʿūd, the same Companion who received this very sūrah from the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ in the cave, narrated that the Messenger of God ﷺ — the truthful and believed one — told them:

> "The gathering of the creation of one of you takes place in his mother's womb: forty days as a drop of fluid, then it is a clinging clot for a like period, then it is a lump of flesh for a like period; then an angel is sent to him, who breathes the spirit into him, and is commanded with four words: to write his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether he is wretched or blessed." (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

The hadith places the "known measure" of this verse within the full decree: the measure of the gestation, written with the four decrees — provision, term, deeds, and final state — recorded by the angel at the breathing of the spirit. The verse of the sūrah and the words of the Prophet ﷺ together teach that the human being is measured before he is born: his stay in the secure dwelling has a term, his life in the world has a term, his provision has a bound, and his deeds are witnessed and written as they occur. Nothing in the biography of the drop — and nothing in the biography of the man — falls outside the measure; the same *qadar* that fixed the months of gestation fixed the years of life, and the same Hand that brings the fetus forth at its term brings the soul forth at its term: *"And God does not grant any soul respite once its term has come"* (63:11); *"And to every community there is a term; when its term comes, they cannot delay it by a single hour, nor hasten it"* (7:34; 10:49).

**The Measure Known, and the Hour Unknown**

The verse's description of the measure as *maʿlūm* — known — conceals a distinction that the sūrah will exploit to the end: the measure is known, but not to the creature. The fetus does not know the day of its birth; the mother does not know it in advance with certainty; only the One who decreed the measure knows it completely. And the Qur'an draws the analogy between the birth and the Hour with deliberate repetition: the Day of Division is described in exactly the same terms as the birth — fixed by a measure, known to God, hidden from the creature, arriving suddenly: *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its anchorage? Say: 'The knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none shall unveil it at its time but He. It weighs heavy upon the heavens and the earth; it comes upon you not but suddenly'"* (7:187). The believer who reflects on this verse understands why the Hour is hidden: the fetus that knew the day of its birth would not live its life in the womb differently — it has no choices to make there — but the human being who knew the day of his death and judgment would live his life in the world wrongly: the wicked would wait until the eve of the Hour to repent, the faithful would lose the constant vigilance that the hidden term alone can produce. The known measure of the womb is the pattern of the unknown measure of the world: both are fixed, both are hidden, and both arrive — which is why the sūrah can move, in a few verses, from the drop in the womb to the Day of Division, and why the Qur'an can say: *"Your creation and your resurrection are but as a single soul"* (31:28).

**Security Without Complacency**

There is a final lesson in the pairing of the secure dwelling (v. 21) with the known measure (v. 22), and it is a lesson of exquisite balance. The dwelling is secure — *makīn* — but it is not permanent; the measure bounds the security, so that the creature is simultaneously safe and temporary. The fetus is perfectly protected and perfectly provisional: its safety is real, but its safety is not its destiny — the secure dwelling is a passage, not a home, and the measure exists precisely to end the stay at the right moment. The human being in the world stands in the same double condition: the world is a secure dwelling of another sort — a place of shelter, provision, and rest, which the Qur'an itself describes as a cradle and a resting place (2:22; 43:10; 78:6) — and yet it is bounded by a known measure, a term fixed before the drop was lodged, a stay that is safe but not final. The classical scholars expressed the balance in the aphorism that became proverbial among them: the world is the tillage of the Hereafter — a field whose entire purpose is its harvest, secure enough for the seed to grow, bounded enough that the harvest must come. The believer who reads this verse is taught to live in the secure dwelling of the world as the fetus lives in the womb: gratefully receiving the protection, growing steadily toward the term, and never mistaking the passage for the destination — because the same Measure that set the day of his birth from the drop has set the day of his second birth from the dust, and both are known, though the knowing of them is with God alone.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:23

> **Thus do We determine; what excellent determiners!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exclamation That Crowns the Embryology Argument**

The third stage of the argument closes with an exclamation of praise: *fa-qadarnā fa-niʿma al-qādirūn* — "Thus do We determine; what excellent determiners!" The verb *qadarnā* returns to the root *q-d-r* of the previous verse — the measure has been measured, the term has been set, and now the measuring itself is proclaimed and praised. The second clause, *fa-niʿma al-qādirūn*, is the Arabic formula of admiration — *niʿma* introduces praise, exactly as in the Qur'an's other uses: "What an excellent servant! Truly he was penitent" (38:44); "Excellent is the reward, and excellent the resting place" (18:31). The formula here praises the determiners — and the commentators are agreed that the *qādirūn* are God Himself, the plural of majesty matching the divine "We" of the preceding verses: "Thus do We determine, and what excellent determiners We are!" Some early readers recited the verb with the strengthened form *qaddarnā*, "We determined precisely," and the exclamation then crowns the precise determining with the acknowledgment that no one determines as God determines. The verse is thus a moment of divine self-praise inserted into the argument — and the Qur'an's self-praise is always pedagogical: God praises His own determining so that the creature may learn to see the determining, to marvel at it, and to praise it in turn. The verse is the hinge of the whole passage: the base fluid, the secure dwelling, and the known measure have been described; now the sūrah stops, as it were, before the completed work of the embryo and pronounces the verdict of wonder: thus do We determine — and what excellent determiners!

**Everything Measured, Nothing Left to Chance**

The Qur'an's teaching on the divine determining extends far beyond the womb; the embryo is the nearest example of a universal law. *"And We created everything by measure (qadar)"* (54:49); *"He created everything, and measured it with exact measure"* (25:2); *"God knows what every female bears, and what the wombs shrink and swell; everything with Him has a measure"* (13:8); *"God has appointed a measure for everything"* (65:3). The universe of the Qur'an is not a chaos in which forms emerge by accident and terms expire by chance; it is a measured creation, in which every star has its course, every seed its season, every fetus its term, and every soul its decree. The verse of al-Mursalāt points to the human being as the exhibit of this universal measuring: the formation of the embryo is not a random collocation of cells but a determined development — stage after stage, at measured times, toward a measured form, within a measured term. Modern embryology, for all its precision, has only deepened the picture: the division of the fertilized egg, the migration of cells, the timing of organ formation, the genetic program unfolding on a schedule measured in days — the more that is known, the more the process displays the character the verse names: determination, measure, precision. The denier who attributes the embryo to chance is not offering an explanation of the process the verse describes; he is refusing the inference that the process itself invites — the inference that measure implies a Measurer, as surely as writing implies a writer.

**The Power Behind the Determining**

The word *qādirūn* also carries the sense of ability and power — the *qādir* is the one who is able — and the verse's praise therefore embraces both faces of the divine act: God determines *and* God is able to execute what He determines. The two are joined in the Qur'an's doctrine of creation: *"His command, when He desires a thing, is only to say to it 'Be,' and it is"* (36:82). The determining of the embryo required power at every step: the power to gather the base fluid, to carry it to the secure dwelling, to hold it through the measure, to shape it stage by stage, and to bring it forth at the term. And the commentators drew the inference that the sūrah is driving toward: the Power that measured the embryo is the Power that will measure the Hour. The Qur'an says it explicitly in the verses that join the two measures: *"And the command of the Hour is but as the twinkling of an eye, or nearer still; God is Powerful over all things"* (16:77). The One who determined the drop's development from a base fluid to a breathing infant has demonstrated the very kind of power that the Resurrection requires — the power to bring forth life from what was lifeless, to complete a process at its appointed measure, to do what seems impossible to creatures who measure only their own strength. The exclamation "what excellent determiners!" is thus the sūrah's answer, in advance, to the denial of the Resurrection: the Determiner of the womb has already performed, in every human being, an act as wondrous as the raising of the dead — and He has done it once precisely so that the denier may know what He can do again.

**Why the Creature Must Praise the Determining**

There is a subtle psychology in the divine self-praise of this verse that the classical scholars drew out for the believer's benefit. Praise is the response of wonder, and wonder is the response of seeing — and the verse trains the eye to see the determining of God in the ordinary, so that the heart may learn to praise. The believer who recites "what excellent determiners!" over the embryo learns to recite the same exclamation over everything measured: the return of the seasons, the course of the stars, the growth of the child, the healing of the sick, the provision that arrives at its term. The Qur'an trains this habit of praise relentlessly: *"And in the earth are signs for those of certainty, and in your own souls — do you not see?"* (51:20–21). And the praise of the determining is not mere sentiment; it is the acknowledgment of the creature's own place in the order — the confession that the speaker is himself a determined thing, measured by the same Measure that measured the embryo, and that his welfare lies not in escaping the measure but in trusting the Measurer. The fetus does not choose its term; the believer does not choose his provision, his life-span, or his circumstances in their ultimate contours; and the peace that the verse offers is the peace of the creature who has stopped competing with his own Creator's measure and has begun to praise it — the peace of the soul that says, with the Qur'an: *"God is sufficient for us; what an excellent Guardian!"* (3:173).

**The Door That the Exclamation Leaves Open**

The verse's exclamation is praise — but in the structure of the sūrah it is also a threshold. Immediately after the exclamation, the refrain will fall for the third time: "Woe that Day to the deniers!" (v. 24). The juxtaposition is deliberate and stark: the sūrah has exhibited the determining of God in the creation of every human being, has praised the Determiner in the most admiring terms the language affords, and then pronounces woe over those who — having been themselves determined, measured, and formed by that determining — deny the Determiner and the Day that His power promises. The praise and the woe stand back to back so that no hearer can mistake the issue: the denial that the refrain condemns is not the skepticism of the ignorant; it is the refusal of the creature who is himself the exhibit of the power he denies. The exclamation leaves the door of wonder open — the denier is invited to marvel at his own formation and to pass from the marvel to the Maker — and the woe that follows closes nothing except the excuse of those who were shown the determining and answered with denial. The believer, for his part, is taught to carry the exclamation as a habit of the heart: to look at his own hand, his own breath, his own reflection, and to say with the sūrah — thus does my Lord determine, and what excellent determiners!

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:24

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Woe Falls on the Denial of the First Creation**

The refrain returns for the third time, and its position is the most pointed yet: it falls directly after the exclamation of verse 23 — "Thus do We determine; what excellent determiners!" — like a verdict pronounced between two exhibits of a trial. The first woe (v. 15) followed the description of the Day; the second (v. 19) followed the destruction of the guilty nations; this third woe follows the creation of the human being himself — the exhibit that every hearer carries in his own body. Its target is the denial of the first creation: the people who look at the embryo, at the secure dwelling of the womb, at the known measure of their own gestation — and still deny the Power that formed them and the Day that Power promises. The Qur'an names this denial in its starkest form in the sūrah of Yā Sīn, where the denier, holding up a decayed bone, disputes the resurrection — and the answer comes: *"Say: 'He will give them life Who brought them forth the first time'"* (36:79) — and in the sūrah of Maryam: *"Does man not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?"* (19:67). Al-Shawkānī recorded that some commentators read this third occurrence of the refrain in connection with verse 23: the woe falls upon those who deny God's determining — who dispute the measured, decreed creation that the verse has just described, whether by attributing it to chance, to nature, or to their own ingenuity. The sūrah's courtroom logic is remorseless: the first exhibit was the ruins of history; the second exhibit is the embryo of every hearer; and after each exhibit the same verdict is pronounced over those who refuse the inference.

**The Specific Denial: Chance in the Place of the Determiner**

The denial that this woe condemns has taken different forms in different ages, but its structure is constant, and the verse exposes it with remarkable precision. The ancient deniers attributed the creation of the human being to time and chance — the Qur'an quotes their creed of the blind operations of time: *"They say: 'There is nothing but our present life; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time'"* (45:24). The modern form of the same denial is the claim that the measured, ordered, information-rich process of embryonic development — with its fixed stages, its precise timing, its astonishing economy — is the product of undirected accident. The verse meets every form of the denial with the same question: the measure is before you — the known measure of the womb, the determined stages of the drop — and measure demands a Measurer, determination demands a Determiner, as the order of letters demands a writer and the order of the stars demands a Placer. The *qādirūn* of verse 23 are not an optional addition to the process, a hypothesis among hypotheses; they are the only explanation that accounts for the measuredness of the measure. The woe of verse 24 falls upon those who look at the most exquisite determining in nature and insist — against the evidence of their own formation — that no one determined it.

**The Womb and the Resurrection: Denial That Contradicts Itself**

The sūrah's placement of this woe after the embryology passage also exposes the inner contradiction of the denial it condemns. The denier who accepts that he was formed in the womb — and no denier can deny it without denying his own eyes and his mother's testimony — has already conceded every premise the Resurrection requires. If a base fluid can become a living human being by the determining of God, then the dead can become living again by the same determining; if the secure dwelling of the womb could be entered and exited by the decree, then the secure dwelling of the grave can be entered and exited by the same decree; if the first creation — from what was less than dust in dignity — was possible, then the second creation — from dust that once was living — is not more difficult but less. The Qur'an states the inference as a law: *"And He it is Who begins creation, then repeats it — and that is easier for Him"* (30:27). The deniers of the Resurrection were not people who found the doctrine difficult; they were people who had already accepted, by the mere fact of their existence, the power that the doctrine announces — and then denied it in words. This is why the sūrah can place the woe so immediately after the wonder: the denial is not an intellectual position; it is an act of ingratitude and self-contradiction performed by creatures who are living proofs of what they deny — the very portrait that another sūrah paints: *"Has man not seen that We created him from a drop? Yet he is an open adversary!"* (36:77).

**Why Denial of the First Creation Destroys Every Ground of Hope**

The commentators observed a further dimension of this woe that deserves attention: the denial of the first creation does not only contradict the evidence; it destroys hope. If the determining of God that formed the embryo is denied, then the human being is left with no ground to expect anything beyond the world's last day but the grave's last day — no justice beyond the world's partial justice, no reunion beyond the world's partings, no recompense beyond the world's incomplete accounts. The Qur'an presents the belief in the second creation as the ground of every human hope: the hope of the wronged for vindication, of the mourner for reunion, of the penitent for acceptance, of the laborer for the reward of his labor. The woe over those who deny the determining is thus a woe over those who have locked themselves out of hope itself — who have chosen, against the evidence of their own formation, to live in a universe whose last word is the dust. And the mercy in the verse's severity is that the woe is still a warning: the denier addressed by this refrain is still breathing, still formed, still carrying the proof of the determining in every cell of his body — still able to look at his own hand and infer the Hand, still able to hear the question of verse 20 — "Did We not create you from a base fluid?" — and to answer it, at last, not with denial but with the acknowledgment that is the beginning of every return: yes, my Lord, You created me; and the One who created me from a base fluid, lodged me in a secure dwelling, and brought me forth at a known measure — what an excellent Determiner — will bring me forth again.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:25

> **Did We not make the earth a receptacle**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Moves From the Womb to the World**

With verse 25 the sūrah's argument reaches its third exhibit: having shown the deniers the ruins of history and the embryo of their own bodies, it now shows them the ground beneath their feet: *a-lam najʿali al-arḍa kifātan* — "Did We not make the earth a receptacle?" The verb *jaʿalnā* is the same deliberate "We made, We placed" used of the lodging of the drop in the womb (v. 21): the earth, like the womb, is something *made* for a purpose by an Agent. The word *kifātan* is the crux of the verse. It comes from the root *k-f-t*, whose sense is to gather, hold together, and contain: the *kifāt* is that which gathers things into itself and keeps them — and the commentators explained the word in exactly these terms: the earth is a *kifāt* because it gathers and holds the living and the dead, containing them as a vessel contains what is placed in it, as a house gathers its inhabitants. The verse completes its meaning in the next line: the earth is a receptacle "for the living and the dead" (v. 26) — the living who move upon its surface and the dead who are entrusted to its interior. Al-Shaʿbī, the celebrated scholar of the generation after the Companions, expressed the meaning with a lapidary economy that Ibn Kathīr preserved in his commentary: he said of the earth, "Its interior is for your dead, and its surface is for your living." The verse is a question of the same *a-lam* form as the previous exhibits, expecting the answer "Yes indeed": every hearer knows that the earth holds him now and will hold him later — the living stand upon it at this moment, and the dead of all the generations lie within it — and the question turns that universal knowledge into evidence.

**The Earth as the Common House and the Common Grave**

The Qur'an's description of the earth's service to humanity is one of its most sustained themes, and this verse gathers both halves of it. The earth as the house of the living: *"He it is Who made the earth a bed for you and the sky a canopy"* (2:22); *"He Who made the earth a cradle for you and made paths for you therein, that haply you may be guided"* (43:10); *"Did We not make the earth a resting place, and the mountains as pegs?"* (78:6–7). The earth as the grave of the dead: every generation that has ever lived is, at this moment, within it — *"And We caused the earth to swallow him and his house"* (28:81, of Korah); *"Therein you shall live, and therein you shall die, and therefrom you shall be brought forth"* (7:25); *"From it We created you, and unto it We shall return you, and from it We shall bring you forth a second time"* (20:55). The commentators noted the extraordinary economy of the arrangement: the same earth serves both functions without conflict — the living build upon the ground that holds their dead, the farmer plows the field beneath which his fathers lie, and the two communities, the living and the dead, occupy the same planet in a partnership that no other creature shares. Al-Qurṭubī observed that the verse thereby answers a question every human being has asked: where do the dead go? They go where the living already stand — into the keeping of the same earth that has never yet failed to hold what was entrusted to it, and that will one day be commanded to return what it holds: *"When the earth is shaken with its shaking, and the earth casts forth its burdens"* (99:1–2).

**The Receptacle That Must One Day Give Back Its Trust**

The argumentative force of the verse for the sūrah's purpose lies in what a receptacle implies: everything that is placed in a container is placed there for a time, and the container is not the final destination of its contents. The dead are not destroyed by the earth; they are *held* by it — the Qur'an's word for the grave's action is not annihilation but keeping, and the earth is described not as the end of the dead but as their *kifāt*, their gathering-place, until the term of the gathering ends. The sūrah of the Earthquake describes the end of the holding: *"That Day it will tell its tidings, for your Lord has inspired it"* (99:4–5) — the earth itself becomes a witness, reporting what was done upon it; and the sūrah of the Splitting describes the release: *"When the earth is stretched out and casts forth what is within it and empties itself"* (84:3–4). The verse of al-Mursalāt plants the Resurrection in the most familiar ground imaginable: the same earth that receives the dead in every funeral is the earth that will give them back; the burial that the deniers take as the final proof of extinction — "When we are dead and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be raised?" (37:16) — is, on the Qur'an's reading, only a deposit, and the Depositor keeps His own books. The One who made the earth a receptacle for the dead has the keys of the receptacle: *"And We have made the earth disgorge its dead... the earth is the Lord's; He gives it as inheritance to whom He wills"* (7:128 in sense; cf. 19:40: "Truly We inherit the earth and all who are upon it, and unto Us they are returned").

**Burial as an Act of Mercy and an Act of Faith**

The verse also carries the Qur'an's quiet elevation of the human practice of burial, which the commentators connected to the very first burial in human history. When the son of Adam killed his brother and did not know what to do with the body, the Qur'an records: *"God sent a raven that scratched the earth, to show him how to conceal the nakedness of his brother"* (5:31). The earth as the receptacle of the dead is thus not a mere fact of nature; it is an arrangement of mercy — the dead are not left to rot upon the surface, exposed to the beasts and the sky; they are gathered into the earth's keeping, concealed, honored, and returned to the dust from which they came, as the Qur'an says of the human origin and end: *"Thereof We created you, and thereunto We return you, and therefrom We shall bring you forth a second time"* (20:55). The classical scholars drew from this verse and its sisters the dignity of the funeral rites — the washing, the shrouding, the burial, the prayer — as acts that honor the receptacle's trust: the body that was once the lodging of the spirit is returned to the earth with reverence, because it will be called forth again. And for the believer, every funeral is a rehearsal of the Day: the grave is, in the famous saying recorded from the early scholars and transmitted widely, "the first station of the Hereafter" — the first stopping place of the journey that this sūrah describes, where the two destinies begin to diverge, and where the earth's holding of the dead becomes the prelude to the earth's giving back.

**Standing on the Evidence**

The verse leaves its hearers standing, at the moment of recitation, upon the very evidence it cites. The reader of the sūrah is alive; he stands upon the earth; beneath him, within a few feet of the surface on which he stands, lie the dead of his own family, his own city, his own species — every generation that ever lived, held in the receptacle. The question "Did We not make the earth a receptacle?" cannot be answered with denial, because the answer is under the questioner's feet. And the inference is as unavoidable as the fact: the earth holds the dead, but it holds them as a trust, not as a finality; the same Hand that made the receptacle will command its release; the same Power that gathered the generations into the earth will gather them out of it — for the Day of Division, when, as this very sūrah will say at verse 38, God says to all of them: "We have gathered you and those of old." The denier who stands upon the earth is standing upon the proof of his own resurrection; the believer who stands upon it is standing upon the promise — and both are invited by the verse to look down, and to know that the ground beneath them is not the end of the road but the keeper of it until the Day when the road resumes.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:26

> **for the living and the dead?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Populations of the One Earth**

The verse completes the description of the earth's service: it is a receptacle *lil-aḥyāʾi wal-amwāt* — "for the living and the dead." The two words are comprehensive plurals: *al-aḥyāʾ*, the living, and *al-amwāt*, the dead — not some of each but all of each, the entire population of humanity in its two conditions, past, present, and future. The commentators unfolded the division of labor between the two populations with a precision that has never lost its force: the living occupy the surface of the earth — walking, building, planting, praying upon it — and the dead occupy its interior, entrusted to the ground in every generation since the first burial. Al-Ṭabarī explained the verse as the earth's two services: a dwelling for the living and a grave for the dead. The same earth, in the same place, serves both: the village stands where the ancestors lie; the field is sown above the bones of those who sowed it before; the child plays on the ground beneath which his great-grandfather rests. No other creature lives in such continuous intimacy with its own dead; no other creature buries; and the Qur'an presents the arrangement not as an accident but as a making — the *jaʿalnā* of the previous verse — a deliberate design in which the two populations of humanity, the living and the dead, are held by the same keeper, so that no generation ever loses sight, entirely, of what awaits the living.

**The Living From the Dead and the Dead From the Living**

The pairing of the living and the dead in this verse echoes one of the Qur'an's most persistent formulas for the divine power — the bringing forth of the living from the dead and the dead from the living. The sūrah of the Cattle states it as a sign: *"He brings forth the living from the dead, and brings forth the dead from the living. That is God — so how are you deluded?"* (6:95); and the sūrah of the Romans joins it to the resurrection directly: *"He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living, and revives the earth after its death, and so shall you be brought forth"* (30:19). The commentators read the verse of al-Mursalāt in this light: the earth that holds the living and the dead is not a passive warehouse but a scene of constant divine exchange — the living die into the earth, and from the earth, season after season, new life springs; the dead become dust, and from the dust the sown seed rises as living growth; and the same Power that performs this visible exchange, year after year, in every field and every grave, will perform it finally for humanity itself. The grave is planted with a body as the field is planted with a seed — and the Qur'an presses the agricultural analogy relentlessly because it is the most public evidence humanity possesses that what goes into the ground as dead comes out as living: *"And a sign for them is the dead land — We revive it and bring forth from it grain, and they eat of it"* (36:33).

**The Graveyard in the Middle of Every City**

There is a further dimension of the verse that speaks directly to the psychology of modern life: the arrangement it describes — the living and the dead sharing the same earth — is one that every human society has built into its geography, whether it acknowledges the meaning or not. The cemetery stands at the heart or the edge of every city; the dead are not removed from the earth of the living but deposited within it, in the same soil that supports the houses and the markets; and every funeral is a small enactment of the verse's division of labor — the living standing upon the surface, the dead being lowered into the interior, the same earth receiving and the same earth holding. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ turned this universal arrangement into a deliberate spiritual discipline when he encouraged the visiting of graves. He had forbidden it in the earliest period of Islam, when the new community was still close to its idolatrous past, and then, when the faith was established, he lifted the prohibition and made the visitation a means of grace, as Muslim records from Abū Hurayrah:

> "Visit the graves, for they remind you of death." (Muslim 976)

The believer who visits the graves performs, consciously, what every inhabitant of the earth performs unawares: he stands among the living and the dead, upon the receptacle that holds both, and allows the visible geography of the verse to teach its lesson — that the living and the dead are one community, divided only by a term, held by one earth, and bound for one gathering.

**The Two Classes as a Map of the Argument**

For the sūrah's argument, the division of the earth's population into the living and the dead is the final preparation for its great inference. The denier stands among the living — for now; the earth that holds the dead was holding, until recently, people exactly like him: people who ate, traded, built, and denied, and who are now within the receptacle. The verse asks him to look at the two classes and to see in their arrangement the entire case for the Resurrection. First, the dead are not nowhere; they are *somewhere* — held by the earth, known to God, awaiting the release that the earth cannot refuse when its Lord commands: *"And the earth will shine with the Light of its Lord, and the Book will be set in place"* (39:69). Second, the living are not permanent residents of the surface; every living person is a future member of the interior population, and the transition between the two classes is happening at every moment — the Qur'an describes death as the constant companion: *"Every soul shall taste death"* (3:185). Third, and decisively, the two classes are not two species but one species in two states: the dead are the living of yesterday, and the living are the dead of tomorrow — and the God who manages the exchange between the two states, visibly and without fail, in every generation, is the God who can manage the final exchange when the dead are commanded to live again. The verse leaves its hearer standing on the earth with both populations around him — the living he can see, the dead he cannot — and asks him to consider which of the two populations he will join, and what the Keeper of both has promised about the day when the receptacle gives up its trust.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:27

> **And We placed therein firm, soaring mountains and gave you sweet water to drink.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Gifts That Stabilize and Sustain**

The verse completes the sūrah's account of the earth with its two most conspicuous provisions: *wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātan* — "And We placed therein firm, soaring mountains, and gave you sweet water to drink." The mountains are described with two adjectives drawn from two roots that together paint their full service. *Rawāsiya* is from the root *r-s-w*, "to be firm, rooted, fixed": the Qur'an's standing word for the mountains as the earth's stabilizers — *"He cast onto the earth firm mountains (rawāsiya), lest it shake with you"* (16:15); *"And We placed upon the earth firm mountains, lest it shake beneath them"* (21:31); *"And the mountains as pegs"* (78:7). The second adjective, *shāmikhāt*, is from the root *sh-m-kh*, "to be high, lofty, towering": the mountains are not only rooted but soaring — the same word the Qur'an uses for the towering palms (50:10) and for the heights that human pride aspires to scale. The water is *furāt* — from the root *f-r-t*, "to be intensely sweet and fresh": the sweetest, purest drinking water, the word the Qur'an uses in its description of the two seas, one "palatable and sweet (furāt)" and the other "salty and bitter" (25:53). The verse thus gathers the two provisions that make human life on earth possible at all: stability — the ground that does not shake beneath the feet — and sweetness — the water that does not poison the throat. Remove either, and the earth could not be a receptacle for the living; provide both, and the earth becomes the *kifāt* of verse 25, a dwelling worthy of the name.

**The Mountain and the Spring: A Single System of Mercy**

The Qur'an's pairing of mountains and sweet water is not accidental juxtaposition; it describes a single system, and the commentators who reflected on the natural order drew the connection that every observer of mountain country knows: the soaring, rooted peaks are the earth's water-towers. The snow that gathers on the high peaks melts into the springs; the springs gather into the streams; the streams water the valleys where the living dwell; and the same mountains that hold the earth steady hold the water that makes the earth fruitful. The Qur'an points to the same system when it describes the earth's provision: *"And He placed therein firm mountains above it, and blessed it, and apportioned therein its nourishment in four days, equally, for those who ask"* (41:10). And the sūrah's larger argument — that the provisions of the earth are evidence of the Resurrection — is made explicit by the Qur'an in the verses that connect the watering of the dead land to the raising of the dead: *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humbled; and when We send down water upon it, it quivers and swells. Truly He Who revives it is the Reviver of the dead; He is Powerful over all things"* (41:39); *"And He it is Who sends down water from the sky in measure; and We revive thereby a dead land — even so shall you be brought forth"* (43:11). Al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī noted that the verse's reference to mountains and sweet water functions in this very way: God's creation of the towering mountains and the flowing springs is as wondrous as the Resurrection — for those who reflect — and the reflection is the bridge from the gift to the Giver, and from the Giver to His promise.

**Water That No Hand Can Charge For**

The description of the water as *furāt* — sweet — and the verb *asqaynākum* — "We gave you to drink" — carry a subtle lesson that the commentators drew with gratitude: the water that sustains every human being is given, not earned, and it is given sweet. The sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah presses the point as a question that no denier can answer: *"Have you considered the water you drink? Was it you who sent it down from the clouds, or was it We? If We willed, We could make it bitter (ujājan); so why do you not give thanks?"* (56:68–70). The water that gushes from the spring is sweet — and it did not have to be: the same earth that yields sweet water from its depths could yield brine; the same sky that sends rain could send salt; the sweetness of the world's drinking water is a continuous, unearned gift, renewed at every rainfall and every dawn of the spring. The verse of al-Mursalāt places the gift in the middle of its argument precisely because it is the most universal of provisions: the mountain that the denier sees every day and the water he drinks every hour are both *made* — and both are made sweet, steady, and sufficient, not by any power the denier can name but by the Power the sūrah is inviting him to acknowledge. The modern world, with its vast water systems and its desalination plants, has not changed the logic; it has only made the gift more visible by showing what it costs in labor and energy to produce, by human means, a fraction of what the sky and the springs deliver without invoice.

**The Two Provisions and the Two States of the Earth**

The verse's placement between the description of the earth as the receptacle of the dead (vv. 25–26) and the refrain over the deniers (v. 28) gives its two gifts a deeper resonance that the commentators did not fail to sound. The mountains and the sweet water are the provisions of the *living* — the equipment of the surface of the earth, where the living dwell; and the same earth that is so equipped for the living is the earth that holds the dead. The two provisions are, in effect, the furniture of the waiting room: the earth is arranged with everything the living need — stability, sweetness, provision — precisely because it is a place of sojourn, a dwelling for a term, a receptacle whose inhabitants are destined, one by one, to move from the surface to the interior. And the argument closes its circle: the God who equipped the surface with soaring mountains and sweet water for the living, and who made the interior a secure holding for the dead, is the God who will one day command the interior to release what it holds and the surface to become the plain of assembly — *"the day the earth is changed to other than the earth"* (14:48). The believer who drinks sweet water and looks at the mountains is taught by this verse to read both as promises: the provision of the present life is the signature of the Provider, and the Provider's power over the earth — to stabilize it, water it, and one day transform it — is the same power that will raise the dead from the receptacle, gather the living and the dead for the Day of Division, and give the reverent, at last, the drink that no spring of this world can match: *"and their Lord will give them to drink a pure beverage"* (76:21).

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:28

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Woe: The Verdict on the Thankless**

The refrain strikes for the fourth time, and its context marks a shift in the sūrah's indictment. The first three woes followed exhibits of *evidence* — the Day itself, the ruins of history, the embryo in the womb. This fourth woe follows the exhibit of *blessing*: the earth as a receptacle for the living and the dead (vv. 25–26), the soaring mountains that stabilize it, and the sweet water that sustains it (v. 27). Its target, as the commentators observed, is the denial that takes the form of ingratitude — the people who receive the earth, the mountains, and the water as their daily portion and never once acknowledge the Giver; who drink the sweet water and look at the towering peaks and find in them no message, no sign, no summons to thankfulness. The Qur'an names this class precisely in another sūrah: *"They recognize the blessing of God; then they deny it, and most of them are ungrateful"* (16:83). The recognition and the denial can coexist in the same heart: the blessing is known — everyone knows that water comes and the ground holds — and the knowledge is covered over, as the root of *kufr* itself suggests: *k-f-r* means to cover, so that the *kāfir* is literally the one who covers the blessing, wraps it in heedlessness, and lives upon it as though it had no Giver. This is the most common denial in the world — far commoner than the explicit denial of the Resurrection — and the sūrah, having described the blessings of the earth in verses 25–27, now pronounces its woe over those who deny the Giver of the gifts by never thanking Him for them.

**The Logic of Gratitude: Why Thanks Is Not Optional**

The verse rests upon a principle that the Qur'an states with uncompromising clarity: gratitude is not a decorative virtue but the very structure of justice between the creature and the Creator. The gifts of verses 25–27 are not contractual wages; they are unearned, continuous, and indispensable — the ground under the feet, the water in the throat, the mountains holding the earth steady — and the fitting response to unearned, continuous, indispensable giving is unceasing acknowledgment. The Qur'an makes the consequences of the response explicit in the verse that every Muslim recites in every prayer of supplication: *"And when your Lord proclaimed: 'If you give thanks, I shall surely increase you; but if you are ungrateful, truly My punishment is severe'"* (14:7). The logic of the increase is worth pausing on: thanksgiving does not benefit God — He is self-sufficient, as the Qur'an says in the same breath: *"Whoever gives thanks gives thanks only for his own soul; and whoever is ungrateful — truly God is Self-Sufficient, Praised"* (31:12). Thanksgiving benefits the thankless giver himself? No — it benefits the *thankful*: the heart that thanks opens itself to the perception of further giving, multiplies its own awareness of blessing, and thereby multiplies the blessing it can receive; while the heart that never thanks closes itself in a world it has reduced to entitlements, and in that closure loses the very capacity to receive — the punishment of ingratitude being, first of all, the shrinkage of the soul that ingratitude itself produces.

**The Thanklessness That Refuses the Inference**

The sūrah's placement of this woe, however, points to a deeper offense than mere discourtesy: the thankless denial of the blessings of the earth is a refusal of the *inference* that the blessings are engineered to produce. The Qur'an's method — throughout its pages and throughout this sūrah — is to lead the mind from the gift to the Giver and from the Giver to the promise: the earth that holds the living and the dead speaks of the One who holds both; the mountains that stabilize speak of the power that will one day shake the earth and scatter the mountains (as vv. 8–10 of this very sūrah described); the sweet water that revives the thirsty speaks of the water of life that God alone can give. The ingrate who drinks the water and never asks who made it sweet, who stands on the earth and never asks who made it steady, who sees the dead enter the ground and never asks who will call them forth — has refused, at the first step, the entire journey of inference that the sūrah has been conducting. He has, in the Qur'an's phrase, been given "eyes but does not see with them" (7:179, in sense) — and the woe of verse 28 falls upon the denial that hides inside the daily acceptance of gifts: the denial that never speaks but acts — living upon God, from birth to death, and never once turning to thank the One upon Whom it lives.

**The Increase That the Deniers Forfeit**

The woe over the thankless carries within it the quiet announcement of what ingratitude forfeits — and the commentators drew the contrast that gives the refrain its edge. The thankful are promised increase (14:7) — increase in this world's provision and in the perception that turns provision into praise, and ultimately the increase of the Hereafter, where the reward of the grateful is described in terms of the very blessings of this verse transfigured: the Garden with its shade and springs (77:41), its fruits without end, its water that never stagnates. The thankless deniers of verses 25–27 have the same earth, the same mountains, the same sweet water — and they receive them as the beasts receive them, without acknowledgment; and because they would not thank the Giver for the first gifts, the first gifts are all they will have: the sūrah will say it in verse 46, in its stinging address to the guilty — "Eat and enjoy yourselves a little" — the "little" being the measure of a provision that was never acknowledged as a gift and therefore never became a door to the greater giving. The woe of verse 28 is thus pronounced over a specific and terrible form of poverty: the poverty of those who possessed the whole earth and thanked no one — who were given the receptacle, the mountains, and the water, and answered the Giver with nothing — and who will stand, on the Day of Division, before the Giver of all gifts with empty hands, having spent their lives in the one transaction that adds nothing to the soul: receiving without thanking.

**The Warning That Can Still Be Answered**

Yet the refrain — as always in this sūrah — is a warning before it is a sentence, and its position after the description of the earth's blessings marks the exact point where the warning can still be answered. The denier addressed by this fourth woe is still standing on the earth of verse 25, still looking at the mountains of verse 27, still drinking the sweet water — the gifts are still arriving, and the acknowledgment can still be made. The believer's daily practice of gratitude — the *al-ḥamdu lillāh* that Islam has woven into the most ordinary moments: before drinking, after eating, upon waking, upon sleeping, upon every new blessing — is the standing answer to this woe: the continuous, conscious acknowledgment that the earth, the mountains, and the water are gifts, that the Giver is One, and that the fitting response to the first giving is the thanks that opens the door to the second. The classical scholars called gratitude the head of worship, and this woe shows why: the denial that the refrain condemns begins exactly where gratitude is refused — and the return from denial begins exactly where gratitude is offered. Whoever recites this verse and thanks God for the ground beneath him and the water within him has already begun the journey that the sūrah is conducting: the journey from the gift to the Giver, and from the Giver to the Day when the grateful and the thankless are divided, and the thanks of the grateful are answered with the increase that no earth and no water can contain.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:29

> **Away to that which you used to deny!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Voice That Speaks on the Day**

With verse 29 the sūrah crosses, for the first time, fully into the scene of the Hereafter, and the register changes from argument to address: *inṭaliqū ilā mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "Away to that which you used to deny!" The speaker is not identified in the text, and the commentators understood the address to come from God or from His angels — the keepers of the Fire receiving their charge — in either case, the voice of the divine command on the Day of Division. The verb *inṭaliqū* is an imperative of dismissal: "be gone, set off, depart" — the word used for sending someone away, and its abruptness is the point. The command contains no explanation, no negotiation, no appeal: the guilty are ordered to proceed to their destination, and the destination is described in the most withering terms the verse could choose — *mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn*, "that which you used to call false." The thing they denied is now the thing that receives them: the Fire, the punishment, the Day itself — the entire content of the promise that the sūrah's opening oaths affirmed and that the deniers spent their lives refusing. The verse's irony is total and terrible: in the world, the deniers said of the Fire and the Reckoning, "this is a fable of the ancients"; on the Day, the same deniers are ordered into the very reality they called a fable — and the order is phrased so that the denial itself names their destination: away, to the thing you denied!

**The Command That Mirrors the Choice**

The deep logic of the verse — and of the whole scene of which it is part — is that the command of the Day merely ratifies the choice of the world. The commentators drew out the correspondence with care: the deniers were never forced to deny; denial was their own act, repeated daily, in the face of the Reminder that came "to excuse or to warn" (v. 6). They were invited to believe and refused; they were warned and mocked; they were shown the ruins of the ancients, the embryo in the womb, the earth with its mountains and water — and they answered each exhibit with denial. On the Day, the command "Away to that which you used to deny!" does not impose a new destiny on them; it releases them to the destiny they chose: they denied the Fire, so they are sent to the Fire; they denied the truth of the promise, so the promise keeps them; they refused to take the path of faith, so the only path left is the one their denial built. The Qur'an states the same principle of correspondence in the verse that describes the exposure of the disbelievers to the Fire: *"On the Day when those who disbelieve are exposed to the Fire: 'Is this not the truth?' They say: 'Yes, by our Lord!' He says: 'Then taste the punishment for what you used to deny'"* (46:34); and in the sūrah of al-Sajdah: *"And as for those who are iniquitous, their refuge is the Fire. Whenever they wish to come forth from it, they are returned into it, and it is said to them: 'Taste the punishment of the Fire, which you used to deny'"* (32:20). The justice of the command is exact: no one is sent to anything he did not choose, in the sense that matters most — the direction of his own will through the whole course of his life.

**The Sting of the Phrase "You Used To"**

The verse's verb tenses carry a cruelty that is entirely self-inflicted, and the commentators paused over it. The clause *kuntum tukadhdhibūn* — "you used to deny" — is past tense: the denial is over. Its time has passed; its opportunities have closed; the deniers are addressed as those who *used to* deny, because on the Day there is no more denying to be done — the reality is before their eyes, the Fire is in their path, and the faculty of denial, which served them so well in the world, has nothing left to work on. In the world, denial was always possible: the denier could look at the evidence and turn away, could hear the warning and mock, could see the sign and explain it away — the world was constructed so that denial remained possible, because faith had to be free. On the Day, denial becomes impossible: the truth is no longer a claim to be accepted but a scene to be entered, and the only thing left of the deniers' long career of denial is the record of it — the "you used to" that names their past and fixes their sentence. The sting of the phrase is precisely that it is past: the thing they used to do can no longer be done, and the thing they used to deny has become the only reality they will ever know.

**The Contrast With the Address to the Believers**

The full meaning of the command "Away to that which you used to deny!" is sharpened by the contrast the Qur'an draws between this address and the address to the believers on the same Day. The deniers are ordered away with a dismissal — *inṭaliqū*, "be gone" — toward the Fire they denied. The believers are summoned with a welcome — *"Enter it in peace and security"* (15:46); *"Enter the Garden, you and your spouses, rejoicing"* (43:70); and, in this very sūrah at verse 43: "Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you used to do." The two addresses are mirror images: the believers are told to enter what they *used to do* — their deeds of faith and righteousness, which have become their reward; the deniers are told to go to what they *used to deny* — their denial, which has become their punishment. The Qur'an says it explicitly in the sūrah of the Cattle: the believers are told, "This is the Garden you have inherited for what you used to do" (7:43), and the deniers are told, "Taste the punishment for what you used to deny" (in sense of 6:30; 32:20). The Day of Division is the day when every soul's own history becomes its destination: deeds become gardens, denials become fires, and the two commands — "away" and "enter" — fall upon each company according to what each company itself became.

**A Warning for Those Who Still Have Time to Deny**

The verse's position in the sūrah — recited to the deniers of Makkah and to every later generation — gives it one final edge: it is spoken on the Day, but it is heard before the Day. The deniers to whom the Prophet ﷺ recited this sūrah still had time: their denial was still present tense, still revocable, still open to repentance; the command "Away to that which you used to deny!" had not yet been spoken over them, and the whole purpose of the sūrah's recitation was to make sure it never would be. The verse thus functions as a mirror held up to the present: every hearer is invited to ask himself what the command would name if it were spoken over him today — what has he denied, what has he called false, what has he refused to believe, and is the denial still present tense, still within the reach of the return? The believer who recites the verse is taught to answer the command before the Day by undoing the denial now: to believe what he was told to believe, to accept what he was told to accept, to turn from what he was told to turn — so that when the Day comes, and the two companies are divided, he will hear not "Away to that which you used to deny" but the address of the reverent: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:30

> **Away to a threefold shadow**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Repeats, the Destination Refines**

The dismissal of verse 29 is repeated and refined: *inṭaliqū ilā ẓillin dhī thalāthi shuʿab* — "Away to a shadow of three branches!" The repetition of the imperative *inṭaliqū* — the second "Away" in two verses — is itself a rhetorical act: the command is not content to be spoken once; it drives, as the drivers of cattle drive with repeated shouts. And the destination is now described, and the description is the cruelest irony in the sūrah so far: the deniers are ordered to a *ẓill* — a shadow, a shade. The word is the Qur'an's word for the most coveted comfort of the desert: the shade of the tree, the shadow of the rock, the canopy of the cloud — the relief that the parched traveler dreams of and the blessed of the Garden enjoy eternally: the sūrah itself will say of the reverent, in verse 41, "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs." The deniers are ordered, on the Day of Division, to a shade — as though the destination of those who denied the promise were a place of rest. And then the verse describes the shade, and the irony turns to horror: it is a shade *of three branches* — *dhī thalāthi shuʿab* — and the commentators, reading the phrase against the Qur'an's other descriptions of the Fire, explained the branches as the columns of the Fire's smoke, rising in three vast streams from the conflagration: the "shade" offered to the deniers is not the shade of trees but the shadow of burning, the darkness of smoke — the only "shade" that Hell contains.

**The Smoke of the Fire in Its Sister Sūrahs**

The Qur'an describes the "shade" of the Fire in a matching passage in the sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah, and the commentators on this verse read the two descriptions together, since they paint the same scene: *"And the companions of the left — what are the companions of the left? Amid scorching wind and boiling water, and a shadow of black smoke, neither cool nor generous"* (56:41–44). The word used there for the shadow of the Fire is *yaḥmūm*, the black, scorching smoke that rises from the blaze; here the same shadow is described as having three branches. Al-Zamakhsharī and the commentators after him explained the image by reference to the enormity of the Fire: when a conflagration reaches the scale of the Fire of Hell, its smoke does not rise in a single column but in three — vast streams of blackness climbing from the burning, each branch a river of smoke, joining overhead into the canopy that the verse ironically names a "shade." Some of the early commentators pictured the three branches as the three divisions of the smoke itself; others noted that the image of the enormous fire, whose smoke splits into columns visible from the farthest distance, is drawn from the greatest fires human beings have witnessed — and the verse declares that the Fire of the Day exceeds them all, as verse 32 will say of its sparks: "like massive tree trunks."

**The Shade That Is Worse Than the Sun**

The verse's deeper point is the total inversion of comfort in the Fire, and the commentators articulated the inversion with precision. In this world, shade is the relief from heat: the traveler crosses the burning plain toward the shadow of the rock because the shadow is cool, because the shade of a thing cannot be hotter than the thing itself, because shadow means protection from the flame. The "shade" of the Fire violates every law of shade: it is produced by the flame, it is made of smoke, it is the shadow of burning rather than the shadow of shelter — and a shadow that comes from the Fire can only deliver what the Fire delivers. The Qur'an describes the same inversion in its account of the people of the Fire seeking shade from the blaze: the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf pictures them calling to the keepers of the Fire for relief, and the sūrah of al-Furqān says of their abode: *"When they are cast into a narrow place therein, bound in chains, they will call out there for destruction"* (25:13). The "threefold shadow" is thus the Fire's parody of the Garden's shade: the reverent will be "amid shade and springs" (77:41) — shade that is *ẓill ẓalīl*, deep and cool (56:30: "and shade outspread") — while the deniers will be sent to a shadow of smoke, a shade of branches that are not trees but pillars of burning, a canopy that protects from nothing. Al-Qurṭubī noted the lesson: the deniers are offered in the Hereafter the exact image of what they sought in the world — but the image is realized according to what they truly loved: they loved comfort without God, and the only comfort left to those who would not sit in the shade of His mercy is the shade of His Fire.

**The Irony That Cuts Both Ways**

The verse's irony cuts in a second direction that the commentators also drew: the "shade" of the Fire is described as having three branches, and the number carries its own echo of the blessings the deniers rejected. The Qur'an repeatedly numbers the comforts of the Garden and the mercies of the world in clusters — the threefold darkness of the womb from which they were brought forth (39:6), the threefold stages of their creation that verse 22 of this very sūrah described in its "known measure" — and the shadow of three branches stands as the dark counterpart of every threefold mercy: the creature who was formed in threefold darkness, protected by the mercy of God at every stage, is sent at the end to a threefold shadow that protects him from nothing. Some commentators even noted that the "shadow of three branches" is the Fire's answer to the three exhibits of the sūrah's argument — the ruins, the embryo, the earth — as though the Day were saying: you denied the mercy that made you, held you, and watered you, and the only shadow left to you is the shadow of what your denial has become. The image is designed to be unbearable to the imagination — and it is designed that way deliberately, so that the hearer, still in this world, still under the real shade of the real mercy, may look at the threefold shadow of the Fire and choose, while the choice remains, the shade that is outspread.

**The Command Still Spoken as Warning**

For the believer who recites this verse in the present world, the command "Away to a threefold shadow" is heard at a distance that is itself a mercy. The command has not yet been spoken over him — if it were, he would not be reciting but running — and the fact that he can still hear it as a warning rather than experience it as a sentence is the opening through which the entire sūrah operates. The verse does not describe the shadow of the Fire for the sake of description; it describes it so that the denier may see his destination while the road still has turns, and so that the believer may measure, against the darkness of the threefold shadow, the value of the shade he has been given — the shade of faith, the shade of the revealed guidance, the shade of the mercy that the Qur'an describes as the believer's shelter in this world: *"And God is the Protector of the believers"* (3:68, in sense). Whoever recites this verse and then turns, in the same breath, to the mercy of God in repentance has understood the sūrah's method: the threefold shadow is painted so that no one will have to enter it — and the shade of the Garden is promised so that everyone who turns may find, at the end of the turning, not the smoke of the Fire but the shade outspread and the springs that never fail.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:31

> **that provides no shade, nor avails against the flame.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Anatomy of a Useless Shelter**

The description of the threefold shadow completes itself in the most devastating terms the verse could choose: *lā ẓalīlin wa-lā yughnī min al-lahab* — "that provides no shade (no cooling shelter), nor avails against the flame." The first clause negates the essence of shade: *ẓalīl* is the shade that actually shelters — the deep, cool, covering shadow — and the verse declares that the threefold shadow has none of it: it is a shadow without shadowing, a canopy without covering, a shelter that shelters nothing. The second clause negates the purpose of shade: even if the smoke-shadow gave some covering, it could not protect against *al-lahab* — the flame, the blazing fire, from the root *l-h-b*, the same root as the *lahab* of the sūrah of al-Masad ("a flame of fire" awaiting Abū Lahab, 111:3) — the Fire's own heat and blaze. The verse thus performs a double negation with legal precision: the shade of the Fire neither cools (*lā ẓalīl*) nor shields (*lā yughnī min al-lahab*) — useless in itself, useless against the flame — and between the two negations every hope of relief is closed. The commentators noted the completeness of the closure: in this world, even the poorest shelter offers something — the shadow of a wall cools though it cannot stop a fire, water quenches though it cannot stop a flood — but the denizens of the Fire are given a "shade" that has been emptied of both functions of shade: it does not cool, and it does not protect. The verse is the Qur'an's way of saying that Hell contains no mitigation: every element of it is what it is without remainder, and the mercy that in this world mingles with every punishment — the mercy that makes even fire cast a shadow a man might stand in — is entirely absent from the Fire.

**The Scorching Wind and the Boiling Water**

The Qur'an describes the environment of the Fire as the systematic removal of every earthly comfort, and the commentators read this verse within that larger portrait. The sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah describes the companions of the left as being "amid scorching wind (*samūm*) and boiling water (*ḥamīm*)" (56:42) — the wind itself is burning, so there is no breeze to relieve; the water itself is boiling, so there is no drink to quench — and the sūrah of al-Ghāshiyah adds: *"They will have no food but bitter thorn, which neither nourishes nor avails against hunger"* (88:6–7). The verse of al-Mursalāt belongs to the same system of total inversion: the shade does not shade, the food does not nourish, the drink does not quench, the wind does not cool — every element of the Fire is present in the Fire and absent in its mercy, and the damned are surrounded by the forms of comfort that have been emptied of their content. Al-Qurṭubī and others drew the lesson that the Fire's torments are described in the Qur'an as the *exact* privation of the world's goods, because the soul that loved the world's goods without their Giver is punished by the goods themselves becoming its torment: the shade it loved is now smoke, the water it drank without thanks is now boiling, the fire it used without awe is now its element.

**The Futility of Every Refuge on That Day**

The verse also carries a chilling completeness that the commentators articulated: the negation "nor avails against the flame" closes the door on the last resource of the desperate. In this world, when a person is caught in a disaster, there is always something to try: the shadow of a wall, the speed of one's legs, the help of another, the hope of rescue — and even when all fails, death ends the endurance. The denizens of the Fire are pictured in the Qur'an trying every resource, and each is closed: they call for water — and are given boiling water (18:29); they call for their destroyers — and are answered with the sentence (38:61–63, in sense); they ask to be returned to the world to do good — and are refused (32:12); they ask for a respite — and are told there is none (14:44); they seek shade from the flames — and are given the shadow of smoke, which this verse strips of both its functions. The phrase "nor avails against the flame" is the summary of all their failed refuges: the shade does not avail, the intercession of their idols does not avail, their former powers do not avail, their wealth and their children do not avail — *"the day when neither wealth nor sons will avail, save him who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). On that Day, nothing avails against the flame except what was chosen before the flame: faith, and the deeds that faith produced.

**Why the Description of the Fire Is Itself a Mercy**

There is a final perspective on this verse that the spiritual commentators never tired of sounding, and it turns the verse's terror into a present mercy. The shadow of the Fire is described in the Qur'an — described to people who are still in this world, still standing under real shade, still able to choose. The description of the Fire's useless shade is itself a shade: a warning given in advance, a picture of the destination painted before the journey is taken, so that no one may arrive at the threefold shadow by surprise. The Prophet ﷺ said, as recorded in the authentic collections: *"The Fire complained to its Lord, saying: 'My Lord, parts of me devour other parts.' So He gave it two breaths: one in winter and one in summer — the intensity of the cold you feel is from its breath of winter, and the intensity of the heat you feel is from its breath of summer"* (al-Bukhārī 3260; Muslim 617). The scholars drew from this report the mercy within the terror: the Fire's breath is restrained in this world to a bearable fraction, precisely so that the world may remain a place of trial rather than a foretaste of the Fire — and the descriptions of the Fire in the Qur'an are given for the same reason: restrained to words, so that the reality may be avoided. The believer who recites this verse and feels its weight is being given, in that feeling, the very warning that can save him from the reality: the shadow that does not shade is still far off; the flame is still avoidable; and the verse that describes the destination of denial is itself an act of the mercy that the deniers denied — the mercy that warns before it strikes, and that will keep warning, as this sūrah keeps warning, until the last denier has heard the last woe and made his final choice.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:32

> **Indeed, it throws up sparks like massive tree trunks,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire That Casts Its Sparks Like Castles**

The scene of the Fire deepens: *innahā tarmī bi-shararin ka-l-qaṣr* — "Indeed, it throws up sparks like massive tree trunks." The verse pictures the Fire itself now — not its shadow but its blaze — and the image is drawn on a scale that dwarfs every fire its hearers had ever seen. The word *tarmī* means "it throws, it hurls, it casts" — the Fire is an active agent, flinging its sparks abroad as a warrior hurls his missiles. The sparks are *sharar* — the flying fragments of burning matter that leap from a great fire — and they are compared to *al-qaṣr*, the word whose double meaning the commentators preserved with care: *qaṣr* is a massive tree trunk — the bole of an ancient palm or oak, dragged to the fire and consumed — and it is equally the word for a castle or palace, the *qaṣr* of a king. In either sense the image is of size beyond all earthly measure: the sparks of this Fire are not embers that a man could brush from his sleeve; they are as massive as the trunks of great trees, as huge as towers — the Qur'an's way of saying, as the commentators noted, that the flames of the Fire of Hell are larger than anything those who hear the description could have encountered in this life. The same scale appears in the Qur'an's companion descriptions of the Fire's enormity: *"And what shall apprise thee of the Scorching Fire? It is a Fire kindled by God, which mounts over the hearts"* (104:5–7); and the sūrah of al-Maʿārij describes the Day's approach in terms of a fire that no power can put out: *"They will see it from afar, and will hear its raging and roaring"* (25:12, in sense).

**A Fire Fed by Men and Stones**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire's fuel give the image of the hurled sparks its full theological depth: *"O you who believe! Shield yourselves from the Fire, whose fuel is men and stones"* (66:6); *"And as for those who disbelieve — is it not that their deeds are like a mirage... Or like darkness upon an abysmal sea"* (24:39–40, in sense); and the sūrah of al-Baqarah: *"whose fuel is men and stones, prepared for the deniers"* (2:24). The Fire of Hell is not fed by wood alone — it is fed by the very deniers it consumes and by the stones they worshipped, and its sparks are flung on a scale that corresponds to its fuel: a fire whose fuel is souls and idols will cast sparks like towers. The commentators drew the connection between the verse before us and the Qur'an's insistence on the Fire's permanence and magnitude: the fire of this world is fed and therefore dies; the Fire of the Hereafter is fed by its own inhabitants and therefore never dies — *"Whenever their skins are consumed, We replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment"* (4:56). The sparks like tree trunks and the flames beyond earthly measure are the visible signature of a fire whose logic is not combustion but justice: a fire that burns without consuming its fuel to extinction, because the fuel is the guilty themselves and the justice that feeds it is eternal.

**The Scale of the Image as a Barrier to Imagination**

The verse's image functions, paradoxically, by exceeding the imagination it addresses. Every hearer of the Qur'an had stood by a great fire — the campfire of the desert night, the furnace of the blacksmith, the burning of a palm grove — and every hearer knew the largest fire he had ever seen. The verse takes the largest known fire and multiplies its sparks to the size of trees and towers, and the multiplication is deliberate: the verse is saying that the Fire of the Day is not the fire of this world at a larger scale but a different order of reality entirely — as different as the Day of Division itself is from the days of this world. The commentators noted the parallel between this verse's method and the method of the whole sūrah's eschatology: the Qur'an describes the Hereafter in the images of this world precisely because the hearer has no other language, but it always stretches the images beyond their earthly measure — the shade that does not shade (v. 31), the sparks like tree trunks (v. 32), the camels of verse 33 — so that the hearer understands that the reality exceeds the telling, exactly as verse 14 said of the Day of Division itself: "And what shall apprise thee of the Day of Division?" The image that bursts the imagination is the Qur'an's way of pointing to a reality that the imagination cannot contain — the reality whose description must be believed without being exhaustively pictured, and whose terror must be feared without being fully known.

**The Sparks and the Sūrah's Own Structure**

There is a subtle connection between the image of the hurled sparks and the structure of the sūrah itself that the literary-minded commentators appreciated. The Fire "throws" its sparks abroad — *tarmī* — and the sūrah has been throwing its warnings abroad in exactly the same way: the refrain of woe, ten times, flung into the text like sparks from a fire, each occurrence landing in a new context and setting it alight. The image of the Fire that hurls sparks like towers is the sūrah's self-portrait as much as its portrait of Hell: the warnings of the Qur'an are sparks from the Fire, cast into the world before the Fire itself arrives, so that the deniers may see, in miniature and in advance, what they are denying. The Prophet ﷺ described his own warning in exactly these terms when he said, as recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿĀʾishah: *"O people, guard yourselves against the Fire, even if by half a date [given in charity]"* (al-Bukhārī 1417, in sense; Muslim 1016) — the warning itself being a spark thrown before the blaze. And the believer who recites this verse is being shown the Fire's sparks while he is still at a distance from the Fire — still able to turn, still able to shelter, still able to answer the warning of the sparks with the only response that extinguishes them: faith, repentance, and the deeds that the sūrah will describe in its portrait of the reverent, who are "amid shade and springs" — beyond the reach of the Fire and of every spark it hurls.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:33

> **as though they were yellow camels.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sparks Become a Herd on the Move**

The second half of the comparison completes the picture with an image drawn from the deepest well of the desert imagination: *ka-annahu jimālatun ṣufr* — "as though they were yellow camels." The sparks of the Fire, as massive as tree trunks and towers (v. 32), are now seen in motion, and the motion is compared to a herd of camels — *jimālah* being a collective for camels, and *ṣufr* meaning yellow. The commentators explored the image in both of its dimensions, and the readings are preserved in the sources with unusual richness. Al-Zamakhsharī explained the comparison as one of size and length: the sparks are like camels — large, long-bodied, moving in a stream — and yellow, like the sparks of a blazing fire, so that the verse pictures the Fire hurling its enormous sparks in a continuous torrent, as a herd of tawny camels pours across the plain. But other commentators — including al-Jalālayn and al-Shawkānī, who preserved the reading — noted a second dimension that changes the color of the image entirely: the Arabs called certain camels "yellow" (*ṣufr*) whose hair was in fact dark, almost black, with only a touch of yellow in it; and on this reading the sparks of the Fire are not bright and golden but black — pitch-black fragments, like the blackest camels, flung from a Fire whose blaze is itself dark. The verse's image, on this reading, carries the same inversion that verse 31 established for the Fire's shade: the Fire of Hell is not the fire of this world — bright, warm, useful — but its total opposite: a fire whose sparks are black, whose shade is smoke, whose light, if it has any, serves only to show the damned the enormity of what surrounds them.

**The Black Fire: An Inversion of Every Earthly Fire**

The reading of the sparks as black camels opens the verse's deepest meaning, and the commentators who adopted it drew out the theology of the image with care. Fire in this world is one of the greatest of God's mercies: it is light in the darkness, warmth in the cold, the means of cooking food, forging metal, and signaling across distances — the Qur'an counts it among the signs: *"He it is Who made for you fire from the green tree, and behold, you kindle from it"* (36:80); *"Have you considered the fire you kindle? Was it you who made its tree grow, or was it We? We made it a reminder and a comfort for the travelers"* (56:71–73). The Fire of Hell is that mercy inverted point by point: its smoke is the only shade, its sparks are black, its heat burns without light's comfort, its fuel is the guilty themselves — a fire with none of the positive properties that make fire in this world a blessing. The verse's "yellow camels," read as the black camels the Arabs called yellow, is the Qur'an's most economical statement of the inversion: even the color of the Fire is wrong — the sparks that should be golden are black, the blaze that should illumine only reveals, and the damned are surrounded by a fire that gives neither light nor warmth in any useful sense, but only the reality of burning. Al-Shawkānī and others noted that the image of the blackened herd streaming across the sky gives the scene a weight of dread that no bright flame could carry: the darkness of the sparks suggests the darkness of the whole scene — the Fire whose smoke covers the horizon, whose only "shade" is the threefold shadow of verse 30, and whose black sparks stream through the black air like a herd of black camels in the night.

**The Herd and the Scale of the Day**

Whichever reading of the color one adopts, the image of the camels carries the comparison to the scale of the desert itself, and the commentators drew out the geography of the simile. The camel is the largest thing in motion that the desert dweller knows; a herd of camels in full drive is the most formidable sight in the landscape of the Arabs — the ground shakes, the dust rises, the stream of bodies pours without stopping, and nothing in its path stands against it. The verse says: the sparks of the Fire stream as a herd streams — in number beyond counting, in size beyond the single spark, in motion beyond stopping — and the Fire hurls them continuously, as verse 32 said (*tarmī*, present tense, ongoing). The image also carries an echo that the commentators noted: in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Day itself, the camels appear again in the dissolution of the world's wealth — *"And when the pregnant camels are neglected"* (81:4) — the she-camels, the pride of Arabian wealth, abandoned and unmilked because the Day has made all wealth worthless. The sparks like yellow camels are thus the dark mirror of that image: on the Day, the wealth of the world is neglected because the Fire of the world to come has taken its place — and the herds that once measured a man's riches stream now as sparks from the Fire of the guilty, the wealth that was loved without its Giver becoming part of the very punishment of those who loved it.

**What the Image Does to the Hearer**

The verse's power over its first hearers — and over every hearer since — lies in the intimacy of its imagery, and the commentators understood that the Qur'an chose the camel precisely because it was the most familiar measure of magnitude and movement in the Arabian landscape. The Makkan deniers to whom this sūrah was recited had seen herds; they had seen fires; they knew exactly how large a spark is and exactly how large a camel is; and the verse takes the two measures and multiplies them across each other — sparks the size of trunks, streaming like camels, black as the blackest beasts — until the imagination, built from the familiar, is forced beyond the familiar into a scene that no desert fire and no desert herd could produce. The verse thereby does what all the Qur'an's eschatological imagery does: it builds the Hereafter from the bricks of this world and then sets the building beyond anything this world contains. For the denier, the image is a warning that the thing he denied has a scale his denial never measured; for the believer, it is a reminder of the mercy that saved him from the scene — and both are invited, while the recitation is still in their ears and the sparks of the verse are still in the air, to answer the image with the only act that can place them beyond the reach of the Fire: the act that the sūrah's next verses will describe, when the scene turns from the Fire's sparks to the silence of the guilty — and then, at verse 41, from the silence of the guilty to the shade and springs of the reverent, who heard the same warnings, saw the same sparks, and chose, while the choice was open, the mercy that the deniers denied.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:34

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth Woe: The Verdict on the Deniers of the Fire**

The refrain strikes for the fifth time, and its context is now the Fire itself — the threefold shadow that gives no shade (vv. 30–31), the sparks like massive trunks (v. 32), the stream of sparks like yellow camels (v. 33). Al-Shawkānī noted that this occurrence of the woe can be read, with particular fitness, as the verdict on those who denied the Fire itself — who denied that there is any such punishment, who mocked the descriptions of Hell as fables, who said, as the Qur'an records the deniers of every age saying: *"They say: 'There is nothing but our present life; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time'"* (45:24). The placement of the woe directly after the Qur'an's most sustained portrait of the Fire gives the refrain a new and terrible function: the portrait has been painted — shadow, flame, sparks — and the woe pronounces the sentence over those who, having heard the portrait, denied the original. The logic of the sequence is the logic of the whole sūrah: every exhibit is followed by its verdict, and the exhibit of the Fire is followed by the verdict over those who deny the Fire — the deniers who are told, in the words of the sūrah of al-Sajdah: *"And as for those who are iniquitous, their refuge is the Fire. Whenever they wish to come forth from it, they are returned into it, and it is said to them: 'Taste the punishment of the Fire, which you used to deny'"* (32:20). The fifth woe is the sūrah's way of saying that the denial of the Fire is the most dangerous of all denials, because the thing denied is the very thing that waits.

**Why People Deny the Fire**

The verse invites reflection on the psychology of the denial it condemns, and the Qur'an itself provides the material. People deny the Fire, the commentators observed, for a cluster of reasons that the revealed text names one by one. Some deny it out of *hope without ground*: they trust that God's mercy will save them whatever they do, and the Qur'an answers: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against their own souls, do not despair of the Mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins"* (39:53) — but the same verses call the transgressors to return penitently *before* the punishment arrives, because mercy meets the penitent, not the defiant. Some deny it out of *presumption of worth*: they measure themselves by their lineage, their wealth, or their own opinion of their goodness, and the Qur'an answers those who said the Fire would not touch them: *"And they say: 'The Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days.' Say: 'Have you taken a covenant with God? God will not fail His covenant — or do you say of God what you do not know?'"* (2:80). And some deny it out of the *hardening of habit*: the repeated refusal of the warning gradually convinces the refuser that the warning was false — *"Nay, but that which they used to earn has covered their hearts with rust"* (83:14). The fifth woe falls upon the whole cluster of denials at once, and its placement after the portrait of the Fire declares that the portrait was painted precisely so that none of these denials would remain possible: the Fire has been described in words that strain the imagination, so that no hearer may say he was not told, not warned, not shown.

**The Fire That the Deniers Carry With Them**

There is a further dimension of this woe that the spiritual commentators drew from the Qur'an's own descriptions, and it gives the fifth occurrence of the refrain a piercing inwardness. The Qur'an describes the Fire not only as an external scene but as an internal reality: *"It is the Fire of God, kindled, which mounts over the hearts"* (104:6–7) — the Fire that "mounts over the hearts" of those who, in this world, fed their hearts on what the Fire punishes: envy, arrogance, greed, and denial. The commentators who reflected on this verse said: the deniers of the Fire are carrying its fuel within them — the habits of the heart that the Fire is made of, the loves and hates and refusals that will become, on the Day, the element in which they burn. The fifth woe thus condemns not only the denial of a future reality but the present reality of the denial itself: the heart that denies the Fire is already being shaped by what the Fire punishes; the soul that refuses the warning is already hardening into the state that the warning describes; and the Fire that the deniers deny is, in the most precise sense, already burning within the dispositions they refuse to examine. The woe that follows the portrait of the Fire is the woe over the heart that has become its own fuel.

**The Portrait as a Mercy to the Deniers**

And yet — as with every occurrence of the refrain — the fifth woe is preceded by the portrait that makes it avoidable, and the commentators never lost sight of the mercy within the menace. The Fire has been described in this sūrah with images of unbearable vividness — the shadow that does not shade, the sparks like towers, the stream of black camels — and the description was not given to the deniers of Makkah as a foretaste of their sentence but as a warning before their sentence: the portrait is painted while the Fire is still future, still avoidable, still escapable by the one act that the whole sūrah is calling for — the cessation of denial. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said: *"The Fire was shown to me, and most of its inhabitants were women who were ungrateful"* — and when asked whether they were ungrateful to God, he answered: *"They are ungrateful to their husbands and ungrateful for kindness"* (al-Bukhārī 1052, in sense; Muslim 907) — showing that the Fire's population is recruited, in every age, not only from explicit deniers of the Resurrection but from those who denied the mercies placed daily before them. The fifth woe is recited so that its hearers may examine themselves while the examination can still change the outcome: to ask whether they have denied the Fire, whether they have denied the mercies, whether the fuel is already gathering in their hearts — and to answer the woe, while it is still a recitation rather than a reality, with the turning that the sūrah will describe in its portrait of the reverent: the turning that leads, at verse 41, out of the range of the Fire's sparks and into the shade and springs that the Fire cannot reach.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:35

> **That is the Day on which they speak not;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Silence of the Guilty at the Moment of the Verdict**

With verse 35 the sūrah turns from the scene of the Fire to the scene of the judgment itself, and the turn is marked by the most unnerving detail in the entire portrait: *hādhā yawmu lā yantiqūn* — "This is the Day on which they speak not." The demonstrative *hādhā* — "this" — points directly at the Day that the whole sūrah has been describing: the Day of Division announced in verse 13, the Day for which the messengers were slated (vv. 11–12), the Day whose denial the refrain has been condemning. And the first detail of that Day is silence — the silence of the guilty. The verb *yantiqūn*, from the root *n-t-q*, "to speak, to utter," is negated absolutely: they do not speak, they cannot speak, they are not permitted to speak. The commentators explained the silence with care, drawing on the Qur'an's own accounts of the Day's procedure: the silence of the guilty is not the silence of ignorance — they know exactly what they did; it is the silence of the case being closed. The Qur'an describes the Day as the day when speech is replaced by evidence: *"On the Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet bear witness against them for what they used to do"* (24:24); *"This Day We seal their mouths, and their hands speak to Us, and their feet bear witness to what they used to earn"* (36:65); *"Until, when they come to it, their ears and their eyes and their skins bear witness against them for what they used to do"* (41:20). The silence of verse 35 is the silence of the defendant whose every organ has become a witness and whose every excuse has been preempted: there is nothing left to say, because everything has already been recorded, and the records themselves have taken the stand.

**The Many Speeches That End in Silence**

The Qur'an elsewhere gives the guilty their speech on the Day — and then records the silence into which every speech collapses. They speak to deny: *"Then they will have no excuse save to say: 'By God our Lord, we were not idolaters!'"* (6:23) — and their own bodies contradict them. They speak to bargain: *"And if you could see when the guilty hang their heads before their Lord: 'Our Lord, we have seen and heard; so return us, and we shall do right — now we are certain'"* (32:12) — and the answer is refusal. They speak to shift blame: *"They will say: 'Our Lord, we obeyed our leaders and our elders, and they led us astray from the way'"* (33:67) — and their leaders answer: *"Nay — you were not believers"* (37:29, in sense). They speak to appeal: *"Our Lord, bring us out of it; if we return [to evil], then we are truly wrongdoers"* (23:107) — and the answer comes: *"He will say: 'Be cast into it, and speak not to Me!'"* (23:108). The sūrah of the Believers records the final silencing with a starkness that matches this verse. Every avenue of speech is explored in the Qur'an's accounts and every avenue closes: denial is contradicted by the witnesses, bargaining is refused by the decree, blame-shifting is rejected by the truth, and appeal is cut off by the sentence. Verse 35 of al-Mursalāt is the summary of all these scenes in one line: the Day on which they speak not — because every word available to them has been spent, and every word spent has become evidence.

**Why the Guilty Are Not Even Asked**

The verse's companion (v. 36) will add that permission to speak is not even granted — and the commentators explained the refusal by the futility of the speech itself. In the courts of this world, the defendant is heard because his testimony might alter the outcome: he might be innocent, or mistaken, or able to explain. On the Day of Division, no such possibility remains: the records are complete, the witnesses are the sinner's own limbs, the Judge is the One who knows the hidden and the more hidden (20:7), and the sentence follows the record with a necessity that no speech could interrupt. The Qur'an says of the Day's procedure: *"On that Day, no soul will be wronged in anything, and you will not be recompensed except for what you used to do"* (36:54); and the records are read openly: *"And the Book will be set in place, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say: 'Oh, woe to us! What is this Book that leaves nothing, small or great, but has counted it!' And they will find all they did present, and your Lord wrongs no one"* (18:49). The silence of the guilty is thus the silence of total accountability: there is nothing to add to a record that omits nothing, nothing to explain to a Judge who witnessed everything, nothing to appeal against a justice that wrongs no one. The scholars said: in this world, speech is a mercy — the accused may defend himself because human judgment is fallible; on that Day, speech is closed because divine judgment is perfect, and the only speech that could have saved the guilty was the speech of this world: the confession, the repentance, the plea for mercy made while mercy was still being offered — the speech of the believer's prayer, which the deniers never prayed.

**The Silence of This World as a Warning**

The verse's placement in the sūrah gives it a present function that the commentators drew out for the living: the Day of silence is described now, while speech is still possible. In this world, the deniers speak constantly — they speak against the messengers, they mock the Reminder, they argue the Resurrection, they fill the air with their denials and their excuses; the Qur'an records the verbosity of denial: *"And when Our signs are recited to them, they say: 'This is nothing but fables of the ancients'"* (83:13, in sense). The verse announces that this torrent of speech ends in a single Day of silence — that every word spoken against the truth in this world is stored, and that the Day will not be a continuation of the debate but its end. For the believer, the verse carries the corresponding lesson: the speech that matters is the speech spoken while speech is still efficacious — the word of faith, the word of repentance, the word of prayer, the word of truth spoken in the world where words still change things. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47) — the believer's whole life is a discipline of speech in the light of the Day of silence: every word weighed, because every word is recorded; every silence chosen when speech would be evil; and the tongue trained, in the world of speech, to say what it would be glad to have said when the Day comes in which the guilty speak not — and the reverent, as the sūrah will show at verse 43, are addressed with the only speech that the Day of Division still contains for its blessed: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:36

> **nor is permission granted them, such that they might offer excuses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Door of Speech Closed From the Other Side**

Verse 35 established the silence of the guilty as a fact; verse 36 establishes it as a decree: *wa-lā yuʾdhanu lahum fa-yaʿtadhirūn* — "nor is permission granted them, such that they might offer excuses." The first clause is a passive with the sense of prohibition: they are not given leave — *lā yuʾdhanu* — the permission is withheld, and the withholding is an act: the silence of the guilty is not an accident of shock but an order of the court. The second clause states the purpose that the withholding frustrates: *fa-yaʿtadhirūn* — "so that they might offer excuses" — the subjunctive after the negative showing that the excuse is exactly what they would attempt if the floor were theirs, and that the floor is precisely what is denied them. The verse thus pictures the guilty as straining toward speech — the excuse rising in their throats, the defense forming on their tongues — and the permission that would release it withheld. The commentators preserved two complementary readings of the verse's second clause, as the source tradition notes. On the first reading, the clause states what the guilty desire: they are not permitted to speak, so that they cannot offer their excuses — the permission is denied precisely because the excuses are worthless. On the second reading, which al-Qurṭubī recorded and favored, the negation governs the whole: "they are not permitted to speak, and they do not offer excuses" — the guilty are so utterly silenced that even the attempt at excuse is beyond them: they do not speak, and they do not even make the move of excusing, because the reality of their deeds lies fully manifest before them and every excuse has died in their throats before it could be born.

**The Excuses That Would Have Been Offered**

The verse's mention of excuses opens a window onto what the guilty would say if they could — and the Qur'an elsewhere gives their intended speeches in full, so that the reader may measure the justice of the silencing. They would excuse themselves by *ignorance*: "Had we but listened or reflected, we would not be among the inhabitants of the Blaze" (67:10) — and the record answers that they listened and refused. They would excuse themselves by *following*: "Our Lord, we obeyed our leaders and our elders, and they led us astray from the way" (33:67) — and the record answers that no one compelled their obedience. They would excuse themselves by *environment*: "Our Lord, we were driven out from our land and our children" — the excuse of the oppressed (in the sense of 4:97, where the angels ask those who wronged themselves why they remained in oppression when emigration was possible). And the sūrah of the Cattle records the most desperate excuse of all: the denial of the denial — *"Then they will have no excuse save to say: 'By God our Lord, we were not idolaters!'"* (6:23). Every excuse that the human being can offer for his evil is anticipated in the Qur'an's accounts of the Day, and every one is answered before it is spoken: the excuse of ignorance by the Reminder that was delivered, the excuse of following by the conscience that was free, the excuse of environment by the examples of those who resisted, the excuse of denial by the records that were kept. The verse of al-Mursalāt declares that the Day does not even open the floor for these speeches: the excuses are known in advance to be excuses — and the permission that would allow them to be spoken is withheld, so that the guilty may not add the sin of false excuse to the burden of their deeds.

**Why Excuses Are Not Heard on That Day**

The withholding of permission is an act of justice, and the commentators articulated its grounds with precision. In this world, the excuse is heard because it may be true: the human judge cannot see the heart, cannot trace every influence, cannot weigh every circumstance, and must therefore listen — the very foundation of the procedural mercy of earthly courts. On the Day of Division, the Judge is the One from Whom no secret is hidden: *"And if you speak aloud, truly He knows the secret and what is even more hidden"* (20:7); *"He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal"* (40:19). The excuse is not heard because its truth or falsehood is already fully known — and because the record of deeds, not the speech of the defendant, is the basis of the verdict: *"This Day every soul is recompensed for what it has earned; no wrong is done this Day"* (40:17). The scholars drew the deeper principle: the excuse that could have saved the guilty was the excuse spoken *before* the Day — the excuse of repentance offered to God while the offer of acceptance still stood: *"And it is He Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons evil deeds"* (42:25). The repentance of this world is heard precisely because it can change the future; the excuse of the Day would change nothing — the future is over, the record is sealed, and the speech that could not be spoken at the time when speech was efficacious is not permitted at the time when it would be merely theatrical. The verse is thus the Qur'an's most economical statement of the urgency of repentance: the door of excuse stands open now — not on the Day; the permission to speak is granted in this world — not in the next; and the only excuse that will ever be accepted is the one offered before the record closes.

**The Witnesses That Replace the Tongue**

The silence of the tongue is filled, on the Day, by a testimony that the guilty cannot stop — and the Qur'an describes the substitution with images of unforgettable strangeness and justice: the very organs of the body, which served the sinner in this world, take the stand against him in the next. *"On the Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet bear witness against them for what they used to do"* (24:24); *"Until, when they come to it, their ears and their eyes and their skins bear witness against them for what they used to do, and they say to their skins: 'Why did you bear witness against us?' They say: 'God gave us speech, as He gave speech to everything; He created you the first time, and unto Him you are returned'"* (41:20–21). The commentators on verse 36 of this sūrah connected the two scenes: the guilty are not permitted to speak, because the speaking has been delegated — the tongue is silent because the hands speak, the excuses are barred because the skin testifies, and the defendant who would have used his tongue to defend himself finds that his own body has become the prosecution. The justice of the substitution is exquisite: in this world, the sinner used his tongue to deny and his limbs to sin, and both were his servants; on the Day, the servants become witnesses, and the master who would not speak the truth with his tongue while truth could save him is silenced while the truth is spoken by his own members. The verse's picture of the denied permission is thus not an arbitrary muzzle but the last act of a perfect correspondence: the tongue that was free in the world and refused the truth is not free on the Day to refuse it again — and the only speech left to the guilty is the speech of their own members, telling the truth they spent their lives suppressing.

**The Present Silence That Prepares the Future One**

For the believer, the verse carries a discipline that the classical scholars drew from its starkness: the practice of truthfulness now, so that the silence of the Day holds no terror. The believer who speaks truthfully in this world — who confesses his sins to God in repentance, who refuses the false excuse, who answers for his deeds honestly before they are answered for on the Day — has already, in a sense, given the testimony that the Day would otherwise extract: his tongue has said what his records contain, his repentance has preceded his judgment, and the permission to speak that the Day denies to the guilty is not needed by the one who spoke while speaking was still fruitful. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believer to anticipate the Day's accounting in the daily accounting of the self: *"The intelligent man is he who holds his soul to account and works for what comes after death; and the incapable man is he who follows his whims and hopes for God's pardon without repentance"* (al-Tirmidhī, from Shaddād ibn Aws; he graded it ḥasan) — a saying the commentators loved to quote beside this verse. The verse thus turns the reader back from the scene of the Day to the scene of the present: the excuse that will not be permitted then can be offered now — not as an excuse but as a repentance; the speech that will be silenced then can speak now — the speech of the return, the confession, the plea; and the permission that the Day will withhold from the guilty is standing open, at this very moment, to everyone who will use it while it stands.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:37

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sixth Woe: The Verdict on the Deniers of the Day**

The refrain strikes for the sixth time, and its position after the description of the Day's silence (vv. 35–36) gives it a pointedness that the commentators did not fail to register. The first five occurrences of the woe followed the Day itself, the history of the nations, the creation of the human being, the blessings of the earth, and the portrait of the Fire. This sixth occurrence follows the picture of the guilty standing speechless before their Lord — the permission to speak withheld, the excuses barred, the records complete — and it falls, with particular fitness, upon those who denied that this Day would ever come. Al-Nasafī noted that this occurrence can be read as the verdict on those who denied the Day itself: the people who said, in this world, that there is no reckoning, no resurrection, no judgment — who lived as though the silence of the grave were the last word — are now shown the Day whose coming they denied, and the woe is pronounced over their denial at the very scene that refutes it. The placement is the sūrah's most economical demonstration of the connection between denial and its consequence: the deniers denied the Day when the Day was future; the Day arrives, and the denial is not merely punished but *displayed* — the woe falls upon the mukadhdhibīn at the exact moment when their mukadhdhab bihi (the thing denied) stands before their eyes, and the silence of verse 35 is the silence of denial itself, struck dumb by the arrival of what it refused to believe.

**The Silence That Denial Earns**

There is a deeper continuity between verses 35–36 and this woe that the commentators drew out: denial, cultivated through a lifetime, ends by producing exactly the silence that the Day exhibits. In this world, the denier is never silent: he argues, mocks, disputes, and explains away — the Qur'an's portrait of the deniers is full of their speech: *"The deniers say to those who believe: 'Follow our path, and we shall carry your sins'"* (29:12, in sense); *"And they say: 'What! When we are lost in the earth, shall we indeed be in a new creation?'"* (32:10). But every act of denial is a rehearsal of the Day's silence: each refusal of the truth hardens the faculty by which the truth could be received, each dismissed warning deadens the ear that could have heard, each mocked messenger strengthens the habit of refusal until the soul reaches the state that the Qur'an describes: *"We have placed covers over their hearts, lest they understand it, and deafness in their ears"* (6:25, in sense; 18:57). The silence of verse 35 is the terminal stage of a deafness that began in this world: the denier who would not hear the Reminder when hearing could save him is given, on the Day, the silence of one who has nothing left to hear with — and the woe of verse 37 pronounces the verdict over the whole trajectory: the denial that began as a choice became a habit, the habit became a covering, and the covering became the silence of the Day.

**The Two Silences: The Silence of the Guilty and the Silence of the Blessed**

The sixth woe is sharpened by a contrast that the Qur'an draws elsewhere between the silence of the guilty and the speech of the blessed on the same Day — and the commentators read this occurrence of the refrain against that contrast. The guilty are silent because their case is closed; but the blessed are not silent — the Day is full of their speech: the speech of praise — *"And the last of their call is: Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds"* (10:10); the speech of thanksgiving — *"And they will say: 'Praise be to God, Who has fulfilled His promise to us and made us inherit the earth, dwelling in the Garden wherever we will'"* (39:74); the speech of greeting — *"Their greeting therein is: Peace"* (10:10, in sense; 14:23); and the speech of address from their Lord — *"Peace — a word from a Merciful Lord"* (36:58). The Day of Division divides speech as it divides everything else: the guilty are silenced because their speech is exhausted in denial, and the blessed are given speech because their speech was spent in remembrance — the believer's tongue, trained in this world on the dhikr of God, the prayer, the praise, and the truth, finds on the Day that its training was the preparation for an eternal conversation: *"Faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). The woe over the deniers is thus also the promise to the believers in disguise: the silence that the deniers enter is the absence of the speech that the believers will enjoy forever — and the believer who recites this woe is taught to measure, against the silence of the guilty, the value of every word of remembrance he speaks in this world.

**The Refrain's Rhythm and the Approach of the Verdict**

The sixth occurrence of the refrain also marks a structural turn in the sūrah that the reciter feels in the text's pulse. The woes have been falling at intervals of three to five verses since verse 15, and the reader has learned their rhythm; but the content between the woes has been moving steadily closer to the Day itself — from the promise (v. 7) to the dissolution of the world (vv. 8–13) to the history of the nations (vv. 16–18) to the creation of man (vv. 20–23) to the earth's provisions (vv. 25–27) to the Fire (vv. 29–33) to the judgment (vv. 35–36). The sixth woe falls at the point where the sūrah's scene has reached the judgment seat itself, and the next verses will complete the scene: the gathering of all generations (v. 38), the challenge to the schemers (v. 39), and then the great reversal — the turn from the guilty to the reverent (vv. 41–44), when the sūrah's long darkness breaks into the light of the Garden. The repetition of the refrain has been building toward this turn: six times the woe has fallen on denial, and the sixth fall marks the completion of the indictment of the guilty; the seventh (v. 40) will close the scene of judgment, and then the sūrah will show the other side of the Division. The believer who recites the sūrah experiences the sixth woe as the last stroke of the hammer before the turning — the moment of maximum weight, after which the text itself turns from the silence of the guilty to the springs of the reverent, and the recitation moves from the terror of the Day to the mercy that the Day also contains.

**The Woe as a Mirror Held to the Present**

For the hearer in this world, the sixth woe performs the refrain's eternal function one more time: it holds up the Day of silence as a mirror to the present day of speech. The deniers of the Day are not a distant people; they are whoever hears the description of the Day and refuses its implication — whoever lives as though the reckoning will not come, the records will not be read, the silence will not fall. The woe is recited while the hearer still has the floor of this world: still able to speak the word of faith, still able to offer the repentance that the Day will not permit to be offered as an excuse, still able to answer the sūrah's summons before the Day answers his denial. The classical scholars said that the Qur'an's threats are all invitations in disguise — every woe is a warning, and every warning is a door — and the sixth woe stands at the door of the sūrah's great reversal: the hearer who lets the woe do its work — who turns from denial to faith before the Day of silence — will find himself, at verse 41, on the other side of the Division, among those of whom the sūrah says: "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs" — where the silence of the guilty is replaced by the speech of peace, and the woe that was recited over the deniers is heard no more.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:38

> **This is the Day of Division, "We have gathered you and those of old."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Names the Day Itself**

The scene of judgment reaches its climax in a double pronouncement: *hādhā yawmu al-faṣl* — "This is the Day of Division" — and the words that follow, spoken by God: *jamaʿnākum wal-awwalīn* — "We have gathered you and those of old." The verse completes the circle that the sūrah opened at verse 13, where the Day was first named "the Day of Division" in answer to the question "for what day is it appointed?" (v. 12). Now, at verse 38, the name is pronounced again — *hādhā*, "this," with the demonstrative of present reality: this, which you denied, is the Day of Division; this, which you mocked, is the Day whose name the sūrah's opening apocalypse announced; this is it — not a promise to be deferred, not a tale to be dismissed, but the scene in which the speakers now stand. And the pronouncement is followed by the declaration that gives the Day its population: "We have gathered you and those of old." The verb *jamaʿnā* — "We have gathered" — is past tense, the tense of the accomplished decree: the gathering that the sūrah's earlier verses described as future (the messengers slated, the stars blotted out, the mountains scattered) is here declared complete — the assembly has been convened, the generations have arrived, and the Day of Division is not approaching but present. The "you" of the address are the deniers of every age to whom the sūrah has been speaking; "those of old" are the ancient peoples whose destruction verse 16 cited — and the gathering of both in a single assembly completes the sūrah's argument from history: the ancients and the later ones, whose successive destructions verse 17 described, are now gathered together in a single place, at a single judgment, before a single Lord.

**The Gathering That No Power Could Prevent**

The verse's declaration — "We have gathered you and those of old" — carries the weight of the divine omnipotence that the sūrah has been demonstrating through all its exhibits. The gathering of all humanity — every generation from Adam to the last human being, every soul that ever lived, in a single assembly upon a single plain — is presented in the Qur'an as the act of a power that no force can resist and no calculation can evade: *"And We shall gather them on the Day of Resurrection upon their faces, blind and dumb and deaf; their refuge is Hell"* (17:97, in sense); *"Say: 'The former and the latter shall be gathered to the appointed time of a known Day'"* (56:49–50); *"And they ask you about the Hour: when will it come to pass? Say: 'The knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none shall unveil it at its time but He'"* (7:187). The deniers of Makkah had boasted that no power could assemble the scattered dead — the Qur'an records their objection: *"They say: 'What! When we are lost in the earth, shall we indeed be in a new creation? Nay, they are deniers of the meeting with their Lord'"* (32:10, in sense) — and verse 38 answers the objection with the accomplished fact: the assembly that they called impossible has been convened; the gathering that they said could never occur has occurred; and the same Power that created every generation from a base fluid (v. 20) has gathered every generation to a single plain (v. 38). The commentators noted the connection between the two verses: the gathering of the dead is the mirror-image of the scattering of the mountains (v. 10) — the world's most solid structures are dissolved, and the world's most scattered dust is assembled — and the Power that performs both is the Power whose promise the sūrah's opening oaths affirmed: "surely what you are promised will befall" (v. 7).

**The Plain of Assembly and the End of All Concealment**

The Qur'an describes the place of the gathering in images that the commentators on this verse assembled from its sister passages. The earth that held the living and the dead (vv. 25–26) is transformed into the plain of assembly: the mountains that scattered (v. 10) leave a level expanse — *"And they ask you about the mountains; say: 'My Lord will scatter them as dust, and leave them a level plain, with no curve in it and no rise'"* (20:105–107); the earth is stretched out — *"When the earth is stretched out"* (84:3); and the gathering is complete — *"And We shall leave them on that Day surging against one another, and the trumpet shall be blown, and We shall gather them together"* (18:99). The plain of assembly is the place where all concealment ends: every generation that hid its deeds among its own people stands exposed among all peoples; every secret that was kept from neighbors, spouses, and children lies open before the assembly of all creation — the Qur'an says of the Day: *"The Day on which the secrets are examined"* (86:9); *"And they will find all they did present"* (18:49, in sense). The gathering of "you and those of old" is thus the social dimension of the Day's justice: the denier is judged not in the privacy of his own conscience but in the full assembly of his species — his denial witnessed by the generations that denied before him, his excuses barred by the messengers who warned him, his deeds read before the ancestors whose ruins warned him and the descendants whose fate he shaped. The classical scholars said: the gathering is the final publication of every life — the moment when the private becomes public, the hidden becomes seen, and every soul stands before every other exactly as it stood before its Lord.

**The Justice of the Gathering of the Generations**

The verse's pairing of "you" and "those of old" also completes the sūrah's case for the fairness of the Day's justice, and the commentators articulated the fairness with care. The deniers of the later generations had excused themselves by the example of the earlier ones — "we found our fathers upon a way" (43:22) — and the gathering of both generations in one assembly answers the excuse in the most direct way possible: the fathers and the sons stand together, and each is judged by his own deeds — the fathers cannot be blamed for the sons' denial, and the sons cannot be excused by the fathers' example, because both are present and both are judged individually: *"That no soul may bear the burden of another, and that man shall have nothing but what he strives for"* (53:38–39, in sense); *"And fear a Day when no soul shall avail another in anything, and no intercession shall be accepted from it, nor ransom taken, nor shall they be helped"* (2:48, in sense). The gathering of the generations displays the exactness of the divine justice: the tyrant and his victim from the same age stand in the same assembly, and the tyrant of one age sees the tyrant of every age receiving the same justice; the messenger and his people stand together, and the messenger's testimony is heard against the people who rejected him (v. 11's "messengers are slated" now finds its purpose). The Day of Division divides the generations as it divides the individuals — the believers of every age on one side, the deniers of every age on the other — and the gathering of "you and those of old" is the demonstration that the division is total, just, and final: no generation is missing from the assembly, no excuse is missing from the record, and no soul is judged by anything but what it itself earned.

**The Assembly as the Answer to the Sūrah's Question**

For the reader who has followed the sūrah from its first verse, the declaration of verse 38 is the answer to every question the text has asked. The oaths of verses 1–6 asked whether the promise would befall; verse 7 answered — and verse 38 shows the promise kept. The apocalypse of verses 8–13 asked for what day the messengers were slated; verses 13–14 answered — and verse 38 shows the Day arrived. The history of verses 16–18 asked whether the guilty of old had been destroyed; verse 38 gathers "those of old" with the deniers of the present in the assembly of the final judgment — the destruction of the nations in this world (vv. 16–18) having been the first installment of a justice that verse 38 completes in the gathering of all nations together. The embryology of verses 20–23 asked whether the Power that made the drop could raise the dead; verse 38 shows the raising accomplished — the dead of all the generations assembled, the base fluids of verse 20 become the gathered multitudes of verse 38. And the refrain's tenfold woe finds in verse 38 the scene of its fulfillment: the Day of Division has come, the deniers and the ancients stand gathered, and the remainder of the sūrah will describe what the Division divides — the Fire that receives the deniers (vv. 39–40, 46–50 in part) and the shade and springs that receive the reverent (vv. 41–44). The believer who recites verse 38 stands, in the recitation, at the center of the sūrah's whole architecture: the Day that was promised, denied, warned, and described is here declared present — and the only question left is the one that every hearer must answer for himself: on which side of the Division will the gathering find him?

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:39

> **So if you have a scheme, then scheme against Me!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge That Leaves the Schemers Speechless**

The scene of the Day reaches its most defiant moment: *fa-in kāna lakum kaydun fa-kīdūn* — "So if you have a scheme, then scheme against Me!" The word *kayd* is from the root *k-y-d*, "to scheme, to plot, to devise a stratagem" — the word the Qur'an uses for the plotting of the enemies of the truth in this world: the scheming of Pharaoh (*kayd*, 40:25, in sense), the plotting of the deniers against the messengers, the stratagems of Iblīs. And the verse hurls the deniers' own weapon back at them at the moment when all weapons are dead: the schemers who spent their lives plotting against the truth, the messengers, and the believers are now challenged, in the full assembly of the Day of Division, to scheme against God Himself — to deploy, at the moment of judgment, the stratagems that served them so well in the world. The challenge is the ultimate exposure of the impotence of denial: the schemers' entire career was built on the assumption that cleverness could defeat truth, that plotting could delay the promise, that strategy could outmaneuver the decree — and the Day of Division stands before them as the demonstration that every scheme was spent against a Power that schemes require permission to challenge: *fa-kīdūn* — "scheme, then, if you can!" The verse is one of the Qur'an's most searing examples of the rhetorical figure of the impossible command — the command whose very utterance displays the incapacity of the one commanded.

**The Fate of Every Scheme Against the Truth**

The Qur'an's history is full of the schemes that the deniers of every age devised against the truth, and the commentators on this verse assembled the record: the schemes of Pharaoh against Moses — *"Pharaoh said: 'Let me kill Moses, and let him call upon his Lord! Truly I fear that he will change your religion or cause corruption to appear in the land'"* (40:26) — and the end of Pharaoh's scheming was the drowning of Pharaoh: *"And the evil scheme encompassed its people"* (40:45, in sense); the schemes of the deniers of Makkah against the Prophet ﷺ — the plotting in the house of the council to kill him (8:30: *"And when the deniers schemed against you, to confine you, or kill you, or expel you — and they schemed, and God schemed, and God is the best of schemers"*); the schemes of the hypocrites and the People of the Book in Madinah. And the Qur'an declares the end of all such scheming in the most general terms: *"And they schemed, and God schemed — and God is the best of schemers"* (3:54; 8:30); *"Or do those who devise evil deeds think that We will not seize them?..."* — the sūrah of Fāṭir continues: *"They schemed their schemes, and their schemes are with God, even if their schemes were such as to move mountains"* (35:10, in sense; 14:46). The verse of al-Mursalāt speaks at the end of the whole record: every scheme that was ever devised against the truth is now confronted with the Sender of the truth in the assembly of the Day — and the schemers are invited to produce, from the entire arsenal of their cleverness, a single stratagem that can avail against the decree that has already been accomplished.

**The Two Verses That Strip the Schemers of Every Resource**

The commentators on this verse habitually read it beside its two sister passages, in which the Qur'an issues the same challenge in the same mocking register. The first is the challenge of the sūrah of al-Ḥajj, addressed to those who thought they could cut off the help of God from His messenger: *"Whosoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him stretch out a rope to Heaven. Then let him sever it, and see if his scheming removes that which enrages him"* (22:15) — the image of the schemer climbing to cut the cords of divine help, only to find that the heavens are beyond his rope and the help beyond his knife. The second is the challenge of the sūrah of al-Raḥmān, addressed to the whole assembly of jinn and men: *"O company of jinn and men! If you are able to pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, then pass! You shall not pass, save by an authority"* (55:33). Both challenges share the structure of the verse before us: the impossible command that displays the impotence of the one commanded — stretch the rope, sever it, see if your scheming avails; pass beyond the heavens, if you are able; scheme against Me, if you have a scheme. And in all three, the impotence is not an accident of the Day but the exposure of a condition that was always true: the schemers never had the power they imagined; the rope never reached the heavens; the schemes never touched the decree; and the Day of Division merely publishes, in the full light of the assembly, what was true from the beginning — that the creature who schemes against his Creator is like the wave that schemes against the sea.

**Why the Challenge Is Itself a Mercy**

There is a subtle mercy hidden in the challenge that the commentators did not fail to draw out, and it is the mercy that runs through the entire sūrah. The challenge "scheme against Me, if you have a scheme" is spoken on the Day — but it is recited in this world, where it can still change the schemer's course. The deniers of Makkah heard this verse while their scheming was still possible, while their plots against the Prophet ﷺ were still being laid — and the verse was a warning delivered before the challenge became a sentence: the schemes that you are devising now will one day be confronted with the Sender of the message; the plotting that seems so clever in the councils of this world will one day be invited to plot against the Lord of the worlds; and the only scheme that will ever succeed is the scheme that the schemer turns against his own denial — the stratagem of repentance, the plot of return, the cunning of the soul that outwits its own destruction by surrendering to its Lord. The Qur'an praises exactly this kind of "scheming" against evil when it describes the believer's strategy in the world: the emigration of the believers from Makkah, the defense of Badr, the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyyah — all of them, in the Qur'an's framing, forms of kayd against the schemes of the deniers, conducted within the limits that God established and blessed by the God who "is the best of schemers."

**The Scheme That the Believer Is Commanded to Abandon**

The verse also carries a practical lesson for the believer that the scholars of the heart drew from its starkness: the believer is commanded to abandon scheming against God's decree — the deepest form of the denial that the verse exposes. The human being schemes against the decree when he refuses to accept what God has measured for him; when he plots to secure by forbidden means what God has not written for him; when he exhausts his cunning in the service of his whims and calls the exhaustion freedom. The Qur'an's counsel to the believer is the exact opposite of the schemer's career: *"And whoever puts his trust in God — He suffices him"* (65:3); *"And God — with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them but He... and no soul knows in what land it will die; truly God is Knowing, Aware"* (6:59, in sense). The believer who trusts the decree does not abandon effort — the Qur'an commands effort — but he abandons the illusion that his scheming can outflank the decree: he plans as the one who must act, and trusts as the one who is acted upon; he strives in the world with the full strength of his limbs and places the outcome with the full surrender of his heart. The verse of al-Mursalāt marks the boundary of all human scheming: on this side of the Day, the believer schemes within the limits of the command — the legitimate planning, the permitted strategy, the honest effort; on the far side of the Day, the schemers are invited to scheme against the Commander Himself — and the invitation is the last word on the impotence of every scheme that was ever plotted outside the limits: *fa-in kāna lakum kaydun fa-kīdūn* — scheme, if you can — and the silence that follows the challenge, the silence of verse 35, is the schemers' only answer.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:40

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seventh Woe: The Verdict Over the Schemers**

The refrain strikes for the seventh time, and its position after the challenge of verse 39 gives it the force of a sentence following a dare that has gone unanswered. The challenge was issued: "If you have a scheme, then scheme against Me!" — and the verse that follows records the outcome in the form the sūrah has used ten times: Woe that Day to the deniers. The commentators read this seventh occurrence in the context of the challenge: it falls upon those who denied God's power and therefore schemed against Him — who plotted against the messengers, the revelation, and the believers in the belief that the divine decree could be outmaneuvered, that the promise could be delayed, that the Day could be avoided by strategy. Al-Nasafī and others noted that this occurrence can be seen as the verdict on those who deny God's power to do what He has promised — the deniers whose scheming was built on the assumption that the threat of the Day was a bluff. And the placement of the woe immediately after the unanswered challenge completes the scene of judgment in its darkest key: the schemers have been given their final opportunity to produce a stratagem, the assembly of the generations has witnessed the silence that followed the dare, and the woe pronounces the verdict over the schemers whose only remaining scheme was the one they could not devise.

**The Silence That Answers the Challenge**

The connection between verse 39 and verse 40 is the silence between them — a silence that the reciter of the sūrah feels in the very structure of the text. The challenge ends with an open imperative: "scheme against Me!" — and the next words are not the schemers' reply but the sūrah's verdict: "Woe that Day to the deniers!" The reply that would have answered the challenge — the scheme that would have availed against the decree — never comes; the verse passes directly from the dare to the doom, and the absence of any intervening speech is itself the answer. The Qur'an uses this device of the unanswered challenge in several of its most searing passages: the deniers of the Qur'an are challenged to produce a sūrah like it — *"And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it"* (2:23) — and the challenge stands unanswered across fourteen centuries; the deniers of the Resurrection are challenged to return the dead — *"Then let them produce their proof, if they are truthful"* (27:64, in sense) — and no proof is produced. Verse 40 of al-Mursalāt works in the same way: the gap between the challenge and the woe is the space in which the schemers' impotence is displayed — they had the whole history of their scheming to draw upon, the whole cunning of their careers to deploy, and the verse records that between the dare and the doom there was nothing but silence.

**The Sevenfold Weight of the Refrain**

The seventh occurrence of the refrain also invites reflection on the number of the woes and the structure of the sūrah, and the commentators who attended to the architecture of the chapter found significance in the way the refrain divides the text. The number seven, the commentators noted, carries in the Qur'anic idiom the sense of completeness and fullness — the seven heavens, the seven earths, the seven verses of the Fātiḥah, the seven oft-repeated — and the seventh woe falls at the exact center of the sūrah's dramatic movement: the point where the scene of the guilty is complete and the scene of the reverent is about to begin. The first seven woes (vv. 15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 40) have covered the full indictment of denial: the denial of the Day, of history, of the first creation, of the blessings, of the Fire, of the judgment, and of God's power. The seventh woe closes the account of the guilty as the sūrah has kept it through the first two-thirds of the chapter — and the verses that follow will open the account of the reverent, the long-awaited contrast that the sūrah's structure has been preparing since its opening oaths: the "deniers" of the refrain are about to be answered by the "reverent" of verse 41, and the woe that has fallen seven times on one side of the Division will be answered by the blessing that the next verses pronounce on the other.

**The Challenge That Every Hearer Must Answer**

For the hearer in this world, the seventh woe completes the sūrah's great question and turns it upon the listener: the challenge of verse 39 — "scheme against Me, if you have a scheme" — is not addressed only to the deniers of the Day; it is addressed to everyone who has ever imagined that he can arrange his life against the decree of God — that he can sin in secret and scheme in private, that his cunning can outwit the record, that his strategies can secure what God has not written or avoid what God has written. The woe that follows the challenge is the sūrah's declaration that every such scheme ends in the same silence: the schemer who plots against the truth of God — in his conduct, his dealings, his denial — is invited, at the end, to produce his scheme before the One against Whom he schemed, and the invitation is answered by the silence of the Day. But the believer hears the same challenge as an invitation to abandon the futility: the only scheme that succeeds is the scheme of surrender — the strategy of the servant who stops plotting against his Lord's decree and begins plotting against his own ego's deceits: the plan of repentance, the stratagem of self-accounting, the cunning of the one who outwits his own destruction by placing his whole affair in the hands of the best of schemers. The seventh woe is recited so that the hearer may answer the challenge of verse 39 in this world, where an answer is still possible — and the answer that saves is not a scheme but a surrender: the surrender that leads, in the very next verses of the sūrah, to the company of the reverent, of whom the text now speaks for the first time in its full mercy: "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:41

> **Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Reversal: From the Deniers to the Reverent**

With verse 41 the sūrah executes its most dramatic turn. For twenty-six verses — from the first woe at verse 15 to the seventh at verse 40 — the text has been occupied with the deniers: their denial, their exhibits, their Fire, their silence, their scheming, and the woe that has fallen on them seven times. And now, without warning, the scene shifts: *inna al-muttaqīna fī ẓilālin wa-ʿuyūn* — "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs." The particle *inna* — "truly" — opens the new scene with the same emphasis that has introduced the sūrah's most solemn assertions; the *muttaqīn*, the reverent, appear for the first time in the chapter; and the contrast with the deniers is total. The word *muttaqīn* is from the root *w-q-y*, "to guard, to shield, to protect": the reverent are those who guarded themselves — who took the shield of awareness against sin, who walked through the world with the care of one who knows the path is lined with consequences, who feared their Lord in the unseen and ordered their lives by His command. The sūrah has spent its first forty verses describing what the deniers deny and what denial earns; now it describes what reverence earns — and the description is the exact mirror of the deniers' portion: the deniers are ordered to a shadow of smoke that gives no shade (vv. 30–31); the reverent are amid shade (*ẓilāl*, the plural of the shade that the deniers' shadow only parodies) and springs (*ʿuyūn*, living water). The Day of Division divides the two companies, and verse 41 begins the description of the side of the Division that the sūrah's refrains have been preparing the hearer to choose.

**The Shade That the Deniers' Shadow Only Parodies**

The pairing of the two shades — the deniers' "threefold shadow" (v. 30) and the reverent's "shade" (v. 41) — is one of the sūrah's most deliberate structural ironies, and the commentators dwelt on the correspondence. Both companies are given shade on the Day of Division; the difference is the shade itself. The deniers' shade is the smoke of the Fire — a shadow of three branches that "provides no shade, nor avails against the flame" (v. 31): shade that is not shade, comfort that is the form of comfort emptied of its content. The reverent's shade is the shade that the Qur'an describes in its most generous terms in the sister sūrahs: *"amid thornless lote trees, and clustered bananas, and shade outspread, and water gushing"* (56:28–31); *"And those who believe and perform righteous deeds, We shall lodge them in lofty chambers of the Garden, with rivers flowing beneath them, abiding therein"* (29:58, in sense); and the sūrah of al-Insān, which stands immediately before this one in the written order of the Qur'an: *"And God has shielded them from the evil of that Day, and has granted them radiance and joy, and has rewarded them, for their patience, with a Garden and silk, reclining therein upon couches, seeing therein neither scorching sun nor bitter cold; and its shades hanging low above them, and its fruits made easy to gather, easily"* (76:11–14, in sense). Al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī noted that the verse of al-Mursalāt is close to the description of the Garden in the sūrah of al-Ḥijr: *"Truly the reverent shall be amid gardens and springs"* (15:45) — the same pairing of shade and water, the same company of the reverent, and the same contrast with those who denied. The shade of the reverent is the reality that the deniers' shadow mocked; the springs of the reverent are the water that the Fire's boiling cannot touch; and the two verses of the sūrah — 31 and 41 — mark the two poles of the Division with images that mirror each other across the whole distance between the Fire and the Garden.

**Springs: The Living Water That Never Fails**

The second element of the reverent's portion — *al-ʿuyūn*, the springs — carries the Qur'an's fullest range of water-imagery into the scene of the Garden. The springs are the sources of the rivers that run beneath the Gardens of the blessed — the Qur'an's most repeated formula for the reward: *"Gardens with rivers running below, abiding therein forever"* (5:119, in sense; throughout the Qur'an); and the springs of the Garden are described in their variety in the sūrahs that detail the bliss of the blessed: the spring of *tasmīm* (83:27), the spring of *salsabīl* (76:18), the cup of *kāfūr* (76:5), the cup of *zanjabīl* (76:17), the spring of *rahīq* (83:25, in sense). The commentators on this verse noted that the springs of the Garden are the answer to the thirst of the world: in this life, water is the most necessary and the most contingent of provisions — the caravan that misses the well perishes, the city whose springs fail empties, the desert knows the terror of the dried-up source. The reverent who feared their Lord in the world — who gave water to the thirsty as the Qur'an commands (76:8–9: "and they feed the poor, the orphan, and the captive, for love of Him") — are given in the Garden the water that no drought can touch, the springs that no season can dry, the rivers that run forever beneath the chambers of the blessed: *"This is the similitude of the Garden promised to the reverent: rivers of water incorruptible, rivers of milk whose taste never changes, rivers of wine delicious to those who drink, and rivers of clarified honey"* (47:15). The shade and the springs together complete the picture of the Garden as the place of total refreshment — the end of the burning of the world, the end of the thirst of the world, the rest and the drink that the reverent spent their lives seeking in the only way that finds them: by guarding the command of the One who gives both.

**The Reverent in the World and the World in the Reverent**

The verse also invites the reader to consider what the reverent did to earn this portion — and the sūrah answers by its own structure, for the reverent have been present in the text all along, hidden in the warnings. The reverent are those who heard the oaths of verses 1–7 and believed the promise; who heard the history of the destroyed nations (vv. 16–18) and took warning; who looked at their own creation from a base fluid (vv. 20–23) and acknowledged the Determiner; who drank the sweet water of the earth (vv. 25–27) and thanked the Giver; who heard of the Fire (vv. 29–33) and feared it; who were told of the Day of silence (vv. 35–36) and spoke the truth while speech could still save them; who were challenged to scheme against God (v. 39) and answered the challenge with surrender. The reverent, in other words, are not a third class beside the deniers and the believers; they are the believers in their full description — those whose faith issued in guarding, whose guarding issued in deeds, and whose deeds the sūrah's final verses will reward. And the classical scholars drew the practical lesson that the verse's reward is addressed to a quality that can be cultivated in the present world: taqwā is not a title but a practice — the practice of guarding the heart, the tongue, the limbs, and the dealings; the practice of the believer who, as the Prophet ﷺ said, when asked about the reality of taqwā, pointed to his heart and said, three times: *"God-consciousness is here"* (Muslim 2564, in sense) — the guarding that begins in the heart's awareness of God and spreads from there to every act of the life.

**The Door That the Verse Opens for the Hearer**

The placement of verse 41 at this exact point of the sūrah — after seven woes and before the final addresses — makes it the door through which the hearer is invited to pass. The sūrah has been conducting a trial: seven exhibits of denial, seven woes over the deniers; and at the moment when the case of the guilty is complete, the text turns and describes the other side of the Division — as though the courtroom's doors opened and the Garden itself became visible beyond the bench. The hearer who has followed the sūrah to this point has been placed, by the text, at the choice that the Day will make final: the deniers' portion has been described in full — the shadow of smoke, the Fire, the silence, the challenge — and the reverent's portion is now described in full — the shade, the springs, and the fruits that the next verses will add — and the recitation itself is the moment of choosing. The believer who recites "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs" is not reciting a report about strangers; he is reciting the description of the company he is invited to join — the company of those who guard, who fear, who believe and do right — and the verse's *inna*, its "truly," is the divine guarantee that the invitation is real: as surely as the woe falls on the deniers, the shade and the springs await the reverent.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:42

> **and fruits such as they desire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Element of the Garden's Welcome**

The description of the reverent's portion adds its third element: *wa-fawākiha mimmā yashtahūn* — "and fruits such as they desire." The word *fawākih* is the plural of *fākihah*, fruit — the word the Qur'an uses throughout its descriptions of the Garden for the abundance of its produce; and the clause *mimmā yashtahūn* — "of what they desire" — from the root *sh-h-w*, "to desire, to long for" — describes the fruits as answering the desire of the blessed. The commentators unfolded the meaning of the phrase with an eye to its contrast with the world: in this life, the availability of food and drink is determined by external circumstances — the season, the harvest, the market, the means; a man may desire a fruit that is out of season, out of reach, or out of his means, and the desire goes unanswered. In the Garden, the fruits are "such as they desire": the desire itself becomes the menu — whatever the blessed long for is present, whatever they wish for is brought — and the Qur'an describes the same immediacy elsewhere: *"For them therein will be fruits of every kind, and whatever they call for"* (36:57); *"And they will find therein whatever their souls desire, and their eyes delight in"* (43:71); *"And therein is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in, and you will abide therein"* (43:71, in sense); *"They will be given to drink a sealed nectar, whose seal is musk — so for that let the strivers strive"* (83:25–26). The fruits of the Garden are not a fixed ration dispensed from a store; they are the answer of the Lord of the Garden to the desires of His guests — the generosity that in this world is filtered through seasons and markets and means, in the Garden flows directly from the wish to the fulfillment: *"Their call therein: 'Glory be to You, O God!' and their greeting therein: 'Peace'"* (10:10, in sense).

**Desire Transformed: The Appetite That Was Made Holy**

The verse's mention of desire invites a reflection that the spiritual commentators developed at length, because the Qur'an's treatment of desire in the Garden is the completion of its treatment of desire in the world. In this world, desire is the great test: the human being is a creature of appetites — for food, drink, comfort, beauty, love, honor — and the Qur'an's ethics is built on the ordering, not the abolition, of appetite: the believer eats of the good things God has provided (2:168; 5:88), drinks in gratitude, marries in purity, seeks provision by honest means, and guards his desires from the excess that the Qur'an calls transgression: *"O children of Adam! Take your adornment at every place of worship, and eat and drink, but do not be prodigal; truly He does not love the prodigal"* (7:31). The desires that were disciplined in the world — the appetite trained to wait, the longing trained to seek what is lawful — are not extinguished in the Garden but fulfilled: the blessed do not become desireless; they become satisfied — their desires, purified by the discipline of the world, are met without remainder by the generosity of the Garden. The fruits "such as they desire" are thus the reward of the desires that were governed: the believer who trained his appetite to obey the command in the world finds his appetite in the Garden crowned rather than cancelled — every lawful longing he restrained for the sake of God returned to him as a fruit present at the moment of the wish.

**The Fruits of the Garden and the Fruits of the World**

The verse also carries the Qur'an's characteristic double use of the fruit-image: the Garden's fruits are the fulfillment of the world's fruits, and the world's fruits are the parables of the Garden's. The Qur'an describes the fruits of the Garden in the language of the world's most beloved produce — *"and fruits of palms and pomegranates"* (55:68); *"therein are fruits and palms with sheathed spathes"* (55:11, in sense) — while insisting that the reality exceeds the names: *"No soul knows what comfort is hidden for it, as a recompense for what it used to do"* (32:17). And the world's fruits are described as the first installments of the Garden's — the sūrah of al-Raʿd says of the earth's produce: *"And in the earth are neighboring tracts, and gardens of vines, and sown fields, and palm trees sharing one root and others with separate roots, watered by one water; and We make some of them excel others in fruit. Truly in that are signs for a people who understand"* (13:4). The commentators on this verse of al-Mursalāt noted the connection between the fruit of the world and the fruit of the Garden: the fruits that the reverent ate in the world with thanks were the pledges of the fruits they will eat in the Garden with fulfillment — the sweet water of verse 27 was the pledge of the springs of verse 41, and the fruits of the earth were the pledges of the fruits of the Garden, "such as they desire." The believer who eats the fruit of this world with gratitude is, in the Qur'an's logic, rehearsing the eating of the next: the table of the world is the training table for the table of the Garden, and the thanks offered over the first meal qualifies the servant for the second.

**The Contrast With the Portion of the Guilty**

The verse's mention of the fruits "such as they desire" also completes the sūrah's contrast between the two portions of the Day of Division, and the commentators drew the two pictures against each other. The guilty are told, at verse 46, to "eat and enjoy yourselves a little" — the eating of the guilty in this world is brief, bounded by death, and followed by the Fire; the reverent are told, at verse 43, to "eat and drink in enjoyment" — the eating of the blessed in the Garden is endless, unbounded by any term, and preceded by the deeds that earned it. The guilty desired and were given a little; the reverent desired and were given all. The fruits of the Garden answer the desire of the blessed because their desire was purified; the portion of the guilty is bounded because their desire was never purified — they wanted the world without its Giver, and they received the world's little without the Garden's all: *"Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We increase for him his harvest; and whoever desires the harvest of this world, We give him some of it, and he has no share in the Hereafter"* (42:20). The two eatings — the little of the guilty and the endless of the reverent — are the two outcomes of the one appetite, divided by the one choice: the desire that was governed by reverence becomes the fruits of the Garden; the desire that was never governed becomes the brief enjoyment that ends in the Fire. The verse of al-Mursalāt sets the choice before the hearer in the most concrete terms the Qur'an uses: the appetite is not the enemy — the appetite is the raw material of either destiny — and the desire that is trained by taqwā in this world becomes, in the next, the very measure of the Garden's giving: fruits such as they desire.

**The Discipline That Earns the Fulfillment**

For the believer, the verse carries a practical program that the scholars of the tradition derived from its pairing of desire and fulfillment: the desires of this world are to be trained, not killed, so that the desires of the Garden may be fulfilled. The classical spiritual writers described the training in concrete terms: the appetite for food trained by fasting — the believer who hungers voluntarily in this world for the sake of God will eat in the Garden without end; the appetite for drink trained by the water given to the thirsty — *"and they give food, despite their love of it, to the poor, the orphan, and the captive"* (76:8) — will be answered by the springs that never fail; the appetite for beauty trained by the modesty of the eye will be answered by the beauty that no eye has seen; the appetite for rest trained by the night prayer will be answered by the shade of the Garden's outspread canopy. The Prophet ﷺ summed up the whole exchange in his saying about the fasting person: *"Every deed of the son of Adam is multiplied — the good deed ten times to seven hundred times. God says: 'Except the fast, for it is Mine, and I reward it; the fasting person abandons his desire and his food for My sake'"* (al-Bukhārī 1904; Muslim 1151, in sense). The fruits of verse 42 are the end of that exchange: the desire abandoned for God in the world is returned by God in the Garden — "fruits such as they desire" — and the believer who recites this verse is taught to measure his present hungers against the fulfillment that awaits, and to spend his desires now in the only market that pays in the currency of the Garden.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:43

> **"Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you used to do."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Welcome That Answers the Dismissal**

The scene of the Garden reaches its summit in the words of welcome spoken to the blessed: *kulū washrabū hanīʾan bimā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do." The verse is the exact mirror of the dismissal spoken to the guilty in verses 29–30 — "Away to that which you used to deny!" — and the commentators set the two addresses side by side. The guilty are ordered away with the imperative of departure: *inṭaliqū* — "be gone" — toward the thing they denied. The reverent are invited with the imperatives of hospitality: *kulū washrabū* — "eat and drink" — toward the fruits they desired. The guilty are dismissed to the shadow of smoke that gives no shade; the reverent are welcomed amid shade and springs. The guilty hear the past tense of their denial — "that which you used to deny" — naming what they refused; the reverent hear the past tense of their deeds — "that which you used to do" — naming what they performed. The two sentences are the two faces of the Day of Division: the same Day that says "away" to one company says "eat and drink" to the other; the same record that condemns the denial of the guilty rewards the deeds of the reverent; and the distance between the two addresses is the distance that the choices of this world created — the distance between the Fire and the Garden, between the shadow of smoke and the shade outspread. The verse of the welcome is the sūrah's first direct speech to the blessed, and it is spoken in the imperative of generosity: not "you may eat," but "eat" — not "you may drink," but "drink" — the command of the Host to His guests, the permission that is itself a gift.

**The Enjoyment Without Alloy**

The word *hanīʾan* — "in enjoyment, with relish, wholesomely" — is the key to the verse's meaning, and the commentators unfolded its depth. *Hanīʾ* is that which is enjoyed without any admixture of harm: food that is delicious and salutary at once, drink that quenches and delights together, pleasure from which no regret, no indigestion, no hangover, no consequence follows. The Qur'an uses the same word for the blessed's entry into the Garden: *"Enter it in peace, secure"* — and in the sūrah of al-Ḥijr for the address to the reverent: *"Enter it in peace and security"* (15:46). The commentators on this verse explained that *hanīʾan* negates everything that mixed with the enjoyments of this world: in this life, no pleasure is pure — the food is followed by want, the drink by thirst, the joy by sorrow, the health by sickness; every enjoyment of the world carries within it the seed of its own end, and the wise have always known that the world's pleasures are mixed: *"The enjoyment of this world is little, and the Hereafter is better for the reverent"* (4:77, in sense). The eating and drinking of the Garden are *hanīʾ* — pure enjoyment without consequence: the food does not end, the drink does not weary, the pleasure does not cloy, and nothing in the Garden's enjoyment carries the tax that the world's pleasures always exacted. The verse is thus the divine description of the perfection of the Garden's bliss: not merely more of the world's pleasure but pleasure of a different order — pleasure from which the world's hidden costs have been entirely removed.

**The Wages of the Deeds: Grace Disguised as Payment**

The second half of the verse — *bimā kuntum taʿmalūn*, "for that which you used to do" — raises one of the most delicate questions of Qur'anic theology: the relation between the deeds of the reverent and the reward of the Garden. The verse states the reward as a recompense for deeds, and the Qur'an is full of such statements: *"Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you sent ahead in the days gone by"* (69:24); *"This is the Garden that you have inherited for what you used to do"* (7:43); *"Enter the Garden for what you used to do"* (16:32, in sense). And yet the same Qur'an insists that no deed of a creature can purchase the Garden: *"That is the bounty of God; He gives it to whom He wills"* (57:21, in sense); and the Prophet ﷺ said: *"No one's deeds will admit him to the Garden." They said: "Not even you, O Messenger of God?" He said: "Not even me, unless God wraps me in His mercy"* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, in sense). The classical scholars reconciled the two truths with care: the Garden is a bounty that no deed earns, and the deeds are the path along which the bounty travels — God, in His mercy, attached His reward to the deeds of the believers and then rewarded the deeds with a generosity that exceeds their measure: *"Whoever comes with a good deed shall have ten like it"* (6:160); *"God multiplies for whom He wills"* (2:261, in sense). The verse's "for that which you used to do" is thus grace speaking in the language of wages: the reverent are told that their reward is for their deeds, and the statement is true in the way a father's gift to a child is "for" the child's small help — the help did not buy the gift, but the father attached the gift to the help, and the attachment is itself a mercy that honors the child. The commentators said: God calls it a recompense so that the believers may take pride in their Lord's generosity, and the reality behind the recompense is the bounty that no deed could measure.

**The Deeds That the Verse Rewards**

The verse's phrase — "that which you used to do" — invites the hearer to ask what deeds the reverent performed, and the sūrah answers implicitly from its own structure. The reverent are those who did what the sūrah's warnings called for: they believed the promise that the oaths affirmed (v. 7) when the deniers denied it; they took warning from the destroyed nations (vv. 16–18) when the deniers ignored them; they acknowledged the Determiner of their own creation (v. 23) when the deniers disputed Him; they thanked the Giver of the earth's provisions (vv. 25–27) when the deniers were thankless; they feared the Fire whose portrait the sūrah painted (vv. 29–33) when the deniers mocked it; they spoke the truth before the Day of silence (vv. 35–36) when the deniers exhausted their speech in denial; and they answered the challenge of verse 39 with surrender when the schemers answered it with silence. The deeds of the reverent, in other words, are the exact inverse of the denials of the deniers: where the deniers denied, the reverent affirmed; where the deniers were thankless, the reverent gave thanks; where the deniers schemed, the reverent submitted. And the reward that verse 43 pronounces over these deeds is the reward that the whole sūrah has been preparing: the eating and drinking of the Garden — the shade and springs and fruits of verses 41–42 — are the deeds of faith and guarding, converted by the divine generosity into the hospitality of the Garden, and the "you used to do" of the verse is the record of a life that the believer himself wrote, deed by deed, in the world where deeds still counted.

**The Meal That Begins Eternity**

For the believer, verse 43 is the destination of the entire sūrah's journey — the table at which the journey ends. The sūrah began with oaths about the sending of the Reminder; it moved through the promise, the apocalypse, the history, the embryology, the earth, the Fire, the judgment; it warned the deniers seven times; and now it seats the reverent at the meal of the Garden and speaks to them the first words they hear in their eternal home: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do." The identical verse appears in the sūrah of al-Ṭūr, addressed to the blessed in the same words: *"Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do, reclining upon couches arranged in rows"* (52:19–20) — and the commentators on both passages noted that the repetition of the address marks the constancy of the divine welcome: the same words are spoken wherever the blessed are gathered, because the same deeds are rewarded and the same Host presides. The believer who recites this verse in this world is hearing, in advance, the words that will be spoken to him if he remains among the reverent — and the hearing is itself a summons: the meal of the Garden is prepared for those who do, and the doing is the only preparation that matters. The verse's imperatives — eat, drink — are spoken in the present recitation to the ear of the living believer as a promise and a question at once: the promise of the hospitality that awaits the deeds of faith, and the question of whether the deeds that the verse will reward are the deeds that the hearer is doing now — while the eating and drinking of this world still teach, by their brevity, the value of the meal that never ends.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:44

> **Thus do We recompense the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of the Garden Stated in the Present Tense**

The welcome of verse 43 is sealed by the sūrah's statement of the law that governs the Garden's hospitality: *innā ka-dhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn* — "Truly thus do We recompense the virtuous." The verse is the mirror of verse 18 — "Thus do We deal with the guilty" (*ka-dhālika nafʿalu bil-mujriminīn*) — and the commentators set the two laws side by side as the two statutes of the Day of Division. The same *ka-dhālika* — "thus, in like manner" — introduces both: thus do We deal with the guilty, and thus do We recompense the virtuous; the dealing and the recompense are the two applications of the one divine justice, and the Day of Division is the day when both applications become fully visible. The verbs carry the same present tense: *nafʿalu* in verse 18 and *najzī* here — "We deal" and "We recompense" — not confined to the past or the future but the standing custom of God, operative in every age and completed on the Day. And the beneficiaries are named: *al-muḥsinīn*, "the virtuous" — from the root *ḥ-s-n*, "to be good, to do good, to perfect" — the word that the Qur'an uses for those who do good with excellence, who worship God as though they see Him (the famous definition of *iḥsān* in the ḥadīth of Gabriel: *"To worship God as though you see Him, for though you do not see Him, truly He sees you"* — al-Bukhārī 4777; Muslim 8), and who meet the trials of life with goodness. The verse announces that the Garden's welcome of verse 43 is not an arbitrary favor but the application of a law: the virtuous are recompensed for their virtue, as certainly as the guilty are dealt with for their guilt — and the two laws, stated in the same form and the same tense, are the twin statutes of the Day.

**The Virtue That Earns the Recompense**

The word *muḥsinīn* carries the full depth of the Qur'an's understanding of virtue, and the commentators unfolded its range. The *muḥsin* is the one who does good — and the Qur'an defines the doing of good by its objects: good to God — the worship that is sincere: *"And they were commanded only to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him"* (98:5, in sense); good to the self — the guarding of the soul from what destroys it: *"Whoever does a good deed — for himself; and whoever does evil — against himself"* (41:46, in sense); and good to others — the kindness whose forms the Qur'an enumerates: *"Worship God and associate nothing with Him, and be good to parents, and to kin, and to orphans, and to the needy, and to the neighbor who is kin, and to the neighbor who is a stranger, and to the companion at your side, and to the traveler, and to what your right hands own"* (4:36). The Qur'an promises the *muḥsinīn* throughout its pages the reward that verse 44 states as a law: *"And whoever submits his face to God, and is virtuous, has grasped the firmest handhold"* (31:22, in sense); *"Truly God is with those who are virtuous"* (29:69, in sense; 16:128); *"And We shall surely recompense the virtuous"* — the formula appears repeatedly with this very wording (6:84; 12:22, 90; 28:14; 37:80, 105, 110, 121, 131 — the commentators on this verse cite the whole family of occurrences). The virtue that the verse rewards is not the heroism of the extraordinary but the excellence of the ordinary: the believer who does the good that is before him — the prayer on time, the honest trade, the kind word, the forgiven injury, the charity given secretly, the anger swallowed — with the excellence of one who knows that God sees: that believer is the *muḥsin*, and the law of verse 44 attaches his recompense to his virtue.

**The Excellence of the Deed and the Excellence of the Doer**

The root *ḥ-s-n* also carries a dimension that the spiritual commentators drew out with care: the *muḥsin* is not only the one who does good deeds but the one who does them *well* — the deed done with excellence, the work perfected, the worship performed with presence of heart, the charity given with gentleness, the forgiveness offered without reproach. The Qur'an commands this quality of excellence in the doing: *"And do good (aḥsinū), truly God loves the virtuous (al-muḥsinīn)"* (2:195); and the Prophet ﷺ made excellence the measure of every act: *"Truly God has prescribed excellence (iḥsān) in all things; so when you kill, kill well, and when you slaughter, slaughter well"* (Muslim 1955). The verse of al-Mursalāt recompenses the *muḥsinīn* — those whose goodness was performed with the excellence that raises a deed from the merely correct to the beautiful: the prayer that is not only valid but present, the charity that is not only given but kind, the patience that is not only borne but graceful, the faith that is not only held but lived. And the commentators noted the correspondence that runs through the Qur'an: as the believer's deeds were excellent (*ḥasan*), the recompense is excellent (*ḥusnā*): *"For those who do good is the best (al-ḥusnā) and more"* (10:26) — the *ḥusnā* being the Garden and "more" being the vision of the Lord that the verse itself explains: *"and faces that Day shall be radiant, gazing upon their Lord"* (75:22–23). The excellence of the deed in this world is recompensed by the excellence of the reward in the next: the good that was done beautifully is returned as a Garden whose beauty exceeds it, and the virtue that was practiced for the sight of God is crowned by the sight of God.

**The Law That Answers the Sūrah's Refrain**

The placement of verse 44 immediately before the eighth occurrence of the refrain gives the law of the virtuous its full context in the sūrah's architecture. Seven times the woe has fallen on the deniers; the scene of the Garden has opened at verse 41 with the shade and springs of the reverent, the fruits of verse 42, the welcome of verse 43 — and verse 44 states the law that governs the whole scene: "Thus do We recompense the virtuous." The verse stands, as it were, at the hinge between the two companies: the law of the guilty (v. 18) has been applied in the woes; the law of the virtuous (v. 44) is now stated at the moment when the Garden's welcome has been described; and the refrain that follows at verse 45 will fall on the deniers one more time — not because the scene has returned to them but because the contrast itself is the final indictment: the deniers could have been among the virtuous; the recompense was standing; the law was published; and they chose the portion of the guilty instead. The commentators noted that the verse's law, like the law of verse 18, is stated in the present tense precisely so that no reader may relegate it to the future: the recompensing of the virtuous is God's standing custom, as operative in the present life as on the Day — the believer who does good is being recompensed even now, in the peace of his heart, the barakah of his provision, the love that God casts for him — and the completion of the recompense awaits the Day when the law is applied in full: the shade, the springs, the fruits, and the welcome of the eternal meal.

**The Choice That the Two Laws Place Before Every Hearer**

Verse 44 completes the sūrah's legislation of the two destinies, and the reciter of the chapter stands, at this point, before both statutes: "Thus do We deal with the guilty" (v. 18) and "Thus do We recompense the virtuous" (v. 44). The two laws cover every human being: there is no third statute, no unclassified soul, no middle company in the final division — every hearer of the sūrah is either among the guilty, whose dealing has been described in the woes and the Fire, or among the virtuous, whose recompense has been described in the shade and the springs and the welcome. And the choice between the two classifications is made in the present, by the present deeds: the virtue that the law recompenses is the virtue that can be performed today — the good deed, the guarded tongue, the sincere worship, the kindness rendered, the repentance offered, the faith affirmed. The believer who recites "Thus do We recompense the virtuous" is being shown the statute under which he may place himself; the verse is an invitation written in the form of a law — the invitation to join the *muḥsinīn* while the joining is still possible, to perform the good while the performing still counts, and to enter, at the end, the company of those to whom the same words will be spoken that verse 43 spoke: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:45

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eighth Woe: The Verdict Spoken Across the Garden's Wall**

The refrain returns for the eighth time, and its placement is the most paradoxical in the entire sūrah: it falls directly after the description of the Garden's welcome — the shade and springs of the reverent (v. 41), the fruits such as they desire (v. 42), the words "Eat and drink in enjoyment" (v. 43), and the law "Thus do We recompense the virtuous" (v. 44). The woe is now pronounced from the very edge of the Garden, as it were — across the boundary of the Day of Division, in the hearing of the blessed as well as of the deniers. The commentators read this eighth occurrence in relation to what precedes it with a special attention. Al-Shawkānī recorded the reading that this woe falls upon those who despair — who denied, by their despair, the manifold reward that verse 43–44 has just described: the deniers who looked at the invitation to the Garden and refused to believe that it could be real, who looked at the recompense of the virtuous and denied that virtue could be so rewarded, who heard the promise of "Eat and drink in enjoyment" and dismissed it as the same "fable of the ancients" with which they had always dismissed the promises of the messengers. The woe at this position declares that the denial of the reward is as fatal as the denial of the punishment: the Day of Division has two sides, and the deniers denied both — they denied the Fire when the Fire was described (v. 34) and they deny the Garden when the Garden is described (v. 45) — and the woe falls on the denial of the mercy as certainly as it fell on the denial of the wrath.

**The Denial That Refuses the Good News**

The psychology of this eighth denial deserves examination, because it is subtler than the denials that preceded it. The earlier woes condemned the denial of threatening truths — the Day, the destruction, the judgment, the Fire. This woe condemns the denial of a promising truth — the Garden, the reward, the welcome — and the commentators noted that the denial of the good news is the more revealing denial of the two. A man may deny a threat out of fear, or out of the wish that it were not true; but to deny a promise of boundless happiness — to hear that virtue is rewarded with shade and springs and fruits and an eternal welcome, and to refuse the hearing — requires a hardness that fear alone cannot explain. The Qur'an describes this hardness in the deniers' response to the good news of the messengers: *"And when they are told: 'There is no god but God,' they wax arrogant"* (37:35); and their refusal of the message of mercy: *"And when Our signs are recited to them as clear proofs, the deniers say of the truth when it comes to them: 'This is manifest sorcery'"* (43:30, in sense). The denial of the reward reveals the deeper structure of denial itself: the denier does not refuse the messengers because the news is bad; he refuses because the news comes from God — and the refusal of God's authority extends to His promises as certainly as to His threats. The woe of verse 45 falls upon this deepest refusal: the deniers who would not be moved by the terror of the Fire and would not be moved by the beauty of the Garden — whose denial closed the door of hope as firmly as it closed the door of fear.

**The Despair That Denies the Reward**

The reading preserved by al-Shawkānī — that this occurrence of the woe touches those who fall into despair — opens a further dimension that the spiritual commentators developed with great care: despair itself is a form of denial. The believer who despairs of the mercy of God denies, in effect, the reward that verse 44 promises to the virtuous; the sinner who concludes that his sins are beyond forgiveness denies the welcome that verse 43 extends; the heart that believes God will not accept the returning servant contradicts the Qur'an's own description of the divine character: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against their own souls, do not despair of the Mercy of God; truly God forgives all sins, truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (39:53). The classical scholars counted despair among the gravest of sins precisely because it denies God's most emphasized attribute — His mercy — and the Qur'an says of those who despair: *"And do not lose hope in the Spirit of God; truly none lose hope in the Spirit of God but the deniers"* (12:87) — the verse that the commentators on this passage of al-Mursalāt loved to cite, with its explicit linking of despair to denial. The eighth woe, on this reading, is a warning to the heart that would surrender to hopelessness: the reward of the virtuous is real, the recompense of verse 44 is standing, the welcome of verse 43 is spoken — and the denial that refuses to hope is a denial that the woe condemns as certainly as the denial that refuses to fear.

**The Contrast That Constitutes the Final Indictment**

Structurally, the eighth occurrence of the refrain completes the sūrah's most powerful rhetorical device: the juxtaposition of the two portions without transition. The Garden has been described in four verses of pure mercy — shade, springs, fruits, welcome, recompense — and then, without a single word of connection, the refrain falls: Woe that Day to the deniers! The juxtaposition leaves the hearer to supply the connection, and the connection is the Day of Division itself: the same Day on which the reverent are seated at the meal of the Garden is the Day on which the deniers meet their woe; the same recitation that describes the one describes the other; and the distance between the two descriptions — the space of four verses — is the whole distance between the two companies. The commentators said: the sūrah places the woe after the Garden's description so that the deniers may know what they forfeited — the denial of the reward is punished not only by the Fire but by the sight, from the edge of the assembly, of the reward refused: the Qur'an describes the people of the Fire addressing the people of the Garden across the divide — *"And the companions of the Fire call out to the companions of the Garden: 'Pour upon us some water, or some of what God has provided!'"* (7:50) — and the answering refusal is part of the punishment. The eighth woe pronounces the verdict over the denial that cost the deniers both worlds: they denied the warning and earned the Fire; they denied the promise and forfeited the Garden; and the woe that falls at the very edge of the Garden's description is the sound of the door closing on the side of mercy.

**The Woe That Still Leaves the Door Ajar**

And yet — as always in this sūrah — the eighth woe is recited in the world, where the door it marks is still ajar. The deniers who hear this verse in the recitation of the Qur'an have not yet met the Day; the Garden that verse 45 places beyond their reach is still within their reach — the shade and springs of verse 41 are still attainable by the turning of this world; the welcome of verse 43 is still spoken to whoever will perform the deeds of verse 44. The woe is pronounced over the denial of the reward so that no hearer may add that denial to his others: the believer who hears the eighth woe is warned against the despair that would deny him the mercy; the denier who hears it is warned against the hardness that would close him out of the promise; and both are invited, by the very juxtaposition of the Garden's mercy and the deniers' woe, to cross the line of the Division while the crossing is still possible — to leave the company of those who deny and join the company of those who do, so that when the Day comes, and the refrain is heard for the last time, they will hear it not as their sentence but as the description of a company they have left forever: "Woe that Day to the deniers!" — while they, by the mercy of God, are seated amid the shade and the springs.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:46

> **Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; truly you are guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ironic Invitation to the Deniers of the Present**

With verse 46 the sūrah turns from the Day back to the world — and the turn is made with one of the most searing ironies in the Qur'an: *kulū wa-tamattaʿū qalīlan innakum mujrimūn* — "Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; truly you are guilty." The verse is spoken to the deniers in their present life, and its first clause mimics the welcome of verse 43: the same imperative *kulū* — "eat" — that was spoken to the reverent in the Garden is now spoken to the deniers in the world. But the mimicry is ironic, and the commentators unfolded its layers with care. The reverent were told: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do" — an invitation without limit, in a Garden without end. The deniers are told: "Eat and enjoy yourselves **a little**" — an invitation with a term, in a world that is passing — and the clause that follows states the reason: "truly you are guilty." The verse is not a permission but a sentence pronounced in the form of a permission: the deniers are granted their enjoyment — but it is measured, bounded, terminal: the "little" is the whole of what they will have, the world is all the Garden they will ever see, and the eating and enjoying of the present are the entire portion of those who denied the promise of the future. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators after him read the verse in exactly this sense: God addresses the deniers of this world with an irony worthy of the Day of Division — eat, enjoy, for your portion is small and your term is near; the respite that you take for good fortune is in fact the rope of your own undoing.

**The Respite That Deepens the Pit**

The verse's irony cuts deeper when it is read against the Qur'an's repeated teaching about the divine respite — the *imlāʾ*, the granting of rein to the guilty. The deniers of Makkah saw their prosperity as the proof of their rightness: they were rich, powerful, and unpunished, while the believers were poor and persecuted — and they drew the conclusion that the Qur'an records: *"And they say: 'We are greater in wealth and children, and we shall not be punished'"* (34:35, in sense); *"Let the deniers not suppose that the respite We grant them is good for their souls; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin, and theirs is a humiliating punishment"* (3:178). The sūrah of Ibrāhīm states the same warning with the same irony: *"We grant them enjoyment a little, then We compel them toward a grave punishment"* (31:24); and the sūrah of al-Ḥijr: *"Let them eat and enjoy, and let their hopes beguile them; they will come to know"* (15:3). The verse of al-Mursalāt belongs to this family of verses, and its irony is the irony of the trap: the denier who eats and enjoys in the present believes he is feasting, and the feasting is in fact the filling of the pit beneath his feet — every pleasure enjoyed without gratitude is a brick in the wall of the reckoning, every respite granted without return is an increase of the account, and the "little" of the world's enjoyment is the down payment of a debt that the Day of Division will collect in full: *"So let not their wealth and children impress you; God only desires to punish them thereby in this world's life, and that their souls depart while they are deniers"* (9:55, in sense).

**The Address That Names the Company**

The second half of the verse — *innakum mujrimūn*, "truly you are guilty" — gives the irony its edge and its justice. The verse does not say "eat and enjoy, for you will soon be punished" — it says "eat and enjoy; truly you are guilty" — the guilt is stated as the ground of the sentence: the enjoyment of the deniers is bounded *because* they are guilty; the "little" is the measure that guilt deserves; and the eating and enjoying are, in effect, the last meal of the condemned — the portion granted before the execution of the sentence. The word *mujrimūn* is the same word that verse 18 used in the law of the divine dealing — "Thus do We deal with the guilty" — and the verse of 46 applies the law to the present moment: the dealing of God with the guilty is not deferred entirely to the Day; it operates now, in the very form of the deniers' prosperity — the guilt that blinds them to the Giver of their enjoyment, the guilt that makes their enjoyment a snare rather than a grace, the guilt that will convert, on the Day, the "little" they enjoyed into the measure of their loss. The commentators noted the terrible precision of the address: it is spoken to people who are eating and enjoying at the very moment they hear it — the verse meets them at the table, names them, and tells them the meaning of the meal: you are guilty, and this is your portion — eat, for the portion is little and the account is coming.

**The Two Eatings and the Choice Between Them**

The sūrah's placement of verse 46 immediately after the Garden's banquet (vv. 41–44) sets the two eatings side by side, and the commentators drew the contrast with full force: the reverent eat in the Garden with a welcome that never ends; the guilty eat in the world with a sentence that has already been pronounced. The reverent's eating is the reward of deeds done — the fruits of the life of guarding; the guilty's eating is the portion of deeds denied — the "little" of the life of transgression. The reverent's eating is followed by more eating, forever; the guilty's eating is followed by the Fire — the verse says it in the sūrah's own structure: the "eat and enjoy yourselves a little" of verse 46 is followed by the ninth woe at verse 47, and the ninth woe is followed by the scene of the refusal to bow (v. 48) and the final woe (v. 49), and the last word of the sūrah (v. 50) leaves the deniers with nothing but the question of what discourse they will believe after this one. The two eatings are the two destinies in miniature: the meal of the world is the only meal the guilty will ever have — and even that meal is poisoned by the knowledge that the verse itself places in their mouths: "truly you are guilty." The believer who recites verse 46 is taught to see his own eating in the light of the two tables: the food of this world is either the foretaste of the Garden's welcome — when it is eaten with gratitude and earned by virtue — or the portion of the guilty's "little" — when it is eaten without acknowledgment and earned by transgression. The same bite of bread is the bridge to either destiny, and the verse sets the choice before every eater: eat as the reverent eat — in thanks, in measure, in the remembrance of the Giver — or eat as the guilty eat — and find, at the end of the little, that the meal is over and the account remains.

**The Irony as a Mercy in Disguise**

And yet — the sūrah's ironies are always mercies in disguise — the verse that sentences the guilty to their "little" is recited to them while the "little" is still running. The deniers of Makkah heard this verse while their tables were still spread, their term was still open, and their guilt was still pardonable — the very irony of the verse was the warning that could have saved them: the "little" was meant to be recognized as little, the enjoyment was meant to be recognized as bounded, the guilt was meant to be recognized as removable — and the recognition was the door of the return. The verse's irony is thus a diagnostic: it tells the deniers the truth about their condition so that they may change it — the meal is small, so do not build on it; the guilt is real, so remove it; the term is near, so use it. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to live in the light of this verse's warning: *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler upon the road"* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar) — the traveler eats lightly, enjoys little, and keeps his eyes on the destination — and the believer who lives as the traveler has answered the irony of verse 46 with the only answer that defeats it: he has made his eating in this world the eating of the reverent — the meal of the traveler who eats to reach the Garden, and who will hear, at the end of the road, not "eat and enjoy yourselves a little" but the welcome that never ends: "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for that which you used to do."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:47

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ninth Woe: The Verdict Over the Heedless Enjoyers**

The refrain strikes for the ninth time, and its position directly after the ironic invitation of verse 46 — "Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; truly you are guilty" — gives it the force of the gavel falling on the sentence just pronounced. The deniers have been told the meaning of their enjoyment — it is little, it is bounded, their guilt is real — and the woe that follows seals the meaning: the enjoyment that they took as their portion is the enjoyment that the woe condemns. Al-Shawkānī recorded the reading that this ninth occurrence repeats the force of the eighth — a doubled warning for a doubled heedlessness — while other commentators read it in the context of verse 46 as the verdict on those who enjoy the world's "little" with no thought of its consequences: the deniers whose enjoyment is itself a denial — a denial, in practice, that all they receive is from God, that the Giver will ask an accounting, that the little will end and the reckoning will begin. The verse condemns, in other words, the denial that never speaks but lives: the denial of the man who eats and does not thank, enjoys and does not remember, prospers and does not ask, lives as though the world were all and the term were never — and whose whole existence, in the Qur'an's diagnosis, is a continuous act of covering the truth with heedlessness: *"Nay, but man is a tyrant, for he sees himself as self-sufficient"* (96:6–7); *"And when We bestow favor upon man, he turns away and withdraws; and when evil touches him, he is in despair"* (17:83, in sense; 41:49–51).

**The Enjoyment That Denies Its Own Source**

The ninth woe's connection to verse 46 focuses the sūrah's indictment on the most universal form of denial: the denial that hides inside enjoyment. The commentators articulated the mechanism with precision: every enjoyment of the world is a gift — the food, the drink, the health, the wealth, the family, the ease — and every gift carries within it a testimony to its Giver; the enjoyment that is received without acknowledgment becomes, in the Qur'an's terms, a covering — the gift itself is turned into the veil that hides the Giver. The sūrah of al-Naḥl describes the process: *"And they assign to what they do not know a portion of what We have provided them... They recognize the blessing of God; then they deny it, and most of them are ungrateful"* (16:56, 83, in sense). The deniers of verse 46 are eating and enjoying — the very verbs of the verse — and the woe of verse 47 declares that the eating and enjoying, without the acknowledgment of the Giver, constitute the denial that the refrain has been condemning through the whole sūrah: the denial is not only in the creed that is spoken but in the life that is lived; the man who lives on the gifts of God as though they had no Giver is denying the Giver in the only language that his daily life speaks. The ninth woe thus completes the sūrah's widening of the indictment: the first woes condemned the explicit deniers of the Resurrection; the later woes have descended through the denials of history, creation, blessing, the Fire, the judgment, and the reward; and this woe reaches the lowest and most common level — the denial of the man who simply enjoys, and never once turns to the One who gave him the enjoyment.

**The Prosperity That the Qur'an Refuses to Envy**

The verse also carries a quiet rebuke to a temptation that afflicts believers in every age: the temptation to envy the prosperity of the deniers. The Makkan believers saw the deniers eating and enjoying — wealthy, secure, unpunished — while the believers themselves were often poor and persecuted, and the temptation to conclude that prosperity proved rightness was real: the Qur'an records the believers' trial in the sūrah of al-Kahf: *"And do not extend your eyes to what We have given to parties of them to enjoy — the flower of this world's life — that We may test them thereby; the provision of your Lord is better and more lasting"* (20:131). The ninth woe answers the temptation at its root: the enjoyment of the deniers is not a blessing to be envied but a sentence to be pitied — "eat and enjoy yourselves a little" is the portion of the condemned, and the woe that follows it declares the worth of the portion: the prosperity of the deniers is the down payment of their loss, the "little" that precedes the Fire, the meal that ends in the woe. The commentators said: when the believer sees the denier prospering, let him read verse 46 and verse 47 together — the prosperity is the "little," and the woe is the "that Day" — and let him measure the prosperity of this world, which ends in woe, against the shade and springs of the Garden, which never end. The believer's own portion may be smaller in this world — but it is the portion of the traveler, the provision for the road, the "little" that is spent on the journey to the welcome that has no end.

**The Accumulation of the Woes and the Hardening of the Hearers**

The ninth occurrence of the refrain also invites reflection on the cumulative effect of the repetition — an effect that the sūrah itself thematizes in its own structure. Nine times the woe has fallen; nine times the hearer has been warned; and the commentators observed that the repetition itself divides the hearers exactly as the Day of Division will divide them: for the believer, each recurrence deepens the fear and the turning — the heart that is alive hears the ninth woe as freshly as the first, and each repetition adds to its vigilance; for the denier, the repetition has the opposite effect — the warning that is repeated without consequence becomes, in the hardened heart, a noise: the Qur'an describes the process: *"Nay, but their hearts have become rusted by what they earned"* (83:14, in sense); *"And when We recite to them Our signs, they say: 'We have heard — if we willed, we could say the like of this'"* (8:31, in sense). The ninth woe is the sūrah's test of the hearer's state: whoever hears it and is moved is alive; whoever hears it and is unmoved has reached the state that the tenth woe will seal. The classical scholars drew the practical lesson: the Qur'an's repetition is a mercy that the hearer can exhaust — the warning repeated too often without response hardens what it was meant to soften — and the believer is commanded to answer each recitation of the woe with a fresh turning, so that the repetition of the Qur'an never becomes the rust that the Qur'an describes.

**The Penultimate Warning and the Door That Remains**

For the hearer of the sūrah in this world, the ninth woe is the penultimate stroke of the refrain — and its penultimacy is itself a message: the sequence is almost complete, the tenth woe is approaching, and after the tenth the refrain will never be recited again in this chapter — the text will close with the question of verse 50, and the hearer will be left with the choice that the whole sūrah has been pressing. The ninth woe is the last woe that falls while the scene is still the world — after it, the sūrah will return to the Day for the final scene: the command to bow (v. 48), the tenth woe (v. 49), and the closing question (v. 50) — and the hearer who has not turned by the ninth woe will hear the tenth and then the silence of the sūrah's end. The mercy within the structure is the mercy of the countdown: the hearer knows the refrain is nearly done, knows the sūrah is nearly closed, knows the recitation will soon leave him with the question of verse 50 — and the knowledge is the final summons: while the woes are still being recited, the door is still open; while the refrain is still a warning, the sentence is still suspended; and the hearer who lets the ninth woe do its work will meet the tenth woe not as his sentence but as the last stroke of the warning that saved him — the warning that drove him, before the sūrah closed, into the company of the reverent, of whom the text has already spoken, and of whom it will speak no more: "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs."

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:48

> **And when it is said unto them, "Bow!" they bow not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Indictment: The Refusal of the Bow**

The sūrah's last scene of the deniers is its most concrete: *wa-idhā qīla lahumu irkaʿū lā yarkaʿūn* — "And when it is said unto them: 'Bow!' they bow not." The verb *irkaʿū* is the command to bow — the *rukūʿ*, the bowing of the prayer, the bending of the back before God that is the very posture of submission — and the verse describes the deniers' response to the command in the most economical form possible: they are told to bow, and they do not bow. The commentators unfolded the verse's reference in two complementary directions, both preserved in the sources. The first reading takes the command in its primary sense as the command to perform the prayer — bowing being the essential act of the canonical prayer, so that the command "bow" stands for the whole of the worship that prayer comprises — and the verse condemns the deniers who, when called to prayer, refused: al-Qurṭubī recorded that the verse was said to have been revealed concerning some disbelievers who protested that the postures of the prayer were an insult — the bending of the back, the placing of the forehead on the ground seemed to them a humiliation beneath their dignity, and they refused the command on the ground of their pride. The second reading, also preserved by al-Qurṭubī and others, takes the command in its eschatological sense: the verse refers to the Day of Judgment itself, when the deniers will be called to prostrate before their Lord and will not be able to do so — the prostration that the believers performed in the world being granted to them on the Day as their final honor, and the prostration that the deniers refused in the world being withheld from them on the Day as their final disgrace.

**The Prayer That the Deniers Refused**

The first reading connects the verse to the central institution of the religion that the deniers rejected — the prayer — and the commentators gathered the Qur'an's portrait of the deniers' relation to it. The prayer is described in the Qur'an as the very mark of faith: *"Successful indeed are the believers, who are humble in their prayer"* (23:1–2); and the deniers are described by their absence from it: *"What led you into the Scorching Fire? They will say: 'We were not among those who prayed'"* (74:42–43). The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, in a saying that the commentators on this verse cite: *"There is no good in a religion that has no bowing and no prostration"* (recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī in their commentaries on this verse) — the bowing and the prostration being the bodily signature of the religion's whole meaning: the creature's acknowledgment, in the most physical form possible, that he is a servant and God is the Lord. The deniers of Makkah refused this acknowledgment: the command "Bow!" was spoken to them by the Prophet ﷺ and by the call to prayer itself, and they answered with the refusal that the verse records — a refusal rooted, as the occasion of revelation preserved by al-Qurṭubī indicates, in pride: the bowing seemed to them a lowering, and they would not lower themselves before the One who had created them from a base fluid (v. 20) and lodged them in a secure dwelling (v. 21). The verse's irony is total: the creature who refused to bow before his Creator on the ground of dignity was the creature whose dignity was entirely a gift of the One before Whom he refused to bow.

**The Prostration That the Day Withholds**

The second reading gives the verse its eschatological depth, and the commentators developed it with the Qur'an's other descriptions of the prostration of the Day. The Qur'an describes the Day of Judgment as a day of prostration — a final prostration to which the believers are invited and the deniers are not: *"On the Day when the shin is bared, and they are called to prostrate, but they cannot. Their eyes are humbled; humiliation covers them — for they were called to prostrate while they were yet whole"* (68:42–43). The sūrah of Yā Sīn adds the detail of the call: *"The Day when the shin is bared and they are called to prostrate, they will not be able"* (68:42, in sense); and the commentators on this verse of al-Mursalāt connected the two passages: the deniers are called to prostrate on the Day — and cannot, because the prostration of the Day is granted only to those who prostrated in the world, and the backs that refused to bow in the world have lost, on the Day, the very capacity to bow. The scene is one of the most terrible in the Qur'an's eschatology: the believers, who spent their lives bowing and prostrating in the prayer, are invited on the Day to the final prostration before their Lord and are honored by it; the deniers, who refused the bow whenever it was commanded in the world, are called to the same prostration and find their bodies rigid — the refusal that was a choice in the world has become, on the Day, a condition: they are called to bow, and they bow not — not because they are not commanded but because they cannot, and the humiliation of the inability is the punishment of the pride that refused.

**The Insult of Worship and the Worship of Insult**

The verse's occasion — the deniers who protested that the postures of prayer were an insult — opens a window on the psychology of the refusal that the commentators explored. The deniers measured worship by the standards of the world's dignities: in the courts of kings, men stand; the bowing and the prostration are for servants, and the Makkan aristocrats — the great men of Quraysh — could not imagine themselves in the posture of servants before a Lord they did not see. The Qur'an records their protest in another sūrah: *"And when it is said to them: 'Prostrate to the Compassionate,' they say: 'And what is the Compassionate? Shall we prostrate to what you command us?' — and it increases their aversion"* (25:60). The commentators noted the inversion at the heart of the protest: the deniers refused the bow because it seemed an insult to their dignity — and the refusal itself was the real insult: the creature who will not bow to his Creator on the ground of dignity is like the wave that refuses to fall before the sea on the ground of its height. And the sūrah's structure exposes the irony completely: the deniers who found the bow an insult are the same deniers who, at verse 46, were told to "eat and enjoy yourselves a little" — the aristocrats who would not bend their backs to God spent their lives bent over their tables — and the Day of Division will show which posture was the true dignity: the believers' backs, bowed in the world, will be raised in honor on the Day; the deniers' backs, unbowed in the world, will be unable to bow when the final prostration is commanded — and the humiliation that they feared in the worship will overtake them in the judgment.

**The Command That Is Still Being Spoken**

For the hearer of the sūrah in this world, the verse's two readings converge on one present reality: the command "Bow!" is still being spoken. The call to prayer still rises five times a day; the command of the revelation is still recited; the posture of the bow and the prostration is still available to every back that will bend — and the verse's warning is that the refusal of the present command is the loss of the future prostration: the deniers of Makkah refused the bow when the bow was a choice, and the Day will call them to a prostration that is no longer a choice but an impossibility. The believers, by contrast, are those who answered the command while the answering was possible: the five daily prayers are, in the Qur'an's framing, the daily rehearsals of the Day's prostration — the believer who bows in the prayer at the command of his Lord is practicing the bow that will be granted to him on the Day, and the believer who prostrates in the world is securing the prostration of the Hereafter: the Prophet ﷺ said: *"The nearest a servant comes to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so multiply supplication"* (Muslim 482). The verse of al-Mursalāt is thus the sūrah's final summons in the most physical terms it uses: the whole religion is contained in the bow — the acknowledgment of the Lord, the surrender of the pride, the posture of the servant — and the whole of the Day's outcome is contained in the answer to the command: those who bowed when the bow was commanded will prostrate in honor on the Day; those who refused when the bow was commanded will be called to prostrate and will not be able — and the last woe of the sūrah, which now follows, pronounces the verdict over the refusal of the bow.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:49

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tenth Woe: The Refrain's Final Stroke**

The refrain sounds for the tenth and final time: *waylun yawmaʾidhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" Its position, after the description of the refusal to bow (v. 48), makes it the seal of the sūrah's entire indictment. Nine times the woe has fallen — on the denial of the Day, of history, of the first creation, of the blessings, of the Fire, of the judgment, of God's power, of the reward, and of the Giver of enjoyment — and the tenth woe falls on the denial that verse 48 has just described: the denial of the command itself. Al-Nasafī and al-Shawkānī read this final occurrence as the verdict on those who deny the commands and prohibitions of God — who refuse the obligations that the religion lays upon them, foremost among them the prayer, the bow that verse 48 described. The sūrah has moved through the whole range of denial — from the denial of the Resurrection to the denial of the blessings to the denial of the command — and the final woe closes the indictment at its most practical point: the denial of the command to worship is the denial in which all the other denials come to rest, because the man who will not bow to his Lord has denied, in the posture of his own body, everything that the sūrah's earlier verses affirmed — the Lordship that the oaths swore, the Day that the apocalypse described, the Power that the embryology proved, the Mercy that the earth displayed. The tenfold refrain completes its course exactly where the religion completes its course: at the command to worship, and the refusal of the command.

**The Prayer as the Sum of the Sūrah's Argument**

The final woe's connection to the prayer gathers the whole sūrah into a single practical conclusion, and the commentators articulated the connection with care. The prayer is the act in which all the truths that the sūrah has been establishing are performed: the believer who stands to pray affirms the Lordship that the oaths of verses 1–7 swore; the believer who bows affirms the majesty that the dissolution of the heavens (vv. 8–10) announced; the believer who prostrates affirms the creating power that the embryology of verses 20–23 demonstrated and the determining of verse 23 praised; the believer who prays in gratitude affirms the blessings of verses 25–27 and their Giver; the believer who prays in fear affirms the Fire of verses 29–33; the believer who prays in hope affirms the Garden of verses 41–44; and the believer who prays through the days of his life is answering, daily, the command of verse 48 — "Bow!" — in the only way that the command can be answered while the world still stands. The prayer is, in this sense, the practical summary of the entire sūrah: the ten woes condemned the denials of the truth; the prayer is the daily affirmation of the same truth; and the final woe, falling on the refusal of the bow, declares that the denial that matters most in the end is the denial that refuses the worship — because the worship is the act in which the creature acknowledges everything that the sūrah has said about God, the Day, and the human being. The commentators noted that the Prophet ﷺ made the prayer the boundary between belief and denial: *"The covenant that is between us and them is the prayer; whoever abandons it has disbelieved"* (al-Tirmidhī 2621; he graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; also recorded by others, from Buraydah) — the prayer being the practical line of the Division that the sūrah has been drawing through all its verses.

**The Ten Woes and the Ten Denials: The Completed Count**

The tenth occurrence of the refrain also completes the count that the sūrah has been keeping, and the commentators who attended to the number drew out its significance. Ten times the woe has been pronounced; ten denials have been condemned; and the number ten — the number of completion in the Qur'an's arithmetic, the number of the days that completed the fast of the expiation (2:196), the number of the nights of the covenant (2:51, in sense), the number of the rewards of the single good deed (*"Whoever comes with a good deed shall have ten like it,"* 6:160) — marks the completeness of the indictment: the case against the deniers has been fully stated, each denial named and condemned, and the tenth woe leaves nothing uncondemned and nothing unstated. The sūrah's refrain is now exhausted: it will not sound again in this chapter; the tenth stroke is the last stroke, and the text that follows the tenth woe will not repeat the warning but will close the whole with the question of verse 50 — the question that leaves the deniers, at the end of the sūrah, with nothing to say and nothing to believe in. The classical scholars noted the finality of the tenth occurrence: the repetition that began at verse 15 as a warning ends at verse 49 as a seal — the ten woes have completed their work, the warning has been fully delivered, and whatever follows the tenth woe is not another warning but the closing of the book: "So in what discourse after this will they believe?"

**The Woe That the Believer Turns Into Worship**

The tenth woe, like all its predecessors, is recited in the world — and its position after the command to bow gives the believer the sūrah's final practical instruction: the answer to the tenth woe is the bow itself. The believer who hears "Woe that Day to the deniers!" and answers by bowing in the prayer has understood the whole sūrah: the woe falls on those who refuse the command, and the refuge from the woe is the performance of the command — the bow that acknowledges the Lord, the prostration that surrenders the pride, the prayer that performs the faith. The sūrah's ten woes and the believer's daily prayers stand in the most exact correspondence: the woes condemn the ten denials; the prayer affirms the truths that the denials refused; and the believer who prays is, in effect, answering each woe as it is recited — the woe over the denial of the Day is answered by the prayer that awaits the Day, the woe over the denial of the blessings by the prayer that thanks the Giver, the woe over the refusal of the bow by the bow itself. The classical spiritual writers said: the Qur'an's threats are all invitations, and the tenth woe is the sūrah's final invitation — the invitation to answer the last warning with the first act of worship, to let the completed count of the woes drive the hearer into the completed count of the prayers, and to meet the Day of Division not as one of the deniers over whom the woe is pronounced but as one of the reverent of whom the sūrah said: "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs" — the company that the tenth woe cannot touch, because they answered the command to bow while the answering was still possible, and the bow that they performed in the world has become, on the Day, the prostration of honor before the Lord of the worlds.

**The Last Word Before the Question**

For the reciter who reaches the tenth woe, the sūrah has arrived at its final threshold: one verse remains, and the remaining verse is not a warning but a question — the question with which the whole chapter will close, leaving the hearer in the presence of the choice that the sūrah has been pressing since its first oath. The tenth woe stands at the threshold as the last statement of the sūrah's case: the deniers have been fully described, fully warned, fully condemned; the refrain has sounded its complete tenfold course; and the text now turns from the indictment to the question that the indictment makes unavoidable. The hearer who has followed the sūrah to this point — who has heard the oaths, the promise, the apocalypse, the history, the embryology, the blessings, the Fire, the judgment, the Garden, and the ten woes — is now left, at the end of the chapter, with the question of verse 50, and the question is addressed to him as directly as the woes were addressed to the deniers: after all of this — after the Reminder that was sent, the evidence that was exhibited, the warnings that were repeated, and the refrains that were struck ten times — in what discourse will he believe, if he will not believe in this one? The tenth woe is the last word of the sūrah's warning; the question that follows is the first word of the hearer's answer — and the answer that the whole sūrah has been constructed to elicit is the answer that the believer gives in the bow: I believe; I bow; I submit.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:50

> **So in what discourse after this will they believe?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Closes the Case**

The sūrah ends with a question — and the question is the most devastating close the Qur'an gives to any of its Makkan chapters: *fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn* — "So in what discourse after this will they believe?" The particle *fa* — "so, then" — draws the conclusion from everything that has preceded: after the oaths and the promise, after the apocalypse and the history, after the embryology and the blessings, after the Fire and the Garden, after the ten woes and the command to bow — *after all of this* — in what discourse will they believe, if they will not believe in this? The word *ḥadīth* — "discourse, saying, report" — is the Qur'an's own word for itself in its most general sense: the message, the recitation, the news; and the verse's question is addressed to the deniers who have just heard the entire sūrah and refused it. The question is rhetorical in form but total in its reach: it does not ask whether the deniers will believe; it asks what they *could* believe — the implication being that the deniers who reject this discourse have no discourse left: the Qur'an is the final message, the last Reminder, the conclusive discourse, and the rejection of the conclusive leaves nothing to accept. The verse is closely paralleled in the sūrah of al-Jāthiyah: *"These are the signs of God that We recite to you in truth. So in what discourse after God and His signs will they believe?"* (45:6); and the Qur'an asks the same question in its other closing passages: *"Then in which of the discourses of your Lord will they believe?"* (7:185, in sense); *"And what — after the truth — is there but error?"* (10:32).

**The Logic of the Final Question**

The question's logic deserves to be examined, because it is the sūrah's whole argument in its most compressed form. The deniers of Makkah were not without beliefs: they believed in their idols, their ancestors, their customs, their own judgment — the Qur'an records the full inventory of their creeds: *"And they have taken gods besides God, that they might be helped"* (36:74, in sense); *"We found our fathers upon a way"* (43:22, in sense); *"There is nothing but our present life"* (45:24, in sense). And the sūrah's closing question exposes the vacuity of every alternative: the idols were stones that could neither hear nor help — in what discourse would they believe, after the discourse that showed the idols' nothingness? The ancestors were men who had died and been gathered — in what discourse would they believe, after the discourse that showed the fate of the ancestors who denied? The present life was a little enjoyment bounded by death — in what discourse would they believe, after the discourse that showed the littleness of the little and the Day that follows it? The Qur'an's question strips the deniers of every alternative one by one: after the truth, there is nothing but error (10:32); after the discourse of God, there is only the discourse of the deniers themselves — and the discourse of the deniers is the discourse of those who deny. The commentators said: the question is the sūrah's way of saying that unbelief is not a position but a void — the denier who rejects the Reminder does not stand on another ground; he stands on nothing, and the question leaves him standing there, with no discourse to believe in and no ground beneath his feet.

**The Two Questions That Frame the Sūrah's End**

The closing question of verse 50 answers, in the deepest sense, the question that opened the sūrah's apocalyptic scene: verse 12 asked "for what day is it appointed?" and the verses that followed answered: for the Day of Division. The sūrah's last question — "in what discourse after this will they believe?" — completes the frame: the first question asked what the Day was; the last question asks what the deniers will believe in its place; and between the two questions the whole sūrah has been laid — the evidence that the Day is real and the Reminder is true. The deniers who were asked in verse 12 to name the Day and could not answer with anything but denial are asked in verse 50 to name the discourse they will believe, and the question stands unanswered, as it has stood for fourteen centuries: no denier of the Qur'an has ever produced the discourse that replaced it — no scripture, no philosophy, no system of the deniers' own devising has ever filled the place that the Qur'an leaves empty when it is rejected. The commentators noted the finality of the structure: the sūrah opened with oaths and closes with a question; it began by swearing to the truth and ends by challenging the deniers to name their alternative; and the silence that follows the final question is the silence of the verse 35 — the silence of those who have nothing to say because they have nothing to believe.

**The Discourse That the Believer Answers With the Bow**

For the believer, the closing question is not a challenge but a confession — and the commentators drew the two sides of the question with care. To the denier, the question says: you have rejected the discourse of God; name the discourse that remains. To the believer, the question says: this is the discourse — the only discourse — and the believer answers the question not with another discourse but with the act that verse 48 commanded: he bows. The believer's whole life is the answer to the sūrah's final question: the prayer that he prays is the affirmation that this discourse is the truth; the Qur'an that he recites is the acknowledgment that no discourse after it is needed; the faith that he lives is the demonstration that the question of verse 50 has an answer — and the answer is the discourse itself, believed, performed, and loved. The sūrah's end thus returns the believer to its beginning: the oaths of verses 1–6 swore by the sending of the Reminder; the final question asks what discourse will be believed after the Reminder; and the believer's answer is the recitation itself — the Qur'an that he holds, the sūrah that he has just completed, the discourse that he believes and that has brought him, verse by verse, from the oaths to the question, and from the question back to the recitation: the believer believes in this discourse, and his belief is the bow of verse 48, the prayer, the guarding, the reverence — the whole of the life that the sūrah has been describing in its portrait of the reverent: "Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs."

**The End That Leaves the Choice Open**

The sūrah closes, and the closing question leaves the hearer in the one place where the sūrah has wanted him all along: at the choice. The Qur'an does not end al-Mursalāt with a verdict — the verdicts have been pronounced in the ten woes; it ends with a question, because the question is the only thing that can still be answered by the hearer in this world: after this discourse, what will you believe? The denier hears the question and has no answer — and the absence of the answer is the beginning of the answer: if there is no discourse after this one, then this one is the discourse, and the denial of it is the denial of everything. The believer hears the question and answers it with the recitation itself — with the sūrah that he has just completed, with the Qur'an that contains it, with the bow that verse 48 commanded and the prayer that the bow performs. And between the two hearings stands the whole of the human freedom that the sūrah's argument has been addressing: the freedom to believe the discourse or to deny it, to join the deniers over whom the ten woes are pronounced or the reverent who are amid shade and springs, to answer the final question with the silence of verse 35 or with the bow of verse 48. The sūrah of those sent forth ends where it began — with the Reminder delivered and the choice open; and the last word of the chapter is not a period but a question, because the chapter's whole purpose was to bring its hearer to the question and to leave him standing before it — the question that every hearer answers with his life, and that the Day of Division will answer finally: "So in what discourse after this will they believe?"
