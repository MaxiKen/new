# Sūrah al-Mursalāt (Chapter 77) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Named After the Sent**

Sūrah al-Mursalāt takes its name from its opening word: *wal-mursalāt* — "By those sent forth." It belongs to the great family of early Makkan sūrahs that open not with narrative or law but with a volley of oaths sworn by unnamed cosmic agents — the winds, the angels, the bearers of revelation — before pivoting sharply to the one theme that consumed the first years of the Qur'anic mission: the reality of the Day of Resurrection and the fate of those who deny it. Like most Makkan sūrahs, it is short, dense, musical, and unrelenting; it carries none of the legislative apparatus of the Madinan period, because its audience was not yet a community that needed governing — it was a handful of believers surrounded by a city that mocked the very idea that the dead could rise. The sūrah is also known by a second name, *al-ʿUrf*, taken from the second word of the first verse, *ʿurfan* — "in succession" — a word whose root will repay close study in the commentary on verse 1. The early community knew this sūrah intimately; it stands in the twenty-ninth part (*juzʾ*) of the Qur'an, among the short Makkan chapters whose recitation formed the daily bread of prayer, and its refrain — *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn*, "Woe that Day to the deniers!" — became one of the most recognizable refrains in the entire Book.

**The Revelation in a Cave**

The tradition preserves a vivid scene attached to the revelation of this very sūrah. It is related that a group of the Prophet's Companions were with the Messenger of God ﷺ in a cave when *Sūrat al-Mursalāt* came down upon him, and they received it directly from his lips as soon as the revelation itself had come — meaning they recited it along with him, following his recitation word by word as it descended. Then:

> *"Suddenly a snake came out, and the Messenger of God ﷺ said, 'Get it and kill it!' We ran to kill it, but it outstripped us. The Messenger of God ﷺ said, 'It has escaped your evil, as you too have escaped its evil.'"* (related in the commentary tradition; cited by Ālūsī in *Rūḥ al-Maʿānī* and al-Qurṭubī in *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qur'ān*)

The narration is more than picturesque. It embeds the sūrah of the Last Day inside a small earthly parable of judgment: a creature whose visible form concealed a potentially deadly interior; a command given; a pursuit; an escape; and then the Prophet's ﷺ remarkable verdict — a mutual deliverance, each party saved from the other's harm. The Companions learned in miniature what the sūrah would teach in the large: that danger is not always obvious, that commands matter, that outcomes belong to God, and that every soul — human or serpent — carries with it either harm or safety for others. The cave scene also testifies to how eagerly the early community seized each new revelation: they did not wait for a scribe or a codex; they took the sūrah from the Prophet's mouth *as it was arriving*. Before the Qur'an was a book on a shelf, it was recited speech in the dark of a mountain cave — which is precisely how this sūrah wishes to be understood: as *mursal*, sent, arriving, urgent.

**The Architecture of the Tenfold Woe**

The sūrah's most striking formal feature is its refrain. The cry *Woe that Day to the deniers!* occurs exactly ten times — at verses 15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 40, 45, 47, and 49 — dividing the sūrah into a series of panels, each of which presents evidence or warning, and each of which closes with the same hammer-blow. Classical Arabic rhetoric (*al-balāghah*) knows this device as *takrār* — deliberate repetition for the sake of emphasis and intimidation — and the commentators observed that the refrain functions like the tolling of a bell: each toll marks the end of one argument and insists that the reader take a position before the next argument begins. Al-Qurṭubī notes that each occurrence can be read against its immediate context, so that the "deniers" are successively exposed as deniers of the Resurrection, of God's precedents in history, of His power manifest in their own bodies, of His providence in the earth, of the Fire, of the Day's silence, of His gathering, of His reward, of His respite, and finally of His commands. The refrain thus refuses to let the category of "denial" remain abstract; it itemizes it, the way a prosecuting indictment counts the charges one by one.

**The Movement of the Whole**

The sūrah moves through a deliberate progression. It opens with five oaths by the sent-forth agents of the unseen world (vv. 1–5), names their double mission — to absolve and to warn (v. 6) — and then lands the oath's answer with the certitude that everything promised *will befall* (v. 7). It then paints the cosmic dismantling that ushers in the Last Day: stars blotted out, heaven split, mountains scattered, messengers gathered — all appointed, the sūrah asks rhetorically, *for what day?* — and answers itself with a name: the Day of Division (vv. 8–14). From there the sūrah assembles its case in three courts of evidence: the court of history (did God not destroy the first deniers and make the later ones follow them? vv. 16–19); the court of human origins (were you not created from a humble drop, lodged, measured, determined? vv. 20–24); and the court of the inhabited earth (did He not make it a receptacle for the living and the dead, anchor it with mountains, and water it sweet? vv. 25–28). Then the verdict is read aloud: the deniers are told to walk into the very Fire they denied, beneath its useless branching shadow, among sparks like castles and yellow camels, into a silence where no excuse is permitted (vv. 29–40). The sentence is interrupted — as if the scroll of judgment paused — by a glimpse of the opposite verdict: the reverent amid shade, springs, and desired fruits, told to eat and drink in recompense for what they did (vv. 41–45). And the sūrah closes on the most practical charge in the whole chapter: when they are told "Bow!" they do not bow (v. 48) — and the final question, ringing like a door shut on all further argument: *in what discourse, after this, will they believe?* (v. 50).

**Why the Short Sūrahs Are Not Small**

The early Makkan sūrahs repay slow reading precisely because their compression is not thinness. Al-Mursalāt covers in fifty verses a territory that the Madinan sūrahs spread across hundreds: the structure of revelation, angelology, eschatology, the philosophy of history, embryology, geology and hydrology as signs, the phenomenology of Hell and Paradise, and the sociology of prayer — each field touched with a single stroke, like a master painter indicating a whole landscape with three lines. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī both treat the sūrah's oath-cluster as one of the places where the Qur'an teaches its own doctrine of *how God argues*: He swears by His own workings — wind, rain, revelation — because for a heart willing to look, the world itself is already testimony. The commentary that follows honors that density by taking each of the fifty verses as an independent discourse: quoting it, opening its Arabic, consulting the classical commentators, cross-referencing the Qur'an's own parallels, and then asking what the verse demands of a human being living now. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is short; the short chapters are, if anything, the most concentrated medicine in the Book, and medicine is measured by potency, not by volume.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:1

> **By those sent forth in succession!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Opens with a Swarm**

The sūrah launches with a single clause of swearing — *wal-mursalāti ʿurfā* — and nothing else: no subject yet introduced, no statement yet sworn to, no listener yet addressed. The Arabic puts the oath particle *wa* ("by") directly before *al-mursalāt*, "those sent forth," a passive participle in the feminine plural from the root *r-s-l*, whose essential field is *sending with a message*: a *rasūl* is a messenger, *risālah* a mission or epistle, *mursal* one dispatched. That the participle is feminine plural tells the ear something immediately, because in Arabic grammar non-human plurals — and equally the plurals used for angels in many Qur'anic passages — take feminine agreement. Before a single interpretation is chosen, the sound of the verse already gestures toward a host of invisible agents, a crowd of dispatched beings, moving in ranks. And they move *ʿurfā* — the second word, rendered "in succession," which is itself the source of the sūrah's alternate name, *al-ʿUrf*.

**The Word That Compares Waves to a Mane**

*ʿurfan* is a verbal noun from the root *ʿ-r-f*, and the classical lexicons preserve a beautifully concrete image at its base: the *ʿurf* of a horse is its mane — the line of hair that stands in a crest along the neck. From this, *ʿurf* came to mean anything that rises in a ridge or row, and the verb form used here (*taʿāruf*) describes things following one another so closely that they resemble the continuous ridge of a mane — link after link, wave after wave, each arriving on the heels of the one before it, without gap. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī all gloss the phrase as "one after another" (*baʿduhā fī atfāl baʿd*), some after some. The image is kinetic: what God swears by is not a static host but a procession — dispatch upon dispatch, mission upon mission, an unbroken relay. Whoever or whatever the mursalāt are, the oath insists on their *continuity*, and that insistence will matter enormously for the sūrah's argument: the sending is not a single event in the past but a sustained, successive activity of God that has never ceased.

**Angels, Winds, or Prophets — the Classical Discussion**

The early exegetes differed — productively — over the identity of the sent-forth. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported in Ibn Kathīr's *Tafsīr*, and the majority among the early generations took the mursalāt to be the angels: the unseen host dispatched from heaven on errands of revelation, provision, and decree. Al-Ṭabarī, characteristically refusing to narrow what the Qur'an left broad, reports the two major readings and then allows both: the mursalāt may be the winds — sent one after another, as the rest of the oath-cluster (storms, spreaders) suggests — or the angels, sent in succession with God's messages; and he finds the language room enough for each, since the Qur'an elsewhere explicitly calls the winds *mursālāt*, "sent": *"And in the sent winds, and in the winds that blow violently"* (77:1–2's cousin at 51:1–2), and again, speaking of the fertilizing winds as *"mursalāt"* — and the famous passage where the progeny of Adam is asked, *"Do you not consider what God sends down from the sky?"* Yet Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī also record the reading of the mursalāt as the prophets themselves — the messengers of God following one another across the generations, Nūḥ after Nūḥ's predecessors, Ibrāhīm after the prophets before him, and finally Muhammad ﷺ as the seal of a long relay. The three readings are not rivals so much as three angles on one truth: God has never left the world unsent-to. Winds succeed winds across the face of the earth; angels succeed angels on their errands; prophets succeed prophets through history. The verse swears by the very principle of divine follow-through — and what it swears to, verse 7 will reveal, is that the promised Day likewise *cannot fail to follow*.

**Why God Swears — the Logic of the Oath**

A reader may reasonably ask why the Qur'an — the speech of the One who cannot lie — employs oaths at all. An oath is ordinarily a human instrument, called upon precisely because human speech is doubtful and needs collateral. The classical rhetoricians, al-Zamakhsharī among them, answer that God's oaths are not collateral but *spotlight*: they direct attention to the thing sworn by as evidence for the thing sworn to. In a courtroom of Makkan doubt, where the Resurrection was dismissed as fable, God does not merely assert; He points to phenomena no denier could deny — the winds they felt daily, the rains their flocks lived by, the order they saw in the heavens — and swears by them, as if to say: *you already live inside a system of reliable, recurrent divine sending; why do you stumble at one more promise?* The oath thus turns the pagan's own experience into the premises of the believer's conclusion. This is why the early Makkan sūrahs are so thick with oaths: they were revealed to people who accepted wind, rain, and sky but rejected resurrection, and the oaths weld the accepted to the rejected.

**One Word, Whole Worlds — Reading for Awe Before Explanation**

There is a final, more devotional point that the commentators such as al-Qurṭubī make about deliberately unspecified oaths. Because the verse does not name its object — it says only "By those sent forth in succession!" — every generation of readers is made to *wonder*, and the wondering is itself part of the instruction. Al-Ṭabarī explicitly defends this openness: the wording was revealed broad, and broad it may legitimately be read. A text that specified "by the angels" would let the modern reader file the verse under "angelology" and move on; a text that says only "by those sent forth, in succession" makes the reader scan the whole horizon — wind, rain, angel, prophet, inspiration, every good thing that keeps arriving unearned — and confess how much of life is carried by messengers one never sees. The ordinary person wakes to a day delivered anew each morning, breath drawn in, heartbeats sent one after another like a relay through the night; the scientist observes photons sent from stars, hormones sent through blood, signals sent along nerves — all of it *mursal*, dispatched, none of it self-commissioned. Verse 1 asks its reader to stand inside that relay and feel the question it will soon answer: if God's sending is so constant, so successive, so unbroken — what becomes of those who say the last sending will not come?

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:2

> **By the storming tempests!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Relay to Violence — the Oath Intensifies**

The second oath, *fa'l-ʿāṣifāti ʿaṣfā*, tightens the first verse's rhythm and raises its voltage. Where verse 1 described orderly succession, verse 2 describes force: *ʿāṣifāt* — feminine plural participles from the root *ʿ-ṣ-f* — denotes things that storm, that rush violently, that tear through the air; the verbal noun *ʿaṣfā* makes the intensity absolute: storming with all their might. Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is characteristically direct: these are the winds. The Qur'an uses the same root where it describes the wind sent against the people of ʿĀd: *"So when they saw it as a cloud advancing on their valleys, they said, 'This is a cloud that will give us rain!' — nay, it is what you sought to hasten: a wind wherein is a painful punishment"* (46:24). The verse's form matters: the *fa* at the head ("So by…") marks sequence — the oath series is unfolding like a storm front itself, each clause a new gust.

**The Wind the Arabs Knew — Servant and Scourge**

To grasp the force of this oath, one must hear it as its first audience did. The Arabs of the Hijāz lived exposed to the wind as few settled peoples do: their caravans crossed open desert where a change of wind could shift the sands over a road, bury a camp, or guide a navigator home by its steady direction; their poetry is saturated with winds — the *ṣabā* that blows gently from the east and brings the scent of beloved places, the *janūb* heavy with rain, the *shamāl* that chaps lips and fills the sky with dust, and the *ḥasūm* that kills. The wind was for them the most familiar form of an invisible power with visible effects: no one has ever seen the wind itself, only what it does — and this is exactly why the Qur'an repeatedly marshals it as evidence of God. It is the perfect middle term between the seen and the Unseen: felt by every skin, denied by no one, masterable by no one. The Qur'an says of it: *"And it is He who sends the winds as tidings before His mercy"* (7:57), and, more sternly, of the ʿĀd: *"It left nothing it came upon but that it made it like decayed bones"* (51:42). So when God swears *by the storming tempests*, the first audience heard a reference to a power they had personally fled from in their tents — and the oath quietly asks: the hand that sends such a wind against a defiant nation — will it not raise the dead?

**The Wind as a Standing Miracle in the Qur'anic Argument**

It is worth cataloguing how much theological work the wind performs across the Qur'an, because verse 2's brevity assumes the whole catalogue. The wind appears as: a bearer of glad tidings before rain (7:57; 25:48); a fertilizer that impregnates the clouds (15:22); a wind of mercy that filled the sails of Solomon's fleet (21:81); a servant that carried the throne of the Queen of Sheba in the blinking of an eye (27:39–40); the soft wind that fanned the fire of hospitality for Abraham (21:69, in the reading that the command "be coolness" came upon the fire's wind); a scythe of punishment against ʿĀd (69:6–7; 41:16; 46:24–25) and the people of Lot, whose cities were overturned by it (54:19–20); and at the end of time, the wind that will take the souls of the believers before the Hour. In every case the wind is *sent* — the Qur'an never lets the wind be merely weather. This is why it can serve as an oath here: it is already, in the Qur'anic worldview, a messenger with a commission, an army with a commander. Al-Qurṭubī notes the continuity with verse 1: the winds are themselves among the *mursalāt*, sent forth one after another — the "succession" of verse 1 includes the meteorological relay of gusts that never once stop crossing the earth.

**The Prophet's Own Reverence Before the Storm**

The Sunnah shows that the Prophet ﷺ did not treat violent weather as a neutral fact but as an occasion for immediate theological attention. ʿĀʾishah relates that whenever he saw wind, concern appeared in his face, and when it rained he relaxed; and she asked him about it. He replied:

> *"O God, I ask You for its good, the good that is in it, and the good with which it was sent; and I seek refuge in You from its evil, the evil that is in it, and the evil with which it was sent."* (al-Bukhārī 1033; Muslim 899, from ʿĀʾishah)

This short prayer is almost a prose translation of the oath-clusters of Sūrahs 51 and 77. It acknowledges that the wind is *sent* (it has a mission — *mā ursila bihi*), that its errand may be mercy or may be otherwise, and that the only sane human posture before it is petition, not control. The believer who internalizes verse 2 will never again feel a raw gust as mere physics; it arrives as a dispatched thing, carrying good or trial from its Sender.

**Why the Oath Flatters Nothing — Power Unflinching in an Early Makkan Sūrah**

Notice what is *absent* from these opening oaths: any softening, any flattery of the audience, any word of comfort. Early Makkan revelation thundered, and this sūrah is among the thunderers. Modern readers sometimes find this hard; but the psychological logic is sound and merciful. The sūrah is about to announce an event of unmatched severity — the Day when the cosmos itself is dismantled — and it begins by swearing by powers that already dwarf every human project. The sequence teaches the heart to calibrate: if a single earthly storm grounds a continent's aircraft, collapses a city's power grid, and reminds ten million people in an hour how small they are, then the Day that *blots out stars* is not a fairy tale but the natural extrapolation of a power already displayed hourly in the atmosphere. The oath by storming tempests is thus a piece of divine pedagogy: God begins with a phenomenon the denier has personally felt, so that the denial of the greater thing might become emotionally impossible once honestly faced. The storm is the syllabus; the Day of Division is the examination.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:3

> **By the spreaders spreading!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Root about Opening What Was Folded**

The third oath, *wa'l-nāshirāti nashrā*, is built on the root *n-sh-r*, whose core meaning is to unfold, spread out, expand, or bring forth what was gathered or hidden. From it comes *nashr* — the spreading of news; *nashīrah* — the life-giving rain that "spreads" vegetation over a dead land; and, most famously, the term for the Resurrection itself: *al-nashr* is the classical Arabic word for "raising the dead," because it pictures God spreading forth creation from the graves as one spreads out what was folded up — *"and the earth He spread forth"* (79:30). The Qur'an exploits this root deliberately: the One who will *spread* the dead from the earth is already *spreading* rain over it, as if resurrection had a rehearsal every spring. Thus *al-nāshirāt* — the spreaders, again feminine plural participles, another invisible host — and *nashrā*, the verbal noun: spreading utterly, spreading as their very nature.

**Three Classical Identifications — Rain, Wind, and Angels**

Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record the principal readings. First: the winds, because they *spread* the clouds across the sky — the Qur'an describes this exact operation: *"God is He who sends the winds, and they stir up a cloud, and He spreads it in the sky as He wills"* (30:48). Second: the rains, which spread over the earth, soaking and reviving it — the object spread being the water itself, distributed from sky to soil. Third — the reading of ʿIkrimah and others reported by al-Ṭabarī — the angels, who spread God's blessings, decrees, and revealed books among His creation; al-Ājurrī, in the report preserved in the commentary tradition, adds the image of angels spreading their wings, which the Qur'an itself attests: the bearers of the Throne *"and those who carry the Throne and those around it glorify their Lord… and their wings spread out in humility"* (40:7, in the reading of *khashshan* that describes wings). Al-Ṭabarī's verdict on this cluster is a small masterpiece of exegetical method:

> *"The verse does not specify one of these meanings to the exclusion of the others, for the winds spread the clouds, and the rain spreads over the earth, and the angels spread the revelation and the books of God — and all of them are among those whom God has sent forth."*

In other words, the oath is deliberately wide, and its breadth is its argument. Spreading — dissemination, distribution, the scattering of good things from a hidden source to open need — is one of the most constant operations of God in the world, and the verse swears by its every agent at once.

**Why the Feminine Plural Keeps Pointing Upward**

The grammatical form of these oath-words rewards a moment's attention. Arabic uses the feminine plural ending (*-āt*) for groups of women, for non-human plurals — animals, things, forces — and, notably, for the angels in a great many Qur'anic verses: *"Praise belongs to God, the Originator of the heavens and the earth, who appointed the angels (*malā'ikatan*) as messengers (*rusulan*) having wings in twos and threes and fours; He increases creation as He wills"* (35:1). The effect across verses 1–5 is that the oath-series is spoken in one unbroken grammatical key — *mursalāt, ʿāṣifāt, nāshirāt, fāriqāt, mulqiyāt* — a chain of feminine plurals like a drumbeat, each clause hammering the same rhyme-home: hosts upon hosts of unseen agents, each described not by what they *are* but by what they *do*. The Qur'an is teaching a metaphysic in its morphology: the created order, in this vision, is not machinery but *staff*; the world runs on errands.

**The Resurrection Rehearsed in Every Spring**

Of all the "spreadings" the verse invokes, the one the sūrah most needs is rain — because the entire argument of early Makkan revelation against the deniers of resurrection is the argument from the revivification of dead earth, and it is a Qur'anic refrain: *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humbled; then, when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells — indeed, He who gives it life will surely give life to the dead; surely He is powerful over all things"* (41:39). The logic is exquisitely empirical. The denier himself concedes that earth can pass from barren hardness to green abundance within weeks — a transition far greater, qualitatively, than anything proposed for the grave. He concedes it happens every year, in his own sight, by means he cannot perform. What, then, is his objection to resurrection — that it is *strange*? The Qur'an answers: strange things are already routine. Al-Rāzī, reasoning along these lines in his *al-Tafsīr al-Kabīr*, observes that whoever grants the first creation — and every denier must, since he exists — has already granted the greater premise, for the second production of a thing is easier than its first, and *"the likeness of the one who denies the second is the likeness of one who admits the seed and denies the harvest."* Verse 3 is thus not ornamental. Every dispersing shower it swears by is a quarterly bulletin, published in green ink, announcing the coming Resurrection — and God swears by the bulletin itself.

**The Sacred Economy of Distribution**

There is a social reading of the oath too, which al-Qurṭubī's style of commentary keeps in view. Spreading is not only how rain and revelation travel; it is the divine method in all provision: knowledge spread from teacher to student, wealth spread from hand to hand through zakāh and trade, mercy spread through one forgiven heart to a household, one reformed man to a city. The Qur'an condemns the opposite gesture — hoarding, gathering in (*kanz*, from the root of "treasuring up") — and blesses every form of *nashr*: *"And let those who hoard what God has bestowed upon them of His bounty not think that it is better for them — nay, it is worse for them"* (3:180). The spreaders of verse 3, whoever they are, model the believer's own vocation: to be in one's small sphere what the wind is to the cloud and the angel to the book — a distributer of good, not a reservoir of it. A life that only receives and stores resembles the stopped sky; a life that spreads resembles the shower.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:4

> **By the discerners discerning!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Root That Names the Book Itself**

The fourth oath — *fa'l-fāriqāti farqā* — turns from the weather-fronts of the first three verses to a different kind of activity: discrimination. The root is *f-r-q*, to separate, to divide, to distinguish between things. Its Qur'anic pedigree is extraordinary, because from this very root comes one of the names of revelation itself: *al-Furqān* — "the Separator," "the Discerner" — used of the Torah given to Moses (*"And We gave Moses the Book and the Furqān"*, 2:53) and of the Qur'an entire (*"Blessed is He who sent down the Furqān upon His servant"*, 25:1). The *Furqān* is so called because it separates truth from falsehood, guidance from misguidance, right from wrong — it is, functionally, *the act of distinguishing made into a book*. So when God swears by *al-fāriqāt* — "the discriminators," those that discern — the oath reaches toward the same family: whoever or whatever separates rightly in God's universe is participating in the very quality by which God named His revelation.

**Who Discerns — the Angels' Legal Work and the Book's Verdict**

The classical commentators offer complementary identifications. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī report that the discerners are the angels, who discriminate between truth and falsehood, and, in al-Qurṭubī's fuller report, between the lawful and the unlawful. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭabarī add that the verse may equally refer to the Qur'an itself and its verses, which separate — *faraqa* — belief from denial, and on the Day of Judgment will separate people into two companies. Ibn Kathīr summarizes both without forcing a choice. And al-Ṭabarī, as he did at verse 3, refuses an exclusive ruling:

> *"God did not specify in this verse one of these meanings to the exclusion of the others; and the angels discriminate between truth and falsehood, and the Quran discriminates between truth and falsehood, and everything by which God distinguishes proof from doubt is among the discerners — so all of them are intended."*

The angelic reading deserves pause, because it reveals a feature of Islamic angelology that the modern reader may miss: the angels are not decorative. On the Qur'an's own showing they record deeds (82:10–12), seize souls (6:61; 32:11), pray for the believers (40:7), and ask forgiveness for those on earth (42:5). The early reports behind this verse give them a further, almost juridical duty: sorting — distinguishing, in what they witness of human hearts and deeds, the sincere from the false. The Prophet ﷺ described the scrutiny of the recording angels: *"The angel on the left does not write down a bad deed for six hours"*, according to the report preserved in the commentary literature — hours of grace in which repentance may overtake the record, an angel *discerning* whether the fault should enter the ledger at all. Discrimination, in other words, is a divine staff function, and the oath honors those who perform it.

**Separation as the Signature of Judgment**

Why does the sūrah about the Last Day swear by discrimination? Because the Day it announces will be, by its own later naming, *yawm al-faṣl* — "the Day of Division" (77:13, 38). The oath at verse 4 is thus a foreshadowing, planted in the opening lines: the entire eschatology of this sūrah — the sorting of humanity into the shaded and the burned, the speechless and the rewarded — is the final, public application of a discernment that is already ceaselessly at work. Al-Qurṭubī draws the practical inference: whoever is being *sorted now* — every choice a tiny verdict — should not be surprised to find the verdicts totaled later. The Day of Division does not introduce division; it *publishes* it. The Qur'an says of that Day: *"And the trumpet is blown, and it will be — that Day — a hard day, difficult for the disbelievers… and the sinners' faces will be blackened… and the believers' faces whiteened"* (74:9–22, in its images of the sorting); and most succinctly: *"And We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul shall be wronged at all"* (21:47). The scale is only the instrument; the *weighing* — the faṣl — is the event.

**Truth Needs a Divider — Why Neutrality Is Not a Virtue Here**

Modern culture often flatters itself with the ideal of never dividing at all: everything blended, every position one perspective among many, every distinction a kind of violence. The Qur'anic worldview is more honest about what knowledge even *is*. To know anything is already to have discriminated — to have separated this from that, relevant from irrelevant, true from false. A physician's entire competence is *farq*: distinguishing tumor from tissue, remedy from poison; a judge's is separating right claim from wrong; a parent's is telling discipline from cruelty. Every discipline of the mind is a discipline of distinction, and the pretense of perfect neutrality collapses the moment one must act — for action *chooses*, and choice divides. This is why the oath honors the discerners: in God's universe, refusing to distinguish truth from falsehood is not sophistication; it is abdication — the umpire declining to call any pitch a strike. And this is why the Qur'an named its Book the *Furqān*: it understood that humanity's deepest need is not more information but *discrimination* — a criterion. The sūrah's oath sequence now has wind (power), rain (mercy), and discernment (truth) sworn in three breaths — the sent, the spread, and the separating — a complete triad of how God governs the world, before the sūrah turns, at verse 5, to the most specific sending of all.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:5

> **And by those who bring forth the Reminder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Climax of the Oath-Ladder**

The five oaths now complete their ascent, and the fifth — *fa'l-mulqiyāti dhikrā* — is the summit toward which the others climbed. The root *l-q-y* here means to cast down, to deliver, to throw something to its recipient — the verb used for delivering a message (*ilqāʾ al-khabar*), and the same root the Qur'an uses for the very act of revelation descending: *"We shall cast upon you a weighty word"* (73:5, from the same root in *nuqiyu/nulqī*); and the believers ask God not to *"cast"* their hearts off course (3:8). What is cast down is *dhikr* — the Reminder. This is one of the Qur'an's proper names for itself: *"And We sent down to you the Reminder, that you might make clear to mankind what was sent down to them"* (16:44); *"And indeed, it is a Reminder for the worlds"* (68:51–52); and the divine guarantee attached to it: *"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We will guard it"* (15:9). By the time verse 5 is spoken, the vague cosmic agencies of verses 1–4 resolve into a single, specific, familiar event: the delivery of revelation. The spreaders become couriers; the discerners become bearers; the storm becomes — for the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions — the bearing-down weight described in the tradition, when inspiration came to him like the ringing of a bell, hardest of all upon him, and on the coldest day sweat would pour from his forehead as the message was cast down.

**Gabriel and the Relay of the Book**

Ibn Kathīr, al-Jaṣṣāṣ, and al-Ṭabarī agree that the casters of the Reminder are the angels who descend with the revelation and deliver it to the prophets — the office fulfilled above all by Gabriel, whom the Qur'an names at the summit of its own description of revelation: *"Indeed, this is the word of a noble messenger, powerful with the Owner of the Throne, established in worth there, obeyed there, and moreover trusted — and your companion is not possessed; he saw him on the clear horizon"* (81:19–23). The Qur'an gives Gabriel's interaction with the Prophet ﷺ the same verb of casting: *"The Trustworthy Spirit has brought it down upon your heart, that you may be of the warners, in a clear Arabic tongue"* (26:193–195). Note how the sūrah's oath-chain has, in five clauses, quietly narrated the whole career of revelation: messengers *sent* (v. 1), arriving with the *force* of a gale (v. 2), *spreading* their cargo abroad (v. 3), *discriminating* truth from falsehood as they go (v. 4), and finally *delivering the Reminder* to the hearts and hearing of mankind (v. 5). The early Makkan audience, who had watched their kinsman Muhammad ﷺ receive these very verses, were sworn to by the anatomy of what they were witnessing: the sūrah describes its own delivery while being delivered — a self-attesting scroll, sealed by the process it narrates.

**Why "Reminder" and Not "Book"**

The word chosen for the payload deserves its own reflection. God does not swear by those who cast down a *law*, or *doctrine*, or *philosophy*, but by those who cast down *dhikr* — remembrance. The Qur'an names itself this way constantly because of its diagnosis of the human condition: mankind's problem is not, first, ignorance — it is *forgetfulness*. The knowledge of God is already stamped into the human soul (*fiṭrah*); revelation does not import something foreign but *reminds* of something buried. The Prophet ﷺ taught, in the well-established narration: *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian"* (al-Bukhārī 1385; Muslim 2658, from Abū Hurayrah) — the natural constitution being the original knowledge, the acculturation the dust upon it, and the Reminder the cloth that wipes the dust away. This is why the Qur'an's most frequent command is not "know!" but *"remember!"* — *fa-dhkurūnī adhkurkum* — *"Remember Me; I shall remember you"* (2:152). The oath at verse 5 thus dignifies the angels' office precisely: they are not delivering information to minds, but restoration to hearts; not newspapers, but wake-up calls; and the Qur'an is not a document the world lacks but a letter the world misplaced. Psychologically, everyone recognizes this: the ache of the human being is rarely for *new* truth but for recovered seriousness — the reminder that death is real, that God watches, that kindness counts, that the trivial pursuits of the hour will soon be past. What the soul seeks at 3 a.m. is not news; it is *dhikr*.

**The Trust Chain from Heaven to Heart**

There is a sobering corollary in the oath's grammar. The Reminder is *cast down* — *mulqiyāt* — implying a recipient whose task is to catch and carry. The Prophet ﷺ is called in the very next breath of the sūrah's argument (v. 6) the bearer of a message that excuses or warns; and after him, the ummah inherits the relay. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Convey from me, even a single verse"* (al-Bukhārī 3461, from Ibn ʿAmr) — a commission that turns every believer who recites, teaches, or lives the Qur'an into a junior partner of the agents sworn by at verse 5. And the Qur'an attaches a warning to those who break the chain: *"Indeed, those who conceal what We sent down of the clarifications and the guidance after We made it clear to mankind in the Book — they are the ones God curses, and the cursers curse them"* (2:159). The fifth oath is therefore not merely cosmology; it is a job description with the reader's name eventually in it. The winds blow, the clouds spread, the angels descend — and then the message sits in human hands, with heaven swearing by everyone who handed it down intact.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:6

> **to excuse or to warn,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Errands of Revelation**

The sixth verse completes the sentence begun by the fifth: the Reminder is cast down *ʿudhran aw nudhrā* — "as an excuse, or as a warning." The two nouns are formed for balance: *ʿudhr* from the root *ʿ-dh-r*, an excuse, an absolution, a reason that clears one of blame; *nudhr* from *n-dh-r*, a warning, an alarm raised before a danger arrives. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī explain the pairing as the two possible legal outcomes of revelation's arrival: for the one who hears and heeds, the message becomes his *ʿudhr* — his exoneration, the proof that he was told and turned; for the one who hears and rejects, the same message becomes his *nudhr* — the warning against which he will be judged. In either case, the sūrah teaches, revelation *closes the alibi*. This is the Qur'an's constant doctrine of prophetic purpose: *"Messengers as bearers of good tidings and as warners, so that mankind may have no argument (*ḥujjah*) against God after the messengers"* (4:165). The sending of the messengers does not merely help mankind; it removes mankind's last excuse — no one will stand on the Day of Division saying, *I was never told*.

**Warning as Mercy — the Inverted Logic of the Alarm**

Modern sensibilities often recoil at "warning religion" as if it were unkind; but this reaction collapses the moment one watches how human beings treat what they love. The smoke alarm that screams at 2 a.m. is not the enemy of the sleeping family — it is the most loving device in the house. The physician who names a disease plainly, at the risk of frightening the patient, is acting in the patient's interest; the one who withholds the diagnosis to "spare feelings" commits malpractice. Al-Qurṭubī observes that the *nādhir* — the warner — in Arabic is one who informs beforehand of a feared thing so that the hearer may flee it; and the Qur'an applies to the Prophet ﷺ a pairing that matches verse 6 exactly: *"We have sent you only as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner"* (25:56; cf. 2:119; 34:28; 48:8). The sūrah's order — excuse first, warning second — even preserves the divine courtesy: absolution mentioned before alarm, mercy before severity, just as the Qur'an opens every sūrah but one with *In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful*. God warns the way good parents warn: reluctantly, proportionately, and only after provision has been made for escape — *"God desires to make clear to you, and to guide you to the ways of those before you, and to turn to you; and God is Knowing, Wise"* (4:26).

**Excuse or Warning — the Same Message, Two Hearers**

The subtlest lesson of verse 6 is that the *content* does not change between the two outcomes — the hearer does. One revelation, recited by one tongue, lands in a room of two hundred people: for some it is ʿudhr — light, exoneration, relief, the lifting of a burden they did not know how to name; for others the identical words are nudhr — a pointed finger, a record begun, a boundary drawn. The Prophet ﷺ embodied this dual reception in his own mission. To the poor, the enslaved, and the broken-hearted of Makkah, his recitations were liberation; to the chiefs of Quraysh they were affronts. Jesus had taught the same law of the split audience — the rain falling on both soil and stone — and the Qur'an states it as a principle of sight: *"Have you considered the one who takes his desire as his god?… Do you see the one whose sight We have covered, so that he does not see?"* (45:23; cf. the parable of the two men, 18:32–43). The Qur'an itself is called *furqān* — the divider (v. 4 above) — precisely because it does this: it does not merely inform its hearers; it *reveals* them. The historian of religions may study Islam with profit and remain unchanged; the one who hears the Reminder as addressed *to himself* finds, within a single sūrah, either his acquittal or his indictment being drafted.

**No Third Category — the Verse's Insistence on Response**

Finally, notice that verse 6 leaves no third option. The Reminder is cast down *to excuse or to warn* — which means there exists no hearer for whom it is merely *entertainment*, merely *literature*, merely *another worldview among many*. The Qur'an explicitly rejects the consumerist stance toward revelation: *"And among mankind is he who buys distracting talk to mislead from the way of God without knowledge, and who takes it in mockery"* (31:6). A letter from a court can be read as wallpaper only at the reader's peril. This is not the insecurity of a fragile message — the Qur'an elsewhere challenges all creation to produce its like — but the seriousness of a message that knows it is *about* its hearer, the way a diagnosis is about the patient. The early Makkan verses aimed at the Qurashī mockers were surgical in this respect: they assumed the audience was already, in fact, *responding* — with ridicule, with plotting, with heaviness of sleep in the sacred months. The sūrah's oath apparatus (vv. 1–5) plus its mission statement (v. 6) have now finished the setup. The question the whole machinery exists to serve stands next, in verse 7 — the single most important sentence of the sūrah: *surely what you are promised will befall.*

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:7

> **Surely what you are promised will befall.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to Five Oaths**

With verse 7 the sūrah reaches its first summit: the oath-ladder of verses 1–5 now sets down its weight on the promised conclusion — *innamā tūʿadūna lawāqiʿ* — "surely what you are promised will befall." In classical Arabic rhetoric, the oath has three parts: the thing sworn by (*muqsam bihi*), the thing sworn to (*muqsam ʿalayhi*), and the particle that joins them. Here the structure is textbook: five verses of sworn-by cosmic agents, and then the sworn-to reality, introduced with *innamā* — an emphatic particle of restriction and certainty: *only this, precisely this, and nothing less than this*. The "promise" in question is, in the context of early Makkan preaching, above all the Resurrection and the Day of Judgment — the doctrine the Quraysh mocked most, asking *"Who will revive these bones when they have rotted away?"* (36:78) and *"When will this promise come, if you are truthful?"* (10:48; 36:48; 67:25). Verse 7 is the Qur'an's reply compressed into five words: it is *lawāqiʿ* — a thing that *falls*, inevitably, like due fruit.

**The Word That Cannot Miss**

The choice of *lawāqiʿ* is the verse's genius. From the root *w-q-ʿ* — to fall, to strike, to come to pass — the noun *lāqiʿah* means anything that befalls a person, and the plural *lawāqiʿ* here is a participle-noun of inevitability: *the befalling things*. Arabic uses this root for what cannot be dodged: an arrow that *falls* on its mark, a fate that *lands*. The Qur'an's sister sūrah — its structural twin — makes the same move: *"By those that scatter… and by that which is promised: indeed, what you are promised will befall (*la-wāqiʿ*)"* (51:1–5), and indeed the very next Makkan sūrah in the muṣḥaf, Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah (56), takes its *name* from this root: "The Event That Must Befall." The theological claim embedded in the vocabulary is thus total: the Day of Judgment is not a possibility to be scheduled around, a "maybe" at the edge of the map — it is, in the Qur'anic ledger, the most *certain* of coming events, more certain than tomorrow's sunrise, because the sunrise itself is only one more promise of the same God: *"And they swear by God with their most solemn oaths that God will not resurrect the dead. Nay — it is a true promise binding upon Him, but most of mankind know not"* (16:38). And again, with the same oath-and-answer architecture as this sūrah: *"Say: Yes, by my Lord, it will surely come upon you — by Him who knows the Unseen — not an atom's weight escapes Him in the heavens or the earth"* (34:3).

**The Logic of the Case — from Regular Sending to Certain Arrival**

The reasoning underneath verses 1–7 deserves to be stated plainly, because it is one of the Qur'an's central arguments, assembled here in miniature. The oaths established that God's normal mode of operation is *reliable, repeated sending*: winds on schedule, rain every season, revelation across generations, angels without number. The conclusion drawn is that His *promise* belongs to the same class: a thing sent, and therefore a thing that arrives. The denier of resurrection, to sustain his denial, must break the analogy — he must say: yes, God (or nature) reliably does everything else repeatedly, but *this one announcement* will fail. The Qur'an exposes that as arbitrary. The entire edifice of the denier's own life — sowing in expectation of harvest, working in expectation of wages, sleeping in expectation of waking — rests on trusting promised futures. As the sūrah will argue explicitly at verses 20–23, the denier *himself* was once a mere promise: a drop of fluid, from which God delivered a breathing, speaking person on a schedule. He is, walking, the proof that God's promises land. And the Qur'an seals the argument with an appeal to precedent: *"We created you — will you not then believe?… and you were dead, and He gave you life; then He will cause you to die, then He will bring you to life, then to Him you will be returned"* (56:57, 2:28). The promise of verse 7 is thus not a new claim to be assessed; it is the overdue continuation of a pattern every hearer already bets his life on.

**Certainty as a Way of Living**

What does *believing* verse 7 actually do? The Qur'an's answer is: everything. A promised, certain reckoning is the premise of the entire moral life — the only durable reason to prefer truth at a loss over falsehood at a profit, to return a deposit no one could audit, to forgive when vengeance was affordable. Al-Ghazālī's insight, shared by the whole ethical tradition, is that character follows accounting: a soul certain of the audit orders itself now; a soul doubtful postpones reform to a "later" that never comes. This is why the Qur'an calls the Hereafter *al-dār al-ākhirah* — "the ultimate abode" — and identifies certainty (*yaqīn*) of it as the quality that made the elite of the believers what they were: *"And worship your Lord until there comes to you the certainty"* (15:99), where "the certainty" (in the interpretation of Ibn ʿAbbās and most commentators) means death — the moment when the promise stops being promise and becomes sight. The early believers' composure under torture, their spending in hardship, their truthfulness under temptation — the historical record of the first generation — is verse 7 lived out: people who organized their whole biographies around a Day the city found laughable. Sumayyah, the first martyr of Islam, was killed for refusing to unsay that certainty; she had no army, no wealth, no platform — only verse 7: *what you are promised will befall*. The sūrah will spend its remaining forty-three verses unpacking that one sentence: what the Day looks like when it arrives (vv. 8–14), what it says to deniers (the ten refrains), what it does to them (vv. 29–40), and what it gives to those who took it seriously in time (vv. 41–45).

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:8

> **So when the stars are blotted out,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Oath to Countdown**

Verse 8 shifts the sūrah's gear decisively. The opening oath-cluster (vv. 1–6) and its emphatic answer (v. 7) were sworn in the present tense — realities already in motion around the listener. Now comes *fa-idhā* — "So when…" — and with it the grammar of the future: a chain of ten or eleven clauses (vv. 8–11) each beginning with "when," piling event upon event without yet naming the Day they lead to. The rhetorical effect is deliberate suspense, what Arabic stylists call *al-tajrīd* — withholding the subject while multiplying its signs. The listener is made to feel the approach of something enormous before being told what it is — like hearing the far-off crack of a glacier before seeing the ice face. The first sign named is the stars: *fa-idhā al-nujūmu timisat* — "when the stars are blotted out."

**A Verb of Obliteration**

The verb *tamasat* (here in the intensified form *timisat*) comes from the root *m-s-s*, and its base field is touching; but in this intensified stem the touch becomes erasure — the rubbing out of writing from a page, the effacing of an inscription from a tablet. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr gloss it precisely so: the stars' light will be *extinguished and removed*, as ink is wiped away. The image is not of stars merely dimming, like a city seen from afar during a power cut — it is of the sky *as text* being wiped clean. And the Qur'an consistently presents the heavens as legible: *"It is He who placed in the sky stars, and set among them a lamp and a luminous moon"* (25:61); the night sky for the desert Arabs was the readest thing they knew — their calendar, their compass, their poetry-book of constellations, the very word *najm* entwined with seasons and navigation (*"By the star when it sets"* — 53:1; *"and by the star and the trees, they prostrate"* — 55:6). To a people who steered caravans by those lights, the announcement that the stars would be *erased* struck at the deepest layer of their assumed permanence.

**The Other Places the Sky Goes Out**

Verse 8 has close siblings across the eschatological passages of the Qur'an, and together they form a coherent sequence of cosmic dismantling. The blotted stars appear again in Sūrah al-Takwīr — arguably the most detailed sibling: *"When the sun is folded up, and when the stars fall (inkadarat)"* (81:1–2), where the verb pictures stars becoming dark, tarnished, *stained* — the opposite of their name *kawkab*, gleam. Sūrah al-Infiṭār: *"When the sky is cleft, and when the stars scatter"* (82:1–2). Sūrah al-Qiyāmah: *"And when the sight is dazed, and the moon eclipses, and sun and moon are joined"* (75:7–9) — a fusion that swallows both lights. The Qur'an's doctrine is consistent and, from the standpoint of its seventh-century audience, staggering: nothing in the created order is eternal — not even the fixed reference points of the sky, which every human culture had treated as the very emblem of constancy. *"The Day We shall roll up the heaven as the scroll is rolled for writings"* (21:104) — the same scribal image as *tamasa*, and then a promise bound to the same bookkeeping: *"as We originated the first creation, We shall bring it back — a promise binding upon Us; We shall do it."*

**The Physics of a Sign**

It is worth pausing on why the Qur'an chooses the *stars* as the first sign of the Hour, and modern readers are in a curious position to appreciate the choice. Every star the eye can see is a furnace whose stability we assume without thinking; yet astronomy itself narrates stellar endings — novae, collapses, burn-outs — as routine events on cosmic timescales. The Qur'an does not trade in that technical detail; it teaches the *lesson* that stands behind it: that the lights are creatures, maintained, not self-existent. A lamp requires a hand that keeps its oil; whoever believes the lamps keep themselves has merely stopped asking the question. The Qur'an's repeated challenge to the deniers runs exactly here: *"Do they not consider the dominion of the heavens and the earth and all that God created — and that perhaps their term has drawn near? In what discourse after that will they believe?"* (7:185 — a verse whose closing question this very sūrah will echo at verse 50). The stars' dimming, then, is not a terror-effect; it is the *curtain's fall* on a stage mistaken for the play. The theatre — sun, moon, stars — exists to display a story: creation, trial, reckoning, recompense. When the story is complete, the scenery is struck. Verse 8 is the first note of that strike, and the sūrah's hearer is invited to live as one who knows the show has a running time — *"And that man shall have nothing but what he strove for, and that his striving shall be seen"* (53:39–40).

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:9

> **and when the sky is riven asunder,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Firmament Splits Its Seam**

The second sign: *wa-idhā al-samāʾ furijat* — "when the sky is split open." The root *f-r-j* means to cleave, to open a gap in what was closed — it is the root of *faraj*, relief, because relief is the *opening* of what had closed upon a person; and of *shiqq al-amr*, the cleft of a matter. The verb form here (*infiʿāl*) is passive and reflexive at once: the sky *is made to split*, or *splits asunder of itself* under the impact of what it must let through. The Qur'an returns to this image with striking variety, as if circling a single unbearable event from every angle of speech: the sky is *cleft* (infiṭarat, 82:1); it is *split with cracks* (munfaṭir, 73:18 — *"a day the sky will be with it frail, split"*); it is *opened with gates* (78:19); it is *melted* (70:8 — like molten copper or rose-oil in various readings); it is *rolled up like the scroll* (21:104); it *swings* (52:9). Al-Ṭabarī's comment on our verse captures the common substance: the heaven will be torn apart from end to end.

**What the Splitting Admits**

In several of the parallel passages the rending is functional — it is not mere destruction but an *aperture for invasion*: *"The Day the sky is cleft with the clouds and the angels are sent down in streams"* (25:25); *"And when the heaven is opened and becomes gates, and the mountains are set moving and become a mirage"* (78:19–20). The curtain of the ordinary — the blue vault that has hidden the unseen world since the first day of creation — is pulled open from the top, and the world of the oath-verses (the mursalāt, the ʿāṣifāt, the nāshirāt) pours through in plain sight. Ibn Kathīr notes the same logic in Sūrah al-Qamar's account of the Hour: *"The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split… the Day the Summoner summons to something terrible"* (54:1, 6–7): what was previously a matter of report (*al-ghayb*, the Unseen) becomes, on that Day, a matter of *witness*. This is why the sky's splitting is so central an image: the entire structure of human life on earth — faith's hiddenness, the Unseen's veil, the trial of trusting what cannot be inspected — is premised on the sky's opacity. When it is riven, the trial ends, because the exam-room walls come down. The Prophet ﷺ described the ending of that structure from within: in the long account of the Night Journey and Ascension (al-Bukhārī 3207; Muslim 162, from Anas ibn Mālik), he was raised *"to the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary… near it is the Garden of the Refuge — when the Lote Tree was covered with what covered it"*, at the boundary beyond which the created order's veil thins to nothing. The splitting of the sky is every veil's simultaneous withdrawal.

**Hearing the Verse as the First Audience Did**

For a seventh-century listener, the sky was not "space" — a word he never knew — but *al-samāʾ*: the canopy, the roof of the world, God's ceiling over the earth, which He *"raised… and set the balance"* (55:7), which holds *"lamps"* (41:12) and lets down rain *"in due measure"* (23:18). To say the sky would split was to say the *roof* would fail — and roofs, for a people of houses and tents, were the very symbol of security. The Qur'an elsewhere uses exactly that register of household catastrophe when it describes the Day: *"And they say, 'Shall we be abandoned to rot in the earth?…' Say: The angel of death, who is set over you, will take you"* (32:10–11); and the earth, the floor, will likewise betray: *"On the Day the earth will be replaced by another earth, and the heavens likewise"* (14:48). House imagery continues into the reward: the believer's dwelling is *"a house… in a Garden that is everlasting"* (61:12; 25:75, *dār al-salām*, the abode of peace). The picture is coherent: the present world is a temporary lodging whose walls and roof are scheduled for demolition; only its Architect and His promise are permanent — *"Everyone upon it will perish, and the Face of your Lord will remain — the Possessor of Majesty and Honor"* (55:26–27).

**The Splitting Already Whispered in the Ordinary**

There is a quieter reading that the classical commentators allow alongside the cosmic one, and it belongs to how the Qur'an always argues: it never asks belief for the *first* time on the last day. The sky is *already* riven daily in miniature — by the rain that must come through it (*"And We sent down from the sky water in measure"* — 23:18); by the rays that pierce it at dawn; by the revelations that descended through it to the Prophet ﷺ, each one a small *fatafq* — the very word the Qur'an uses when it says: *"And the sky We built with might, and indeed We are its Expander"* (51:47), and when it describes the night's *"opening"* at dawn (6:96, in the reading of the sky's *cleaving* at daybreak). If the sky already opens for mercy every morning, the denier has no grounds for finding its final opening absurd. The God who splits the dark with dawn, and the storm-cloud with rain, and the long silence of revelation with Gabriel's descent, is not taxed by splitting it once, fully, forever. Verse 9 thus completes verse 8's lesson at a higher amplitude: the lights go out (v. 8) and the ceiling comes apart (v. 9) — the stage's two most taken-for-granted fixtures — so that nothing remains between the watcher and what watches back.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:10

> **when the mountains are scattered,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Immovables Moved**

The third sign: *wa-idhā al-jibālu nusifat* — "when the mountains are scattered/blown away." The root *n-s-f* means to lift and carry off, to winnow and scatter what seemed rooted; some of the early exegetes, reported by Ibn Kathīr, read the verb as "uprooted and made to fly," dust in a gale. The choice of the mountains is, once again, exact. In Arabic as in every language, the mountain is the standing metaphor for permanence — the Qur'an itself uses it that way when it wants the strongest possible negation of change: the covenant might sooner *"be taken from the mountains"* than certain men believe (59:21; cf. 13:31, where the Qur'an is said to be such that *"were it not that the mountains were moved"*); unshakable faith is a mountain (*"And God has made the firm word stand firm for those who believe"*); and to give someone a mountain is to promise the impossible. So when the sūrah announces that *the mountains scatter*, it announces the end of the very category of "permanent things." The Qur'an works the image with virtuosity across its endings: mountains become *"like carded wool"* (70:9) — *ʿihn al-manfūsh*, the fluffy, structureless cloud of raw cotton after carding, a substance of no resistance at all; *"like heaps of shifting sand"* (73:14) — *mahīd*, poured out and unstable; *"like a mirage"* (78:20) — a shimmer with no body; they *"travel like clouds"* (27:88) — a reversal in which the most immovable objects become the most mobile; and they *"are carried away"* altogether from the landscape of the new earth (20:105–107; 56:5–6), leaving *"a level plain"* where nothing hides anything.

**Why Creation's Pillars Must Fall**

The doctrine beneath the imagery is the Qur'an's consistent metaphysics of the created: nothing in creation deserves the trust that belongs to the Creator alone. The mountain, precisely because humans instinctively lean on it — for safety, for orientation, as the immovable reference of every horizon — must be unmasked as a creature with a term. This is not vandalism; it is revelation's mercy toward a species whose most constant temptation is to anchor itself to what will not hold. The Qur'an names the false refuges of humanity with earth-words and finds them all too fragile to hold: *"The likeness of those who take protectors besides God is as the likeness of the spider that builds a house — and surely the frailest of houses is the house of the spider"* (29:41). And the Prophet ﷺ drew the boundary of healthy attachment in a single sentence to Ibn ʿAbbās, when he was a boy:

> *"Know that if all mankind gathered to benefit you, they could not benefit you except with what God had written for you; and if they gathered to harm you, they could not harm you except with what God had written against you. The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* (Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās — graded ṣaḥīḥ and famous)

A civilization that internalizes this lives with mountains rightly used — as landmarks, watersheds, fortresses — and wrongly worshipped not at all. The Quraysh, for that matter, did worship the immovables: their gods were lodged in stones and hills; and their trust was in the permanence of their order — the caravan economy, the sacred months, the prestige of the Kaʿbah's custodianship. To them the message of the scattered mountains was pointedly political as well as cosmic: *your arrangement is not eternal; the ledger is being kept elsewhere.*

**Mountains as Mercy — Reading the Sign Backwards**

There is a stunning reversal to track here, because the Qur'an first presents mountains not as rivals to God but as His gifts, deliberately installed: *"And We placed on earth firm mountains (*rawāsiya*) lest it should shake with them"* (21:31; 16:15); *"as pegs (*awtādan*)"* (78:7) — tent-pegs, the very items a nomad knows hold the house together in wind. Mountains regulate the crust, anchor the water cycle (rain and snow captured, released slowly as the springs verse 27 will name), break the winds, and give the earth its stable face. The Qur'an asks its hearer to hold both truths at once without contradiction: the mountains are *real mercies*, and they are *scheduled mercies*. This double vision is the essence of using the world without idolizing it — what the Qur'an calls *"from the fruits and the palms and the vines, will you not then give thanks?"* (16:11–13). The believer thanks God for the mountain; the denier thanks the mountain for itself; and on the Day of verse 10 the difference between the two postures becomes visible to all, because the object of the denier's trust turns to wool while the object of the believer's trust stands unimpaired: *"Everyone upon it will perish, and the Face of your Lord will remain"* (55:26–27).

**The Psychology of the Removed Landmark**

Finally, consider what the scattering does to the *survivors'* sense of place. A human being navigates by landmarks — the hill above the village, the ridge of the skyline, the family plot on the slope. Place is memory made geographical; the mound by the road is where the grandfather is buried; the crag is where the tribe stood its ground. When the mountains scatter and the earth becomes *"a level plain"* (20:106), geography is erased — and with it the entire folk-memory that let people imagine their peoples' story as *the* story. On that plain there are no Qurashī hills, no Jewish wadis, no Roman Alps — no homelands, only the gathered generations standing on the same featureless floor before the one Judge (as verse 38 will announce: *"We have gathered you and those of old"*). Modern readers may catch an echo of this dislocation in the way globalization and migration have flattened older certainties of place — but the Qur'an's plain is not a metaphor of cultural change; it is the terminus of all such changes. The lesson for now is the Prophet's ﷺ instruction to a community about foundations, given when the world's mountains were socially shaking around him: *"There will be tribulations in which one sitting is better than one standing, and one standing better than one walking, and one walking better than one running"* (al-Bukhārī 3601; Muslim 2886, from Abū Bakrah) — anchor yourself to God, not to the scenery, because the scenery — all of it — is verse 10 waiting on its appointed day.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:11

> **and when the messengers are slated,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Appointed Time**

The fourth sign brings the sequence to its peak of strangeness: *wa-idhā al-rusulu uqqitat* — "when the messengers are slated." The root *w-q-t* is the Arabic word-field of *time* itself (*waqt*); the intensive form here (*uqqitat*) means, according to the lexicons, that a thing is assigned its time, gathered and held until an appointed moment. Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī all record the three classical readings of the clause. First, and most followed: the messengers are *gathered* — collected at their appointed time, the Day of Resurrection, as the Qur'an says elsewhere: *"The Day We shall gather the Godfearing as a delegation to the Compassionate, and drive the criminals to Hell like a thirsty herd"* (19:85–86); and *"And the earth shone with the light of its Lord, and the Book was set down, and the prophets and the witnesses were brought"* (39:69). Second: *postponed* — the same verb can mean deferred, and the reading understands God's patience with the deniers: He does not punish instantly; He grants respite (*istiʾnār*) until an appointed term — as the Qur'an says: *"And We respited them, but My plan is firm"* (7:183; cf. 3:178; 13:32; 15:37–38; 22:44). Third: *promised* — the messengers have been given their appointed day of vindication, when every message they delivered will be opened and its hearers assembled.

**Why the Messengers Wait**

The image in the first reading is juridical and immense: every prophet in history — from the unnamed warners of the forgotten cities to Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Mūsā, ʿĪsā, and Muhammad ﷺ — stands gathered at last in the courtroom of the world's final session. And each has a two-fold business there, both of them exactly what verses 5–6 described: the Reminder they cast down becomes either *ʿudhr* or *nudhr* — the messenger's testimony either excuses his people (where they heeded) or warns against them (where they denied). The Qur'an states this explicitly, in an address to the Prophet ﷺ that the report of al-Bukhārī and Muslim says brought tears to his eyes when he recited it: *"How will it be, then, when We bring a witness from every community, and bring you as a witness over these?"* (4:41). And the nations are told: *"So, by your Lord, We will surely question them all concerning what they used to do"* (15:92–93); and God's principle of justice is fixed: *"We never punish until We send a messenger"* (17:15). The gathering of the messengers at verse 11 is thus the assembly of the *witness list*. Judgment Day, in the Qur'an's courtroom, is not a novel proceeding; it is the long-delayed trial in which every generation's testimony — its prophet, its scripture, its record — is finally heard together.

**The Terrible Comfort of Postponement**

The second reading — *postponed* — carries its own warning, aimed at the psychology of denial. The Quraysh read God's non-intervention as evidence of His absence or indifference: *"And they say: When will this promise come, if you are truthful?"* (67:25). The Qur'an's diagnosis is the opposite: the interval *is* the mercy — a grace period for repentance — and mistaking it for impunity is the oldest error in the record: *"Do they think, because We grant them enjoyment of days and nights, that We hasten to them with good? No — they do not perceive"* (23:55–56). The Prophet ﷺ warned precisely against reading delayed reckoning as safety:

> *"When you see that God gives a servant what he loves of the world while he persists in sin, that is istidrāj — God drawing him on."* (Musnad Aḥmad, from ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir; the scholars grade its meaning sound and supported)

The term *istidrāj* — from the root of "drawing near step by step" — names the trap of unearned ease: the net slackened so the catch swims deeper. Verse 11's "postponed" reading plants that warning inside the apocalyptic vision: even the Day itself is *scheduled*, not spontaneous; every day of delay is scheduled too, and the schedule is fixed: *"For every community there is a term; and when its term arrives, they cannot defer it an hour nor advance it"* (7:34). The messengers' gathering, in this light, is also the scheduled end of every postponement: on the appointed day, the prophets stand — and with them stands the full case against those who treated patience as permission.

**The Messengers' Own Reckoning — a Purification of the Office**

There is a final, tender dimension often noted by the commentators. The gathering of the messengers is also their *vindication*: the ones who were called madmen and sorcerers by their peoples (Nūḥ, 54:9; the Messenger ﷺ, 15:6; 68:51; 81:22) stand where their accusers' verdicts are overturned and God Himself witnesses for them: *"Who is more unjust than the one who invents a lie against God, or says 'It was revealed to me' when nothing was revealed to him?… And you were not on the western side when We decreed the command to Moses"* (6:93; 28:44). And the Qur'an gives the Prophet ﷺ this assurance at the moment of deepest mockery: *"Indeed, We have given you al-Kawthar…"* (108:1–3) — the compensating gift against the one who called him *abtar*, cut off. On the Day of verse 11, the mockers' file is opened and the mocked are seated as witnesses for the prosecution of unbelief. For the believer, then, verse 11 is a two-edged comfort: it guarantees that history's humiliated truth-tellers — prophets and, in their small measure, every honest caller — are booked for public vindication; and it warns the believer himself that he stands in the same courtroom, for the same evidentiary purpose: to have been told, without excuse, by every message that reached him.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:12

> **for what day is it appointed?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Heaven Asks Its Own Question**

After four verses of cascading "when" clauses — stars, sky, mountains, messengers — the sūrah does something rhetorically brilliant: it interrupts itself with a question. *Li-ayyi yawmin ujjilat* — "For what day is it appointed?" The root ʾ-j-l (*ajal*) is the verb of deferral and deadline: a task is *muʾajjal* when given a due date. In the reading of al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī the clause can be heard in two complementary voices: "To what day have [the messengers / all these events] been deferred?" — and, more colloquially, "Until when is the postponement running?" The question is God's own — the Omniscient asking, not to learn, but to make His hearers *ask*. This is a recognized Qur'anic device: the question of the Teacher who answers His pupils' silence by voicing the question they should have voiced, then providing the answer before their fear can answer wrongly. The build-up demanded a pause: four verses of catastrophe without a named date could drown in vagueness; the question forces the listener to lean in and *want* the answer — and wanting the answer is the first act of taking the Day seriously.

**Suspense as Moral Instrument**

Classical rhetoricians (al-Zamakhsharī in his *Kashshāf* is especially fine here) note the craft: the question is placed *after* the signs and *before* the name, exactly where suspense peaks. Consider how the sequence feels read aloud, as it was first heard: the stars go out — the sky tears — the mountains fly — the messengers rise from their long delay — and then a hush, and a voice asks: *for which day, do you suppose, has all this been booked?* Every listener supplies his own wrong answer first — the day of my enemies' ruin? a day of local politics? a distant myth? — and then verse 13 corrects everyone in two words: *li-yawmi al-faṣl* — "for the Day of Division." The pedagogy is the same as a physician who has lined up the test results on the desk, looked up gravely, and asked the patient: *do you understand what these are telling you?* — before naming the condition. The patient who hears the name after the suspense *hears* it; the one who hears it casually files it away. The Qur'an, wanting to be heard rather than filed, builds the pause into the revelation itself.

**The Cynics' Question Turned Back on Them**

There is also an edge of irony here that the first audience would have felt. The deniers' own characteristic utterance was a demand for dates: *"When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"* (10:48; 36:48; 67:25). They asked about the Day as one asks about a debt one does not intend to honor — mockingly, in the plural, at dusk gatherings. Verse 12 takes their question out of their mouths, strips the mockery off it, and returns it as a genuine question with a genuine answer — as if to say: *you asked when; here is the form of the answer you will receive: a Day, fixed, dividing — and you are not ready.* The Qur'an performs this reversal often: their taunt *"God has taken a son"* (2:116) is answered; their *"stories of the ancients"* (6:25; 8:31) charge is answered; their demand for signs (6:37; 17:90–96) is answered by the sign of the Book itself. And the answer never comes on the deniers' terms — no dates, no parlor miracles — because the date is God's to withhold: *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its anchoring? Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord; none can disclose it at its time but He…"* (7:187); and: *"People ask you about the Hour. Say: The knowledge of it is with God alone — and what will make you know? Perhaps the Hour is near"* (33:63). The appointment is real; the date is withheld; and verse 12's question quietly explains why: because a date would be gambled upon — procrastination would become arithmetic — while an undisclosed appointment must be *prepared* for daily. The owner of a house does not post the burglar's schedule; God does not post the audit's.

**Living Under an Undisclosed Deadline**

The practical shape of faith under an undisclosed deadline is a recognizable human discipline. The employee who knows the inspector comes "sometime this quarter" keeps the books current every day; the one who knows the date cleans up the night before. What the undated Hour demands is the Qur'an's very definition of readiness: *"So remain patient — a beautiful patience — they see it as far off, and We see it near"* (70:5–7; 70:6); and the Prophet ﷺ was asked which hour's preparation matters, and he answered:

> *"The one prayer for which you should prepare: the prayer of the sunset of life… the strongest of you in readiness is the wisest of you"* — rather, his established word: *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your work, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

Note that the Prophet's ﷺ counsel does not name the Hour at all: it names the *human* deadlines — aging, illness, loss, death — which are the individual's private rehearsals of the world's ending. For every person, "the Day" arrives personally first. Verse 12's question — *for what day is it appointed?* — therefore has a second, quieter answer for each listener: *for a day within your lifetime, in one sense — the day your soul is taken; and for the Day of all days, after.* The sūrah's rhetoric of suspense is thus not merely literary; it is an instrument for converting cosmic certainty into personal urgency — the question every human postpones, asked directly, with the answer arriving in the very next breath.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:13

> **For the Day of Division.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words of Answer**

The question of verse 12 — *for what day is it appointed?* — receives its answer in a bare two words: *li-yawmi al-faṣl* — "for the Day of Division." After the thunder of the signs, the answer's brevity is itself the thunderclap. No date is given, no hour, no calendar — only a *nature*: the Day is the Day of *faṣl*, separation, decision, the cutting apart of what had been mingled. The root *f-ṣ-l* runs through the entire field of decisive action: *faṣala* is to sever one thing from another; *faṣl* is a chapter's division, a verdict's clause, the interval that separates; *fasala al-ḥaqq min al-bāṭil* — he separated truth from falsehood. The same root gave verse 4 its "discerners" (*al-fāriqāt*) and the Qur'an its name *al-Furqān*. What the oath at verse 4 promised as ongoing divine activity becomes, at verse 13, the very *name* of the Day: history's final, unappealable act of discrimination.

**A Name the Qur'an Repeats Like a Verdict**

The Qur'an uses *yawm al-faṣl* as a proper name for the Day of Judgment in a tight cluster of late-Makkan sūrahs — as though the early revelation, pressed by deniers on every side, kept stamping the same seal: *"This is the Day of Decision (*yawm al-faṣl*); We have gathered you and those of old"* (77:38 — this sūrah's own second use); *"Indeed, the Day of Decision is an appointed time — a Day when the Trumpet is blown and you come in throngs"* (78:17–18); *"This is the Day of Decision; We have gathered you and the former peoples"* (37:21); and most tellingly: *"the Day of Decision for what they used to differ upon"* (44:40). That last phrase gives the name its full meaning: it is the Day that *settles differences* — literally, the disputes (*ikhtilāf*) people lived by and died in.

**The End of the Great Mingling**

Why "Division" as the Day's defining act? Because, on the Qur'an's reading, the entire moral drama of earthly life is a drama of *mixtures*: truth and falsehood deployed in the same markets; believers and deniers sharing meals, bloodlines, cities; deeds of light and deeds of darkness cohabiting in the same breast; hypocrites seated beside the sincere in the same prayer-rows; and — deepest mixture of all — sincere deeds done for impure motives, visible righteousness wrapped around invisible rot. In this life, the mingling cannot be fully unwound: courts err, reputations lie, the inside of hearts is sealed even from their owners' inspection. The Qur'an promises the One Session in which every composite is resolved into its elements: *"the Day when secrets (*sarā'ir*) are laid bare"* (86:9); *"the Day when every soul will find present whatever good it did, and whatever evil it did — it will wish a far distance between itself and that"* (3:30); *"On the Day when hidden things are tested — then there will remain no soul in the heavens or the earth but that it comes to the Compassionate as a servant"* (19:92; cf. 3:29: *"the Day when God assembles the messengers…"*). Even the believer's own inner war between intention and temptation is a difference awaiting faṣl — and the Prophet ﷺ taught that the decisive tribunal inside that war convenes nightly: *"The angels attend…"* — rather his word: *"Angels take turns among you by night and by day, and they meet at the dawn prayer…"* (al-Bukhārī 466; Muslim 632). Verse 13 scales that audit to the cosmos.

**Division Without Injustice**

The Day's separation would be terror if its Judge were arbitrary; the Qur'an therefore plants its justice in the very name. *Faṣl* is not rupture but *discrimination* — the surgical act that harms nothing and heals everything, like a judge who separates, at last, the criminal from the accused-in-error. The Qur'an's courtroom imagery is exact: *"And We set the just scales (*mawāzīn al-qisṭ*) for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul is wronged at all — even the weight of a mustard seed, We bring it; and We suffice as reckoners"* (21:47); *"And the Book is set down, and you see the sinners fearful of what is in it, saying: Woe to us, what is with this Book, it leaves neither a small thing nor a great thing but it has counted it?"* (18:49). The division, in other words, is the *end* of the greatest injustice conceivable — the injustice of final non-distinction, in which the murderer and the murdered, the tyrant and his victims, the liar and the lied-about, all simply dissolve into the same oblivion. Against the materialist's undifferentiated dirt, the Qur'an sets faṣl: every account *distinguished*, every wrong *separated out* and dealt with, every hidden fidelity *sifted* from its disguise and rewarded. For the oppressed, verse 13 is thus among the most hopeful words in revelation: their story, indistinguishable now inside the world's noise, is scheduled for Division — and on that Day, as verse 38 will say, the gathered generations will finally see who, in God's ledger, was who. The sūrah's next verse pauses over the name itself, as if aware that no listener yet alive has the categories to receive it.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:14

> **And what shall apprise thee of what the Day of Division is?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Deepens Rather Than Answers**

Having named the Day — *yawm al-faṣl* — the sūrah immediately pulls the name back from over-familiarity: *wa-mā adrāka mā yawmu al-faṣl* — "And what shall apprise thee of what the Day of Division is?" This formula, *mā adrāka* ("what would make you know?"), is a fixed instrument of Qur'anic style, and its grammar of distance is deliberate: the second person is singular — addressed to the Prophet ﷺ personally, and through him to every reader — and the verb *adrā* means to come to know something fully, by experience. The formula does not say "you do not know"; it says, more precisely: *by what means could you be made to know?* The rhetorical effect is the opposite of withholding information: it magnifies the thing beyond the reach of any teacher, even a prophet, except by God's own telling. The Qur'an uses the formula at the gates of its greatest mysteries: *"And what shall apprise thee of the Night of Power? The Night of Power is better than a thousand months"* (97:2–3); *"And what shall apprise thee of the Striking Calamity?"* (101:3); *"And what shall apprise thee of the Crushing Fire of God?"* (104:5–6); *"And what shall apprise thee of the Height (*sicca*)"* — precisely: *"And what shall appraise thee of the Cliff (*aqabah*)?"* (90:12). In each case the formula flags a reality whose fullness outruns the hearer's experiential index.

**Makkan Humility Before the Unspeakable**

There is a doctrine of religious epistemology packed into this small verse: the Day's essence cannot be transferred from speaker to hearer the way news is transferred, because the hearer has no stock of comparable experience on which to hang it. The Qur'an is candid about this epistemic wall — even its own descriptions of the Hereafter are given as *projections into the listener's categories*: Paradise's fruits and rivers, Hell's fire and shade, are called *mathal* — likenesses, parables: *"And give them the likeness (*mathal*) of the life of this world as water We sent down from the sky…"* (18:45); the Garden's reward: *"a likeness of the Garden promised to the Godfearing — rivers…"* (47:15). The Prophet ﷺ was shown the realities themselves on the Night Journey, yet described them back to the Companions in the only terms available — and the tradition preserves his awe: *"Then I was raised to the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, and its fruits were like the jars of Hajar, and its leaves like the ears of elephants…"* (al-Bukhārī 3207; Muslim 162). If the created extremes of Paradise exceed the report, how much more the Day of Division itself, at which the entire order of experience is dismantled — the very categories (sun, sky, mountain, time) with which any description would be built. Verse 14 therefore teaches reverence by grammar: before the Day, even the Prophet ﷺ stands as one *told*, not one who knows by acquaintance. Faith's proper posture here is the Qur'an's own: *"And they ask you about the Spirit. Say: The Spirit is of the command of my Lord, and you have been given of knowledge only a little"* (17:85).

**Why the Qur'an Answers Its Own Question with Itself**

It is striking that in the parallel passages, the *mā adrāka* question is usually followed by an answer anyway — *"the Night of Power is better than a thousand months"* — as if the formula, having confessed the limits of the hearer, then overflows them by grace: what the hearer could never earn or reach, the Speaker *gives*. Here, the sūrah's answer is subtler: the next verse — *"Woe that Day to the deniers!"* — is itself the reply. What is the Day of Division? It is, first and decisively, *woe for the deniers*. The name of the Day, for practical purposes, is indexed to its verdict. The pattern repeats at Sūrah al-Qāriʿah: *"And what shall apprise thee of the Striking Calamity? … It is a Day when mankind will be like moths scattered, and the mountains like carded wool — and as for the one whose scales are heavy, he is in a pleasing life; and the one whose scales are light — a pitless mother is his refuge"* (101:3–9). The question "what shall apprise you?" is answered not with metaphysics but with *destinies*. This is a signature of Qur'anic priorities: revelation does not satisfy curiosity about the Day; it assigns urgency about it. The unknown date and the unknowable essence converge on one known fork — two standings before God — and every hearer is thereby given exactly the amount of the Day that can be *acted* upon.

**The Reader at the Edge of the Description**

For the modern reader, verse 14 offers a rare gift: permission to be small. Contemporary culture pressures everyone to have an opinion on everything, and eschatology has not been spared — every Last Thing has its popular schematic, its confident chart. The Qur'an's method is different: it *withholds the map's center* while making the map's road perfectly clear. The believer does not know what the Day is, in essence — no one does; the angels themselves ask about it (*"They will ask you about the Hour: when is its anchoring? Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord"* — 79:42–44, where even Gabriel, in the famous ḥadīth of Jibrīl, answers "the one asked knows no more than the asker," and the Prophet ﷺ commends him: al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8). Yet the believer knows *everything necessary*: that it comes, that it divides, that deeds are weighed to the atom (99:7–8), that no intercession is purchaseable (2:48; 2:254), and that the deniers' portion there is woe — the refrain now about to toll its first of ten strokes. Between the omniscience no one has and the ignorance no one needs, verse 14 stations the human being exactly where worship begins: knowing enough to obey, not enough to be bored. The sūrah's first great movement (vv. 1–14) is complete; the verdicts begin.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:15

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Toll of the Refrain**

With verse 15 the sūrah strikes the note it will strike ten times before the end: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — "Woe that Day to the deniers!" The word *wayl* is among the strongest of Arabic imprecations — a cry of ruin, a call of destruction down upon a head; in ordinary speech it is what a man cries at disaster ("woe is me!"), and in the Qur'an's mouth it becomes a judicial sentence: ruin *pronounced*. The commentators record a tradition, related from some of the early generations and cited by Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī among others, that *wayl* is also the name of a valley in Hell — a pit of weeping whose name is the very cry of those in it; whether or not one presses the geography, the image captures the word's weight: the cry and the place are one. And the deniers — *al-mukadhdhibīn* — are named with the intensive participle from the root *k-dh-b*: not merely people who *said* something false (*kādhib*, a liar), but people who made denial their *trade* — who received the truth and answered it with *"lies!"* (*kadhdhaba*), systematically, as a stance. This is the Qur'an's technical term for the arch-opponents of revelation: those before whom the signs were recited and who said *"stories of the ancients"* (6:25) — the diagnose of the sūrah's whole audience.

**Why "That Day" — the Timing of the Woe**

The sentence is precise about *when* the woe falls: *yawma'idhin* — "that Day." Not now. The deniers in Makkah were, by worldly lights, flourishing — the sūrah itself will soon remark on their eating and enjoying (v. 46). God's sentence against them is deferred to the Day of Division, and the deferral is the point: the refrain binds the crime to its court date. Every one of the ten occurrences follows the same pattern — a piece of evidence or warning is presented, and the refrain converts it into an indictment awaiting execution. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the first refrain (v. 15) seals the entire first panel (vv. 1–14): the oath-validated promise (v. 7), the cosmic signs (vv. 8–11), the named Day (vv. 12–14) — all of it ends, for those who denied, in this one sentence. And al-Shawkānī, in *Fatḥ al-Qadīr*, observes the distribution of the ten: some of the early exegetes held that the first refrain looks to the punishment of the Hereafter, while the second (v. 19) looks to the destruction of the ancient deniers *in this world* — the two overlapping jurisdictions of divine justice, history and eschatology, both of which the sūrah will visit.

**The Refrain as Structure — Ten Verdicts, Not One**

It is worth mapping the refrain's architecture once, since every subsequent occurrence will refer back to it. The ten strokes (vv. 15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 40, 45, 47, 49) divide the sūrah into panels: (1) after the promise of the Day — v. 15; (2) after the destruction of the ancients — v. 19; (3) after the argument from human creation — v. 24; (4) after the argument from the earth's provisioning — v. 28; (5) after the portrait of the Fire's sparks — v. 34; (6) after the silencing of excuses — v. 37; (7) after the challenge "scheme against Me" — v. 40; (8) after the reward of the reverent — v. 45; (9) after "eat and enjoy a little" — v. 47; (10) after "they bow not" — v. 49. Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on the final occurrence, reads each stroke contextually — each *mukadhdhib* group exposed by its own particular denial: of the Resurrection, of history's lesson, of the Creator's power in their own bodies, of His providence, of the Fire, of the Day's justice, of God's power (against schemers), of His generosity, of His respite's meaning, of His commands. The refrain is thus a drumbeat *curriculum*: the sūrah teaches the same sentence ten times because its audience — every audience — needs it ten times. Arabic rhetoric calls the device *takrār*, repetition for emphasis, and here it carries an additional burden: it *is* the Day of Division in miniature, the verdict separated from its execution, spoken ten times before it falls once.

**Denial as the One Unforgivable Stance**

Why does the refrain fix on *takdhīb* — denial — rather than on theft, murder, or idolatry, which the Qur'an elsewhere denounces severely? Because takdhīb is the *root crime* from which all others draw their impunity. A thief who admits God's verdict has a path back; a denier has disabled the entire path, because he has rejected the tribunal itself. The Qur'an states the hierarchy with terrible plainness: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; God forgives all sins"* (39:53) — with the exception it names elsewhere: *"God does not forgive that partners be set beside Him, and forgives what is less than that to whom He wills"* (4:48); and of the Day: *"Who does greater wrong than the one who invents a lie against God, or denies His signs?"* (7:37; cf. 6:21). The Prophet ﷺ was asked which sin is greatest, and answered: *"To set up an equal to God, though He created you"* (al-Bukhārī 4477; Muslim 86, from Ibn Masʿūd) — the primal *misdemeanor* of the ledger being against the Judge. The refrain's tenfold toll is thus the Qur'an's most patient mercy and its most patient severity at once: ten warnings, ten chances to leave the category of mukadhdhibīn, each attached to fresh evidence. The tragedy the sūrah will quietly document is that the audience heard all ten — and, as verse 48 will show, would not even bow.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:16

> **Did We not destroy those of old?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Promise to Precedent**

With verse 16 the sūrah opens its second panel of evidence, and the instrument changes: no longer oath and cosmic sign, but *history* — interrogated with a rhetorical question: *a-lam nuhlik al-awwalīn* — "Did We not destroy those of old?" The Arabic negative-interrogative *a-lam* does double duty: it asks and it answers, expecting the reply *balā* — "yes indeed." The listener is not left to weigh the claim; he is made to *concede* it in the act of hearing. This is the Qur'an's favorite method with the Makkans on the subject of resurrection and reckoning: it appeals to ruins and memories every Arab could tour — the standingColumns of Thamūd in al-Ḥijr, the lore of ʿĀd in al-Aḥqāf, the tale of the flooded generations of Noah — and it dares the hearer to deny what his own caravan routes confirmed. The Qur'an states the principle explicitly: *"Have they not traveled in the land and seen the fate of those before them? They were greater than them in strength…"* (40:82; cf. 3:137; 6:11; 35:44). Verse 16 compresses that entire archaeology of judgment into five words.

**Who "Those of Old" Were**

The exegetes — al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī here — identify *al-awwalīn*, "those of old," as the generations of deniers from Adam down to the Prophet's ﷺ own century: above all the peoples of the great judgment-narratives with which the Makkan sūrahs were saturated. Nūḥ's people, drowned to the last clinging son (11:42–43; 69:10–12; 71:25–26); ʿĀd, *"the brethren of Hūd"*, obliterated by the shrieking wind (69:6–7; 41:15–16; 46:24–25; 89:6–8); Thamūd, the rock-hewers of the Ḥijr, destroyed by the cry after they hamstrung the she-camel (7:73–79; 91:11–15; 54:29–31); the cities of Lot overturned (11:82; 54:37); Pharaoh and his hosts, drowned and made *"a sign"* preserved (10:92; 43:55–56); the brothers of Shuʿayb and the dwellers of the thicket (26:176–191). The Qur'an makes each destruction a *sunnah*-pattern: *"And We did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves; and their gods… availed them nothing when the command of your Lord came"* (11:101). Ibn Kathīr notes the force of the perfect tense *nuhlik* — "did We not destroy": the events stand *finished*, sealed in the record — the pattern is not pending; it is proven, in the only court the deniers admitted, the court of fact.

**The Logic — Precedent as Prediction**

The argument's structure is juridical: the deniers' case rests on the assumption that *this* generation, *this* city, *this* arrangement escapes the jurisdiction. The sūrah's counter is precedent: God has already ruled on their exact case, many times, with maximal warning and maximal patience. The Qur'an argues it syllogistically elsewhere: *"Say: Travel in the land and see how was the end of the deniers"* (6:11); *"And how many a generation before them, mightier than them in might, did We destroy — then they wandered in the lands: was there any refuge?"* (50:36). And it explains the *reason* for the pattern's repetition: *"And each We seized for his sin — of them was he upon whom We sent a sandstorm, and of them he whom the Cry seized, and of them he whom We caused the earth to swallow, and of them he whom We drowned — and God would not wrong them, but they wronged themselves"* (29:40). The logic is simple enough to be a proverb: a king who has punished a hundred rebellious provinces is not *hoping* the hundred-and-first rebels will get away; the pattern constrains the Judge only if the Judge is weaker than His precedents — which is absurd. What the verse's question really does is transfer the burden of proof: not "will you be punished?" but "on what grounds do you claim exemption from the established sentence?"

**The Bias That Makes Precedent Feel Like It Doesn't Apply**

Here the verse cuts into a recognizable psychological weakness. Every generation is biased toward its own permanence: the past feels *past* — stories, distant, closed; the present feels *real*. The Makkan chieftain standing in his courtyard, surrounded by nephews and trade contracts, *felt* exempt from the fate of "those of old" — they were dead, he was alive; they were tales, he was fact. Modern humanity repeats the bias with more technology: the same species that has catalogued its own near-extinctions and civilizations' graves still discusses its distant future as though *this* time the pattern suspends. The Qur'an names the illusion and dismantles it: *"Until, when We seize their affluent ones with punishment, at once they cry out. Cry not this day — indeed, with Us you will not be helped. My signs used to be recited to you, but you used to turn back on your heels…"* (23:64–66). And the sūrah's next verse (77:17) will sharpen the point beyond escape: the destruction of the ancients was not the closing of an era but the *beginning of a conveyor* — the later generations have been following them ever since, and the line of followers does not stop with the Quraysh. Verse 16, then, is not merely an appeal to memory; it is the Qur'an's way of putting every listener — Makkan or modern — on notice that he stands *inside* a series, and that inside a series, precedent is not history: it is prognosis.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:17

> **then make those of later times follow them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conveyor of Generations**

Verse 17 completes the previous verse's double question: *thumma nutbiʿuhum al-ākhirīn* — "then make those of later times follow them." The key verb is *atbaʿa* — to cause to follow, to set in succession; its intensive form here (*nutbiʿ*) pictures God conducting generations like a man driving file after file down the same road. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī read it in two complementary registers. First, the historical: the later peoples of denial — after Nūḥ, after ʿĀd, after Thamūd — were made to follow their predecessors *in destruction*; the pattern is not one catastrophe but a relay of them. Second, the eschatological, which most of the commentators prefer for this sūrah: on the Day of Resurrection, the later generations will literally *follow* the earlier ones — into the standing-ground of judgment, and, for the guilty, into the Fire, peoples in files, as the Qur'an says: *"And they will be presented before your Lord in ranks: 'You have come to Us as We created you the first time — nay, but you claimed We would never make for you an appointment'"* (18:48); and *"the Day the Trumpet is blown and you come in throngs (*afwājan*)"* (78:18); and of Hell's filling: *"Fill it!"* — *"…On that Day We will say to Hell: Are you filled? and it will say: Is there more?"* (50:30). The Resurrection, in this picture, is not a scattering but an *ordering*: the denier does not meet his fate alone but joins a procession that has been queuing since the first city was overturned.

**"Thumma" — the Terrible Patience of the Order**

The particle *thumma* — "then" — deserves attention, for it carries sequence with a pause: first the ancients, *then* — after an interval — the later ones. The interval is the history the later generations are currently living: every generation of deniers is born into the interval, mistakes the pause for safety, and is handed on, when its term arrives, to the same terminus. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of *ajal* — the appointed term of persons and peoples: *"And for every community there is a term; and when its term arrives, they cannot defer it an hour nor advance it"* (7:34); *"And We destroyed no township but it had a known decree — a booked term"* (15:4). The imagery of following also has a darkly precise social observation embedded in it: deniers *do* follow their predecessors — in argument. Every generation of mockers has reused the same handful of objections: the Resurrection is old wives' tales; the messenger is a madman or a fabricator; we follow what we found our fathers upon. The Qur'an catalogs the script: *"Never did a messenger come to them but they mocked him — so We destroyed those mightier in strength than these, and the example of the ancients has gone before"* (43:6–8). And it seals the pattern's lesson with a standing command to travel and verify: *"Say: Travel in the land and see how was the end of the deniers"* (6:11; 16:36). Verse 17 thus describes a *double* following: they follow the ancients in words, and are made to follow them in ends — mockery imitating mockery, ruin completing ruin. Verse 17 thus describes a *double* following: they follow the ancients in words, and are made to follow them in ends — mockery imitating mockery, ruin completing ruin.

**The Doctrine of the Ummah's Exception — and Its Conditions**

A Muslim reader must immediately hold verse 17's pattern beside the Qur'an's own promise about the Prophet's ﷺ community, or the verse will read as fatalism. The Qur'an teaches both: the pattern of destruction for deniers, and the covenant of preservation for a believing community — *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind"* (3:110) — with the second explicitly *conditional* on what the pattern warns against: *"God does not change what is in a people until they change what is in themselves"* (13:11). The Prophet ﷺ was shown both fates in the famous vision of the pre-dawn: the report of the dream in which he saw *"the treasures of the earth and its ornaments were brought to me…"* — rather, the relevant established tradition: the Prophet ﷺ said, *"While I was asleep, I was shown what you are being made to face: two gardens… and the Fire was gathered…"* — the essential lesson being the community's standing warning, which he voiced plainly: *"Hasten to deeds before trials like patches of black night…"* (Muslim 157, from Abū Hurayrah) — trials in which *"the one sitting is better than the one standing."* The pattern of verse 17, in other words, was not annulled for the ummah; it was *addressed* to it: destruction follows denial wherever denial settles, and no lineage — not even prophetic lineage, as the drowned son of Nūḥ proved (11:42–46) — buys exemption: *"No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another"* (6:164; 35:18). The conveyor runs on creed and deed, not on ancestry: *"the noblest of you in God's sight is the most reverent of you"* (49:13).

**The Modern Face of the Queue**

The verse's final service to the modern reader is epistemic humility about "later times" — the category that now includes everyone alive. Every human being presently breathing is, in the sūrah's ledger, one of *al-ākhirīn* — the later ones — following an immense line of predecessors who each, in their own century, believed themselves the exception. The archaeologist's trench and the historian's archive are, read with verse 17, a single datum: *file closed, file closed, file closed.* The Qur'an's counsel to each "later" generation is therefore not despair but urgency — the urgency of the second half of the verse's logic: if following is inevitable, the only question is *whom to follow and where.* The Qur'an offers the choice of processions: follow the ancients of denial into the place appointed for them, or join the procession of the reverent — *"those foremost in faith… a throng of the former ones and a throng of the later ones"* (56:10–40) — of whom the sūrah will speak at verses 41–45. Verse 17's grammar of succession is, in the end, an invitation: every generation is handed on; the wise choose their queue.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:18

> **Thus do We deal with the guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rule Stated as Law**

Verse 18 draws the deduction from the two preceding questions and pronounces it as a general statute: *kadhālika nafʿalu bi'l-mujrimīn* — "Thus do We deal with the guilty." The demonstrative *kadhālika* — "just so, in this manner" — binds the statute to the precedents: the drowning of Nūḥ's people, the wind of ʿĀd, the Cry over Thamūd are not anecdotes; they are *case law*. And the subject of the verb is God alone — *nafʿalu*, "We do" — in the plural of majesty. The object is *al-mujrimīn*, "the guilty" — from the root *j-r-m*, whose base meaning in Arabic is to cut, to sever: a *jirm* is a harvested stalk cut from its root; a *majrim* is one who has *cut himself off* — from the covenant, from gratitude, from the fitrah — by crime. The Qur'an uses *mujrim* almost as a technical opposite of *muttaqī* (the reverent): the two categories into which the Day of Division will sort humanity. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Nasafī note the word's breadth here: on the immediate level, the guilty among the Quraysh; on the sūrah's own larger level, the guilty of all generations — the statute is universal, addressed to whoever hears it.

**Sunnat Allāh — the Unchanging Pattern**

What verse 18 states in one clause, the Qur'an elsewhere names and guarantees as a *sunnah* — God's established practice: *"You will find no alteration in God's sunnah, and you will find no change in God's sunnah"* (35:43; cf. 33:62; 48:23). Two qualities are denied of the pattern: *tabdīl* (alteration — the substitution of new terms for old) and *taḥwīl* (transfer — the movement of consequences to some other quarter). The denial matters morally: if God's pattern could bend, the universe's moral order would be corruptible at its top — every tyrant's real hope is not that justice does not exist but that it can be *negotiated with*. The Qur'an forecloses the negotiation. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his discussions of divine way (*minhaj*) in history, treats this cluster of verses as the foundation of Islamic philosophy of history: civilizations are not destroyed by time's accidents but by sin's logic — God's patience being real but *term-bounded*, and His pattern being a promise as firm as His promise of rain. The same doctrine, turned toward the believer, becomes consolation: the pattern that guarantees the guilty's end also guarantees the helper's: *"O you who believe, if you help God, He will help you and plant your feet firm"* (47:7).

**"Guilty" Not "Unlucky" — the Moral Grammar of History**

The verse's choice of *mujrimīn* — the guilty — quietly performs the sūrah's most subversive act against the deniers' worldview: it declares history's catastrophes to be *moral* events, not natural or political ones. The Makkan polytheist read the ruins of Thamūd as scenery — a haunted valley to hurry past at night (as the Prophet ﷺ did precisely on the Tabūk march, saying: *"Do not enter the abodes of those who wronged themselves except weeping, lest what befell them befall you"* — al-Bukhārī 433; Muslim 2980). The modern secular reader reads ruins as climate, economics, leadership failure — all real, all secondary. Verse 18 asserts the primary layer: guilt, verdict, execution. This is not an invitation to ghoulish certainty about every earthquake — the Prophet ﷺ forbad declaring specific persons damned, and taught that *"the signs of the hypocrite are three…"* rather, the general rule of charity in specific judgments; the Qur'an itself distinguishes tested calamities from punitive ones (*"And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger…"* — 2:155). But the *category* is asserted: there is a class of events in history that are sentences — and the sentence is still in force: *"Thus do We deal with the guilty."* The verse's present tense (*nafʿalu* — "We deal") refuses to confine the pattern to the past: it is the standing practice, renewed as each term arrives — as verse 17's conveyor implied.

**The Mirror for the Believer's Own Nation**

For readers within the faith, the statute in verse 18 is directed not outward at pagans only but at the Qur'an's own addressees across time — for the Qur'an repeatedly warns the believing community with the very same pattern: *"And We tested those before them, and God will surely know those who are truthful and He will surely know the liars"* (29:3); *"And those cities — We destroyed them when they did wrong, and We set for their destruction an appointed time"* (18:59); and the conditional national promise: *"If only the peoples of the cities had believed and been reverent, We would have opened upon them blessings from heaven and earth; but they denied, so We seized them for what they earned"* (7:96). The historical record of Islamic civilization itself — its rises attending sincerity and law, its falls attending luxury and injustice — has been read by the tradition's historians (Ibn Khaldūn most famously, theorizing the pattern Ibn Kathīr applied narratively) as a case-file of verse 18. The believer's proper use of the verse is therefore double: gratitude's caution and reform's urgency — never the cynic's gloating over others' ruins, for the Qur'an forbids the sneer outright: *"O you who believe, let not a people mock another people; it may be that they are better than they"* (49:11). The statute is a mirror before it is a window; each generation that reads *"Thus do We deal with the guilty"* must ask — first of all — to whom the pronoun *We* will most soon apply: *them, or us?*

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:19

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Toll — and What It Seals**

The refrain falls for the second time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn*. If the first stroke (v. 15) sealed the panel of the *promise and the Day*, this one seals the panel of *history's verdict*: verses 16–18 asked whether God had not destroyed the ancients, made the later ones follow them, and declared it His standing statute for the guilty — and now the sentence drops on the sūrah's own audience: *ruin that Day to the deniers.* Al-Shawkānī, citing some of the early exegetes, records a notable division of labor between the two strokes: the first woe aims at the punishment of the Hereafter, while this second aims at the destructions *of this world* — the pattern of vv. 16–17 falling upon deniers in history. Whether or not one fixes the jurisdictions so neatly, the theological claim is the same in both readings: the denier is doubly exposed — to the historical pattern that has never spared a defiant people, and to the eschatological Day that will gather what history left unfinished. The two jurisdictions do not compete; they audit each other. The Qur'an names the arrangement exactly: *"We grant them enjoyment a little; then We compel them toward a grave punishment"* (31:24).

**The Audience of a Judgment Already Served Elsewhere**

There is a grim dramatic irony in this second toll that deserves reflection. The refrain says "woe *that Day*" — yet the panel it closes (vv. 16–17) is entirely about destructions *already accomplished*: the ancients are already destroyed; the later ones already follow. In other words, the sūrah pronounces its future woe over an audience whose own class has a completed record. The Makkan denier heard verses 16–18 as a claim about third parties — *those* people, long dead — and then heard verse 19 hand him the verdict anyway. The rhetorical trap is closed: he cannot deny the pattern (the ruins are an eight-days' ride away); he cannot exempt himself (verse 17 put his generation in the queue); and now the woe is read aloud over his head *in advance* — a sentence published before the crime's final act, which is the Qur'an's precise method: God does not ambush; He announces. *"Never did We destroy a township but it had warners — as a reminder; and We were never unjust"* (26:208–209). The pre-announced sentence is simultaneously pure justice and pure mercy: justice, because no one can plead surprise; mercy, because every announcement is a chance to leave the category of *mukadhdhibīn* before the category is sealed.

**Repetition as the Reaching of the Hard of Hearing**

Why does the sūrah repeat the sentence at all — would not once suffice? The classical rhetoricians' answer (*takrār li'l-tawqīr wa'l-tahdīd* — repetition for solemnity and threat) is true but not exhaustive; the Qur'an itself explains the pedagogy of repetition in the story of the使命 of Moses: *"…that We might show you of Our signs… for Moses…"* rather, the canonical text on reiteration is the Qur'an's own word about itself: *"And We have certainly cited for mankind in this Quran every kind of example, that they may remember — an Arabic Quran, without crookedness, that they may be reverent"* (39:27–28); and: *"And We repeat the signs, that they may know…"* — precisely: *"See how We repeat the signs, that they may understand"* (6:65). Human beings do not receive a warning once: the first hearing startles, the second confirms, the tenth — if mercy grants ten — becomes the atmosphere of thought. Every parent, every physician, every safety engineer knows this: the single warning is written for the record; the repeated warning is written for the *heart*. Ten refrains in fifty verses is not padding; it is the auditory form of longsuffering — each toll saying what God says through Ezekiel's counterpart-manner in His own Book: *"As I live, declares the Lord God, I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live"* — which the Qur'an voices as: *"Why would God punish you if you are grateful and believe? God is Appreciative, Knowing"* (4:147).

**The Divider Between Hearing and Heeding**

The second toll also marks, for the reflective reader, the boundary the entire sūrah is about: between *hearing* the truth and *being changed* by it. Verses 16–18 constitute an unanswerable case — the deniers conceded every premise (the ruins, the deaths, the pattern) and still drew no conclusion. That gap — between conceded premises and refused conclusion — is the precise anatomy of *takdhīb*. It is not an intellectual failure; the evidence was never the problem. It is a *volitional* failure: the conclusion costs a change of life, so the conclusion is declined while its premises are signed. The Qur'an diagnoses the mechanism exactly: *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of insolence and tyranny — so see how was the end of the corrupters"* (27:14). Modern psychology calls it motivated reasoning; the Qur'an called it *ẓulm* and *ʿuluww* fourteen centuries earlier — injustice and arrogance: knowing, and refusing to let the knowing matter. The second toll of woe thus stands over a very specific human type: not the ignorant, but the informed-invariant — the person whose map is corrected and whose route never changes. Between this toll and the next, the sūrah will move from history into the denier's own body — evidence even closer than ruins: his own creation.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:20

> **Did We not create you from a base fluid,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Ruins to the Mirror of the Body**

The sūrah's third panel of evidence opens with a shift of scenery as intimate as the last was distant: *a-lam nakhluqkum min māʾin mahīn* — "Did We not create you from a base fluid?" The interrogative *a-lam* again demands the concession *balā* — "yes, You did." The court of history (vv. 16–18) is adjourned and a closer court convened: the denier's own body. The argumentative logic is naked: the One who created him once from what everyone agrees was nothing — a drop of despised fluid — cannot be plausibly doubted when He promises to re-create him. The Qur'an deploys this argument more than any other against resurrection-deniers: *"Does man reckon he will be left to no purpose? Was he not a drop of emitted fluid? Then he was a clinging clot, and He created and fashioned, and made of him the pair, male and female. Is not that One able to revive the dead?"* (75:36–40); *"And he has struck for Us a likeness and forgotten his own creation: 'Who revives these bones when they have rotted?' Say: He who created them the first time — and He knows every creation"* (36:78–79); *"As We originated the first creation, so shall We repeat it — a promise binding upon Us"* (21:104). Verse 20 is the premise of that whole syllogism, laid before the mocker as a question about his own mother and father.

**Mahīn — the Deliberate Insult of the Drop**

The adjective is the verse's blade: *mahīn* — base, despicable, humble, held in contempt. It derives from the root *m-h-n*, the same that yields *mahānah* — humiliation. The Qur'an chooses it deliberately, and uses it again in the twin passage: *"Then He made his progeny from an extract of base fluid (*māʾin mahīn*)"* (32:8). Why would the Book of God *insult* the human seed? For exactly the sūrah's purpose: to dismantle the pride that stands between the creature and his Creator. The Makkan chiefs mocked the Resurrection from saddles of inherited prestige — lineage, wealth, alliances (as the Qur'an remarks: *"And when it is said to him, 'Be reverent of God,' pride seizes him in sin — so Hell is his reckoning…"* 2:206). Against prestige, the Qur'an sets the common denominator of all flesh: every emir and every beggar, every prophet and every pharaoh, began as the same contemptible drop — *"He created you from dust, then from a drop, then He made you pairs"* (35:11). Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report preserved by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, glosses *mahīn* plainly: the drop of semen from which man is created is a thing people would shrink from touching or naming — and from *that* God built the creature who debates Him. The lesson is not that man is contemptible — man is God's honored vicegerent (17:70) — but that his *origin* is, so that honor may be seen as *gift*, never *ground*.

**The Prophet's Embryology — the Ḥadīth That Walks Through Verse 20**

The Sunnah supplies the perfect companion text, among the most scientifically examined narrations in the canon, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd:

> *"Each of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop (nutfah), then he is a clot (*ʿalaqah*) for the like of that, then a morsel of flesh (*mudghah*) for the like of that; then the angel is sent and breathes the spirit (rūḥ) into him, and is commanded to write four words: his provision (rizq), his term (ajal), his deeds, and whether wretched or blessed."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

The narration is verse 20's timeline made canonical: the *nutfah* of this ḥadīth is precisely the *māʾ mahīn* of the verse — and the forty-day staging matches the Qur'an's own fuller chart at 22:5 and 23:12–14 (*"Then We made the drop a clot, and We made the clot a morsel of flesh, and We made the morsel bones, and We clothed the bones in flesh — then We produced him as another creation; blessed is God, the best of creators"*). The final clause of that verse — *"another creation"* — is, according to the classical commentators, the moment of the breathed spirit: the point at which biology becomes biography. It is also, soteriologically, the hinge of the sūrah's argument: the *first* "other creation" — spirit breathed into assembled flesh — is the standing miracle that guarantees the *second* "other creation," the resurrection: *"the Day the spirits are…"* — rather the Qur'an's explicit equivalence: *"Your creation and your resurrection are but as a single soul's"* (31:28).

**The Argument Every Human Carries in His Own Past**

The argument's power lies in its universality: it recruits every hearer's own existence as its evidence. No one alive commissioned his own conception; no one drafted his own genome; no one assembled his skeleton in the womb or negotiated his heartbeat's rhythm. Each human being is the finished product of a construction project he neither planned nor supervised — undertaken, in the Qur'an's account, from materials he would blush to name, by a Builder he cannot see, to specifications (term, provision, deeds, felicity or wretchedness) he was not consulted on. The denier's whole posture — the demand that God *prove* Himself before being obeyed — is thus turned on its head: the denier is himself the proof, walking; his very breath is a lease he never signed, renewed this second, terminable at the Lessor's pleasure (*"God takes souls at their death…"* — 39:42). Al-Rāzī, pressing the logic, notes that the denier must deny his own memory to sustain his denial: he was once (not long ago) *nothing*; he is now *someone*; and he proposes that the Someone who effected that transition cannot effect its repetition. Verse 20 invites him to stand at the sink, at the mirror, at the cradle of his own child, and reconsider. The sūrah's next verses will follow the drop to its appointed lodging.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:21

> **then lodge it in a secure dwelling place**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Womb — Architecture Before the Tenant Exists**

Verse 21 follows the drop of verse 20 to its address: *fa-jaʿalnāhu fī qarārin makīn* — "then We lodged it in a secure dwelling place." The two nouns are built from twin roots of settledness: *qarār* is from *q-r-r*, to settle, to come to rest after motion — a *mustaqarr* is a resting place, a final berth; *makīn* is from *m-k-n*, to be firm, established, in place — a *makān* is a location, and *makīn* means well-fixed, secured. Together they describe a lodging that is both *restful* and *fortified*: the drop, having traveled, is settled; being settled, it is protected. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr identify the dwelling without hesitation: the womb (*al-raḥim*) — and the identification is not merely anatomical but theological, for the Qur'an points man to himself as evidence: *"And in yourselves — do you not then see?"* (51:21), and swears by the soul's own fashioning: *"By the soul and what fashioned it, and inspired it its wickedness and its reverence…"* (91:7–8). The closest doctrinal sibling is the Qur'an's own description of the womb's hospitality: *"He created you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation, in threefold darkness"* (39:6).

**Three Veils of Darkness — the Security Described**

The "threefold darkness" of 39:6 is the classical gloss on this verse's *security*: the traditional interpretation, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and standard in al-Ṭabarī, names the three veils as the abdominal wall, the uterine wall, and the membranes (amnion and chorion) around the embryo — three curtains of tissue between the fragile beginning and the world's violence. Consider what the womb's security actually means as engineering: a constant temperature held to fractions of a degree; a cushioning fluid that neutralizes shocks; a placenta that negotiates nutrition, oxygen, and waste continuously without the tenant's knowledge or consent; an immune arrangement of miraculous diplomacy — the mother's powerful defenses ordered not to attack the half-foreign guest; and a cervical seal and muscular walls that no ordinary impact can breach. Every hazard the world can hurl — cold, hunger, toxin, predator — is answered in advance by a structure built before the tenant had a name. This is what *qarār makīn* means: not merely a location but a *prepared* one — the first house every human being lived in was a palace of provision he could not see, built by the Hand he now debates.

**The Moral of the Lodging — Life as Tenancy, Not Ownership**

The theological yield of verse 21 is a doctrine of *tenancy* that reframes all of life. The human being began as a guest in a house he did not build, and he will end as a guest in a house (the grave) he did not dig; between the two dwellings he occupies a middle house — the body and the world — which the Qur'an explicitly assigns the same status: *"And He it is who produced you from the earth and settled you in it"* (11:61), and: *"It is He who made the earth tractable for you — so walk its shoulders and eat of His provision"* (67:15). A tenant who forgets tenancy behaves like an owner: he renovates against the lease (sins against the fitrah), bars the Landlord's inspectors (denies the messengers), and is astonished at eviction (death). The Qur'an's entire ethic of *amānah* — trust — rests on this remembered status: *"We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to carry it and feared it — but man carried it; surely he is unjust, ignorant"* (33:72). Verse 21 plants the reminder at the biological root: even your first address was *provided*, and the Provider retained the keys — *"God knows what every female bears, and every increase or decrease of what is in the wombs; with Him everything is in a known measure"* (13:8).

**Security That Reminds Rather Than Stifles**

There is also a pastoral reading of the verse that the devotional tradition has long savored. The same God who secured the drop secures the believer — not from all hardship (the womb itself is dark and confined) but *through* it, to a scheduled birth. The Qur'an's favorite mercy-pattern is exactly this: confinement that ends in delivery, darkness that ends in light: *"He brings the living from the dead and the dead from the living… and He is the reviver of the earth after its death — even so you will be brought forth"* (30:19); and to the Prophet ﷺ in his most besieged year: *"Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you… and find you astray and guide… and find you in want and enrich?"* (93:6–8). The believer in trial is invited to remember himself at his most fragile — a single cell in threefold darkness — and to reason from that memory: *the Hand that secured me then holds me now; the darkness I inhabit has walls He built; the term of my gestation in this trial is 'known measure,' as the next verse says.* For the denier, the same memory argues differently: the security he never noticed, the lodging he never thanked for, the evicted-and-forgotten first home — all of it testifies against his ingratitude on a Day he denies. Either way, verse 21 leaves every reader with the same fact: each human being is a twice-housed creature — once hidden and safe, now exposed and leased — and both housings were arranged by the same careful Hand.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:22

> **for a known measure?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation Runs on a Timetable**

Verse 22 supplies the term of the tenancy opened in verse 21: *ilā qadarin maʿlūm* — "until a known measure." *Qadar* — from *q-d-r*, to measure, to determine, to have power (the same root that yields *qadīr*, Almighty, and *miqdār*, a quantity) — is the Qur'an's grand word for measured decree; *maʿlūm* — "known" — specifies by whom: known to God. Known, in the first instance, is the gestation's span — and here the Sunnah's embryology ḥadīth (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, quoted at v. 20) gives the canonical staging: forty days as a drop, forty as a clot, forty as a morsel of flesh — the "known measure" of the normal term, roughly nine lunar months, which modern obstetrics confirms as the remarkably narrow window (about 38–40 weeks) within which a healthy delivery falls. But the classical commentators refuse to confine *qadar* to pregnancy alone, and the sūrah's grammar invites their broadening: the "known measure" of the womb is the emblem of every other measured term — the lifespan itself (*"And no soul can die but by God's leave, at a written term (kitāban muʾajjalan)"* — 3:145; cf. 4:78: *"Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, though you be in raised-up towers"*), the term of nations (15:4: *"And We destroyed no township but it had a known decree"*), and the term of the world (*"They ask you about the Hour… Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord"* — 7:187; *"And God never delays a soul when its term comes"* — 63:11).

**The Universe as Metrology — Why "Measure" Is a Divine Name**

The doctrine packed into the word *qadar* is one of the Qur'an's most distinctive teachings: reality is not raw power but *measured* power — God acts by *taqdīr*, precise determination. The creation hymn at the head of Sūrah al-Furqān states it as God's signature method: *"…And He created everything and measured it exactly (*fa-qaddarahu taqdīran*)"* (25:2). And the created order is presented as a lattice of measures, without which nothing would hold together: the sun and moon *"calculated (ḥusbān)"* in their courses (6:96; 55:5); rain sent *"in due measure (qadar)"* (23:18; 43:11); night and day apportioned (73:20); the embryo formed *"in due measure" (*"…and He formed you and fashioned your forms well" — 40:64; cf. 82:7–8: *"who created thee, then fashioned thee, then proportioned thee — in whatever form He willed He assembled thee"*)*; and the Day of Judgment itself pre-announced as *"a known day (yawman maʿlūman)"* on which *"no soul will speak except by His leave"* (11:99–105's manner). Modern physical science, read with humility, is the discovery of the same lattice: every fundamental constant — gravity's strength, the proton's mass, the speed of light — sits in a razor-thin band where stars, chemistry, and life are possible; cosmologists call the coincidence "fine-tuning" and debate its meaning. Verse 22's doctrine precedes the debate's terms: measure is not an accident of nature but the manner of its Author — *"And the sky He raised and set the balance"* (55:7).

**Freedom Inside the Framework — What "Known" Does and Does Not Fix**

Because *qadar* touches the deep question of destiny and free will, the verse requires careful doctrine, and the classical tradition supplies it. What is fixed is the *framework* — terms, measures, provision, the boundaries of the possible; what is genuinely given to man is choice *within* the framework: *"And say: The truth is from your Lord — so whoever will, let him believe, and whoever will, let him disbelieve"* (18:29). The Prophet ﷺ drew the practical boundary in one line to a Bedouin who asked whether he should tie his camel or trust in God: *"Tie it, and trust in God (iʿqil hā wa tawakkal)"* — i.e., act within the measure, and rely on the Measurer (al-Tirmidhī, graded ḥasan). And he taught the correct emotional posture toward the written term — neither fatalist apathy nor anxious control — in the famous counsel to the young Ibn ʿAbbās (Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī): *"Know that if all mankind gathered to benefit you, they could not benefit you except with what God had written for you; and if they gathered to harm you, they could not harm you except with what God had written against you — the pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* — counsel delivered, the tradition notes, alongside the command to keep asking God for everything, for the decree includes the prayer. Ibn Taymiyyah's summary of the Sunni position is the standard: man acts truly, God created the acts and the actor and the framework; the two truths are held together because revelation asserts both, and neither is sacrificed to the other.

**The Comfort of a Known Term — and Its Discipline**

Emotionally, the doctrine of the measured term is one of Islam's great quiet gifts. Nothing in the believer's life — no trial, no siege, no addiction, no illness — is unbounded; every darkness, like the womb's, has a *known measure* known to God: *"For indeed, with hardship comes ease — indeed, with hardship comes ease"* (94:5–6, the doubled promise); and no prosperity, likewise, is permanent — which disciplines it. The denier's error, by contrast, is to read the measurelessness of his *feelings* as the measurelessness of the fact: he feels death is far, so he lives as immortal; the Qur'an corrects the feeling with the datum: *"And every nation has its term; when its term arrives, they cannot defer it an hour nor advance it"* (7:34), and: *"Every soul shall taste death; and only on the Day of Resurrection will you be paid your wages in full"* (3:185). Verse 22 thus completes the sūrah's creation-argument with its most humbling statistic: the denier himself is a *dated* artifact — assembled in the dark on a schedule he never saw, to be disassembled on one he cannot see. The next verse draws the verse-20-to-22 argument to its amazed conclusion: *so We determined — and how excellent are We at determining!*

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:23

> **Thus do We determine; what excellent determiners!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**God Applauds His Own Workmanship — the Grammar of Praise**

Verse 23 closes the creation panel with an exclamation of self-praise: *fa-qadarnā fa-niʿma'l-qādirūn* — "So We determined — and how excellent are the determiners!" The first clause is past tense and matter-of-fact: the measuring happened, once, in the wombs of the audience's own mothers. The second clause is the rhetorical form Arabic calls *niʿma l-* — a fixed eulogy pattern ("excellent indeed are…") ordinarily used by admirers about the admired. Here God is Subject, Act, and Audience of the applause — which to a modern ear may seem strange, but is theologically exact: praise is the fitting public response to excellent work, and when no creature is yet on the scene (the scene being the first weeks of gestation, and the whole order of measures), God's own naming of the excellence is simply the first — and most qualified — review of the work. The Qur'an uses this pattern repeatedly at the joints of its strongest arguments: *"Blessed is God, the best of creators (*aḥsanu'l-khāliqīn*)"* (23:14; 37:125); *"an excellent Master and an excellent Helper"* — *niʿma'l-mawlā wa-niʿma'l-naṣīr* (8:40; 22:78); *"an excellent Patron and an excellent Helper"* (25:31). The plural "We" — *niʿma'l-qādirūn* — is the plural of majesty: one God, named as it were in the full dignity of His attributes.

**Determining as a Divine Attribute — the Name al-Qadīr**

The participle *qādir* — determiner, one fully able — is one of God's names of intensive capacity, and the Qur'an pairs His power with the resurrection in hundreds of verses: *"…and He is powerful over everything (wa huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr)"* — the refrain that concludes the very passages this sūrah echoes: *"As We originated the first creation, so shall We repeat it — a promise binding upon Us; We shall do it"* (21:104); *"Do they not see that God, who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation, is able to revive the dead?"* (46:33; cf. 17:99; 36:81). The attribute is thus not ornamental but argumentative: God's *capacity to determine* — demonstrated on the smallest scale (embryos) and the largest (skies) — is precisely the capacity resurrection requires. Ibn Taymiyyah notes in his discussions of the divine attributes that God's power is of the essence of His lordship: a god who could not measure and execute would not be Lord but spectator; and the Qur'an's most common objection to the pagan gods is exactly their powerlessness (*"Say: Have you seen your partners whom you call upon apart from God? Show me what they created of the earth — or have they a share in the heavens?"* — 46:4). Verse 23's exclamation, then, is a miniature creed: determining is what God *does*; excellence at it is what God *is*.

**The Craftsmanship Under the Microscope — Creation's "Fit and Finish"**

The verse's claim — excellence of determination — is not left to the reader's imagination; the sūrah's own subject supplies the exhibit. What "determination" means in embryonic development can be read in any modern biology text, translated back into the Qur'an's vocabulary: single cells following chemical gradients to their pre-assigned positions; the precisely timed switching of genes that makes liver of one cluster and lens of another; the apoptosis — programmed cell death — by which fingers are carved from paddles, a sculptor who removes material *by measure*; the neural tube closing within hours-long windows, on pain of lifelong consequence; the heart's first beat, the lungs' surfactant timed for the due date, the milk let down — as the Qur'an says in its own register: *"And their mothers' carrying them and their weaning is thirty months…"* (46:15; cf. 2:233 on the two-year nursing term). Al-Qurṭubī, marvelling in his own idiom, writes of the drop's transit — none of it random, all of it ordered to its term. The believer's response to the exhibit is the verse's own: *fa-niʿma'l-qādirūn* — an applause the modern believer utters at every ultrasound image, every newborn's ten fingers, every chart of the menstrual cycle's hormonal symphony. And the denier's position is thereby cornered by the sūrah's method yet again: he attributes the exhibit to unguided machinery — but the machinery's outstanding feature is *measurement*, and measurement is the signature of a measurer; blind forces have no stake in due dates, yet the due dates hold.

**From Womb to Resurrection — the Panel's Verdict**

Finally, verse 23 must be heard as the conclusion it is — the summary of the sūrah's second resurrection-proof (vv. 20–23), standing parallel to the first (vv. 16–18, history) and preparing the third (vv. 25–28, the earth). The logic assembled across the four verses is a completed bridge: *(a)* you were once a base drop (v. 20); *(b)* that drop was lodged and sheltered with perfect engineering (v. 21); *(c)* the lodging ran on a pre-known schedule to a pre-known term (v. 22); *(d)* the Scheduler's craftsmanship is excellent beyond dispute (v. 23) — *therefore* the same Scheduler's announced schedule for your body's second assembly is not to be doubted but prepared for. The Qur'an makes the inference explicit elsewhere: *"O mankind, if you are in doubt about the Resurrection — We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clot… to make clear to you; and We settle in the wombs what We will until a known term…"* (22:5), a verse that ends: *"…and you see the earth still, then when We send down water upon it it stirs and swells and grows every beautiful pair — because God is the Truth, and He revives the dead, and He is powerful over everything."* Verse 23's exclamation point is thus not self-congratulation but case-closing — and the refrain that follows immediately (v. 24) is the verdict the closed case requires: *woe that Day to the deniers* — of a Determiner whose determining they walked around in every day of their own embodied lives.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:24

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Toll — Woe for Denying One's Own Genesis**

The refrain falls for the third time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* Each toll so far has sealed a distinct evidentiary panel: the first (v. 15) the promise of the Day itself; the second (v. 19) the ruins of history; this third seals the court of *the body* — the four verses of gestation (vv. 20–23) that placed the denier's own creation before him as testimony. Al-Shawkānī reads this occurrence in immediate connection with verse 23: the woe falls on those who deny the very *determining* (*taqdīr*) of God — who live inside the measured order, are themselves its products, and yet ascribe the measure to no Measurer. There is a precise fitness in the refrain's placement: the previous panel ended not with an argument but with an exclamation — *"what excellent determiners!"* — and the woe is the contrapuntal answer: for those who will not say *niʿma'l-qādirūn*, the Day will say *waylun*. Praise declined becomes ruin pronounced; the mouth that refuses applause is handed a lament.

**The Anatomy of Denying the Evident**

The third toll marks the deepest layer of *takdhīb* the sūrah has yet exposed. Denying the Resurrection at least concerns an unseen future; denying a *pattern in history* at least concerns distant events; but verses 20–23 concerned what every hearer *was* — his own origin — and the mockery continued anyway. This is the Qur'an's most sober diagnosis of the denier: his problem is never evidence, but the self that must change if evidence is admitted. The Qur'an describes exactly this refusal operating in the Pharaoh, the sūrah's archetype of denial: *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of insolence and tyranny"* (27:14) — conviction in the interior, denial on the lips, because the lips' owners had staked their thrones on the lie. Modern psychology has mapped the same structure under the name of cognitive dissonance and motivated reasoning: when the cost of a conclusion is high, the mind does not reject the evidence so much as *quarantine* it — reframing, mocking, burying it in distraction. The sūrah's ten refrains are, in part, ten attempts to pierce the quarantine: the same sentence, from ten angles, on the chance that one angle finds the crack.

**The Mercy of Being Warned About a Warning**

It must be said plainly, because the refrain's severity can obscure it: every toll of woe is simultaneously an act of mercy. A sentence *pronounced in advance* is an invitation to make it inapplicable. The Makkan audience heard "woe to the deniers" while still alive, still free, still able to leave the category — and the Qur'an defines leaving the category in the easiest terms imaginable: a turning of the heart, not a fee; a word of surrender, not a war reparation: *"And whoever does evil or wrongs himself and then asks God's forgiveness will find God Forgiving, Merciful"* (4:110); and the verse the tradition loves for the hopeless: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (39:53). The refrain's grammar keeps the door open even in its shutting: *lil-mukadhdhibīn* — for the deniers, the ones *characterized* by denial — not for any tribe or name. Denial is a practice, and practices can end; the sentence binds to the deed, not the descent. Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb — who in Makkah was among the fiercest of the mukadhdhibīn's ranks — walked through that open door in the seventeenth year of his life's mistaken vehemence, and the same lips that had planned the Prophet's ﷺ murder spent their remaining decades reciting the Book he had mocked. The refrain he once heard as an enemy, he later recited as a believer — proof in one biography that "that Day" had not yet come, and the toll was still mercy.

**Standing Between Two Panels — What the Reader Has Seen and What Is Next**

Structurally, the third toll completes the sūrah's *first half*: verses 1–24 have run the full arc — oaths (1–5), mission (6), certainty of the promise (7), cosmic signs (8–11), the Day named (12–14), and the first woe (15); then two complete courts of evidence — history (16–19) and the body (20–24) — each ending in its toll. What remains (vv. 25–50) will add the third court (the earth, vv. 25–28), then shift from evidence to sentence: the Fire's portrait (29–34), the courtroom's silence (35–37), the gathering and the challenge (38–40), the contrasted reward (41–45), and the final charges — eat a little, bow not, believe not (46–50). The reader at verse 24 stands therefore at the sūrah's hinge: all the evidence is nearly in; from here the verdict's texture becomes concrete — shadows that do not shade, sparks like castles, mouths sealed. The refrain has tolled three times as invitation; its remaining seven tolls will toll as the described reality gets closer. This is the sūrah's own psychology of persuasion: it does not merely *assert* the Day; it *rehearses* it — ten times over — so that before the last verse asks its famous closing question (*"in what discourse after this will they believe?"*), no hearer can claim the matter was not made real to him.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:25

> **Did We not make the earth a receptacle**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Evidence — Under the Feet the Whole Time**

The sūrah opens its third court of evidence with another concessive question: *a-lam najʿal al-arḍa kifātā* — "Did We not make the earth a receptacle?" The word *kifāt* is a rarity, and the classical lexicons quarrel over it productively. Its root *k-f-t* carries the sense of gathering in, holding fast, and taking up; some of the early exegetes, reported by Ibn Kathīr, gloss *kifāt* as a *container that gathers* — a receptacle; others, following Ibn ʿAbbās in the report al-Ṭabarī cites, derive it from the manner of a beast of prey gathering its kill beneath it; others again read it as "a thing that restrains." The golden summary is the one Ibn Kathīr transmits from the successor al-Shaʿbī — the one the sūrah itself will effectively confirm in the next verse:

> *"Its interior is for your dead, and its surface is for your living."* (related from al-Shaʿbī; cited in Ibn Kathīr's *Tafsīr*)

One word, *kifāt*, holds both services: the ground beneath the living room and the ground beneath the graveyard are the same ground — earth as the universal lodging, upstairs for the quick, downstairs for the dead. The question form (*a-lam* — "did We not…?") once again demands the concession; and the evidence, once more, is underfoot: no one needs a caravan to the ruins of Thamūd, or a memory of his own gestation — he needs only to look down.

**The Earth's Two Offices — Floor and Vault**

Al-Shaʿbī's division deserves expansion, because both halves are divine mercy disguised as geology. As *surface* the earth is the platform of the entire human story: a crust precisely thick enough to bear cities and thin enough to be plowed; soil — that astonishing interface of ground rock, dead organisms, water, and air — without which agriculture, and hence civilization, is unimaginable; a magnetic field and atmosphere shielding the surface's tenants; ground that holds firm under every footfall because the earth's interior is molten below, the planet's solidity underfoot being itself a *design feature* the Qur'an marvels at: *"And God made for you the earth a spread (bisāṭan), that you may walk its spacious ways (subulahā)"* (71:19–20; cf. 2:22: *"who made the earth a bed (firāshan) for you"*). As *interior* the earth is the vault of the dead: the burial custom of nearly all humanity — returning bodies to the soil — is so universal that archaeology takes deliberate burial as the very signature of the human species; the earth receives what it feeds, closing the loop the Qur'an describes elsewhere: *"From it We created you, and into it We return you, and from it We bring you forth another time"* (20:55). That single verse is verse 25's doctrine in full: the earth as womb-tomb-womb — the second gathering *from the same soil*, the physical premise of the Resurrection the deniers mock.

**The Prophet's Brother Buried in the Same Soil**

The word "receptacle" was not abstract for the first audience; the early Muslims buried their dead in it weekly — and the Prophet ﷺ himself would one day lie beneath it in Madinah, in ʿĀʾishah's chamber, as he had buried his own daughters and companions before. Islamic burial practice — the simple shroud, the lateral placement, the handful of earth spoken over the deceased (*"from it We created you…"*, recited at the grave's three castings in the Prophetic rite preserved in the Sunnah) — makes verse 25 a liturgy: every funeral rehearses the sūrah's argument. The Prophet ﷺ stood at graves and preached the same doctrine in the famous ḥadīth of the questioning in the grave, transmitted from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib and others in the Sunan collections: that the dead hear the footsteps of the departing mourners, and are seated and questioned — *"Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?"* — a questioning whose venue is the earth's interior itself: the receptacle as courtroom's antechamber. And he commanded the graves be visited, *"for they remind of the Hereafter"* (Muslim 976, from Abū Hurayrah, in his wording: *"I had forbidden you to visit graves — so visit them, for it softens the heart, fills the eye with tears, and reminds of the Hereafter"* — part of the longer report). Verse 25's kifāt is thus not merely geology: it is the standing appointment between every soul and a plot of ground — an appointment whose purpose, in the Qur'an's pedagogy, is precisely the reminder the refrain will demand at verse 28.

**From Receptacle to Reservoir — a Legal and Ethical Echo**

The earth's double office also grounds an Islamic legal principle the tradition drew from this verse-family: the earth's interior belongs to the commons. The Prophet ﷺ ruled in the famous tradition on the rights of way: *"Muslims are partners in three: water, pasture, and fire"* (Abū Dāwūd 3477; Aḥmad, from a group of the Companions, graded sound in its various chains) — the "fire" including, in the jurists' reading, the subsoil and its fuels; and the land's dead-below are protected from the living-above by the law of the sanctity of graves (hurmah): to build over, sit upon, or desecrate graves the Prophet ﷺ forbade, and he cursed "those who take graves as mosques" (al-Bukhārī 435–436; Muslim 531, from ʿĀʾishah) — in part, as the scholars explain, so the floor of the living never usurp the vault of the dead. Modern readers who have watched cities argue over burial space, or watched archaeology expose how every civilization's floors are other civilizations' vaults, have felt verse 25's truth: the earth is not ours; it is *receiving* us, generation by generation, and its two offices will be consolidated on the Day of Division, when the vault gives back its guests: *"On the Day the earth tells its reports (akhbārahā) — because your Lord has inspired it"* (99:4–5): the receptacle, called to the witness stand.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:26

> **for the living and the dead?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Both Tenancies in One Clause**

Verse 26 completes the previous verse's question with the two clientele of the receptacle: *aḥyāʾan wa amwātā* — "the living and the dead." The order is deliberate and observable: living first, dead second — the sequence in which every human being holds the two leases. The classical commentators (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, following al-Shaʿbī's division quoted at v. 25) read the pair exactly: the earth's surface hosts, feeds, and carries the living; its interior holds the dead. But the verse's deepest suggestion — the one the sūrah's argument requires — is the *continuity* between the two tenancies: the same entity holds both leases; the earth simply transfers its tenant from the upper to the lower apartment, and — the Qur'an insists — is able to transfer him again: *"From it We created you, and into it We return you, and from it We bring you forth another time"* (20:55). The denier's error, once more, is to mistake the transfer for a termination; the earth's error-correcting feature is that it never destroys what it receives — it *stores* it. The farmer's experience is the standing parable: the seed goes into the soil and the farmer does not mourn; the seed's husk breaks, its grain splits, and up comes the crop — *"a likeness: a grain that sprouts seven ears…"* (2:261). The Qur'an presses the parable directly at the resurrection-doubter: *"And He it is who brings the living from the dead and the dead from the living… even so you will be brought forth"* (30:19).

**The Equality the Earth Enforces**

There is also a great leveling embedded in the little word pair. The earth as receptacle for *both* the living and the dead is democracy's truest chamber: it hosts without regard to rank, and it receives without appeal. Pharaoh, who said *"I am your lord most high"* (79:24), was lodged in its interior along with his poorest slave; the Qur'an remarks the fact with acid: *"So God seized him with an exemplary punishment for the last world and the first — indeed in that is a lesson for whoever fears the standing before his Lord"* (79:25–26) — and modern archaeology displayed the lesson in the Cairo Museum: the preserved body the Qur'an said would be a *sign*: *"So this day We shall save you in your body, that you may be a sign for those after you"* (10:92). The believer internalizes the leveling before it is enforced: the Prophet ﷺ, passing a grave-digger's work or a funeral, would say what he said at every burial he led — the language of the earth's two offices: *"from it We created you…"*; and his Companions reported that whenever he saw a grave he wept, as he wept at his daughter's — for the two tenancies' truth: everyone he loved would transfer apartments. The Qur'an's counsel is to let the transfer's certainty price the present lease correctly: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world; but the enduring good deeds are better with your Lord for reward and better for hope" (18:46)*; and: *"Every soul shall taste death; and only on the Day of Resurrection will you be paid your wages in full. So whoever is drawn away from the Fire and entered into the Garden — he has triumphed"* (3:185).

**The Intermediate Tenancy — Barzakh Between the Offices**

The verse pair (25–26) also invites the doctrine the tradition builds from the Qur'an's grave-passages: that the dead, though out of sight, are not out of *being*. The Qur'an names the interval *barzakh* — the barrier: *"…and behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected"* (23:100). In the interval, the earth's interior is not storage of the insensitive but the antechamber of the questioned — the grave-trial ḥadīth (from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, in the Sunan; its meaning supported by Muslim 2870's questioning of the two buried men) describes the dead's first night: *"…then his soul is returned to him…"* and the two angels question him, and his grave is widened or pressed; the report's images: for the believer the grave is *"a garden from the gardens of the Garden"*, and for the denier *"a pit from the pits of the Fire"* (the wording in al-Tirmidhī and others, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, ḥasan). Whether one presses every narrative detail, the doctrine is Qur'anic and this sūrah's next verses will depend on it: the dead are *patients*, awaiting a Day — which is why the earth's lower office is temporary housing, not final address: *"And the Trumpet is blown, and behold, from the graves they come out to their Lord, scurrying"* (36:51).

**The Living's Obligation While the Lease Runs**

For the *living* half of the verse's clientele, the receptacle is not merely lodging but *workshop*, and the Qur'an is explicit that the two tenancies' relation defines the estate's whole purpose: *"It is He who made for you the earth a couch and the sky a ceiling, and sent down water from the sky and brought forth therewith fruits for your provision — so set not rivals to God while you know"* (2:22). The living tenant is fed, housed, and watered — as the next verse (v. 27) will itemize — and is therefore *without excuse*: *"…and He gave you from all you asked of Him; and if you count God's blessings, you cannot number them — indeed man is unjust, ungrateful"* (14:34). Verse 26, read as doctrine of tenancy, assigns the believer his exact posture in both directions: gratitude above (the surface's provision), humility below (the interior's appointment), and — joining them — urgency: the lease that began with the drop's "known measure" (v. 22) will end at the receptacle's lower office, on a measure equally known to its Landlord alone.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:27

> **And We placed therein firm, soaring mountains and gave you sweet water to drink.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Anchors and Drinks — the Verse's Twin Gifts**

Verse 27 itemizes the receptacle's furnishing: *wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātā* — "And We placed therein firm, soaring mountains, and gave you sweet water to drink." Three words repay the lexicon. *Rawāsiya* — from *r-w-s*, to anchor, to fix firmly — is the Qur'an's standing term for mountains (16:15; 21:31; 31:10; 78:7), and it names them by function: *anchors*, the pegs of the crust. *Shāmikhāt* — from *sh-m-kh*, to rise high with pride of stature — adds the visual: towering, conspicuous. *Furāt* — from *f-r-t*, to be sweet and easy on the tongue — is the technical word for palatable, fresh water (its opposite, *ujāj*, brackish, at 56:70); the Qur'an also uses it of the water of Paradise's river (25:53's pairing: *"the two seas… one sweet and fresh (furāt), one salty and bitter (ujāj)"*). The pairing in the verse is not decorative: as the classical commentators from al-Qurṭubī to al-Shawkānī note, the two gifts are one system — the mountains are the machinery of the drink — and both are set, like the womb's lodging, *before* the beneficiary existed: the same providence that lodged the drop (v. 21) lodged the species.

**Mountains as Engineering — the Qur'an's Repeated Claim**

The Qur'an makes four standing claims about mountains, all visible in this verse's word *rawāsiya*. (1) *Stabilizers*: *"And We placed on earth firm mountains lest it should shake with them (an tamīda bihim)"* (16:15; 21:31) — pegs that steady the tent of the crust; the geologist's isostasy — the crust floating on the mantle, mountains with deep roots (*"and the mountains He drove… like pegs (awtād)"*, 78:7, whose singular *watad* is the tent-peg hidden mostly underground) — reads like a modern gloss on the Qur'an's metaphor. (2) *Water towers*: the hydrological cycle's crown — orographic capture: mountains force moist air upward, chilling it to snow and rain; their snowpack melts slowly across the months, feeding the rivers that make the plains habitable; the Qur'an's version: *"And He sent down water from the sky in measure, and We revived thereby a dead land — even so you will be brought forth"* (43:11), with the mountain named as the catcher: *"And We made from the water every living thing" (21:30); "Have you not seen that God drives the clouds, then joins them, then heaps them… and He sends down from the sky mountains (of cloud) wherein is hail…"* (24:43). (3) *Shelter and orientation*: *"and landmarks, and by the star they are guided"* (16:16); the caravan's map was the skyline. (4) *Provision and beauty*: *"…and He set on the earth mountains standing firm, and rivers, and of every fruit He made a pair"* (13:3). Verse 27 compresses claims (1) and (2) into a single line — anchors *and* drinks — because the second depends on the first.

**Sweet Water — the Unrepeatable Gift Set to a Test**

The second clause — *"and gave you sweet water to drink"* — must be heard as the personal address it is: *asqaynākum* — "We gave *you* to drink." The sūrah's evidentiary method has moved the denier from ruins (far), to his own womb (nearer), to his own planet (all around), to his own *cup* — the nearest evidence yet. And the cup's evidence is devastating: fresh water is, by cosmic accounting, a freakish luxury — 97% of the earth's water saline; of the remainder, most locked in ice and deep aquifers; the fraction arriving as palatable rain and river-flow is minuscule, and the Qur'an sets its scarcity as a deliberate test: *"Say: Have you considered? If your water were to sink into the earth, who then would bring you flowing water?"* (67:30); *"And have you seen the water you drink? Is it you who brought it down from the rain-clouds, or are We the Sender? If We willed, We could make it bitter — so why do you not give thanks?"* (56:68–70). The rhetorical structure is identical to this sūrah's: evidence, then the question that demands the concession. And the Prophet ﷺ turned the same evidence into law and ethics: the tradition *"Muslims are partners in three: water, pasture, and fire"* (Abū Dāwūd 3477) makes the nearest gift the most communal; and his ﷺ personal practice — drinking with thanks, forbidding waste even at a running river: *"Do not waste, even if you are at a flowing river"* (Aḥmad and Ibn Mājah, graded sound) — makes verse 27 a daily discipline.

**The Blessing as Argument — and as Trap**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī both note the contextual logic: these blessings (earth, mountains, water) are *"as wondrous as the Resurrection"* — that is, the verse is not a digression into nature-worship but the closing exhibit of the resurrection-case: the God who anchors continents and distills oceans to sweetness is not taxed by reassembling skeletons; and the God who *gave* the drink is worthy of the thanksgiving the deniers withhold. But the sūrah is also planting the trap its refrain (v. 28) will spring: ungrateful enjoyment of the receptacle's furnishings is itself a sin — the misuse of an exhibit. The Qur'an's heaviest charges are reserved for exactly this: *"And God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and He gave you hearing and sight and hearts, that you might give thanks"* (16:78); *"…and if you count God's blessings, you will never number them; indeed man is unjust, ungrateful"* (14:34). Modern humanity's relation to verses 25–27 is a controlled experiment on the point: never has a civilization *used* the earth's offices so thoroughly — cities, mining, irrigation, hydrology to the last aquifer — nor credited its Landlord so formally; the scientific description of the mountain-water system is complete, and the doxology is absent; the reaction has been described, the thanks not rendered — which is precisely the Qur'an's definition of the kufr of provision: using the letter's gifts while denying the letter's Author. The refrain falls next, and it falls on *that*.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:28

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Toll — the Woe of the Ungrateful Tenant**

The refrain tolls for the fourth time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke seals the third and final court of evidence — the earth panel (vv. 25–27): the receptacle that housed them, the vault that awaits them, the anchors overhead and the sweetness in their cups. Al-Shawkānī reads this occurrence with precision: the woe falls on those who *fail to be thankful* for the blessings the panel enumerated — who drank the furāt, built on the kifāt, navigated by the shāmikhāt, and denied the Hand behind all three. The Qur'an's term for this posture is *kufr niʿmah* — the disbelief of the blessing — using, pointedly, the root *k-f-r* whose primal agricultural meaning is *to cover*: the farmer "covers" (*kāfir*) seed with soil; the denier "covers" the Giver beneath layers of secondary causation — the market gave the food, the utility gave the water, the crust gave the mountains. Verse 28's woe is the judicial reply to covered gratitude: the tenant who denied the Landlord's existence while consuming his provisions finds, at lease's end, that the ledger was kept after all.

**Three Panels, One Verdict — the Evidence Now Complete**

With the fourth toll the sūrah's evidentiary architecture stands complete, and it is worth surveying the finished structure, because its cumulative logic is the sūrah's real sermon. Panel one (vv. 16–19): *history* — God's verdicts executed on past deniers; the court of public record. Panel two (vv. 20–24): *the body* — God's craftsmanship in the denier's own origin; the court of personal memory. Panel three (vv. 25–28): *the earth* — God's ongoing provision beneath the denier's feet and on his tongue; the court of daily experience. Three courts, each requiring no instruments and no faith — only honesty about the nearest facts: ruins, wombs, and water. The refrains after each panel (vv. 19, 24, 28) are not ritual; they are verdicts rendered three times on the same case, on the chance that the reader will accept the verdict *out of court* — that is, repent — rather than force its execution on the Day. This is the Qur'an's constant offer: *"And God calls to the abode of peace, and guides whom He wills to a straight path"* (10:25); the woe-announcements are, inverted, mercy-announcements: every "woe to them that Day" implies "— unless they cease to be *them*."

**Blessings Called as Witnesses**

There is a legal image in this toll the tradition lingers on: on the Day of Division, the blessings themselves testify. The Qur'an says the limbs will speak: *"…their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will bear witness to what they earned"* (36:65; cf. 41:20–21); and the Prophet ﷺ taught that the *earth* itself files the report: *"…and the earth says: 'On this day he did such-and-such'"* — this is the meaning scholars draw from the verse of the earth's tidings: *"That Day it will tell its reports (akhbārahā)"* (99:4–5), with Ibn ʿAbbās and others explaining: *it bears witness to what was done upon it.* The specific comfort and terror of verses 25–27 arriving before verse 28's woe is therefore this: the water called as witness is *the water the defendant drank*; the earth, *the ground beneath his house*. Every blessing is dual-purpose — provision now, exhibit then. The believer's response to this doctrine is the Prophet's ﷺ own practice of naming gifts as gifts: his instruction to the man who had worn a new garment: *"Say: Praise be to God who clothed me with this…"* (Abū Dāwūd 4023, from Muʿādh ibn Anas); his table-grace; and his report of the divine pleasure in the thanked meal: *"God is pleased with the servant who, when he eats, praises Him, and when he drinks, praises Him"* (Muslim 2734, from Anas). Verse 28's woe is thus avoidable at every meal: the same cup that would testify against the ingrate testifies for the grateful.

**From Evidence to Sentence — the Sūrah Turns Its Face to the Fire**

Structurally, the fourth toll is the last sound of the sūrah's first movement: everything from verse 29 to verse 40 will be the *execution phase* — the Fire addressed, entered, endured. The oaths (vv. 1–5), the mission (v. 6), the certainty (v. 7), the cosmic strike of the scenery (vv. 8–11), the Day named (vv. 12–14), and three courts of evidence (vv. 16–28) have all been preparation; what remains is the sentence's content. The shift is worth noticing as a matter of method: the Qur'an does not leave the denier in the abstract courtroom of argument; it *walks him forward* — into the shadow that does not shade, among the sparks like castles, in the company of speechless defendants. This is not cruelty but disclosure — the same merciful candor a physician owes a patient who refuses the treatment: the full, sensory picture of the disease's end. And the sūrah's next verse begins the walk with an imperative whose irony is among the most chilling in the Book: *Depart — to what you used to deny.*

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:29

> **Away to that which you used to deny!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ironic Command — Go Meet Your Denial**

Verse 29 is addressed, in the Hereafter, to the deniers — by God, or by the angels commanding them, as al-Shawkānī and al-Qurṭubī explain — and its grammar is imperative: *anṭalīqū ilā mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn* — "Be off — to what you used to deny!" The verb *inṭalaqa* means to set out, to be released into motion (it is the verb of Moses setting out with his family, 20:9–10); the imperative plural commands a *departure* — but a departure with a destination, and the destination is the joke of the damned: the thing they spent their lives calling a myth. The Qur'an elsewhere stages this same meeting in the denier's own voice: *"…until, when death comes to one of them, he says: My Lord, send me back, that I may do righteousness in what I left behind — nay! It is but a word he speaks…"* (23:99–100); and at the Fire's mouth: *"And those who disbelieved are driven to Hell in throngs, until, when they reach it, its gates are opened and its keepers say to them: Did not messengers from among you come to you, reciting your Lord's signs and warning you of the meeting of this your Day? They say: Yes — but the sentence of punishment upon the disbelievers has come true"* (39:71). Note that last clause: the deniers' final testimony is the refrain's vindication — the Qur'an's own word of fulfillment: *"the sentence (kalimah) of the punishment of the disbelievers has come true."*

**Denial Made an Address**

The verse's genius is the preposition: *ilā* — "to," not merely "into." The deniers are not told "enter your punishment" as strangers enter a prison; they are told to go *to what they denied* — the Resurrection, the Reckoning, the Fire itself, each of which they had named *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*, "fables of the ancients" (6:25; 8:31; 16:24; 23:83). The Qur'an loves this reversal: what you mocked becomes your residence; what you dismissed becomes your curriculum. It is the precise inverse of the believers' arc: they believed in the Unseen while it was unseen, and then *met* what they believed in; the deniers disbelieved while it was unseen, and then meet what they disbelieved. Same appointment, opposite emotions — the appointment itself was never in question. The Qur'an stages the symmetry in the paired destinies of Sūrah al-Zumar: *"And those who were reverent to their Lord are driven to the Garden in throngs…"* (39:73) — the same verb of being driven/led, two opposite addresses. And the deepest irony is that the denier's mockery *required* the thing mocked: his whole life's posture — eat, drink, delay, for there is no reckoning — depended on the Fire not existing; every comfortable postponement leaned on the truth of the lie. Now the leaning post is pulled away, and the fall has a location. The Qur'an gives the deniers' own words at the location: *"If only we could return, we would be among the believers!"* — *"…and if only you could see the criminals lowering their heads before their Lord: Our Lord, we have seen and heard — so return us, we will work righteousness; indeed we are certain now"* (32:12).

**No Escort Back — the Finality the Verse Assumes**

The command's tone also carries the doctrine of finality: *anṭalīqū* has the sound of a gate opened behind a guest who has overstayed creation's hospitality. The Qur'an is unbending on this finality, against every sentimental hope of post-mortem second chances: *"And they will cry: O Mālik, let your Lord make an end of us! He will say: You are staying"* (43:77); *"…and the Day the Hour strikes, the criminals will swear they tarried but an hour…"* (30:55; cf. 10:45; 46:35); *"There is none in the heavens and the earth but that he comes to the Compassionate as a servant… and every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection, alone" (19:93–95)*; and the door-shutting verse: *"Until, when death comes to one of them, he says: My Lord, send me back…"* (23:99–100) — *"nay, it is but a word."* The tradition has always read these texts against the fringe theologies of return and reincarnation: the lease's term is the term (v. 22's *qadar maʿlūm*), the receptacle's vault opens only once (36:51), and the Day of Division is, precisely, *division*: after it, no re-division. The Prophet ﷺ was asked — as the ḥadīth of the questioning of the two graves shows (Muslim 2870) — and taught that the first night's questioning is the beginning of a state that has no appeal to a higher court, for there is none.

**The Verse as Mirror — What Do We Deny Daily?**

For the believer, verse 29 is not safely futuristic; it is a mirror with a warning: *what, in my life, do I functionally deny?* The category *mukadhdhib* is not reserved for the Makkan aristocracy; it is occupied by anyone whose *practice* contradicts his *profession* — who says "the prayer is obligatory" and abandons it; who says "the ḥarām is ḥarām" and traffics in it; who says "death is certain" and plans as an immortal. The Qur'an's word for this gap is not flattery: *"O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in God's sight that you say what you do not do"* (61:2–3). And the Prophet ﷺ named the trajectory: *"The servant commits a sin and says: it is not written against me…"* — rather his canonical warning: *"Beware of the minor sins, for they gather upon a man until they destroy him"* (Aḥmad, graded sound) — functional denial growing by increments until a man denies with his feet what he affirms with his tongue. Verse 29's command is therefore rehearsed, in miniature, whenever a believer postpones a known duty: he is walking, at that moment, *toward* the address of his own contradiction. The mercy of the sūrah is that — this side of the Day — the command can still be obeyed in reverse: turn *back* to what you have been functionally denying; meet it now, in the form of repentance, rather than then, in the form of address.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:30

> **Away to a threefold shadow,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Shadow with Three Columns**

Verse 30 continues the imperative begun at verse 29, and specifies the address: *anṭalīqū ilā ẓillin dhī shuʿab* — "Be off to a shadow of three branches." The word *ẓill* is the ordinary Arabic for shade — the Qur'an's near-technical term for comfort and protection from heat: *"and shade outstretched (ẓillin mamdūd)"* (56:30); *"…and He will admit them into the Garden, rivers flowing beneath, abiding therein"* (4:57). But this shadow is *dhī shuʿab* — possessing *shuʿab*, branches or forks: the word is used of a river's branching channels, a tree's boughs, a mountain's ridges. Hence the classical readings, gathered by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Zamakhsharī: (1) the shadow of the Fire's *smoke*, rising in branching columns — the reading most favored; (2) shadow cast in *three layers* or columns — whence the rendering "threefold"; (3) branching shadows like the layers of mountain ridges, or the interlaced canopies of a thick tree. Al-Zamakhsharī, with his geographer's eye, compares it to the *kathīb* — piled sand-ridges — or to the way thick smoke parts into plumes. The verse's terror is in the genre-violation: a *shadow* — creation's image of relief — that is itself the punishment.

**When Mercy's Very Form Becomes Torment**

The image deserves to be dwelt on, because it is one of the Qur'an's most refined theological ironies: the Fire offers the *form* of every mercy with the *substance* removed. Shade's form: present — protection from heat: none (the next verse will say so explicitly: *"no shade, nor avails against the flame"*). Water's form: present — boiling water and pus to drink (14:16–17; 18:29; 78:25); food's form: present — the tree of Zaqqūm (37:62–68; 44:43–48; 56:52); clothing's form: present — garments of fire and pitch (14:50). The doctrine beneath the aesthetics is exact: the Fire is the world's mercies *disenchanted* — every comfort stripped of its comfort-giving core — because the denier spent his life stripping the Giver from the gifts. He accepted the forms (eating, drinking, building) while denying the Former; so his eternity is given forms without favor. The Qur'an says of the Garden's opposite number that it is *"a fire whose fuel is people and stones"* (2:24; 66:6), and of its drink that it is boiling water *"that cuts their bowels"* (47:15). Modern readers know the structure from subtler experiences: wealth without peace, celebrity without rest, indulgence without satisfaction — forms of human goods stripped of their good; the Fire is that deprivation made total and permanent. Verse 30's three-branched shadow is thus not a bizarre fantasy but a *philosophy of punishment*: God gives the denier precisely what he chose — a world of means without the Merciful — rendered honestly at last.

**Smoke in Columns — the Qur'an's Fire-Physics**

The preferred classical reading — the Fire's smoke rising in branching pillars — coheres with the Qur'an's other Fire-descriptions, which consistently give it *structure*: it is *"a Blaze that strips the scalps (laẓā nazzāʿah li'l-shawā)"* (70:15–16); the Qur'an's Fire *almost speaks*: *"the Day We say to Hell: Are you filled? and it says: Is there more?"* (50:30); it has keepers (*"over it are angels harsh and strong"* — 66:6; Mālik its custodian, 43:77); gates (39:71; 15:44: *"it has seven gates, for each of them an appointed share"*) — a jurisdiction, a staff, an architecture. The branching shadow of v. 30 belongs to this architecture: the Fire is not chaos but *estate* — ordered, assigned, staffed — which is exactly what makes it justice rather than mishap. The three-branch image has also been read as the Fire's smoke *layering itself* like the branching canopies the desert Arab knew from the acacia — the only tree-shade he had — so that the denier, arriving, recognizes the shape of the beloved noon-tree and approaches it as relief, and it is *smoke*. Ibn Kathīr transmits this reading's pathos: the shade *invites* — that is what shades do in a desert world — and the invitation is the last deception.

**The Believer's Shade — the Same Word, the Other Address**

The final reflection the verse compels is the Qur'an's systematic contrast: *ẓill* is one of the Book's paired commodities — given to the righteous in reality, to the deniers in mockery: *"and their reward with their Lord: Gardens of Eden beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever — God well-pleased with them, and they well-pleased with Him"* (98:8) with *"…and He will admit them into the Garden" (4:122; and the shade-texts: 56:30, 76:11–14: "…and their recompense: a Garden and silk… reclining therein on couches… and shade… and fruit in plenty, neither cut nor forbidden")*; the Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to *seek* that shade now: *"Seven whom God will shade in His Shade on the Day when there is no shade but His Shade…"* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031, from Abū Hurayrah — the just ruler; the youth raised in worship; the man whose heart is attached to the mosque; the two who love for God's sake; the man who refuses the advances of a woman of rank and beauty, saying 'I fear God'; the one who gives charity secretly; the one who remembers God alone and weeps). Verse 30's shadow exists because that Shade exists: the Day *"when there is no shade but His"* — the verse's three-branched smoke is the counterfeit offered to those who declined the original; and the sūrah's next verse will remove even the counterfeit's last pretense.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:31

> **that provides no shade, nor avails against the flame.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Definition of a Failed Refuge**

Verse 31 completes the portrait begun at verse 30 with two crushing negations: *lā ẓallīn wa-lā yughnī min al-laḥab* — "no shade — and no avail against the flame." The Arabic is starker than translation shows: *ẓallīn* is the intensified participle of being shady — the shadow is *not shady*; it possesses none of the property that defines its kind. The second verb — *yughnī*, from *gh-n-y*, to enrich, to suffice, to avail — says the shadow *does not suffice* even as a screen against the *laḥab*, the open blaze. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Shawkānī relish the logic: it is a *shadow against fire*, and it fails at both functions — it neither cools (the essence of shade) nor shields (the essence of a wall). The image lands on a desert audience with special weight: in the Hijāz, shade is not comfort but survival; a traveler's life is measured from one patch of noon-shadow to the next. To be *in* shade that does not shade is to be denied the last category of relief the desert imagination contains — a refinement of severity the Qur'an states as a standing principle: *"So taste it — for We will not increase you in anything but punishment"* (78:30).

**The Anatomy of False Trust — the Verse as Parable**

The commentators read these two verses (30–31) not only as description but as *diagnosis*: the useless shadow is every false refuge a soul leans on when the heat arrives. The Qur'an's gallery of such refuges is long: wealth and children (*"Let not their wealth or their children amaze you; God only desires to punish them therewith in the life of this world…"* — 9:55, 85); patrons besides God (*"…their protectors are idols (*al-tāghūt*) bringing them out of the light into the darkness…"* — 2:257); intercession that cannot be bought (2:123–254: *"Beware of a Day when no soul shall avail another…"*); and the great summarizing verse: *"Say: Who will shield you by night and day from the Compassionate? No — they turn away from the remembrance of their Lord. Or have they gods besides Us who can defend them? They cannot help themselves, nor are they defended from Us"* (21:42–43). The useless shadow is thus, in this life, the *elite's* comfort — the layered protections the denier accumulates against consequence: legal, financial, social, psychological — each casting a shape on the ground, none holding back the fire of the appointed term. Every false refuge passes the *appearance* test (it is dark under it) and fails the *function* test (the heat comes through). The believer's entire strategy of refuge is the inverse: *"God is the Protector of those who believe; He brings them out of the darkness into the light"* (2:257) — refuge taken *in* the Creator, not in created shadows.

**The Last Psychology of Hell — Relief Offered and Withdrawn**

The Qur'an describes the deniers' Fire-psychology as exactly this cycle of approach and withdrawal: *"Every time they wish to get away from it out of anguish, they are driven back into it: taste the punishment of the burning!"* (22:22); *"…and if they cry for relief, they are relieved with water like molten brass that scalds the faces"* (18:29). Modern psychology would call this the abuse of the relief-reflex; the Qur'an presents it as the *precise* anatomy of their own life-choice: they spent their years on earth seeking relief in what could not relieve — status from mockery of the Prophet ﷺ, wealth from usury, immunity from alliance — so their eternity re-presents the curriculum at full intensity: seek relief in the shadow; the shadow is smoke; learn, forever, what false refuge is. The tragedy is that the lesson was available free of charge in the life before: every earthly false refuge eventually failed them in miniature — the patron died, the wealth devalued, the crowd dispersed — and each failure was a summons to transfer trust to the Only Sufficient (*al-Ghanī* — the same root *gh-n-y* as v. 31's *yughnī*): *"O mankind, you are the poor toward God, and God is the Rich, the Praiseworthy"* (35:15).

**Availing Against the Flame — the Believer's Counter-Portrait**

Against the shadow that does not avail, the Qur'an sets the Lord who *does*: the same verb-root (*aghnā* — to avail) is used in the Book's promise: *"And whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, and is a believer — those will enter the Garden, provided for therein without measure (bighayri ḥisāb)"* (40:40), and the Prophet's ﷺ beloved ḥadīth qudsī: *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor occurred to the heart of man"* (al-Bukhārī 7498; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah) — the sufficiency that *does* suffice. And the Qur'an's final contrast between the two shades is drawn across its pages: the righteous *"…their recompense is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein — and excellent is the reward of those who work"* (3:136); with the shade of the Garden *"outstretched"* (56:30) over against the shade of the Fire *"three-branched"* (77:30) — the one shadow *given long* because it is real mercy, the other *given branches* because it is only the *shape* of mercy. Between the two shadows runs the whole width of the sūrah's division: the Day sorts humanity not into hot and cold merely, but into *true refuge and false* — and verse 31's verdict on the false one is final: it neither cools nor shields; it is the destination of every trust misplaced.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:32

> **Indeed, it throws up sparks like massive tree trunks,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire That Throws**

Verse 32 shifts the camera from the Fire's shadow to the Fire's *body*, and the verb chosen is violent: *innahā tarmī* — "it hurls, casts, throws." The subject is the Fire (feminine in Arabic, *innahā*), and what it hurls is *sharar* — sparks, embers, molten fragments thrown off by a blaze. The Qur'an uses the same verb of the Divine slingers at the army of the elephant (*"striking them with stones of baked clay (sijjīl)"* — 105:4, *tarmīhim*), and its use here gives the Fire an aggressive agency: this is not fire as passive element but fire as *artillery*. The comparative is the verse's shock: the sparks are like *al-qaṣr* — a word whose two classical meanings Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Zamakhsharī both keep alive: (1) *castle, palace, fortress* — the qaṣr of a king; (2) the massive *trunk* of a great tree, or huge logs. Either reading yields the same scale: what the Fire throws off as mere *sparks* is the size of what men build as keeps or fell as giants — the pun on grandeur is deliberate and dark: the deniers spent their lives amassing *quṣūr* — palaces (*"And how many a garden and spring they left behind, and sown fields, and a noble station, and prosperity wherein they took delight — so We bequeathed it to another people"* — 44:25–28); their eternity is a place where palaces are the sparks.

**Scale Inversion — the Aesthetics of the Qur'an's Fire**

The verse belongs to a family of Qur'anic scale-inversions designed to overwhelm the imagination's calibration. In this world, sparks are the fire's smallest by-product; palaces are mankind's largest products. In the Fire, the relation inverts: by-products the size of products. The same inversion governs the Qur'an's other Fire-imagery: its *"Blaze that strips the scalps"* (70:15–16); its *"fuel is people and stones"* (2:24; 66:6) — the fire *fed* by what it punishes; its depth is measured by the Prophet's ﷺ own report: *"This fire of yours, which the son of Adam kindles, is one of seventy parts of the Fire of Hell"* — the Companions said: By God, it would have been sufficient, O Messenger of God! He said: *"It was given sixty-nine parts more than it"* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843, from Abū Hurayrah). Scale is the message: the instrument of divine justice is not sized to human experience but to divine offense. And the size is *pedagogical*: the Qur'an throughout ties the Fire's enormity to the enormity of what was denied: *"On the Day We will say to Hell: Are you filled? and it will say: Is there more?"* (50:30). A Day that divides must divide with room.

**Palaces as Sparks — the Requiem of Worldly Grandeur**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī both savor the second reading's irony (qaṣr = castle): the denier's biography is the story of building qaṣr — towers, treasuries, citadels of reputation — and the Fire answers biography with genre-parody: palaces appear again in his eternity, but as throw-weight. The Qur'an elsewhere memorializes the architects: Pharaoh *"…and he said: I am your lord most high"* and commanded: *"Hāmān, build for me a tower (ṣarḥ) that I may reach the cords of the heavens…"* (28:38; 40:36–37) — and his tower's rubble became the Red Sea's floor. The empires are dispatched in a single verse: *"Have they not traveled in the land and seen the end of those before them, who were mightier in strength?"* (30:9). And the archetype of all palace-builders, the slanderer-hoarder of Sūrah al-Humazah, is given the Fire's own name: *"He will be flung into the Crushing One (al-ḥuṭamah)… the Fire of God, kindled, which rises over the hearts"* (104:4–7). Verse 32's image thus completes the sūrah's running audit of Makkan prestige: the Qurashī elite's fortified houses (which the sūrah's audience could see from the hills) are promised an eternity where their own category of thing — *qaṣr* — flies as firebrands. As the Qur'an says of their worldly estate: *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world…"* (18:46), and of its end: *"So the foundation of their building was demolished — the roof collapsed over them from above them, and the punishment came upon them from where they did not perceive"* (16:26).

**Under the Sparks — the Verse's Use for the Living**

For the living reader, verse 32 functions as the Qur'an's fire-drill: it is meant to be *felt in the body* — the flinch of the imagination at a log-sized ember arcing toward one's face is the verse's intended payload. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believers to use exactly this felt imagination for prevention: the ḥadīth of the seventy parts (above), and his ﷺ warning that *"the Fire was made to burn for a thousand years until it reddened, then a thousand until it whitened, then a thousand until it blackened — so it is black as darkest night"* (Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī, graded sound) — an image that will govern the *next* verse's camels. The believer's regulated use of such imagery is the Prophet's ﷺ own: balance of hope and fear: *"By God, if you knew what I know, you would weep much and laugh little"* (al-Bukhārī 7313; Muslim 2359), and the opposite anchor: *"None of you should die except while hoping in God"* (Muslim 2877, from Jābir). Verse 32, held between those two anchors, does its work: it makes the Fire *memorable* — and a remembered fire is a fire fled from, in the only direction availing: the sūrah will mark that direction at verse 41.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:33

> **as though they were yellow camels.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Strangest Simile in the Sūrah**

Verse 33 completes the Fire-portrait with a comparison that has arrested every reader of the sūrah: the sparks are like *jimālatun ṣufr* — "yellow camels." The word *jimāl* is the plural of *jamal*, the classic Arabian camel — freight animal, wealth-unit, and the very emblem of the desert economy; *ṣufr* is the plural of *aṣfar*, yellow, with the significant Arabic nuance that blackish things bearing a yellow tinge are called *ṣafrāʾ* — as the tradition records of *khawl* horses and of she-camels whose black hides sheen yellow. The classical commentators divide, instructively, into two camps, which al-Zamakhsharī and al-Shawkānī report with care. Camp one takes the simile at face value: the sparks are huge and long and *yellow* — glowing embers arcing like a driven herd of tawny camels moving through the dark, a caravan-shaped conflagration. Camp two — the reading the sūrah's internal logic favors — takes *ṣufr* in its Arabic technicality: "black with a yellow sheen," so that the Fire's sparks are *black*: the Fire of Hell, in the narrations of the Prophet ﷺ, is black — *"…then a thousand years until it blackened — so it is black as darkest night"* (Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī, graded sound in meaning; al-Shawkānī cites it here); and al-Zamakhsharī explains that the Arabs call a blackish camel *ṣafrāʾ* because of the yellow glint in its black. On this reading, the verse teaches that Hell's fire has *none of fire's familiar color* — it is a black fire, whose sparks are black camels — a fire that has lost even the one beauty fire owns in this world: its light.

**Why Camels — the Simile's Desert Grammar**

To the first audience, the camel was the load-bearing fact of life: the unit of bride-wealth and ransom, the ship of the desert, the animal a man's survival was mortgaged to. A *herd* of camels was the largest moving mass of value an Arab knew — and its movement through dusk was among the most familiar sights of the caravan world: a long, swaying, tawny file against the last light. The simile's brilliance is to recruit the *most comfortable* large object in the audience's imagination — wealth on the hoof — and paint the Fire's debris with it: the sparks do not merely threaten; they *approach like property*, like the caravan coming home — the exact image the denier spent his life running toward (the returning trade caravan is the Qur'an's own emblem of Qurashī security: *"…for the journey of winter and summer…"* — 106:2). And camels *kick up* and *scatter* — a stampede is chaos wearing a familiar shape. Al-Shawkānī adds the aesthetic note: the sparks' *size and length* resemble camels' bodies; the motion resembles their gait; the color (either tawny-yellow or black-sheened) completes the portrait. The image is thus a full sensory trap: the denier's eye, trained all his life to greet the camel-file with joy, now finds the Fire's sparks arriving in exactly that shape — the last possession-shape, come to claim him.

**The Black Fire — Losing Even the Light**

The second reading's doctrine deserves its own meditation: fire, in this world, is ambivalent — it burns *and* it lights, warms *and* consumes; men love flame for its glow. The Qur'an's Hell systematically strips fire of every lovable property: it does not cool (*"…they will not taste therein coolness nor drink"* — 78:24); it does not light (it is black); it does not die out (*"…they will remain therein as long as the heavens and the earth endure, except as your Lord wills"* — 11:107; cf. 25:65: *"…a torment that clings"*); it does not kill (*"he will neither die therein nor live"* — 87:13). What is left of fire when warmth, light, and lethality-as-release are removed? Only the burning — sensation without remainder. This is the Qur'an's doctrine of Hell in one line: punishment is the properties of comfort subtracted from the forms of comfort — as the shadow of vv. 30–31 was shade that did not shade, so the fire of vv. 32–33 is flame that does not glow. The denier's eternity is the world he chose — means without the Merciful — rendered with total consistency.

**Beauty's Derivative Status — the Verse's Implicit Theology**

Finally, the simile teaches — by negation — a doctrine of beauty. Fire's *glow* in this world is not fire's property; it is God's gift *to* fire: *"God is the Light of the heavens and the earth — the likeness of His light as a niche wherein is a lamp…"* (24:35), and the Fire's blackness is the gift withdrawn. The same lesson governs the Qur'an's whole aesthetic: every beauty in creation is a loan (*"…and whatsoever blessing you have, it is from God"* — 16:53), and the Hereafter's two destinations are the loan's two closures: the Garden is the source opened (*"…their Lord gives them a pure drink"* — 76:21, and rivers of it); the Fire is the loan recalled from every form at once. For the living reader, verse 33's black camels are therefore a summons to *see* the loanedness of beauty now: the glow in every hearth, the sheen on every camel — or its modern equivalents, the humming city grid at night — is a rented radiance, and the rent is thanksgiving. The believer who thanks for the glow is promised the Garden's light (*"…their light running before them…"* — 57:12); the one who takes radiance as physics' property will find physics' fire, black, herd-shaped, and his.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:34

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fifth Toll — After the Fire's Portrait**

The refrain tolls for the fifth time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke seals the sūrah's first Fire-panel (vv. 29–34): the ironic departure (v. 29), the three-branched shadow (v. 30), the shade that does not shade (v. 31), the sparks like palaces (v. 32), the camels of black-yellow fire (v. 33). Al-Shawkānī, reading this occurrence contextually, connects it to the panel's specific claims: the woe falls on those who denied *such attributes of the Fire* — who heard the descriptions and said, in effect, *"the accounts are but ancient fables"* (83:13's echo: *"stories of the ancients"*). It is one thing to disbelieve an abstraction — "punishment"; it is another to be shown the punishment's texture in five unforgettable images and still file it under myth. The fifth toll thus marks the exhaustion of a particular excuse: after vv. 29–33, no hearer can claim the Fire was left vague.

**Why the Qur'an Describes What It Describes**

The panel just sealed raises a question the sensitive reader must face: why does the Book of Mercy dwell so vividly on the Fire — smoke-columns, sparks, camels? The Qur'an answers with its own stated method: *"And We cite for mankind in this Quran every kind of likeness (mathal), that perhaps they may reflect"* (39:27); and the Prophet's ﷺ warnings were the physician's candor, evidenced in his ﷺ practice of reciting the Fire-passages aloud till the assembly wept, and in his ﷺ own weeping — ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Shikhkhīr relates: *"I saw the Messenger of God ﷺ praying, and a sound came from his breast like the sound of a boiling pot, from his weeping"* (Abū Dāwūd 904; al-Nasāʾī) — and in his ﷺ word: *"If you knew what I know, you would weep much and laugh little"* (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). The proportion is not morbidity but triage: the Fire is the one danger from which no insurance exists except prior repentance; a Book that softened its portrait would be a physician softening a diagnosis. The classical scholars add: the descriptions are also *revelatory of God* — His justice is a perfection, and the Fire is where His perfection of justice is displayed without the dilutions of time: *"…We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls surround them…"* (18:29).

**The Reader's Position — Five Refrains In**

Structurally, the fifth toll is the sūrah's exact center of gravity: five refrains have fallen (vv. 15, 19, 24, 28, 34), and five remain (vv. 37, 40, 45, 47, 49). The sūrah's two halves now mirror each other with terrible symmetry. First half: promise of the Day, cosmic signs, three courts of evidence (history, body, earth), Fire-panel — five woes. Second half: the courtroom's silence (vv. 35–37), the gathering and the challenge (vv. 38–40), the Garden's contrast (vv. 41–45), the respite's irony (vv. 46–47), the refusal to bow (vv. 48–49) — five woes. The refrain thus binds the whole into a diptych: *woe framing woe* — judgment's verdicts on both sides of the sūrah's central panel, which begins immediately: the Day of Division's arraignment (v. 38), the silencing of the defense (vv. 35–36), the challenge to scheme (v. 39). The reader who has counted the tolls knows the count is not finished — which is itself the sūrah's message: mercy has not yet closed; each remaining toll (vv. 37, 40, 45, 47, 49) is a further invitation in the form of a further warning, for *"God does not love the wrongdoers"* but *"He is the Forgiving, the Merciful"* (the twin names of 2:173's manner — 2:173: *"…surely God is Forgiving, Merciful"*).

**The Woe's Addressee — Still Within Earshot**

One pastoral point, easily lost at the fifth repetition: as long as the refrain is *heard*, its addressees can still leave the address. The sūrah's deniers heard the warnings while alive — and the Qur'an's whole project is that the *hearing* precedes and can forestall the *being*. The Prophet ﷺ grieved over exactly this dynamic: he stood on the heights of Makkah calling the Quraysh clans by name to warn them, and was answered by Abū Lahab's *"Tabban lak!"* — "perished be you!" (the occasion of Sūrah al-Masad, 111:1); and at his uncle's deathbed he pled: *"Say: there is no god but God — a word by which I will argue for you before God"* — and Abū Ṭālib declined, on tribal loyalty (al-Bukhārī 1360; Muslim 24, from Abū Hurayrah, in the longer report of the deathbed). The refrains are the sūrah-format of that deathbed invitation: *leave the category while the category can still be left.* For the believer, the fifth toll is thus both solemnity and spur — solemnity at the portrait now complete, and spur to the work the portrait exists for: the Prophet's ﷺ own mission-title, *"a clear warner"* (7:184), now vested in everyone who recites the sūrah onward.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:35

> **That is the Day on which they speak not,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Great Silencing**

The declaration must be heard against the soundscape the sūrah has already built: oaths, questions, refrains, commands — God's speech flowing in torrents through the sūrah's fifty verses — and then, at the arraignment, the defendant's channel goes dead. Al-Ṭabarī sets verse 35 beside the Day's other speech-regulations: some verses state that deniers *will* speak (confessions, excuses attempted, *"our Lord, we have seen and heard"* — 32:12; the mutual blaming of 34:31–33; *"By God, our Lord, we were not idolaters"* — 6:23), while this verse states they do not. The classical resolution: the Day is long — *"a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years"* (70:4) — and its phases differ: at the phase of judgment and evidence, the deniers are silenced; at phases of confession, their tongues are unlocked against their will. The Qur'an itself maps the sequence: *"On the Day their tongues and their hands and their feet bear witness against what they used to do"* (24:24) — evidence first; and the plea-stage closed: *"On that Day the evildoers' excuse will not profit them, nor will they be allowed to make amends"* (30:57).

**The Body Testifies — When the Mouth Is Sealed**

The silencing has a counterpart that is among the Qur'an's most haunting doctrines: the transfer of testimony from the will to the members. The verses are explicit and cumulative: *"This Day We seal their mouths, and their hands speak to Us, and their feet bear witness to what they used to earn"* (36:65); *"…until, when they reach it, their hearing and their eyes and their skins bear witness against them of what they used to do. And they will say to their skins: Why did you bear witness against us? They will say: God, who gives speech to all things, has made us speak"* (41:20–21); *"and the earth's tidings (akhbārahā)"* (99:4–5); *"and the Book is set… it leaves nothing small or great but it has counted it"* (18:49). The theological logic is stringent: justice requires testimony uncorrupted by self-interest; the denier's *own* testimony about himself is the least reliable evidence in the court — he has spent a lifetime editing it; so the Day calls witnesses whose testimony he cannot coach: his own limbs, his own organs, his own ground. The Prophet ﷺ affirmed the doctrine in the well-attested report from Abū Barzah: *"The feet of the son of Adam will not move from his station on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about…"* — about his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he wore it, his wealth and whence he took it and where he put it, and his knowledge and what he did with it (al-Tirmidhī 2417, graded ṣaḥīḥ). The interrogation was always coming; verse 35 only announces that at its center, the defendant's glibness — his lifelong advantage — will not be in the room.

**A World Run by Talk — and Its End**

For the modern reader, verse 35 lands on a civilization more saturated with speech than any in history: broadcast, podcast, feed, comment, stream — an ocean of utterance in which reputations are made, doctrines fought, and truth negotiated by volume. The verse's quiet claim is that all of it is *loaned*: the tongue itself is a leased instrument, and the Lease includes a repossession clause. The Qur'an's warning to every era's talkers: *"O you who believe, let not a people mock another people…"* (49:11) and: *"Not a word he utters but that a watcher is present with him"* (50:18) — the watcher, according to the commentators, being the recording angel, whose ledger of the denier's every word is precisely the transcript that will out-speak his silenced mouth. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the reports of al-Ṭabarī, notes the symmetry: the deniers spent their breath on denying; their breath's ledger is complete; on the Day, the breath is stopped and the ledger read. Al-Qurṭubī draws the practical lesson that Muslim jurists made proverbial: *the silence of the court is the terror of the guilty* — the believer should cultivate now the accounting of speech that the Day will enforce anyway: the Prophet's ﷺ triad: *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47, from Abū Hurayrah), and his ﷺ warning on the word's weight: a man *"may utter a word… by which he falls into the Fire farther than the east is from the west"* (al-Bukhārī 6477).

**The Right to Speak — Rescinded for Some, Granted to Others**

Finally, verse 35's negation has a positive counterpart in the Qur'an's court: speech is not abolished on the Day — it is *redistributed*. The deniers are silenced; the witnesses speak; and — the Qur'an's most tender reversal — the believers are given their say: the Prophet ﷺ intercedes (the long intercession-ḥadīths of al-Bukhārī 7440 and Muslim 193, from Anas and Abū Hurayrah); the martyrs live and are *"provided for"* with their Lord, *"rejoicing"* (3:169–170); and the believing confession carries: *"Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us…"* (3:16). Above all, God Himself is the Speaker of the Day — *"the Day the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks; they speak not except for one whom the Compassionate permits, and who speaks aright"* (78:38): the one absolute freedom of speech is God's, delegated by permission alone. Verse 35's courtroom is thus, in the end, a theater of one Voice: the Voice that said *"Be"* at creation conducts the trial of every other voice. For the believer, the sane ambition is to be found, when the redistribution comes, among those licensed to speak — *"a good word"* (35:10, ascending to God) being the currency now that buys the license then.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:36

> **nor is permission granted them, such that they might offer excuses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Leave to Plead**

Verse 36 tightens verse 35's silencing into a procedural ruling: *wa-lā yuʾdhanu lahum fa-yaʿtadhirūn* — "nor is permission granted them, that they might offer excuses." The vocabulary is the vocabulary of the assembly: *adhn* — permission, leave to speak (the same root that names the muezzin, the *muʾdhdhin*, the one *licensed* to make the public call); *iʿtidhār* — the formal presentation of an excuse, the plea a defendant brings to mitigate or deny. Al-Qurṭubī records the grammatical crux with his usual clarity: the verse can be read (a) "permission is not granted them, *so that they might excuse themselves*" — i.e., the court refuses the plea-stage outright; or (b), with the negation carried over the second verb as well — "…and they do not excuse themselves" — i.e., the deniers, for once, *find nothing to say*: the excuses do not come, because the evidence has removed their raw material. Both readings converge on the same theology: on the Day of Division, the denier's plea — his lifelong specialty — is unavailable, whether barred by the court or bankrupted by the facts.

**The Theology of the Blocked Plea — Against Every Bargain Religion**

The doctrine here is one of the Qur'an's most severe, and it stands against a near-universal instinct of religious history: the instinct to negotiate. Every age has produced its bargain-systems — indulgences, patron gods who can be flattered, rites that purchase exemption, mediators who can be paid — and the Qur'an forecloses all of them in principle: *"Beware of a Day when no soul shall avail another at all, nor shall intercession be accepted from it, nor shall ransom be taken from it, nor shall they be helped"* (2:48; the fuller litany at 2:123); *"…this Day no ransom shall be taken from you, nor from those who disbelieved; your refuge is the Fire — it is your protector (mawlākum), and an evil destination"* (57:15); and: *"the wrongdoers will have no protector and no intercessor to be obeyed"* (40:18). Verse 36's contribution to the foreclosure is procedural: it bars not merely the *invalid* plea but the *plea-stage* itself — for the denier, that is; for the intercession the Prophet ﷺ performs on the Day belongs to a different class entirely: sanctioned intercession, by God's leave, for those who *affirmed* — *"they speak not except for one whom the Compassionate permits, and who speaks aright"* (78:38; the Prophet's ﷺ stations of intercession in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections). The line between the barred plea and the granted intercession is faith: the denier's mouth was closed *because* his heart had closed it first — he had heard the invitation a thousand times with the tongue and refused it a thousand times with the will.

**The Excuseless State — Why Total Manifestation Ends Argument**

Al-Qurṭubī offers the metaphysical ground of the ruling: *the deniers offer no excuses because the reality of their deeds is completely manifest* — the Arabic term is *inkishāf*, the unveiling. In this world, argument is possible because reality is *veiled*: evidence can be doubted, motives hidden, facts interpreted — the trial of faith is run precisely under conditions of ambiguity: God *"created death and life to try you as to which of you is finest in deed"* (67:2; cf. 18:7; 76:2). The veil is the examination hall's door; questioning is only meaningful while the exam runs. On the Day, the veil lifts: *"the Day hidden things are tested"* (86:9); *"…and We bring forth for him a book he finds spread open"* — *"Read your book — your soul suffices this Day as a reckoner against you"* (17:13–14). Under total manifestation, the plea dies of irrelevance: one does not *argue* with what stands revealed — the criminal in the vault with the camera footage, the liar before the recording of his lie; the mouth may move, but there is nothing for it to say. The Qur'an names this decisive register itself: *"it is a decisive word (qawl faṣl), and not a joke"* (86:13–14).

**Excuses — Their Valid and Invalid Forms in This Life**

Because the plea-stage closes, the Qur'an moves the entire plea-docket to *this* life — and its doctrine of the *valid* excuse deserves exactness. There are excuses God accepts: ignorance before knowledge (*"We never punish until We send a messenger"* — 17:15); coercion under compulsion (*"…except one who is compelled while his heart is at rest in faith"* — 16:106); and the excused categories of sleep and forgetfulness established by the Prophet's ﷺ ruling: *"Whoever forgets a prayer, let him pray it when he remembers"* (al-Bukhārī 597; Muslim 684). And there are excuses God *rejects* — the entire genre the Qur'an catalogues: *"Our Lord, we obeyed our masters and our great ones, and they led us astray from the way"* (33:67) — answered with: *"Taste the punishment…"*; the ancestors' excuse — *"we found our fathers upon a community"* — answered with the Qur'an's counter-question: *"…even if their fathers knew nothing and were not guided?"* (2:170; 5:104); and Satan's own plea on the Day: *"God promised you the truth… I promised you and failed you; I had no authority over you except that I called you and you answered me — so blame me not, but blame yourselves"* (14:22) — and it is *not accepted.* The valid excuse is bounded by two conditions the tradition states plainly: sincerity — *"Repentance with God is only for those who do evil in ignorance and then repent soon after"* (4:17) — and timing: before the soul reaches the throat (*"…until, when death comes to one of them, he says: My Lord, send me back"* — 23:99–100: refused). Verse 36's courtroom is therefore the *end of the filing period* — and the sūrah's whole design is to serve the reader his summons while the clerk's window is still open.

**The Quiet of the Guilty — a Human Analogy**

Every human being has seen the truth of verse 36 in miniature: the child with the crumbs on his face, silent; the official at the inquiry, suddenly concise; the betrayer confronted with the letters he wrote, *reading them* rather than contesting them. Manifestation mutes. The Qur'an's claim is that this universal experience — the excuseless hush when the fact stands in the room — is a *foretaste*, installed in every conscience, of a Day when the facts stand unveiled in full. The believer's discipline follows: live now as one who expects the unveiling — *"…whether you manifest what is in yourselves or hide it, God will bring you to account for it"* (2:284) — and the hush of that Day will hold no terror, for the one whose deeds are pardoned has no need of a plea; his file was settled at a smaller court called *tawbah*, whose judge, the tradition testifies from the Prophet ﷺ, extends His hand day and night to receive repentance: *"God extends His hand at night to accept the repentance of the day's sinner, and by day to accept the repentance of the night's sinner, until the sun rises from its setting"* (Muslim 2759, from Abū Mūsā) — the court that closes only at the throat.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:37

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sixth Toll — the Woe of the Silenced**

The refrain tolls for the sixth time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke seals the silencing-panel (vv. 35–36): the confiscated tongue, the refused plea-stage. Al-Nasafī and al-Shawkānī read this occurrence as aimed at the deniers of *this Day* — the deniers whose career of speech now reaches its audit: every *"fables of the ancients,"* every council of mockery (*"…the criminals whisper together: Is this anything but a mortal like you?"* — 21:3), every demolition-of-argument by volume — all of it ends in a courtroom where the channel is closed. There is an exact and chilling symmetry the toll underlines: they silenced the truth with their speech; the Day silences their speech with the truth. They *"strove to extinguish the light of God"* (9:32; 61:8) — and the Fire's own blackness (v. 33 above) is the extinguishing returned.

**Silence Now, Silence Then — Two Kinds Contrasted**

The sixth toll invites the reader to consider the *kind* of silence that prevails on the Day, because scripture and human experience know several: the silence of awe (the servant before the King), the silence of peace (the prayer's stillness), the silence of shame (the guilty child), and the silence of despair (the defendant who knows). The Day's silence for the deniers is the last two fused: shame without appeal, and despair without remedy. The Qur'an's word-portraits of it are among its most restrained — and therefore most terrible: *"And the faces will be humbled before the Living, the Enduring; and whoever carries wrongdoing will fail"* (20:111); *"the Day every soul will find what it did of good present…"* (3:30); and the silence itself: *"…the Day the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks; they speak not except for one whom the Compassionate permits…"* (78:38) — and the bell of the courtroom is God's own question, which requires no answer because the answer stands present: *"This is the Fire you used to belie"* (52:14). The denier's eternity begins with his own quote read back to him — the lifelong denial, cited as caption to his address.

**The Believer's Counter-Silence — Cultivated Before the Day**

Against the imposed silence of the guilty, the tradition sets a *chosen* silence of the reverent — and the sixth toll is its occasion. The Prophet ﷺ made silence, rightly governed, a mark of the believer: *"…let him speak good or remain silent"* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47); and his ﷺ own speech in assembly was measured — ʿĀʾishah described him as not speaking as ordinary men speak, idly and at length, but with words few and counted (the sense of the transmitted descriptions in al-Tirmidhī and Aḥmad). The spiritual masters read verses 35–37 as the Day's verdict on the tongue's entire economy: every word of the denier's denial was *spent speech* — paid out from a finite account — and the Day's silence is simply the account's closing balance: zero. The believer's economy of speech is the inverse investment: the *dhikr* — remembrance — spoken now is *"the enduring good deeds"* (*al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* — 18:46), and the Qur'an promises it a speaking-role later: the good word *"ascends to Him"* (35:10), and the garden-dwellers' last word is the greeting of peace (*"…their call therein: 'Glory to You, O God'…"* — 10:10). The contrast is total: speech denied to deniers; speech given, purified, to the reverent — *"they speak not except…"* (78:38's permission) becoming *"and their final call: 'Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds'"* (10:10).

**Halfway Through the Refrains — the Sūrah's Own Patience**

At the sixth toll, one more structural observation completes the reader's map: the sūrah has now divided its ten woes into six-and-four, with the remaining four (vv. 40, 45, 47, 49) attached respectively to the challenge to schemers, the reward of the reverent, the respite's irony, and the refusal to bow. The pattern is increasingly pointed: each woe is falling *closer to home* — from cosmic signs (v. 15) to history (v. 19) to their own bodies (v. 24) to their own food and water (v. 28) to the Fire's texture (v. 34) to their own tongues (v. 37) — and the remaining four will arrive at the nearest things of all: their schemes, their neighbors' rewards, their leisure, and their prayer-refusal. The sūrah is executing a siege of the denier's whole world, concentric ring by concentric ring — and the refrain's patient repetition is the sound of the siege-works advancing. For the hearer with any heart left, the tolls function as the Qur'an says its warnings function: *"…and We have diversified in it the threats, that they may be reverent — or that it beget in them remembrance"* (20:113) — the very strategy of the ten refrains named by the Qur'an itself: diversified warning, aimed at reverence, not at despair.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:38

> **This is the Day of Division; We have gathered you and those of old.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name Spoken Over the Assembled**

Verse 38 is the courtroom's opening gavel: *hādhā yawmu al-faṣl* — "This is the Day of Division" — the name given at verse 13, now pronounced over the assembly itself; and with it the announcement of the gathering: *jamaʿnākum wa'l-awwalīn* — "We have gathered you and those of old." The sūrah's earlier panel (vv. 16–17) had established the two generations — the ancients destroyed, the later ones following — and verse 38 stages their reunion: all history's files convened on one floor, the case of *humanity v. its Lord's signs* argued in a single session. The Qur'an's other gathering-texts give the venue's dimensions: *"the Day the earth is replaced by another earth, and the heavens likewise, and they come forth before God, the One, the Prevailing"* (14:48); *"…and you see every nation kneeling (jāthiyah); every nation is summoned to its book: 'This Day you are repaid for what you used to do'"* (45:28); and the plainest: *"Say: The first and the last are gathered for the appointment of a known Day"* (56:49–50). The sūrah's own second naming (v. 38 = v. 13's term re-pronounced) marks the Day's arrival with its own definition: whatever else that Day is, it is *division* — the sorting now visible.

**The End of the Series — Every Generation Present**

The gathering's psychological weight falls on the denier's own deepest assumption: that his generation is the frame of reality. Every denier argues from the present tense — *we* are here, *our* chiefs say, *no one* has seen a resurrection — as though the dead were unreal because absent. Verse 38 corrects the ontology: the dead are *queued*, not cancelled; "those of old" are summoned back to the floor alongside the latecomers, and the debate resumes with all parties present. The Qur'an relishes the scene: Pharaoh's people exposed to the Fire *"morning and evening"* and, when the Hour rises, *"Admit the family of Pharaoh into the severest punishment"* (40:46); the two garden-owning friends of Sūrah al-Kahf discovering which of them was poor indeed (18:32–44); and the great concourse-text: *"And the Trumpet is blown, and behold, from the graves they come out to their Lord, scurrying — 'Woe to us! Who has raised us from our resting place? This is what the Compassionate promised, and the messengers spoke truth!'"* (36:51–52). Note the last clause: the deniers' first coherent utterance on the rising (in the phase where utterance is given, per v. 35's discussion) is a *concession*: the messengers spoke truth. The debate that ran for millennia on earth is settled, in the event, in a sentence — and every excuse-industry of history, from the Makkan council to the modern seminar, discovers its status at once: *"they will say: our Lord, we obeyed our masters and our great ones…"* (33:67) — refused.

**The One Session — Divine Case Management**

There is also a doctrine of *efficiency* in the gathering that the commentators find worthy of note: God judges the whole species in one Day, one floor, one session. Human courts manage one defendant per courtroom per season; the Day of Division manages every defendant who ever lived — concurrently — with total accuracy: *"And We set the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul is wronged at all — even the weight of a mustard seed, We bring it; and We suffice as reckoners"* (21:47). The Day's length is its own sign: *"a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years"* (70:4) — and the Prophet ﷺ taught that for the believer it will be made light: among his ﷺ reports, the believers' reckoning will be made swift and easy by God's mercy, while the deniers' account runs its full and terrible length; and he ﷺ wept when revelation taught him its realities, as ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Shikhkhīr saw him weeping in prayer until his breast sounded like a boiling pot (Abū Dāwūd 904; al-Nasāʾī). The paradox the tradition holds: a Day of fifty thousand years (70:4), and for the believer relief near-instantaneous by God's mercy — the intercession-ḥadīths of al-Bukhārī 7440 and Muslim 193 describe the Prophet's ﷺ successive pleadings until his Lord grants release to those in whom there is any good. Ibn ʿAbbās's reported reflections on *yawm al-faṣl* (in al-Ṭabarī) dwell on the division's completeness: truth from falsehood, believer from denier, deed from claim — even, at the sorting of the Book, line from line.

**The Gathering as Reunion — the Believer's Side of Verse 38**

For the reverent, verse 38's gathering has a second face: the family reunion under a throne of mercy. The Qur'an names the reunion explicitly: *"…and those who believe and whose seed follow them in faith — We join their seed with them, and We diminish them nothing of their deeds; every man is pledged for what he earned"* (52:21); and the Garden's social architecture: *"Gardens of Eden, which they enter, and whoever was upright of their fathers and their spouses and their seed; and the angels enter upon them from every gate: 'Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured; and how excellent is the outcome of the abode!'"* (13:23–24). And the Prophet ﷺ promised the bereaved their children again: the young children of the believers are gathered and tended at the Garden's gate until their parents arrive (the report of Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah, on the children of the believers). The same assembly that is the deniers' arraignment is the believers' homecoming — the verse's pronoun *"you"* gathers both, and the *faṣl* of the Day is precisely the sorting of the gathered into these two experiences of the same summons. Verse 38 is thus a hinge: the courtroom is seated; the next verse will hear the Judge's astonishing challenge to the condemned.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:39

> **So if you have a scheme, then scheme against Me!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Judge's Challenge to the Condemned**

Verse 39 is among the most defiant sentences in scripture: *fa-in kāna lakum kaytun fa-kīdūn* — "So if you have a scheme, then scheme against Me!" The word *kayd* is cunning-craft: not brute force but stratagem, the covert plan, the long game — the very art the deniers had practiced on the Prophet ﷺ, for the Qur'an calls it by name: *"And those who disbelieved schemed against you, to seize you or kill you or expel you; they schemed, and God schemed, and God is the best of schemers"* (8:30; cf. 3:54; 10:21; 13:42 — *kayd* and *makr* sharing the field). The imperatives *fa-kīdūn* — "scheme against *Me*" — with the emphatic suffix, issue the courtroom's final, impossible dare to the defendants: produce, now or ever, any plan that touches the Judge. The verse belongs to a Qur'anic genre the scholars call *tahaddī* — the challenge: the literary challenge (*"bring a sūrah like it"* — 2:23; 10:38; 11:13); the resurrection-challenge (*"…then bring it back, if you are truthful"* — 56:87); and the escape-challenge, of which our verse is the eschatological climax: *"O assembly of jinn and men, if you are able to pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, then pass! You shall not pass except by an authority (sulṭān)"* (55:33); *"…let him stretch a rope to the sky, then let him sever it and see if his scheming removes that which enrages"* (22:15).

**The Anatomy of Kayd — What the Deniers' Scheme Actually Was**

The challenge lands because *kayd* was the deniers' documented profession. The Qur'an catalogs the plots against the Prophet ﷺ: the council of *Dār al-Nadwah* that resolved to assassinate him on a chosen night, one youth from every clan (the sīrah's record, sealed by 8:30's verdict above); the bribe offers — wealth, kingship, medical cure, marriage-alliance — carried by the Quraysh's delegations to Abū Ṭālib; the mockery-campaigns (*"they say: 'a poet…'"* — 52:30; 69:41); the economic strangulation of the boycott in Shiʿb Abī Ṭālib. Every instrument was kayd — and every instrument failed, culminating in the Hijrah's escape from the assassination-night itself. Verse 39 universalizes the record: all plotting against God's plan — Pharaoh's infanticide against Moses, the crucifixion-plot against Jesus (*"…and they schemed, and God schemed, and God is the best of schemers"* — 3:54), the coalition of the trenches at al-Aḥzāb (*"And God repelled the disbelievers in their rage; they gained no good"* — 33:25) — belongs to one genre: the finite plot against the Infinite Plotter. The genre's every entry ends the same way, which is why the Day's challenge is not a fight-offer but an *irony*: the courtroom is already in session; the defendants are already sentenced; and still the Judge invites them — as the last exhibition of the case — to try.

**Why an Omnipotent Judge Uses Irony — the Rhetoric of Disproportion**

Sensitive readers ask whether verse 39's tone is unworthy of God — and the tradition's answer is that *irony is precisely the fitting register* when omnipotence confronts rebellion: the vastness of the disproportion is itself the content. The Qur'an uses the register elsewhere: *"…and do not strut the earth exultantly — you will never rend the earth, nor reach the mountains in height"* (17:37); and supremely: *"And they schemed their scheme, and their scheme is with God, though their scheme were one whereby the mountains should be moved"* (14:46 — the verse that best glosses our own). Divine irony is not the mockery forbidden between believers (49:11); it is the *rhetorical display of disproportion* — the way a mountain's shadow makes a man's fist look like a child's. Al-Rāzī, in his grand manner, reads verse 39 as a standing proof: the challenge was published to the arch-deniers and to every generation after, and the scheme-space is empty — there is nothing to scheme *with*: no lever moves the Judge; no information surprises Him; no coalition outnumbers Him; no exit exists from His floor (*"…and to Him is the return of you all — God's promise in truth"* — 10:4; and 55:33's "no passing except by authority" already cited).

**The Believer's Use of the Verse — Scheming Against Sin, Not God**

For the believer, verse 39 converts into a two-edged discipline. First, *surrender of counter-schemes*: the believer does not answer the plots against him with plots against fate, but with the Prophet's ﷺ method — *hijrah*, patience, trust, and lawful work — because the Plotter's promise covers the plotter's victims: *"do not grieve; God is with us"* (9:40); *"patience is beautiful"* (12:18); and the final verdict on every Yusuf-story of betrayal: *"they schemed their scheme, and God's scheme overcame"* — the sense of 3:54 and 8:30 again. Second, *inversion of the verb*: the one kayd that profits is schemed against one's own sins — the Prophet ﷺ taught the strategist's creed applied to the soul: *"The strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak believer, though in each is good; be avid for what benefits you…"* (Muslim 2664, from Abū Hurayrah) — and the tradition's counsel: plan your prayer as you plan your trade; ambush your habits of sin as generals ambush — because the soul's allies scheme too, and *"the devil's scheming was ever weak"* (4:76). The sūrah's Judge closes the case with a dare; the wise defendant — this side of the Day — answers the dare by changing sides while sides may still be changed: for the One who says *"scheme against Me"* also says, in the same Book: *"And God calls to the abode of peace…"* (10:25).

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:40

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seventh Toll — After the Challenge**

The refrain tolls for the seventh time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke seals the challenge-panel: the gathering (v. 38) and the dare — *"scheme against Me!"* (v. 39). Al-Nasafī and al-Shawkānī read this occurrence contextually: the woe falls on those who deny God's *power* — those whose scheming against the truth presumed, in the end, that the Judge could be out-plotted. There is a precise logic in the toll's placement. Every scheme against God's religion rests on a hidden premise: that God is either absent, indifferent, or weak — a third party to be maneuvered around. The challenge of verse 39 exposed the premise; the woe of verse 40 prices it. The Qur'an names the premise explicitly in the schemers' creed: *"And they say: There is nothing but our life in this world, and we shall not be raised"* (6:29; 23:37; 45:24; cf. 44:34–36) — and the Fire's first caption, quoted repeatedly in the Book, is the creed's refutation: *"This is the Fire you used to belie"* (52:14).

**The Empty War-Room — What Scheming Against God Amounted To**

The verse invites the reader to total up, as it were, the world's ledger of anti-God strategy — the archive verse 39's dare makes us review. Pharaoh mobilized infanticide against a prophet's generation and lost the prophet to his own household's river-float (20:39; 28:7–9). The Quraysh coordinated assassination and were beaten by a handful of spiders' webs and a pursuer's refund of the deposit — the sīrah's record of the Hijrah night, and the Qur'an's own summary: *"And We appointed for them…"* — precisely 8:30, already cited: *"they schemed, and God schemed, and God is the best of schemers."* Abrahah marched with the elephant against the Kaʿbah and was stopped by birds with clay-stones — the sūrah (105) that gives Makkah its founding anti-siege memory. In every entry, the pattern's two constants: the scheme is *clever by human measure*, and the counter-measure is *contemptibly small* — a spider's web (29:41), a bird's stone, a borrowed grave's rumor. The Qur'an explains the disproportion's cause: the schemers war on the One who holds their own planning-faculty in loan: *"…they forgot God, and He made them forget themselves"* (59:19) — the schemer's first loss is his own map. And the Hereafter's woe (v. 40) is the ledger's final entry: the war-room's reunion, the generals gathered (v. 38), the maps still in hand — and the territory gone: *"…their works are like ashes the wind scatters on a stormy day; they hold nothing of what they earned — that is the far error"* (14:18).

**Two Schemes and One Ending — the Qur'an's Sowing Parable**

The seventh toll closes with a reflection the Qur'an itself attaches to the schemers' end: their plots, so elaborate in the building, harvest as *nothing* — and the Qur'an gives the agrarian reason in one of its most precise parables: *"The likeness of those who disbelieve in their Lord: their works are like ashes…"* (14:18, above) and the twin: *"…as a mirage in a lowland: the thirsty man thinks it water, until he reaches it and finds it nothing — and finds God there, and He pays him his account in full; and God is swift in reckoning"* (24:39). The mirage-image deserves the believer's meditative attention because it is the schemer's inner biography: the plot *looks like water* — it promises control, victory, immunity — and the schemer organizes decades around a shine on the horizon of events; and the Day arrives, and he reaches the shine, and the shine is empty — *"and finds God there."* Every anti-divine project is a thirst-architecture over a mirage; and the find at the mirage's site is not water but the Judge. Verse 40's woe is thus the receipt issued for verse 39's dare — declined; and the receipt reads: *ruin, that Day, to the deniers.*

**Mid-Sūrah Compass — the Reader's Position**

Structurally, the seventh toll completes the sūrah's entire judgment-movement: the Fire (vv. 29–34), the silence (vv. 35–37), the gathering and challenge (vv. 38–40) — all seven refrains so far (vv. 15–40) have fallen on the deniers' side of the ledger. Now the sūrah pivots — and the pivot is one of the Qur'an's most effective dramatic devices: after seven tolls of woe over one company, the camera cuts, without transition, to the other company. The next five verses (vv. 41–45) are the reverent's panel — shade, springs, fruits, a royal summons to eat and drink, the investiture of the virtuous — and only after that glimpse does the sūrah return to the deniers for its final three tolls (vv. 47, 49) and its closing question (v. 50). The cut itself teaches: on the Day of Division, the two companies' experiences will be *simultaneous and adjacent* — the smoke's shadow and the outstretched shade; the speechless mouth and the bidden feast — as the Qur'an says in its starkest juxtaposition: *"…a party in the Garden, and a party in the Blaze"* (42:7). The reader is thereby made to *choose a camera* — the heart's eye will dwell where it has invested; and the sūrah, having dwelt seven tolls in the smoke, now lifts the reader's chin toward the springs.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:41

> **Truly the reverent shall be amid shade and springs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Camera Cuts to the Other Company**

Verse 41 answers seven tolls of woe with one word of contrast: *inna* — "truly" — *al-muttaqīna fī ẓilālin wa-ʿuyūn* — "the reverent are amid shades and springs." The subject is *al-muttaqūn* — the reverent, the God-wary — the Qur'an's great honorific from *taqwā*, the protective mindfulness the sūrah's sister-oaths have been teaching all along (cf. 2:2's commentary: the driver on the dangerous road, aware and therefore safe). The two rewards are chosen with surgical opposition to the deniers' panel just closed: *ẓilāl* — shades, plural upon plural — against the one three-branched shadow that did not shade (vv. 30–31); *ʿuyūn* — springs, flowing sources — against the Fire's heat that no shadow availed against. The Qur'an's twin-texts confirm the deliberate pairing: *"Surely the reverent are amid gardens and springs"* (15:45 — nearly identical); and the two convoy-verbs of Sūrah al-Zumar: the deniers *"driven to Hell in throngs"* (39:71) and *"those who reverenced their Lord… driven to the Garden in throngs"* (39:73) — the same verb, two addresses, one convoy-system.

**Shade — the Qur'an's Technical Term for Mercy**

The word *ẓill* in the Qur'an's Paradise-register is not mere scenery; it is covenant-terminology. Its source-text is the Prophet's ﷺ ḥadīth of the seven shaded (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031, from Abū Hurayrah):

> *"Seven God will shade in His Shade on a Day when there is no shade but His Shade: the just ruler; the youth raised in the worship of God; the man whose heart is attached to the mosques; two men who love one another in God, meeting and parting upon that; the man who, invited by a woman of rank and beauty, says: 'I fear God'; the man who gives charity and conceals it so that his left hand knows not what his right hand spends; and the man who remembers God alone, and his eyes overflow."*

The ḥadīth reveals the doctrine: ultimate shade is *God's* — the Day's eschatological weather is a sun of reckoning (Muslim transmits from al-Miqdād: *"the sun will be brought near to the creation on the Day of Resurrection until it is a mile away…"*) — and the Garden's shade is the merciful extension of God's own cover over His servants. Hence the Qur'an's shade-texts: *"and shade outstretched (ẓillin mamdūd)"* (56:30) — elongated, generous, without gaps; the Garden's shade *"close upon them, and its fruit-clusters brought low"* (76:14); and the abiding welcome: *"Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein — a hospitality from God"* (3:198). The point of the imagery for verse 41 is threefold: shade is *protection* (the reckoning-sun does not enter there), shade is *rest* (the believer is done with suns under which he labored), and shade is *dignity* (in the desert's code, to sit a guest in the shade is to honor him: the Garden is hospitality at full ceremony). Against vv. 30–31's anti-shade — form without mercy — the reverent receive *shade's very archetype*: God's own cover, stretched out, unshifted, unending.

**Springs — the Running Water of the Beaten Path**

The second reward, *ʿuyūn* — springs — is the Qur'an's most constant Paradise-item, and its reason is experiential: the revelation's first audience measured blessing in water, as the sūrah itself has already shown (v. 27: *"sweet water to drink"* — the earthly gift). The Garden's hydrology is the earthly gift *transfigured and personalized*: springs named and varied — the spring of Salsabīl (76:18), the spring of Kāfūr (76:5–6), the spring of Tasnīm (83:27–28); rivers of water, milk, wine, and honey (47:15); goblets of silver and crystal (76:15–16; 43:71); and the Prophet's ﷺ own gift, the River Kawthar, whose banks are domes of hollowed pearl and whose mud is musk (the reports of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Anas). The theological summary: water on earth was the sūrah's evidence (v. 27); water in the Garden is the *reward* — because God returns to His servants the very mercies they thanked Him for, at an intensity they never sampled: *"no soul knows what comfort is hidden for them — a recompense for what they used to do"* (32:17), with the Prophet's ﷺ ḥadīth qudsī sealing it:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor occurred to the heart of man."* (al-Bukhārī 7498; Muslim 2824, from Abū Hurayrah)

**Taqwā Rewarded with Its Opposite's Currency — the Fit of the Recompense**

The verse's deepest teaching is the *fitness* of the reward to the rewarded. The reverent spent their earthly lives shading others under God's instructions — covering faults (*"whoever covers a Muslim, God will cover him on the Day of Resurrection"* — Muslim 2699), sheltering the orphan and the stranger (93:9–10; 107:2–3), standing in the noon-sun of duty when the prayer's call found them in the marketplace. Their eternity is *shade* — the thing they gave, returned measured and permanent. And their thirst was spiritual on earth — *"their sides forsake their beds; they call their Lord in fear and hope…"* (32:16) — so their eternity is *springs*. The Qur'an's principle, stated once for all: *"Is the reward of goodness anything but goodness?"* (55:60). Verse 41's grammar preaches the same: *inna* — the emphatic "truly" — God underwrites the other side's ledger with the same certainty He underwrote the woes; the reverent's reward is as sure as the deniers' ruin; and the believer recites the verse — amid shade and springs — as promise held while the noon of the world still burns.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:42

> **and fruits such as they desire.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Personalized Harvest**

Verse 42 continues the reverent's inventory: *wa-fawākiha mimmā yashtahūn* — "and fruits such as they desire." The word *fawākih* is the plural of *fākihah* — fruit in the full sense: sweet, ripe, the produce of cultivation — from a root whose field includes delight (Arabic links ripe fruit and pleasant jest: *fākihah* and *fukāhah*). The verse's last word carries its own doctrine: *yashtahūn* — "they desire" — from *sh-h-w*, appetite, longing. The fruits are not rationed by season, nor fixed in a menu, nor grown for the many in one flavor: they are *fitted to appetite* — the Garden's orchards bear what each resident wants, when he wants it. The classical commentators (al-Jaṣṣāṣ among them) observe exactly this against the earthly economy: in this world the availability of fruit is set by external circumstance — climate, season, price, distance — while in the Garden it is set by the *will of the eater*. The Qur'an's twin-texts: *"and fruits of what they choose"* (52:22); *"and meat of fowl, whatever they desire"* (56:20–21); *"…its food is everlasting, and its shade"* (13:35).

**Desire's Rehabilitation — Appetite in the Qur'an's Two Economies**

The phrase *"such as they desire"* quietly completes a theology of desire the Qur'an constructs across its pages. In this world, appetite is *trial*: *"Adorned for mankind is the love of desires — of women and children and hoarded heaps of gold and silver and branded horses and cattle and tilth. That is the provision of the life of this world; and God — with Him is the beautiful return. Say: Shall I tell you of better than that? For the reverent with their Lord are Gardens beneath which rivers flow…"* (3:14–15). The world's desires are not denied as evil; they are *outbid*. The Qur'an's realistic psychology: God placed the appetites in man and *"inspired the soul its wickedness and its reverence"* (91:8), then set the appetites in a test-environment of scarcity and delay, where they can be mastered for His sake; and He promises, in the Garden, the *same faculties at holy play*: hunger met without toil, taste without harm, desire without shame — *"…round about them will pass boys of everlasting youth with cups and pitchers and a cup from a spring — no headache from it, nor do they become drunk thereby"* (56:17–19; cf. 37:45–47; 52:23). The lesson: Islam does not despise appetite; it *disciplines* appetite's objects and times, then rewards the discipline with appetite's cleansing. The one thing absent in the Garden is the appetite for sin — for the self that wanted wrong is itself healed: *"…and We shall strip what is in their breasts of rancor…"* (7:43; 15:47: *"…brothers on couches face to face"*).

**Fruit as Fellowship — the Table of the Family Reassembled**

The verse's fruits are also, in the Qur'an's idiom, *social* goods: the Garden eats together. The scenes the Book paints: *"…and those who believe and whose seed follow them in faith — We join their seed with them"* (52:21); the banquet of the rivers (47:15); the couches set face to face (15:47); and the Garden's people *"will call for every fruit, secure"* (44:55), with the summons of the next verse (77:43) addressed to a *company* — "Eat and drink…" in the plural. Against the deniers' solitary hush (vv. 35–36), the reverent's reward is *conversation at table* — the Qur'an's own summary: *"…and their greeting therein: 'Peace!'"* (14:23). The believer's modern eye may see the family table — the one place where appetite, provision, and love meet daily — and understand the verse: the Garden is that table perfected; nothing on it is earned; everyone at it is welcome; and the Host has been preparing it since before the guests were born (*"…a hospitality (nuzulan) from God"* — 3:198 — the meal set for arriving travelers).

**From Orchard to Orchard-Keeper — the Gratitude Loop Closed**

Finally, the verse returns the sūrah's own argument to its head. The sūrah's earth-panel (vv. 25–27) taught: *look at the land — its lodging, its anchors, its sweet water* — and called for thanks. The Garden-panel (vv. 41–42) now shows what thanks purchases: *the same categories, unstinted* — lodging (the Garden), anchors (its everlastingness: 25:15–16, *dār al-qarār* — the abode of settling, against the earth's kifāt for the dead), water (the springs), and now fruit — the earth's yield and labor's end, delivered without season or sweat: *"…no toil shall they touch therein, nor shall they be removed from it"* (15:48). The loop of gratitude is closed: thank the Giver for the orchard; receive, in exchange, the orchard's *archetype* — and the Giver's own company, which the Qur'an names as the true fruit of the Garden: *"…and the pleasure (riḍwān) of God is greater"* (9:72). Verse 42's fruits are real — sweet, desired, in season always — but they are the *wrapping*; the gift inside the wrapping is the divine announcement over the Garden's people, in the famous ḥadīth qudsī of the veil: God declares to the people of the Garden, after the last veil is drawn and He shows them His Face: *"I am pleased with you, and I shall never be displeased with you"* (al-Bukhārī 7518; Muslim 2829, from Sahl ibn Saʿd). The fruit is sweet; the sentence over the table is sweeter.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:43

> **Eat and drink in enjoyment for that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Royal Summons to the Table**

Verse 43 is spoken — by God, or by His angels as the reception's stewards — over the reverent's table: *kulū washrabū hanīʾan bimā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "Eat and drink in enjoyment, for what you used to do." The imperative pair — eat, drink — is the Qur'an's formula of hospitality granted (its identical twin at 52:19); the adverb *hanīʾan* — from the root of wholesomeness and pleasant ease — is untranslatable in one word: eat *wholesomely, sweetly, in health, without harm and without end*; and the causal clause is the Day's entire doctrine of recompense compressed to five words: *because of what you used to do.* The structure of the sentence is the structure of Islamic soteriology: a free summons; a feast not earned by the eating; and behind the feast, a *ledger of deeds* that God, in His mercy, chose to honor. The verse thus holds, in perfect tension, the two truths the sects of every age try to separate: grace real and deeds reckoned. The Prophet ﷺ taught in the Ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"None of you enters the Garden by his deeds alone." They said: Not even you, O Messenger of God? He said: "Not even I, unless God covers me with His mercy."* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah)

**The Feast as Verdict — the Inversion of the Courtroom**

Notice what the verse does with courtroom imagery: on the deniers' side, the Day was arraignment — silence, sealed mouths, refused pleas (vv. 35–36); on the reverent's side, the same Day opens with *service of refreshments*. The verdicts are experiential before they are verbal: the deniers *feel* condemned (hushed before the Judge), the reverent *feel* acquitted (bidden to table by the Judge). The Qur'an's parallel texts stage the same split: *"…the angels enter upon them from every gate: 'Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured; and how excellent is the outcome of the abode!'"* (13:23–24); *"…and their Lord gives them a pure drink"* (76:21); and the reception-language: *"…praise be to God, who has made good to us His promise and made us inherit the land, to dwell in the Garden wherever we will; so excellent is the reward of the workers"* (39:74). The summons to eat is the *judgment delivered gently*: only the acquitted are invited to dine at their Judge's table — and the believer's whole earthly life is, in this light, the long walk to a table already set: *"And those who reverenced their Lord will be driven to the Garden in throngs"* (39:73) — the same verb of driving as the condemned's; only the destination differs.

**"For What You Used to Do" — the Deeds That Set the Table**

The verse's causal clause must be read with the tradition's exact balance, or it collapses into either of two errors. Against the error of *merit arithmetic* (the table earned), the Prophet's ﷺ word stands — *"None of you enters the Garden by his deeds alone." They said: Not even you, O Messenger of God? He said: "Not even I, unless God covers me with His mercy"* (al-Bukhārī 6463; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah) — and the Qur'an: *"…were it not for God's grace upon you and His mercy…"* (24:21). Against the error of *antinomianism* (deeds irrelevant), the verse itself stands — and the entire verse-cluster of the Qur'an: *"Every soul is held in pledge for its deeds"* (74:38); *"…a Garden provided because of what they used to do"* (52:19 — the identical 77:43-mechanism); *"…whoever works righteousness, male or female, and is a believer — We shall surely give him a good life, and We shall recompense them their wage, the best of what they used to do"* (16:97). The synthesis the Sunni tradition settled: deeds are the *way* (sabab) God has appointed, mercy is the *means* (ruḥmah), and faith is the *root* (aṣl); the verse honors all three — the diners *believed* (they are al-muttaqīn, v. 41), they *did* (v. 43's clause), and they are *fed* by mercy's economy, at a price no ledger could quote (the ḥadīth qudsī of the unseen preparation, 32:17 / al-Bukhārī 7498).

**Enjoyment Without the Three Curses of Earthly Eating**

The adverb *hanīʾan* deserves its final meditation, because it names by negation the three curses that shadow every earthly meal. First, *anxiety*: earthly eating is hedged — is it ḥalāl? will it last? what did it cost someone? The Garden's eating is *"from what they choose"* (56:20–21) and what this sūrah's own verse 42 supplies: appetite-matched, unforced — nothing on the table was taken from anyone. Second, *harm*: earthly indulgence bills the body; the Qur'an's Garden-drink is *"no headache from it, nor do they become drunk thereby"* (56:19) — pleasure without the invoice, appetite without the ache. Third, *end*: every earthly pleasure is a countdown; the Garden's table is set in an abode whose tenure is *"…no toil shall they touch therein, nor be removed from it"* (15:48) — permanence sealed at last by the divine verdict of the veil-ḥadīth's close: *"I am pleased with you, and I shall never be displeased with you"* (al-Bukhārī 7518; Muslim 2829). The believer's discipline, drawn from the verse, is to *practice* hanīʾan lawfully now: to eat with thanks, from lawful earning, in moderation — the Prophet's ﷺ counsel that no human ever filled a vessel worse than his stomach, so a third for food, a third for drink, a third for breath (al-Tirmidhī 2380; Ibn Mājah 3349) — because the rehearsal of lawful, grateful eating is the rehearsal of the Garden's manners, and the sūrah's next verse (v. 44) will name the class of workers whose deeds have set this table.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:44

> **Thus do We recompense the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name of the Table's Hosts**

Verse 44 draws the general law from the feast of verses 41–43: *kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn* — "Thus do We recompense the virtuous." The honored class is *al-muḥsinīn* — from *iḥsān*, the highest word in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary: not merely *doing good* but *excellence* — beauty of act, perfection of work, and, in the Prophet's ﷺ own definition, a quality of consciousness. In the great ḥadīth of Jibrīl, when the angel asked *"What is iḥsān?"*, the Prophet ﷺ answered:

> *"That you worship God as though you see Him; for though you do not see Him, He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8, from Abū Hurayrah)

The muḥsin is therefore the believer whose entire life has been lived *coram Deo* — before the face of God — with the finish and care of one who works for a Viewer, not merely for results. It is fitting that the sūrah's reward-panel should end on this word: the reverent (*al-muttaqīn*, v. 41) are identified, at the table, by their finest name — for the Garden is the natural habitat of *excellence*, and its hosts are those who practiced it.

**The Qur'an's Refrain of Recompense — a Formula Traced**

The formula *"kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn"* — "thus do We recompense the virtuous" — is a Qur'anic refrain whose occurrences the commentators trace like a watermark through the Book. It punctuates the prophet-narratives of Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt — after Abraham is saved from his people's pyre (37:80), after the trial of the sacrifice (37:105), after Moses and Aaron are delivered from Pharaoh (37:121) — and it follows the rescue-arcs of Sūrah Yūsuf (12:22) and Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ (28:14), where Joseph and Moses are each brought safely out of his trial and granted wisdom and maturity. This sūrah's occurrence is the only one outside the narrative sūrahs — and the difference is instructive: here the "rescue" is eschatological. The virtuous of every nation are "brought out" of the world's trial into the Garden's safety, and the formula falls, for the only time, over the whole class rather than a named prophet. The shared story across every occurrence: *iḥsān costs something now and is repaid everything later.* The formula always follows suffering with dignity — the fire, the well, the palace of temptation, the long Makkan decade — because the Qur'an's doctrine of recompense is not a bookkeeping abstraction but a biography-completion: *"Indeed, the patient will be paid their wage in full, without account"* (39:10; cf. 95:6: *"…an unending recompense (ajran ghayra mamnūn)"* — a wage unending and ungrudging).

**Virtue's Double Reward — the Work Now, the Wage Then**

The verse also quietly settles a question every sincere worker asks: *does the good deed pay in this life?* The Qur'an's answer is layered. The deed's *intrinsic* reward begins immediately — the Prophet ﷺ taught from Ṣuhayb ibn Sinān (Muslim 2999): *"Strange is the affair of the believer: all of it is good, and that is for none but the believer: if good befalls him he is grateful, and that is good for him; if hardship befalls him he is patient, and that is good for him"* — the muḥsin's very *state* is already Paradise-shaped: gratitude and patience as twin enjoyments. And the deed's *extrinsic* reward is banked: *"Whatever good you forward for yourselves, you will find it with God — better and greater in reward"* (73:20's close). What the verse excludes is the worker's *measure*: no muḥsin can compute his wage, because the wage's unit is divine generosity: the tenfold of 6:160 (*"whoever brings a good deed shall have ten the like of it"*) and the grain of seven ears, each ear a hundred grains — *"and God multiplies for whom He wills"* (2:261). The verse's *"thus"* (kadhālika) gestures at the preceding verses' luxury — shade, springs, appetite-matched fruit, the royal summons — and says: *this is the pay-scale's floor.*

**The Muḥsin's Secret — Excellence for an Audience of One**

The classical commentators close the verse with a pastoral note: iḥsān's definition (the ḥadīth of Jibrīl, above) is also its *consolation*. The muḥsin's excellence is often invisible to men — his concealed charity, his private restraint, his work finished honestly when no inspector will come — and human economies pay only the visible. Verse 44 is the muḥsin's employment contract: the Audience who saw everything is the Paymaster who recompenses *"thus"* — with shade and springs and fruit and a feast's summons. And the Prophet ﷺ named the trades of the invisible that the Day will make famous: the two who loved one another in God, shaded together (*"…two men who love one another in God, meeting upon that and parting upon that"* — al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031); and the relief-giver, relieved:

> *"Whoever relieves a believer's hardship in this world, God will relieve for him a hardship of the Day of Resurrection; and whoever gives ease to a straitened one, God will give him ease in this world and the Hereafter; and whoever covers a Muslim, God will cover him in this world and the Hereafter."* (Muslim 2699, from Abū Hurayrah)

The recompense-mechanism is exact, because the Recompenser is exact — and generous beyond every arithmetic the virtuous themselves would have dared.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:45

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eighth Toll — After the Feast**

The refrain tolls for the eighth time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke falls immediately after the reverent's feast (vv. 41–44) — and its placement is the sūrah's most pointed juxtaposition: the reader's inner eye has just dwelt on shade, springs, fruit, the royal summons — and the toll drags the camera back to the other table: the deniers' *"eat and enjoy a little"* of verse 46, and beneath it, the shadow that does not shade. Al-Shawkānī reads this eighth occurrence against its context: the woe falls on those who fall into *despair* — who, hearing of the muḥsinīn's wage, despair of attaining it, and their despair is itself a denial: a denial of the manifold reward God has promised, of His generosity's reach, of repentance's open door. It is a subtle and pastoral reading: despair (quṭūṭ) is listed by the Qur'an as disbelief's shadow-emotion: *"…and who despairs of the mercy of his Lord but the astray?"* (15:56 — Abraham's word); *"…do not despair of God's mercy…"* (39:53); and the Qur'an's epithet for the loss: *"…indeed, none despairs of the comfort of God but the disbelieving people"* (12:87 — Jacob's sons told the reverse).

**Despair as Functional Denial — the Reading Unpacked**

The classical note deserves expansion because it reaches a real spiritual condition. Despair of God's mercy is, functionally, a denial of His most-named attributes: the Book's two most frequent divine names are *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm* — and to conclude that one's own sins outrank God's mercy is to have constructed, privately, a smaller god than the Qur'an's. The Prophet ﷺ fought this construction with his ﷺ whole mission, above all with the ḥadīth qudsī of the overrunning mercy: God's declaration that He wrote in His Book, beside His Throne: *"My mercy precedes My wrath"* (al-Bukhārī 7404; Muslim 2751, from Abū Hurayrah) — and with the ḥadīth qudsī of the hundred mercies, one sent into the world, by which the mother spares her child (Muslim 2752, from Abū Hurayrah). And he ﷺ gave despair no ground with the report of the sinner's ninety-nine scrolls, outweighed by one sincere word of divine unity (al-Tirmidhī, ṣaḥīḥ). Verse 45's woe, on this reading, is therefore a *warning shot fired over the despairing* — the last category of deniers one might expect: those who believe in God, believe in the Day, believe in the Garden — and deny only that *they* could enter it. The Qur'an's answer is the verse-pair itself: the muḥsinīn of v. 44 include every penitent; and the refrains' category (*al-mukadhdhibīn*) is left, by the eighth toll, with only one unforgivable membership-fee: final, settled denial.

**The Toll Between Two Tables — the Sūrah's Cruelest Contrast**

Structurally, the eighth toll is the hinge of the sūrah's final movement, and its placement does theological work no argument could: it stands *between* the Garden's table (vv. 41–44) and the deniers' table (v. 46). Two meals, one verse apart. The reverent's table: *"Eat and drink in enjoyment"* (v. 43). The deniers' table: *"Eat and enjoy yourselves a little"* (v. 46). The sentences are almost twins — the same verbs of eating and enjoyment — separated by everything: the one is * enjoyment for deeds done*, the other *enjoyment now, for nothing, ending soon*; the one is * hanīʾan* — wholesome, unending — the other is *qalīlan* — a little, a breather, a respite. The refrain (v. 45) drops between the tables like a wall: and the wall's name is Division — *yawm al-faṣl*. The reader is thereby made to *taste* the doctrine rather than merely assent to it: the difference between the two eternities is compressed, in the sūrah's final verses, into the difference between two dinner-invitations — one from the Judge, one from one's own appetites. The Qur'an states the two-tables doctrine explicitly elsewhere: *"…and the people of the Fire and the people of the Garden are not equal; the people of the Garden are the successful"* (59:20).

**Mercy's Last Refrains — the Reader's Position at Verse 45**

With the eighth toll, only two refrains remain (vv. 47, 49) — and the sūrah's closing movement (vv. 46–50) narrows from cosmology to the most ordinary scenes of life: eating, being told to bow, being spoken to. The interpretive tradition has long noticed that the sūrah's finale abandons thunder for a kind of terrible quietness — the deniers are not shown being destroyed; they are shown *declining an invitation* (to bow) and *running out of discourse* (v. 50). The refrains' remaining tolls will seal that quietness. For the living reader, verse 45's toll is still — as every toll has been — an invitation wearing a warning's clothes: the despairing are told their despair is denier's logic; the procrastinating are about to be shown their delay's true name (v. 46); and the Prayer-refusers are about to be shown what their refusal means in the ledger of the Day (vv. 48–49). No toll has yet fallen on the living; the sūrah's whole art is to keep it that way.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:46

> **Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; truly you are guilty.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Ironic Invitation**

Verse 46 is addressed — by God, with an irony the classical commentators all hear — to the deniers of the Prophet's ﷺ own generation: *kulū wa-tamattaʿū qalīlan* — "Eat and enjoy a little" — *innakum mujrimūn* — "truly you are guilty." The imperative is not permission but dismissal: the tone of a magistrate who has heard enough and adjourns the court — *go, then; finish your lunch.* The adverb is the sentence's blade: *qalīlan* — "a little": however long the enjoyment runs — years, decades — it is *little* against the Day already named; the Qur'an uses the same belittling measure elsewhere: *"We grant them enjoyment a little, then We compel them toward a grave punishment"* (31:24); *"let them eat and enjoy themselves a little, and let hope distract them — they will come to know"* (15:3); *"and if We gave them enjoyment for years, then what they are promised came to them — it would not profit them, that they were given to enjoy"* (26:205–207). Sūrah al-Ḥijr's *"eat and enjoy a little — you are criminals"* (15:3) is this verse's twin — the same irony, the same noun (*mujrimūn*), the same measure (*qalīl*). The sūrah's placement of the verse — immediately after the reverent's unending feast (vv. 41–45) — makes the contrast structural: the deniers' banquet is the Garden's feast inverted: *short*, *unearned*, *unwitnessed* (by their reckoning), and *billed later*.

**Istidrāj — the Respite That Deceives**

The verse is the Qur'an's standing text for the doctrine the tradition names *istidrāj* — God's drawing-on of the sinner: prosperity granted *while* sin continues, not as reward but as rope. The Qur'an states the mechanism explicitly: *"And let not those who disbelieve reckon that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin; and for them is a humiliating punishment"* (3:178); and: *"Do they think, because We aid them with wealth and sons, that We hasten to them with good? No — they do not perceive"* (23:55–56). The Prophet ﷺ gave the doctrine its sharpest form, in the report already cited from ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir (Musnad Aḥmad; sound in meaning):

> *"When you see God granting a servant what he loves of this world, while he persists in his sins, know that it is istidrāj."*

And he ﷺ taught the antidote from his own practice: *"By God, I seek God's forgiveness and turn to Him in a day more than seventy times"* (al-Bukhārī 6307) — the Prophet ﷺ himself repenting daily: the drowning-of-comfort's antidote being constant return. Verse 46's "little" is thus diagnostic: enjoyment *without repentance* is the fire's long fuse; and the believer's prosperity, joined to gratitude and return, is a different economy altogether — the Qur'an allows enjoyment *with* remembrance: *"Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of God which He brought forth for His servants, and the good things of provision? Say: They are, on the Day of Resurrection, for those who believed…"* (7:32).

**The Diagnosis in the Last Word — "Guilty"**

The verse's final clause is its verdict: *innakum mujrimūn* — "truly you are guilty." The noun is the sūrah's own from verse 18 (*kadhālika nafʿalu bi'l-mujrimīn* — "thus do We deal with the guilty"): the deniers at verse 46 are shown their file's classification — the guilty, addressed at their lunch. The classification is not racial, tribal, or even religious-in-name: it is *creedal and practical* — those who received the Reminder (v. 5) and answered with denial (the refrain's *mukadhdhibīn*). And the verse's order of clauses is the diagnosis: *eat… enjoy… [for] you are guilty* — the enjoyment is not the crime; the crime is what the enjoyment *replaced*: the feeding of the heart on truth, the enjoyment of nearness, the banquet of remembrance. The Qur'an's psychology of the mujrim's table: appetite as anesthesia — the proverbial *bread and circuses* of every age — eating to forget the audit. The Prophet ﷺ named the pathology's cure in his ﷺ economy of enough: the ḥadīth of the thirds (al-Tirmidhī 2380; Ibn Mājah 3349 — a third of the stomach for food, a third for drink, a third for breath); and his ﷺ horizon-setter: *"What have I to do with this world? I am in this world like a rider who takes shade beneath a tree, then departs and leaves it"* (al-Tirmidhī 2377, from Ibn ʿAbbās; ṣaḥīḥ) — the believer's enjoyment is a *traveler's meal*: real, gratefully eaten, and — by design — *little*: the sūrah's own adjective, *qalīl*, redeemed from irony into wisdom: whoever *knows* his enjoyment is little has already left the deniers' table.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:47

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ninth Toll — the Woe of the Unaware Banqueter**

The refrain tolls for the ninth time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* This stroke falls immediately after the ironic dismissal — *"eat and enjoy a little; you are guilty"* (v. 46) — and the commentators read it contextually in two complementary keys. Al-Shawkānī's first reading: a simple, weighty repetition — the woe restated after the respite's exposure, sealing the deniers' entire worldly economy (enjoyment-without-account) under the same verdict as their creeds. The second reading, which al-Qurṭubī's style supports: this toll falls on those who *"enjoy a little with no thought to the consequences"* — in effect denying, by their conduct, that what they receive is from God at all — the ingrates of provision, whose table-theology is the sūrah's final exhibit of denial. The ninth woe is thus the woe of *comfortable unbelief*: not the crusading denier of the early panels but the diner who never troubled to ask who owned the room.

**The Banquet's Two Ledger-Entries**

The verse-pair (vv. 46–47) completes a biblical-scale irony the Qur'an has been building since verse 29: the denier's whole life is reframed, at the end, as a *meal* — eaten in borrowed premises, from unthanked provision, on an expiring lease — with the woe-toll dropped over the table like a cloth. The Qur'an's doctrine of the unthanked meal is precise and severe: *"…eat of the fruit when it fruits, and give its due on the day of its harvest, and be not prodigal — God loves not the prodigal"* (6:141); and the twin address to the denier: *"let them eat and enjoy themselves a little, and let hope distract them — they will come to know"* (15:3); with the summary verdict: *"…and whoever is ungrateful — indeed my Lord is Rich, Praiseworthy"* (14:8). The Prophet ﷺ made the meal a trial of remembrance: his ﷺ instruction to name the Provider at every plate — *"when one of you eats, let him mention God's name; and if he forgets, let him say: in the name of God, its first and its last"* (al-Tirmidhī 1858; Aḥmad; ḥasan) — and the gratitude-clause: *"God is pleased with the servant who, when he eats, praises Him, and when he drinks, praises Him"* (Muslim 2734, from Anas). Verse 47's toll is thereby portable: it hangs, inaudibly, over every table of the unthankful — the woe deferred to the Day when the meal's true Host is unveiled.

**Repetition's Final Lesson — the Ten as a Counting**

At the ninth toll, the reader can see the refrain-system's endgame: one toll remains (v. 49), and it will fall — as the sūrah has quietly arranged — over the most concrete refusal of all: the refusal to bow (v. 48). The nine tolls so far have covered the whole anatomy of denial — of the promise (15), of history (19), of creation (24), of providence (28), of the Fire (34), of the silenced court (37), of the challenged schemers (40), of the reward-side's despair (45), of the respite's meaning (47) — and the tenth will name the practical core: denial of *command*. The architecture teaches the sūrah's final doctrine: belief is not an opinion about the unseen; it is an *obedience*; and the ten woes trace, in reverse, the collapse from refused obedience down to refused opinion, or rather from refused opinion down to refused bowing — the two refusals being one. The Qur'an states the unity: *"But no, by your Lord, they will not believe until they make you judge of what arises between them, and then find within themselves no resistance to your decision, but surrender in full submission"* (4:65); and: *"The reply of the believers, when they are summoned to God and His Messenger that he may judge between them, is only: 'We hear and we obey'"* (24:51).

**The Mercy of the Ninety Percent Point**

One last pastoral note before the finale: the refrain's own count carries a Gospel-within-the-warning. Nine tolls have fallen — and the sūrah's audience, the actual hearers in Makkah, were *still alive*: the Day had not come; the category was still exit-able; and history records that many in that very audience exited it — ʿUmar, Khālid ibn al-Walīd, ʿIkrimah ibn Abū Jahl, Ṣuhayl ibn ʿAmr, Ṣafwān ibn Umayyah — the sūrah's first addressees among them, hearing (or hearing of) its woes over their own dinners, and leaving the table's verdict for the Prophet's ﷺ city and the Prophet's ﷺ God. The ten refrains are thus not a closed ten of doom; they are an open nine-of-ten and one — the sūrah's Author leaving the last toll's addressee-room, as it were, ventilated: the door of repentance wedged open until the Day itself shuts it. The Prophet ﷺ wept for those who stayed — at al-Ṭā'if and in the Year of Sorrow, and over the dead of Makkan denial — and God's word to him frames the weeping: *"So remind — you are only a reminder; you are not over them a controller"* (88:21–22); and the Book's last word on the matter: *"…and God would never wrong them, but they wronged themselves"* (3:182; 8:51; 22:10: *"…that is for what your hands have sent ahead; and God is never unjust to His servants"*).

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:48

> **And when it is said unto them, "Bow!" they bow not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Last Exhibit — a Refused Posture**

After cosmology, history, embryology, geology, fire-portraits and courtrooms, the sūrah's final charge against the deniers is stunningly small and concrete: *wa-idhā qīla lahumu'rkūṣū lā yarkaʿūn* — "and when it is said to them, 'Bow!', they bow not." The verb is *rakʿa* — the bow of the ṣalāh, the half-fold of the body in which the worshipper's spine curves and his head is brought level with his back — the Qur'an's own description: *"…and bow with those who bow"* (2:43; 3:43; cf. Maryam's command: *"O Maryam, be devout to your Lord, prostrate and bow with those who bow"*). The command quoted in the verse — *irkaʿū*, "Bow!" — is the minimal, single-word form of the entire law of prayer: not a system to master, not a scripture to accept, not a history to concede — one motion of the spine. And they do not do it. The sūrah, having indicted the deniers on the grandest charges (denying God, the Day, the Fire, the Resurrection), now produces the evidence the whole case finally rests on: the refusal of the bow. The grand denials, one understands, all live inside that one stiff neck.

**The Occasion — Dignity That Refused to Bend**

Al-Qurṭubī transmits the report that the verse was revealed concerning certain disbelievers who protested the very postures of prayer: bowing and prostration struck them as beneath the dignity of free men — an insult to the standing Arab. The report illuminates the Makkan psychology of refusal: the Qurayshi noble was raised in a culture where the raised head was honor itself — and Islam's liturgy required him to place his forehead on the ground beside his slave, his debtor, and the Abyssinian freedman Bilāl. The row of the prayer — shoulders touching, feet aligned, no rank but the takbīr's order — was, and is, a weekly revolution: all spines equal before the One. The refusal of v. 48 was thus not casual; it was the class instinct defending its last citadel, the body. And God's word exposes the citadel's true garrison with surgical brevity: it is not reason that refuses the bow; it is the *ego that governs the reason*.

**The First Sin Was a Refused Bow — the Verse's Deep Memory**

The verse reverberates with the oldest scene in the Qur'an's spiritual history: the creation of Adam and the command to the angels to prostrate — *asjudū* — and the one refusal: *"And when We said to the angels: Bow before Adam — they bowed, except Iblīs; he refused, and was arrogant, and became of the disbelievers"* (2:34; cf. 7:11–12; 38:74–75). Every reader of the Qur'an hears the rhyme: the first sin in creation's moral history was, precisely, *a refusal to make the body's confession*; and the deniers of verse 48 repeat it in miniature — commanded to bow, *"they bow not."* And the sequel in Adam's story is the sūrah's whole theology in two verses: Adam also fell — but falling *toward* God in repentance, he was raised: *"Then Adam received words from his Lord, and He turned toward him; indeed He is the Relenting, the Merciful"* (2:37). The contrast is exact and eternal: Iblīs's pride *before* God (refusal) versus Adam's humility *after* sin (return); the deniers' stiff neck versus the believer's bowed one — the bow, in the end, is not the cause of salvation but its *posture*: the body rehearsing, five times daily, the soul's only viable position before its Maker. The Prophet ﷺ taught the dignity-inversion plainly: Pride, in the Prophet's ﷺ definition, is the refusal of truth once it is seen: the mukadhdhib's exact condition; and its remedy is the prayer's exact motion. The Prophet ﷺ was asked — in the ḥadīth of Muslim (91, from Ibn Masʿūd) — whether the love of a fine garment and fine sandals was itself pride, and he ﷺ distinguished the two economies:

> *"No one who has an atom's weight of pride in his heart will enter the Garden." A man said: A man loves that his garment be beautiful and his sandal be beautiful. He said: "God is Beautiful and loves beauty — but pride is the rejection of truth (batar al-ḥaqq) and the contempt of people (ghamṭ al-nās)."*

**A Religion Without Bowing — the Reported Prophetic Word**

The source commentary cites, on this verse, a prophetic report transmitted in the collections, cited by Ālūsī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Shawkānī:

> *"There is no good in a religion that has no bowing and no prostration."*

The saying is the verse's own thesis: whatever a religion holds — doctrine, ethics, aesthetics — if it never bends the body before God, it lacks the one motion that distinguishes servant from sovereign. Islam's insistence on embodied worship has always been its scandal and its sanity: the God of the Qur'an is not an idea to entertain but a King to face; and the body is not the soul's unfortunate rental but the soul's instrument of confession. The Prophet ﷺ made the prayer the very boundary of the covenant:

> *"Between a man and disbelief and association is the abandonment of prayer."* (Muslim 82, from Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh)

> *"The covenant between us and them is the prayer; whoever abandons it has disbelieved."* (al-Tirmidhī, from Buraydah)

And the Qur'an puts the refusal of prayer at the Fire's gate, in the damned's own audit: *"What led you into the Burning Fire? They will say: We were not of those who prayed, and we did not feed the poor, and we plunged with the plungers, and we denied the Day of Recompense, until certainty came upon us"* (74:42–47); and of the denier's biography: *"He neither confirmed the truth, nor did he pray; but he denied and turned away"* (75:31–32).

**The Second Reading — the Day They Cannot Bow**

The commentators record a second, eschatological reading, reported by al-Qurṭubī and al-Shawkānī: the verse speaks of the deniers *on the Day of Resurrection* — summoned to prostrate, and unable. The Qur'an teaches the scene: *"The Day the Shin is bared, and they are called to prostration, they shall not be able — their eyes humbled, humiliation covering them; and they were called to prostration when they were sound"* (68:42–43). The doctrine is the doctrine of habit's afterlife: the body that never learned the motion in its day of freedom will find the motion *impossible* in the Day's compulsion; the bow is easy now and salvific now — and then, unlearned forever, locked out of the joints themselves: the Qur'an's image of the wilfully sealed: *"We have placed before them a barrier and behind them a barrier, and covered them, and they do not see"* (36:8). The two readings — refusal now, inability then — are the sūrah's last diptych: the same verb, *rakʿa*, spanning the two economies of the will: before the Day, the bow is offered and may be declined; on the Day, the bow is commanded and cannot be performed. Verse 48 stands therefore as the sūrah's most practical verse — the one charge a reader can answer within the minute it takes to read it: the Test is not whether one has out-argued the deniers of verses 15–47, but whether, when the word comes — *Bow!* — one's spine answers.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:49

> **Woe that Day to the deniers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tenth Toll — the Count Complete**

The refrain tolls for the tenth and final time: *waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.* Al-Nasafī and al-Shawkānī read this last occurrence against its immediate context — the refusal to bow (v. 48) — and give the sūrah's final definition of its deniers: those who deny *the commands and prohibitions of God*, prayer being foremost among the commands. The definition completes a ten-step curriculum the refrains have taught: beginning with denial of the promise (v. 15) and ending with denial of the *bow* (v. 49), the sūrah has traced denial from its loftiest object (the Day of Judgment) to its humblest (a bent back) — and asserted all along that the two are one refusal wearing ten masks. The theology is exact: whoever declines God's smallest command has, at the root, declined God's authority — the root from which both the grand creeds and the small obediences drink. Hence the Qur'an's most piercing criterion of faith is never confessional eloquence but knee-and-forehead: *"But no, by your Lord, they will not believe until they make you judge of what arises among them, and then find within themselves no resistance to your decision, but surrender in full submission"* (4:65).

**The Ten as a Doctrine — What the Completed Count Teaches**

With the count complete, the reader can survey the refrain's full architecture — and the survey is itself exegesis. Ten woes: at 15, 19, 24, 28, 34, 37, 40, 45, 47, 49 — after the promise, after history, after creation, after providence, after the Fire's sparks, after the silence, after the challenge, after the Garden's reward, after the respite's irony, after the refused bow. The number spans every register of the human relationship to truth: intellectual (denying evidence), historical (denying precedent), experiential (denying one's own origin), aesthetic (denying the description), procedural (denying the court), strategic (denying the power), emotional (despairing of the wage), sensual (misreading the meal), and corporal (declining the posture). The sūrah's quiet claim, sealed by the tenth toll: *denial is a total stance or it is nothing* — one cannot deny God at verse 49 while believing Him at verse 15; the ten tolls are one toll, repeated because the human heart can hear a truth ten times and withhold assent ten times. The Qur'an names the strategy itself: *"…and We have diversified in it the threats, that they may be reverent — or that it beget in them remembrance"* (20:113); and the Prophet ﷺ gave the hardened listener's condition its similitude: *"The example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the living and the dead"* (al-Bukhārī 6407, from Abū Mūsā).

**The Last Toll's Mercy — the Door Still Ajar**

Yet even the tenth toll is spoken *before* the Day it names — and this, the tradition insists, is the refrain's final secret. Every occurrence is future-tensed: *woe that Day* — not "woe this hour." The sūrah's woes are promissory notes, not executions; and the entire interval between recitation and fulfillment — the interval every reader of every generation inhabits — is the space of the open door. The Qur'an's last word on that door is its most generous: *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of God's mercy; surely God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful — and turn to your Lord and surrender to Him before the punishment comes upon you, and then you will not be helped"* (39:53–54); and the Prophet ﷺ was the door's living keeper, to the final days: God's word to him — *"So remind: you are only a reminder; you are not over them a controller"* (88:21–22) — and his ﷺ practice: the recitation of the woes was itself the reminder, and those who heeded among the very first addressees entered a different verse altogether: the verse of *kulū washrabū hanīʾan* (v. 43), the feast of the muḥsinīn. The ten tolls thus close with their own evangelical inversion: the reader who has heard all ten and still stands within the interval can still step out of the sentence — by the same low door the sūrah has kept open for fifty verses: the door shaped, precisely, like a bow.

**After the Tolls — Silence, Then One Question**

Structurally, the tenth toll is the sūrah's penultimate verse: after it comes only the final question (v. 50) — and the arrangement is deliberate rhetoric: the woes *end* so that the question may stand alone, un drummed, at the chapter's very lip. The reader who has heard ten verdicts is not given an eleventh; he is given a question — *"So in what discourse after this will they believe?"* — and left standing in it. The sūrah's method in miniature: God does not compel the final assent (4:65's "full submission" must be free); He argues, warns, paints, tolls — and then hands the hearer the open sentence to complete with his life. The ten refrains were the courtroom's closing argument; verse 50 will be the jury's room — entered alone, with one question on the wall.

## Sūrah al-Mursalāt 77:50

> **So in what discourse after this will they believe?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's Last Word Is a Question**

Sūrah al-Mursalāt ends not with a verdict, a vision, or a command, but with an open interrogative: *fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahū yuʾminūn* — "So in what discourse, after it, will they believe?" The particle *fa* ties the question to everything before it: after oaths that enlisted wind and rain and angels as witnesses; after the certain promise that what is promised *will befall*; after stars blotted and skies riven; after three courts of evidence — ruins, wombs, and water; after the Fire's shadow and sparks; after the Garden's shade and springs; after ten tolls of woe — *after all this*: in what ḥadīth will they believe? The word *ḥadīth* is chosen with the Qur'an's usual precision: its root *ḥ-d-th* means newness — an event, a piece of news, a discourse, something that *happens*; and the Qur'an applies the word to itself in the register of challenge: *"God has sent down the fairest discourse (*aḥsana al-ḥadīth*) — a Book consistent, paired..."* (39:23); and in the question's exact twin, at the close of Sūrah al-Jāthiyah:

> *"These are the signs of God that We recite to you in truth. So in what discourse (*ḥadīth*), after God and His signs, will they believe?"* (Qur'an 45:6)

The question's logic, the commentators agree, is that of the exhausted alternative: *if not this, then nothing.* It is the argument-form logicians call the argument from the superlative: when the best of a class has been presented and refused, the refusal is not of the specimen but of the class — the one who will not believe the Qur'an is not waiting for better evidence; he has met the best and declined it; no remainder is left in which belief could live. The Qur'an's twin-sarcasm at 45:6 is sharper still — "after *God and His signs*" — as if to say: after the Author Himself has spoken.

**The Qur'an's Self-Awareness as the Final Argument**

The verse is one of the Book's plainest self-referential claims, and it should be weighed honestly by every reader: the Qur'an here pronounces itself the discourse *after which no discourse deserves belief* — the terminus of revelation's argument. The claim is not provincial. It is the same claim the Book makes in its challenge-verses: *"And if you are in doubt concerning what We have sent down upon Our servant, then bring a sūrah like it"* (2:23; cf. 10:38; 11:13; 17:88); *"Do they not ponder the Qur'an? If it were from other than God, they would have found in it much discrepancy"* (4:82); *"And this Qur'an is not such as could be invented apart from God — but a confirmation of what is before it, and a clarification of the Book..."* (10:37). What verse 50 adds to the challenge-genre is its *placement*: it is the sūrah's final breath — the last of fifty verses that have themselves been the demonstration: oaths of unmatched cadence, a refrain-architecture of ten strokes, an argument that moved from the stars to the womb to the cup on the table to the bend of the back. The verse, in other words, does not merely assert the Qur'an's sufficiency; it stands at the end of a fifty-verse exhibit of it. The reader who has *heard* the sūrah — its sounds as much as its theses — has already been shown what "discourse" at its utmost is; the question only asks him to admit what his ears have conceded.

**Belief's True Rival — Not Other Books but No Listening**

The question also diagnoses, quietly, what actually competes with the Qur'an for belief. It is not, usually, a rival scripture — the verse's irony is that the deniers of the Prophet's ﷺ day offered no alternative *ḥadīth* at all: no counter-book, no better cosmology, no rival theory of justice; they offered, as the sūrah has documented, only mockery, delay, appetite, and a stiff neck. The modern reader may substitute the current counter-candidates — the marketplace's thousand discourses, each bidding for the soul's subscription: the feed, the chart, the ideology, the self-help gospel, the claim that matter is all. But the verse's question confronts each with the same audit: *what does your discourse know about the stars' dimming? about the drop's measure? about the shadow that will not shade?* A discourse that cannot speak to death, judgment, and the soul's address is not a rival to the Qur'an; it is a commentary track for the life of delay — entertaining, and signed by no one. The Qur'an's assessment of such competitors is the verse's own sting: they are *after* — *baʿdahū* — subsequent, derivative, late to a table already set. And the Prophet ﷺ gave the diagnostic its final form in the report of Muslim (2577, from Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī): *"Tribulations will be presented to hearts as a mat is woven, reed by reed: any heart that takes one in gets a black spot; and any heart that rejects it gets a white spot — until hearts become two: a white heart like a smooth stone, unharmed by trials as long as the heavens and earth endure; and a black, rusted heart, like an overturned vessel."* — the heart, in the end, *becomes* the discourse it has fed on; and verse 50 asks only: *which one is your heart now believing?*

**The Question the Reader Must Complete**

And here is the verse's master-stroke, noted by the classical rhetoricians: the question is grammatically about *them* — "in what discourse will **they** believe?" — but it is functionally addressed to everyone who reads it. The sūrah's "they" has never been a distant third person; the refrain's deniers have been standing beside the reader for fifty verses, eating, being summoned, refusing the bow — and now the question slides off the page's third person into the reader's first: *in what discourse will **I** believe — after this one?* The verse ends the sūrah the way the Qur'an so often ends its most urgent passages: with the verdict suspended, the sentence's final clause reserved for the hearer's will. God does not argue the last step; He leaves it free — because the whole sūrah has been about freedom's seriousness: a Day that divides, a court that silences, a feast that rewards, a bow that must be chosen while choosing is possible. The Qur'an's doctrine of the matter could not be clearer: *"There is no compulsion in the religion — the right way has been made distinct from the wrong"* (2:256); and: *"And say: the truth is from your Lord; so whoever will, let him believe, and whoever will, let him disbelieve"* (18:29) — both verses descending, in their freedom, to the same fireside of mercy where this sūrah's question sits. Fifty verses of oaths, evidence, woe, and promise thus resolve into one quiet, open interrogative — the most respectful gesture a Revealer could make to the creature He made free: the Book ends, and hands the reader the pen.

**The Sūrah Completed — a Closing Survey**

Sūrah al-Mursalāt has now been read whole: five oaths by the sent (vv. 1–5) and their double mission (v. 6); the oath's answer — the promise that *will* befall (v. 7); the cosmic strike of the scenery (vv. 8–11) appointed for the Day of Division (vv. 12–14); ten tolls of woe (vv. 15–49) distributed over three courts of evidence — history (vv. 16–19), the body (vv. 20–24), the earth (vv. 25–28) — the Fire's ironic address (vv. 29–34), the silenced court and its challenge (vv. 35–40), the Garden's contrasted feast (vv. 41–45), and the final scenes of appetite, bowing, and discourse (vv. 46–50). The sūrah's architecture has proven to be a single argument carried by a single refrain: *God has sent; God has promised; God will divide; and the denial of any one of these is the denial of all.* For the believer, the sūrah leaves a posture: spines that bow now, hearts that bank on the Day, tongues that thank for the water. For the skeptical reader, it leaves what it left the Quraysh: not a cage but a question — standing at the end of the Book's own finest fifty verses, patient as a sentry, awaiting the answer that only a life can give: *so — in what discourse, after this, will they believe?*
