# Sūrah al-Insān (Chapter 76) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter That Takes Man Himself as Its Subject**

Of the one hundred and fourteen chapters of the Qur'an, the overwhelming majority take their names from concrete objects, events, persons, or places: the Cow, the Spider, the Ant, the Night Journey, the Heifer, the women of ʿImrān. Only a handful are named after abstract qualities — the Judgment, the Day, the Criterion. And only one, Sūrah 76, is named simply after the human being: *al-Insān*, "Man." That the Book reserves its plainest possible self-designation — the word that means "the human person," the word that recurs throughout the Qur'an as the addressed subject of the moral drama — for this single thirty-one-verse chapter is itself a commentary. Here the subject is not a story, not a beast, not a battle; the subject is you and I. The very first verse opens with the name, in a question: *Has there come upon man a span of time in which he was a thing unremembered?* — and the entire chapter thereafter is the unfolding answer to that question.

The sūrah carries further names that map its contents. It is called, after its first two words, *Hal atā* — "Has There Not Come?" — the voice of a caller sounding the alarm of time. It is called *al-Dahr*, "the Age" or "the passing of time," after the word *dahr* in verse one, because the sūrah measures a human life against the flow of time: a span already passed, a test in progress, a Day looming. And it is called *al-Abrār*, "the Pious" or "the Doers of Good," for the long central section (verses 5–22) is a portrait of the *abrār* — what they drink, whom they feed, what they say, what they fear, and what God gives them in return. Three names, three windows: time, the test, and the blessed end.

**A Madinan Chapter With a Meccan Heart**

According to most scholars the sūrah was revealed in Madinah, though the reports are not uniform: some maintain that all but a small portion was Madinan, others that verses 23–31 (the address to the Prophet ﷺ) belong to the Meccan period, and a minority hold the entire sūrah to be Meccan. The content itself offers the best clue. The opening verses — the unremembered man, the mixed drop, the test, the offered road — are in the classic Meccan register, the anthropology of the individual soul. The middle portrait of the pious and the feast of the Garden is likewise the language of personal reward that Meccan revelation favors. And the closing address — *be patient with thy Lord's judgment, and obey neither sinner nor disbeliever among them* — is unmistakably the counsel given to the Prophet ﷺ under the pressure of opposition, the register of one standing before a hostile assembly. Read as a whole, the chapter moves exactly the way the Prophet's own life moved between the two cities: from the solitary question of the soul in the cave to the sustained struggle of the community in the city, without leaving the first question behind. The scholars' debate over its dating is therefore not a trivial technicality; the sūrah is a bridge, and a bridge is the only kind of chapter that belongs to two cities at once.

**The Story That, It Is Reported, Called the Sūrah Down from Heaven**

The source commentary records, with the caveats that go with it, the occasion most beloved to the tradition. According to most Shiʿi and some Sunni sources, verses 5–22 were revealed in relation to the family of the Prophet: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Fāṭimah, Ḥasan, and Ḥusayn. The account, related with slight variation by al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī, and others, runs as follows: ʿAlī's young sons Ḥasan and Ḥusayn fell ill, and the Prophet ﷺ came with a group of followers to visit them. They suggested that ʿAlī make a vow to God for the children's recovery. So ʿAlī, Fāṭimah, and Fizzah, their house helper, vowed that if the boys recovered they would fast for three days — and, according to some, the two children themselves made the same vow.

The boys recovered. The first day of fasting was kept, and when the family — in dire need of food — was about to break their fast with a small loaf baked from borrowed barley, an indigent person came to the door: "Peace be upon you, the Household of Muhammad. I am a needy Muslim; please give me some food. May God bestow upon you, in kind, from Divine Sustenance." All of them gave him their share. That night there was only water. The second day they fasted again — and an orphan came, and once more the bread was gone and the evening was water. The third day — and a captive, one of the prisoners of war entrusted to the care of the believers, came to the same door, and the same surrender was made.

On the fourth day ʿAlī brought Ḥasan and Ḥusayn to the Prophet, who saw them trembling with hunger and said, "It grieves me to see you in this condition." He walked with them to their home and found Fāṭimah praying — her stomach sunk into her backbone, her eyes sunk with the emptiness of three days. At that moment Gabriel descended with the sūrah, and the report preserves the most unusual words the commentary tradition gives for any occasion of revelation: *O Muhammad, receive this sūrah. God congratulates you for having such a family.*

Whatever the final grading of the report's chain, its place in the sūrah's history is secure, and its meaning is plain. The portrait of the pious in verses 5–22 is not a generalization; it was addressed, in its immediate occasion, to the poorest household in Madinah at that hour — the household whose bread had been given away three times in three days, whose eldest woman was praying with an empty stomach, whose children shook with hunger. The reward described in the chapter — the cup, the fountain, the low-hanging clusters, the immortal youths — was spoken to people who had nothing that day. The sūrah's central paradox is written into its own occasion: the ones whose portion had been taken three times were the ones told they would be given everything. And the word *congratulates* — rare in prophetic reporting — tells the reader what kind of event this was: not a lesson, a commendation.

The classical commentators did not let the occasion swallow the verse, however. al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī treat the story as one powerful instance under a universal heading: the *abrār* of the chapter are every person, in every generation, who keeps a vow in hunger, who gives away the food they love, who says "we do this only for the Face of God" and means it. The occasion explains how the sūrah came down; it does not explain what the sūrah does for every reader after. Both are honored in the treatment that follows.

**The Sūrah in the Mosque: A Standing Practice of the Prophet**

It is reported that the Prophet ﷺ often recited this sūrah together with Sūrah 32, al-Sajdah, in the early morning prayer — *Fajr* — and especially on Friday (Ibn Kathīr cites the practice; the pairing is also found in the ḥadīth literature transmitted from Abū Hurayrah, graded *ḥasan* by al-Tirmidhī): that the Messenger of God ﷺ would join these two sūrahs in the dawn prayer. The pairing is not accidental, and the early community felt its logic. Al-Sajdah speaks of the Lord who "created the heavens and the earth in six days, and His Throne had been upon water, that He might test which of you is best in deed" (Qur'an 32:4), and of the One who "will resurrect you, then He will gather you for the Day of Judgment" (Qur'an 32:5), and who is "the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden, and He is, of all things, Knowing" (Qur'an 32:9). Al-Insān begins at exactly the same point — man, the unremembered thing — and follows him through test, guidance, and the two destinies. One sūrah is the Creator's self-introduction; the other is His portrait of the creature. Reciting them together at Fajr on Friday — the week's opening — orients the entire week from its first light: *this* is the being God made, *this* is what He made him for, *this* is where the road ends. No other chapter occupies that particular slot of the weekly prayer-life of the first Muslims with equal steadiness.

**Three Movements of Thirty-One Verses**

The chapter's architecture is compact enough to be held in a single act of the mind, which is the hallmark of a Qur'anic unit of complete design.

The first movement (verses 1–3) lays down the human story in three strokes. The question: *was there not a span of time when man was a thing unremembered?* The origin: *We created man from a drop of mixed fluid that We may test him, and We endowed him with hearing, seeing.* The fork: *We guided him upon the way, be he grateful or ungrateful.* Three verses, and the entire drama is on stage: a being made from nothing, made for testing, offered a road, and left to choose.

The second movement (verse 4, then verses 5–22) shows the two ends. Verse 4 is a single blunt stroke for the ungrateful: chains, shackles, and a blazing flame. Then the sūrah turns — as it will not turn back — to the pious, and proceeds to describe them at close range with a particularity that nothing else in the Qur'an matches. What they drink (a cup whose mingling is camphor, a fountain that gushes). Whom they feed (the indigent, the orphan, the captive). What they say while feeding ("We feed you only for the Face of God"). What they fear ("a grim, calamitous day"). What God does in return (shielding, radiance, joy, a Garden for their patience, shade, low clusters, silver and crystal, ginger, Salsabīl, immortal youths, green silk, and — most remarkable of all — a drink given directly by their Lord). Verse 22 closes the portrait with the accounting: *This is a reward for you, and your endeavoring is held in gratitude.*

The third movement (verses 23–31) shifts address. The "you" that has been describing the pious is now the Prophet ﷺ himself: *We have surely sent down upon thee the Quran as a revelation.* And then the counsel that follows is at once prophetic and universal — be patient with thy Lord's judgment; invoke His Name morning and evening; prostrate and glorify by night at length; do not let the ephemeral crowd out the weighty Day; know that We replace generations; take a way unto thy Lord if thou wilt; for *you do not will but that God wills.* The sūrah ends, as it began, on the human will — but now the will is standing inside the will of God, at peace.

**The Sūrah Among Its Neighbors**

In the written order the chapter sits between Sūrah 75, al-Qayyimah ("The Standing Up"), and Sūrah 77, al-Mursalāt ("The Emissaries"). The neighbors are significant. Al-Qayyimah opens with a question about the Day of Standing and includes the challenge *Does man think that We shall not gather his bones? We are able to proportion his fingertips* (75:3–4) — and closes, at 75:36, with the sentence *And you do not will but that God wills, the Lord of the worlds* (Asad: "And you do not will, except that God wills"), which is a near-verbatim companion to 76:30, the penultimate verse of this chapter. Al-Mursalāt, on the other side, is the sūrah in which the Qur'an declares itself a remembrance and an intercession. Thus the chapter named after the human being is bracketed by the chapter about the Day on which the human being stands and the chapter about the Book that reminds him — flanked by his end and by his reminder. The ordering of the written Book, in this instance, reads like a table of contents for the sūrah itself.

**What al-Insān Alone Gives**

A few features of this chapter appear nowhere else in the Qur'an, or almost nowhere else, and they deserve to be named before the verse-by-verse reading begins.

First, *al-abrār* — "the pious" — is here the title of a distinct rank with its own drink, its own fountain, and its own table. The word occurs a handful of times in the Qur'an (2:177: "Those are the pious"; 2:189; 2:252), but this sūrah is the one that builds the whole middle of its text around the plural, as though *the pious* were a company one could meet. Second, only here does God serve the drink of Paradise *directly*: the immortal youths wait upon them in verse 19, yet verse 21 interrupts the service with *their Lord shall give them to drink of a drink most pure* — the intermediary removed, the host appearing in person. Third, the list in verse 8 — the indigent, the orphan, the captive — is the most compressed social ethic in the Book: one sentence that names the three categories of person who can never repay (the hidden poor, the dependent child, the enemy prisoner), and makes giving to them, *despite loving the food*, the defining act of the pious. Fourth, the final pair of verses (29–30) is the most compact statement anywhere in the Qur'an of the balance between human willing and Divine willing — free will held inside decree — and the commentary on those verses will show why the tradition has always read them together with 75:36. Fifth, the opening question is the only sūrah-opening question that addresses *man* in the singular-generic — not a people, not a community, but the human person as such, and the reader himself in the person of the whole.

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary on all thirty-one verses. Each verse is treated as a complete, independent discourse, in strict order, as the method requires: the verse is quoted individually — even where the source groups verses 4–5, 5–6, 15–16, and 25–26 — and then developed through the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī, and the earlier reporters), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the logic of the teaching, and its bearing on the modern life of the reader. Nothing is compressed because the chapter is long; the question that opens it — *Has there come upon man a span of time in which he was a thing unremembered?* — is carried, verse by verse, all the way to the mercy that closes it.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:1

> **Has there come upon man a span of time in which he was a thing unremembered?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Instead of an Opening**

No other sūrah of the Qur'an begins the way this one begins. Some open with the letters of the alphabet; some with a self-introduction of God (*Praise be to God*; *The Exalted is God*; *God — there is no deity except Him*); some with a declaration about the Book (*This is the Book*); some with a narrative turn (*And We have certainly...*). Sūrah al-Insān opens with a question — and not even a question addressed to God, but a question hurled at the human being, in the middle of his own life, by a voice the listener immediately recognizes as the Most High: *Has there come upon man a span of time in which he was a thing unremembered?*

The choice is deliberate and, once noticed, inescapable. A question cannot be skimmed the way a statement can. A statement informs; a question demands. The reader has not finished the first syllable — *Hal atā* — before the sūrah has crossed a boundary: it is no longer describing the human being to him, it is asking him something. The entire thirty-one verses of the chapter are, in the strictest sense, one answer to this single question. The sūrah is named after the word the question is about — *al-insān*, man — and the first word of that name is wrapped in the first word of the sentence, *hal*, "has there?": the very title of the chapter is set into motion by the interrogation. From the first breath the reader is inside the argument, and the argument will not be closed until the last verse.

**Two Readings, One Unavoidable Conclusion**

The source commentary records the two ways the question has been taken, and the two are not rivals but complementary faces of one thought.

The first reading is the literal one: *there has indeed come upon man a span of time in which he was a thing unremembered* — a time, before creation, before formation, before the first stir of life, when the person who is now reading this line simply did not exist; when he was not a self at all, not even a possibility anyone spoke of. On this reading the question is an indignant rhetorical question, the Arabic device in which a question that expects a "yes" is answered by the speaker's own emphasis. The sūrah is saying: *look at yourself — you are now, here, speaking and questioning; and there was a time when you were no thing. The two states are before you, and the distance between them is the whole story of this chapter.*

The second reading reverses the emphasis and reads the question as one that expects a "no": *has there come upon man a span of time in which he was unknown to God?* — and the answer the sentence forces is *never*. On this reading, which the source attributes to the classical commentators in the line of Q (al-Qurtubī's circle), the "unremembered" is not the state of man before existence but a state God has always been watching: there is no span of time, however small, in which the human being fell outside the knowledge of the Creator. The *dahr* — the span, the age — rolls, and the human being inside it rolls with it, but the eye that watches the rolling does not blink. Al-Qurṭubī's school of reading makes the verse a statement of Divine omniscience dressed as a question: how could the One who knows what the leaf does when it falls be unaware of what the man will do when he turns?

A third, softer movement is recorded as well: the sentence taken as a simple past — *there has come upon man* — referring to the time when Adam was not yet fully formed, or more generally to the time before humanity entered existence (the reading the source assigns to JJ and others). Whether Adam's clay or the species in general, the reference point is the same: the human story has a *before*, and the *before* was silence.

All three readings converge on the same practical ground, and this is the point where the linguistic debate stops and the moral one begins. The reader cannot remain outside the question. If the first reading is his, he has been reminded, at the very start of the chapter, that his entire existence — his name, his family, his possessions, his anxieties — arrived out of nothing, which is the beginning of humility. If the second reading is his, he has been reminded that no moment of his life has ever been unwatched, which is the beginning of accountability. Either way, by the time the first verse is finished, two of the great disciplines of the Qur'an have been installed in the reader's chest: *tawādhuʿ* (the humility of knowing one's beginning) and *muraqabah* (the vigilance of knowing one's Watcher). The sūrah has not yet given a single command; it has already begun its work.

**Al-Dahr: The Word That Carries the Weight of Time**

The word the question is built upon deserves its own pause. *Al-dahr* — translated here as "a span of time" — is the Arabs' ordinary word for the passing of time, for the age that wears things out. The lexicographers traced it from the root *d-w-r*, "to revolve, to go around": time *goes around*, comes and goes, returns the year upon the year, and nothing it touches escapes the circuit. The classical commentators note that pre-Islamic Arabia gave *dahr* a quasi-divine role: the poets and the people spoke of time itself as the agent that bestows and takes away — the giver of wealth and the taker of the beloved — so that *dahr* functioned in the spoken religion of the Arabs as a hidden deity, a moving power behind the throne of events. The Qur'an confronts precisely this notion elsewhere (cf. 52:35–40, where the ascription of the world's workings to time is met with Divine refutation), and it is against that background that *Hal atā ʿalā al-insāni zayfun min al-dahr* acquires its full force: the span of time is not the actor; man has not been *carried* by an age; the question is put to man by God, and the "time" in which he was unremembered is a span *of God's* watching, not of a blind process.

There is also a quiet irony in the sūrah's title *al-Dahr*. The chapter named after the age — the thing that swallows everything it meets — is a chapter about the one creature in the universe against whom time's power is suspended. Everything in the created order is in the current: the body ages, the word perishes, the kingdom rots, even the sun is "in its appointed course" (21:33). The human being is the only thing the Qur'an addresses as though the current did not apply — the only thing asked to answer, to choose, to be grateful or ungrateful, to keep a vow, to feed a captive. The first verse thus holds a paradox the rest of the chapter resolves: man is *inside* the dahr (he has his span, his aging, his death), and man is *addressed across* the dahr (by a voice that stands outside the circuit and asks him about it). Time is his medium; the question is from beyond it. That is the exact position of the sūrah, and of the believer who stands in it: he lives in time, but his questions and his answers are held by One who is not in time.

**The Unremembered: What It Means to Be lam yekun Shayʾan Madhkūran**

The phrase the question turns on is *lam yakun shayʾan madhkūran* — "he was a thing unremembered," or, more literally, "he was not a thing mentioned, recalled, recorded." The commentators drew out the grammar of *madhkūr*. A thing "remembered" in this usage is a thing that is *held in the mind or the record* — present to some consciousness, entered in some account. Before formation, the future person was in neither: not yet formed in a body, not yet named by an angel, not yet inscribed. He was, in the most precise Arabic, *non-existent in the record*: a page not yet turned.

This is the state the hadith tradition later describes in the most concrete terms. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"When the stay of any one of you in the womb reaches forty days, he sends an angel, who forms him and creates his ears and his eyes and his limbs... Then the soul is breathed into him. Then the angel is commanded with four words: Write his provision, his span of life, his travail, and whether he will be wretched or happy."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, from Abū Hurayrah)

Read the first verse of al-Insān against that report and the theology becomes narrative. The "span of time in which man was a thing unremembered" ends at the moment the angel's pen moves: *write his provision, his span of life, his travail.* From that moment the human being is *madhkūr* — mentioned, recorded, held in the account. He enters the memory of heaven before he enters the memory of his mother. The sūrah's first verse is thus the theological shadow of the creation report: every person exists in a double state, unremembered-then and remembered-now, and the entire arc of his life runs along the line drawn at that first inscription. This is one of the quietest foundations of the Islamic doctrine of the Pre-Eternal Decree: the "unremembered" was never outside the possibility of the One who would remember; the record was kept from before the record was opened.

There is a second layer, closer to the reader's own experience. *Madhkūr* also carries the simple sense of "brought to mind." The question then becomes almost biographical: *has there not come upon you a time when you were no one?* — a time when no one's memory contained you, when your name was not spoken, when the world, in every practical sense, proceeded without you. Every reader knows that time: the years before birth, yes, but also the years of childhood before selfhood, the nights of unconsciousness, the stretches of obscurity when no one was waiting for him and no one missed him if he failed. The sūrah opens by taking that familiar smallness and giving it a cosmic scale. The "unremembered" is not a defect to be ashamed of; it is a fact of origin to be grasped honestly — because the same verse that records the smallness also records, in the very next breath, that out of that unremembered nothing God made *a drop of mixed fluid, endowed with hearing and seeing*. The question is not an accusation. It is a diagnosis that prepares the ground for a promise.

**The Question as a Mirror, Not a Lesson**

The classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī among the first) observed that the verse addresses *al-insān* — the definite, generic, singular man — and that the device is one the Qur'an uses to pull the general into the particular. "Man" in the Qur'an is never a species abstracted from the reader; it is the reader, standing in for himself, the way the Arabic pronoun of the Qur'an's longer addresses ("O you who believe") always catches the one reciting. When al-Insān asks the question in the third person — *has there come upon man* — it is doing a rhetorical move the Arabic calls *al-iltifāt*, the turning: the third person on the tongue is the second person in the meaning. *Has there not come upon you a span of time in which you were a thing unremembered?* The turn is deliberate, because the sūrah needs the reader to be both subject and witness: he must be the one who was unremembered, and he must be the one who can now *look back* at that state and see it. A being who cannot see his own past is not yet a moral agent; the first act of the moral agent is to recognize his own beginning. The sūrah's first command is therefore implicit in its first question: *see where you came from, and see Who was watching while you were no one.*

The neighboring sūrah al-Qayyimah uses the same device a verse later and with a different edge: *Does man think that We shall not gather his bones?* (75:3). That question looks forward to the end; this one looks back to the beginning. Between the two — the unremembered start and the gathered bones — the human being's entire span is bracketed, and al-Insān, which sits between the two sūrahs in the written Book, is the chapter that lives inside the brackets.

**The Logic of Beginning With a Question of Time**

Why open a chapter about reward and the pious with a question about the pre-existence of man? The answer is that the sūrah is building its argument from the foundation up, and the foundation of every moral argument about the human being is his ontological status. Before the chapter can discuss what man *does* (keeps vows, feeds the orphan, fears the Day), it must fix what man *is*: a being made from nothing, formed from a drop, endowed with senses, and — most consequentially — standing under a Watcher who saw him before he was. Every verse that follows is a consequence of the first. The test in verse two makes sense only because the one tested was unremembered and is now remembered — a test is the examination of a recorded possibility. The guidance in verse three makes sense only because the one guided is a self that can be lost. The reward in verses five to twenty-two makes sense only because the one rewarded began as *nothing at all*, so that every blessing he reaches is pure gift, from the first breath to the last cup.

For the modern reader the first verse lands with a particular force, because the modern mind is the first in history to have the scientific picture of the "unremembered" in front of it: the single cell, the lineage, the embryo. Science has given the question its most concrete form yet — the reader can now *see* the span of time in which he was a thing unremembered, down to the day and the millimeter. The Qur'an did not wait for the microscope to make the point; it made the point at the level where it matters, and the microscope has since done nothing but confirm the texture. The sūrah's first verse is thus a case of what the commentators would call *iʿjāz* of address: a text that speaks to the age that read it by candlelight and to the age that reads it by a screen's light in the same words, because the question is posed not at the level of the answer but at the level of the questioner. The questioner is the same in both ages: a self that was no thing, now looking at the fact of being a thing, and being told that the looking was seen all along.

The verse ends, as the sūrah began, on the edge of a second thought — the *how* of the making, which the next verse takes up without pause: from the unremembered thing, to the drop of mixed fluid, to the hearing and the seeing. The question has done its work; the answer begins.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:2

> **Truly We created man from a drop of mixed fluid that We may test him, and We endowed him with hearing, seeing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mixed Drop: One Phrase Holding Biology, Language, and Theology**

The first verse asked about the *before*; the second verse describes the *first*. And it describes it in a phrase so compressed that the classical commentators treated it as a single sentence containing three disclosures at once. *Naṭfatan amsājan* — "a drop of mixed fluid" — is the Qur'an's earliest technical language for the origin of the human body, and it is the first place the Book names what it will name again and again: the mixture.

Take the two words in order. *Naṭfah* comes from the root *n-ṭ-f*, the Arabic word for a drop — specifically a drop that hangs, that has just separated from its source and is suspended in its becoming. The same word family appears in the verse that gives the most detailed stages of creation, where the human being passes "from a clinging clot, and from a lump of flesh" (23:14, Asad's rendering of *ʿalaqatan* and *mudghatan*), and in the parallel address to the one who asks *how* the drop is worked: *We made man from a seed of mixed fluids, and We tried him, so We made him hearing and seeing* (77:20 — "We made him from a drop of mixed sperm and tested him; We made him hear and see"). The pairing is deliberate: wherever the Qur'an states the stage, it states the *purpose* in the same sentence, exactly as here.

*Amsājan* — "mixed" — is the plural of *mimsāj*, a mixture, and it reaches back to the root *ʿ-m-s*, "to mix." The commentators (Ibn Kathīr foremost, gathering the earlier reports) took it to mean the mingling of the two fluids — the male and the female — the two lines of descent joined in one point. The Qur'an confirms the point elsewhere with the same vocabulary: *a seed of mixed fluids* (35:11); *We created you from a drop of mixed fluid* (52:26 — the passage in which God answers Pharaoh's claim of lordship over the generations: *he who created you from a mixed drop and made you a man, a husband and wife*); *a seed of mixed fluids* (75:37; 80:19); the *naṭfah al-khbilā*, "the mixed drop," of 22:5 and 36:37. The mixture is thus not a crude detail; it is a doctrine. The human being, from the very first drop, is not a single line but a *conjunction* — the meeting point of two ancestors, and through them of two families, two tribes, and eventually the whole web of kinship that carries him. The sūrah that will in verse eight order the pious to feed the orphan, the dependent child who exists because of that very web of kinship, has already laid the biological fact beneath the social duty: before you are a person you are a *mixing*, and before you are a family you are a *joining*.

**Why He Was Made: The Test Inside the Design**

Between the origin and the endowments the verse places the pivot of the whole chapter: *nabtalihi* — "that We may test him," or "We test him." Notice the tense the Arabic carries. The verb is the present-form *nabtalihi*, an ongoing action: the test is not a single event the drop passes at formation and then leaves behind; it is the *mode* of the created life, running continuously from the first day to the last. The source also records the alternate parsing — read *naqtalihi* or the clause as a new sentence, "We will test him" (JJ) — which shifts the emphasis from purpose to declaration, and the two readings agree on everything that matters: the making and the testing are not two separate facts about the human being but one fact seen from two sides.

This raises the question the classical scholars did not allow to pass: if God knows in advance what the tested one will do — and the Qur'an says so repeatedly (*He knows what the soul possesses* 50:16; *He knows what is in the breasts* 6:49) — then what is the test *for*? The answer, which al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr draw out from the Qur'an itself, is that the test was never meant to inform God. It is meant to *form* the one tested. A test is the instrument by which a possibility becomes an actuality: the capacity to be patient, existing in the drop as a real power, becomes *patience* only when the occasion arrives and the person endures. The Qur'an states the principle in its most explicit form in the chapter on Luqmān, where God defends the very logic of trial:

> *"And He it is Who created the heavens and the earth in six days — and His Throne had been upon water — that He might test which of you is best in deed."* (Qur'an 11:7)

and again, in the sūrah that answers the demand for a purpose to creation itself:

> *"And We did not create the heavens and the earth and that which is between them in play. We did not create them except in truth — and most of them do not know. The Day when the Vault will be folded up as a scribe folds up his writing... that We may recompense those who have done evil for the worst of what they used to do, and reward those who did well with the good."* (Qur'an 50:38–40)

The human being is, in this theology, *the place where the test happens* — not the victim of it, but the arena in which the divine qualities of Justice and Mercy meet real human willing. The Prophet ﷺ framed the practical meaning in words the tradition has repeated since:

> *"The affair of the believer is wondrous, for all his affair is good for him, and there is no one upon whom this is the case except the believer: if blessing befalls him he is grateful, and that is good for him; and if calamity befalls him he is patient, and that is good for him."* (Muslim 2999, from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

Notice the structure of that report — gratitude when blessing comes, patience when loss comes — is the same structure as this verse: the test is not a series of punishments, it is the standing condition in which a person becomes either grateful or ungrateful, and the sūrah's next verse will give those two words their full weight. The test is thus the *engine of identity*: it is the mechanism by which the drop becomes *a* person rather than merely *the* person.

**Hearing and Seeing: The Two Windows Through Which the Test Is Worked**

The verse closes with the endowments: *fa-jaʿalnāhu samīʿan baṣīrā* — "and We made him hearing and seeing." The grammar is worth pausing on. The Arabic does not say "We gave him ears and eyes" — it says "We made him hearing and seeing," using the intensive participles that name the *faculty in full operation*. The gift is not the organ; the gift is the *working*. A human being, on this account, is defined not by having the apparatus but by having the apparatus *engaged* — and the engaged apparatus is, at the same time, the instrument of the test announced in the same breath.

This is the point at which the commentary tradition draws the sharpest consequence in the sūrah. The senses are the two doors through which the test enters the person, and therefore the two doors through which the person enters the test. The Qur'an states the double potentiality in the most unambiguous terms elsewhere:

> *"Indeed, upon the Fire are thirty-nine [taskmasters]. Whoever steals the command of your Lord and transgresses — We will make the Hellfire a destination for him. He will burn therein in a wretched state. And he used not to hear the recitation of his Lord, nor was he a man of understanding."* (Qur'an 68:19–21 — the figure of the unresponsive listener)

and, more directly, in the verse that names the condition of the one whose senses have been given but not used:

> *"And We did not make his vision and his heart a plaything... Indeed, the one who turns away from the remembrance of the Most Merciful — We assign to him a demon who will be his constant companion."* (Qur'an 43:36, 36:60 — the two verses together stating that the unguided are given a companion who leads their unguided senses)

The source commentary makes the exact connection the classical readers made: the gifts of hearing and sight are part of the test, *because they may be used for obedience or for disobedience*; those who do not use them properly "have eyes with which they see not, and ears with which they hear not" (7:179; cf. 10:43; 7:198). The sūrah thus presents the anatomy of the moral life in two words: the person is the one whose seeing and hearing are *for something*. A sense without a direction is not a gift, it is an opening. The modern reader recognizes the claim immediately: the attention of the twenty-first century is the first in history to be addressed at industrial scale — feeds, notifications, streams, advertisements — and the sūrah's two words, *samīʿan baṣīrā*, are the oldest statement of the law that governs the modern mind: *the test is in what you let in.*

**The Body as the Arena of the Test**

The source commentary draws out one more layer, and it is the layer that turns the verse from biology into anthropology. The physical nature — the body that "comes from a drop of mixed fluid" — is created *for the purpose of being tested and tried*. The body is not the enemy of the soul (as some philosophies later made it), nor is it a neutral container; it is the *instrument* of the moral life, the organ through which the human being acts in the world. To feed the orphan, the verse eight will say, is a bodily act — bread is kneaded, carried, placed in a hand; the vow is kept by a stomach that abstains; the fear of the Day is written on a face that can frown (v. 10). The Qur'an's anthropology is thus embodied from the first: the soul is tested *through* the body, and the body is the soul's instrument of choice.

The blowing of the soul into the formed body, described elsewhere in the same terms, completes the picture:

> *"Then when We had proportioned him and breathed into him of Our spirit — then fall you down in prostration before him."* (Qur'an 15:29)

and

> *"Then We made the sperm-drop into a clinging clot, and We made the clot into a lump [of flesh], and We made the lump into bones, and We covered the bones with flesh; then We developed him into another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:14)

The sequence is the same in every verse that gives it: a form is made, and a spirit is breathed. The body is the architecture; the spirit is the tenant; and the test is the life the tenant lives inside the architecture. The neighboring sūrah states the same doctrine in its opening address:

> *"Indeed, We created man in hardship... Does he think that never will he be able to rise again? He says, 'I have spent wealth in abundance.' Does he think that no one sees him? Were We not witnesses when We made his limbs?"* (Qur'an 90:4–7)

The answer to the first question is the subject of al-Qayyimah; the answer to the last question is the subject of this verse: *yes, We were watching — and the watching is now the test, and the test is now the making of the person who will stand on the Day.*

**The Modern Mirror: The Senses Are Still the Test**

Nothing about this verse has aged, because nothing about it is historical; it is a statement about the human condition, and the condition has not changed even when the technology has. The one who was made "hearing and seeing" in a drop of mixed fluid is the same being who now stands before a screen that addresses his two principal senses simultaneously, twenty-four hours a day, with a content selected to hold exactly those faculties. The sūrah's first two verses therefore read as a diagnosis of the modern age: the test was designed for a creature whose two principal openings were the eye and the ear, and the age that has learned to *industrialize the eye and the ear* is the age in which the test has never been more precisely applied. The drop of mixed fluid is now the embryo in the clinic; the hearing and seeing are now the attention on the feed; and the question the sūrah has already asked — *which of you will be grateful, and which will be ungrateful?* — is the question the modern life is being tested by, moment by moment.

The verse does not end with a warning; it ends with a fact, and the fact is a promise. The person is made for testing, and the testing is not a threat but the only condition in which the person becomes *a* person — in which the capacity to be patient becomes patience, the capacity to be grateful becomes gratitude, and the unremembered thing of the first verse becomes the one who will, in the end, be recognized. The next verse states the second half of the design: the road has been shown, and the walking is the person's.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:3

> **Truly We guided him upon the way, be he grateful or ungrateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guidance That Came Before the Choice**

The third verse is the hinge of the sūrah, and its first clause is the hinge of the verse: *inna-nā hadaynāhu al-sabīlā* — "Truly We guided him upon the way." The tense is the past perfect: the guidance has already happened. The road has already been shown. The person who was made from the drop, endowed with hearing and seeing, and set into the test, has now been *given the map* — and the giving of the map precedes, in the order of the sūrah's argument, the walking. This is the theology of the offer: before the human being is asked to choose, he has been given what to choose between; before he is held responsible, he has been shown the road. The Qur'an repeats the structure in the chapter on the pen, in a verse that is the closest parallel to this one in the whole Book:

> *"Indeed, We guided him to the way, either as grateful or as ungrateful."* (Qur'an 17:15)

The two verses are near-identical in form, and the tradition has always read them together: the offer is universal, the road is real, and the response is individual. The same structure appears in the chapter on the balū, where the sūrah on the horse-courser closes its first address with the same two words and the same fork:

> *"Have We not made for him two eyes, a tongue, and two lips, and guided him upon the two highways?"* (Qur'an 90:8–10)

The "two highways" of 90:10 are the same road as the "way" of 76:3, seen from the side that shows its two edges: the way up and the way down, both of which the guide has shown. The guide does not push; the guide shows. That is the exact limit of the divine function in this sūrah, and it is the exact limit that makes human responsibility possible.

**The Two Words That Divide the Sūrah: shākhir and kafūr**

The second clause of the verse gives the two responses their names: *imma shākhiran wa-imma kafūrā* — "be he grateful or ungrateful." These two words are the whole moral vocabulary of the chapter; every verse from four to thirty-one is a development of one of them or of the contrast between them. It is worth drawing out their roots, because the roots are the arguments.

*Shukr* — gratitude — comes from the root *sh-k-r*, whose basic sense is *to acknowledge, to recognize the source of a benefit*. To be grateful is, in the Arabic, to *see the Giver in the gift*: the grateful person does not merely receive; he *recognizes*. The Qur'an's own definition of the grateful is thus theological before it is emotional:

> *"If you are grateful, I will surely increase you; but if you are ungrateful, indeed My punishment is severe."* (Qur'an 14:7)

Gratitude, on this account, is a *law of increase*: the one who acknowledges the source of the gift keeps the channel open, and the channel, once open, carries more. The ungrateful person, by contrast, does not merely fail to thank; he *closes the channel*, because the act of ingratitude is the act of treating the gift as self-generated, and a person who treats everything as self-generated has, by definition, cut himself off from the source of increase.

*Kufr* — ingratitude, disbelief — comes from the root *k-f-r*, whose basic sense is *to cover, to hide*. The same root gives *kafara al-arḍ*, "the earth covered the seed" — the ground closing over the grain. The ungrateful person, in the language's own logic, does something to the blessing: he *covers it*, hides it, buries it, treats the gift as though it were not a gift. The disbeliever, in the same root, does the same thing to the truth: he covers it, refuses to let it stand in the open. The two words — *shākhir* and *kafūr* — are therefore not synonyms for "believer" and "disbeliever" in a flat sense; they are the two possible *postures toward what has been given*: the one who opens to the source and the one who covers the gift. The sūrah's entire middle section — the pious, the cup, the fountain, the feeding, the fear — is the portrait of the *shākhir* at full development; verse four, the single blunt stroke for the ungrateful, is the portrait of the *kafūr* in one line.

**A Microcosm of the Whole Qur'an**

Read the verse as a whole and it contains, in one line, the method of the entire Book. God gives the road. The person chooses. The choice is recorded. The consequence follows. That is the pattern of every sūrah, from the longest to the shortest; 76:3 is the pattern in its most compressed form. The commentators (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) drew from it the classical distinction that the later theologians made explicit: there is a guidance of *signposting* — *hidāyat al-dalāla*, the showing of the road — which God gives to every person, and there is a guidance of *tawfīq* — the special enabling that carries the person actually onto the road and keeps him there — which God gives to whom He wills. The first is the offer; the second is the assistance. This verse, in its tense and its plainness, states the first: the road has been shown to all. The verses that follow state the second, in the form of a reward that is given to the one who has walked: *and rewarded them for having been patient with a Garden and with silk* (v. 12).

The Qur'an guards the boundary between the two with a precision that has always been the subject of its sharpest theological discussion. The Prophet ﷺ said to the man who came to him seeking a guarantee of guidance:

> *"Indeed, you do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 28:56 — the verse in which the Prophet ﷺ states the limit of his own function)

and

> *"And you do not guide whom you wish, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 34:49; cf. 35:38; 39:53)

The limit is real: the guide shows the road, but the walking is not a mechanical consequence of the showing. The same Qur'an that states the limit states, with equal plainness, the human side of the transaction:

> *"Indeed, We have shown him the way, either as grateful or as ungrateful."* (Qur'an 76:3 — this verse)

> *"So whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."* (Qur'an 18:29)

The two verses are not in tension; they are the two halves of one doctrine, and the sūrah that holds both in its thirty-one verses is the sūrah in which the doctrine is most plainly visible. The road is shown to all; the walking is the person's; the walking, once it has begun, is assisted by a guidance that is itself a gift. That is the whole of the matter, and it is the whole of the sūrah.

**The Fitrah: Why the Way Could Be Offered at All**

The verse's claim — that the way was *given* to the person who was made from a drop — presupposes a faculty in the recipient that can receive a road. The Qur'an names that faculty elsewhere, and the name is *al-fiṭra*: the natural disposition, the original orientation, the set of the human being toward its Maker before the set has been altered. The verse that states it is the verse on the creation:

> *"So direct your face toward the religion, a servant devoting himself to God — inclining to truth — the natural disposition upon which He has created [all] people. There is no alteration of the creation of God. That is the upright religion, but most people do not know."* (Qur'an 30:30)

The Prophet ﷺ gave the same doctrine its most concrete form:

> *"Every child is born upon the fitrah — the natural disposition — and it is his parents who make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658, from Abū Hurayrah)

The fitrah is thus not a blank slate; it is a *pre-set orientation*, the way the eye is pre-set toward light. The sūrah's claim that the way was "given" is the claim that the pre-set orientation was addressed: the road was shown to the one whose nature was already turned toward it. The ungrateful person, on this account, is not the one who missed the road; he is the one who *turned his face away from a road his nature was made to walk*. The tragedy of the kafūr, in the language of the roots, is that he covers a gift his own nature was made to receive.

**The Fork in the Road: Identity Made by the Response**

The final clause of the verse is the one that carries the most weight, and it is the clause the sūrah spends its remaining twenty-eight verses unpacking: *be he grateful or ungrateful.* The grammar is the device of the two *imma* — "either... or" — the Arabic's sharpest expression of a binary with no third term. There is no middle state on this account; the person is either the one who acknowledges the source or the one who covers the gift, and the sūrah's next verse shows what the second state looks like in one line: chains, shackles, and a blazing flame. The verses after that show what the first state looks like in twenty lines: the cup, the fountain, the feeding, the fear, the Garden, the shade, the clusters, the silver, the crystal, the ginger, the spring, the youths, the silk, the direct drink, and the reward held in gratitude.

The two states are, in the sūrah's logic, the two *identities* the person can take. The ungrateful person does not merely fail to be grateful; he becomes the kafūr, the one who covers, and the covering, once it has become the habit, becomes the character, and the character becomes the fate. The grateful person does not merely feel thanks; he becomes the shākhir, the one who acknowledges, and the acknowledgment, once it has become the habit, becomes the orientation, and the orientation becomes the end. The sūrah's central claim — the claim that the middle section of the chapter is devoted to proving in detail — is that the identity is *made by the response*: the person is not found already grateful or already ungrateful; the person is *made* by what he does with the road he was given.

The modern reader meets the claim in the form of the familiar question: *who are you when no one is watching?* The sūrah answers the question before it is asked: you are the one who was given the road, and you are the one who is walking it, and the walking is the making. The two words of the verse — *shākhir*, *kafūr* — are the two names the making can take, and the sūrah's remaining verses are the two portraits, drawn in full, of the two people the making produces. The next verse gives the ungrateful his line.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:4

> **We have surely prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing flame.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Line for the Ungrateful, and Why It Is Only One Line**

The sūrah has spent three verses laying down the human story — the unremembered thing, the mixed drop, the shown road, the two responses — and now, at the fork it has just drawn, it gives the two branches their first concrete form. The second branch receives eighteen verses. The first receives this one. The asymmetry is not an oversight; it is the argument. The fate of the ungrateful is stated in a single line because, on the sūrah's account, it is a single thing: binding, confinement, burning. There is no table, no fountain, no conversation, no garden, no shade, no garment, no guest, no Lord who appears in person. There are chains, shackles, and fire — and the line is finished. The fate of the grateful, by contrast, is so various that it takes eighteen verses to describe, and the sūrah spends them all. The structure says what the content will prove: the life of the pious is full, and the life of the kafūr is empty; the one line is a statement of the emptiness.

The sūrah's companion, al-Qayyimah, states the same contrast in the same form, in the verse that is the closest parallel to this one in the whole Book:

> *"And for those who disbelieved in the Day of Judgment — the Fire is their provision. Every time their skins are scorched, We will replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment."* (Qur'an 4:56)

The one line of the ungrateful is thus not a threat added to the warning; it is the warning's form. The covering, once it has become the habit, becomes the character, and the character becomes the end. The sūrah's purpose in stating the line once, and then turning away from it for eighteen verses, is not to soften the warning; it is to show the reader where the other road leads, so that the road he chooses to walk is chosen with his eyes open. The warning is complete in one line because the warning is one thing: the covering is the end. Everything else in the sūrah is the description of the other thing, and the other thing takes eighteen verses to state.

**Chains and Shackles: The Iron of Accountability**

The two nouns in the middle of the line — *asālihan wa-ghulālin*, "chains and shackles" — are the Qur'an's standard vocabulary for the binding of the disbeliever on the Day. The fullest description is in the chapter on Ghāfir, at the point where the wrongdoer is taken. The sūrah states the delay first:

> *"And never think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare in horror."* (Qur'an 40:42)

and then the entry, in the same chapter, in the verse that gives the binding its form:

> *"And certainly, those who wronged will enter the Hellfire, burning [in it]."* (Qur'an 40:70)

The binding itself is described in the passage immediately after, where the chain of iron, whose link is forty cubits, is fastened upon the one who has been taken (cf. 40:71–72). The forty-cubit link is the detail the tradition has never let go: a length the body cannot bend, cannot shorten, cannot slip. The binding is not a punishment added to the burning; it is the burning's *form* — the shape the soul takes when it has spent a lifetime covering the gift.

The same image appears in the chapter on the Inclinings, at the moment of the Assembly, in the verse that gives the fire its two conditions — the absence of the speaker and the restoration of the skin:

> *"And every time their skins are scorched, We will replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment."* (Qur'an 4:56)

The logic of the image is worth stating plainly, because it is not arbitrary. The body, the sūrah has already said (in verse two), is the instrument of the test: the hand that reaches, the eye that sees, the tongue that speaks, the foot that walks. The person who spent his life using those instruments for the covering — for the gift denied, for the orphan turned away, for the road refused — has made his body the architecture of the covering. The Day of Assembly gives that architecture its consequence: the hand that refused is bound, the body that served the covering is taken in the chain. The binding is not vengeance; it is the shape of the life, made visible. The same principle governs the other side of the fork, and the sūrah will show it in the reverse: the body that served the acknowledgment — the hand that fed the captive, the stomach that kept the vow, the eye that wept for the Day — is the body that receives the light and the joy and the low-hanging cluster. The instrument is the same; the service is different; and the service is what the Day reads.

**The Blazing Flame: Jāhatan Saʿīran**

The third element of the line — *jāhatan saʿīran*, "a blazing flame" — is the fire itself, and the two words together carry the description. *Jāh* is the blaze, the glowing face of the fire, the surface from which the heat is given; *saʿīr* is the burning that sizzles, from the root *ṣ-ʿ-r*, the root of the searing that does not merely heat but *consumes the surface*. The Qur'an uses the same combination for the Fire in the chapter on the Overwhelming, where the fuel is named:

> *"And scalding water, and a black smoke, [prepared] for the one who labored and was sinful, who denied the Great [judgment]."* (Qur'an 88:5–8)

and in the chapter on the Striking, where the fuel is given its most concrete form:

> *"And a Fire whose fuel is men and stones, numbered."* (Qur'an 101:11)

The fire is, in the Qur'an's consistent imagery, the place where the covering meets its consequence: the one who hid the gift is given the fire, and the fire is the covering made real — the self, enclosed in itself, burning in the enclosure. The sūrah on the Standing gives the same fire the same terms, in the verse quoted above, which is the closest companion to this line in the whole Book. The one line of the ungrateful — chains, shackles, fire — is the threefold statement of that state: bound, enclosed, burning. And the sūrah does not add a fourth, because there is no fourth. The emptiness is complete, and the line is finished.

**The Justice of the Line**

The one line is, in the sūrah's design, a statement of Divine justice in its most compact form. The fire is not arbitrary; it is the consequence of the covering, made visible. The chain is not added; it is the shape of the life, fastened. The sūrah states the principle of the justice elsewhere, in the verse that is the standard formula of the Qur'an's eschatological ethics:

> *"The Day when every soul will be recompensed for what it has earned; that Day, no injustice [is done]. Indeed, God is swift in account."* (Qur'an 40:17)

and in the verse that states the scale of the precision:

> *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (Qur'an 99:7–8)

The one line of the ungrateful is thus not a threat; it is a statement of the law. The person who spent his life covering the gift will, on the Day, meet the covering in its full form. The sūrah's purpose in stating the line once, and then turning away from it for eighteen verses, is not to soften the warning; it is to show the reader where the other road leads, so that the road he chooses to walk is chosen with his eyes open. The warning is complete in one line because the warning is one thing: the covering is the end. Everything else in the sūrah is the description of the other thing, and the other thing takes eighteen verses to state.

**The Line's Place in the Argument**

The one line is placed exactly where the sūrah's argument requires it: after the fork (verse three), before the portrait of the pious (verse five). The reader has just been told that the person is either grateful or ungrateful; the sūrah now shows the two branches in their first concrete form, and it shows the ungrateful first, in one line, so that the reader enters the portrait of the pious with the other road in his mind. The structure is the Qur'an's standing method: the warning precedes the promise, and the promise is given the full weight of the description, because the purpose of the sūrah is to draw the reader to the cup, not to the chain. The one line is the threshold; the eighteen verses are the room. The reader who crosses the threshold enters a different world, and the world is described in detail, because the world is real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be in it.

The next verse opens the room.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:5

> **Truly the pious drink of a cup whose mingling is camphor.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Abrār: The Name of the Rank the Sūrah Is About to Describe**

The verse begins with the name that gives the sūrah one of its titles: *al-abrār*, "the pious." The word is the plural of *barr*, and the root *b-r-r* carries, in the Arabic, the sense of *breadth, amplitude, generosity* — the quality that is wide, that is ample, that is expansive. The source commentary notes that the word connotes *beneficence, magnanimity, and the associated virtues*, and that it is the same root from which *birr* — piety, righteousness, the good deed — is derived. The pious, on this account, are not merely the cautious; they are the *generous*, the ones whose inner life is wide enough to hold the giving. The distinction matters, and the sūrah builds its whole portrait on it: the pious are not the ones who keep the minimum; they are the ones whose character has become the giving.

The same root is applied to God, and the application is the key to the human use. The Qur'an names God *al-Barr* — the Righteous, the Generous — in the chapter on the Inclinings:

> *"Indeed, He is the Righteous, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 52:28)

and the tradition preserves the phrase *Allāhu yabarru biʿibādihi* — "God is righteous unto His servants" — as a standing expression of the relation: the human *birr* is the reflection of the Divine *birr*, the creature's generosity answering the Creator's. The pious, in the sūrah's usage, are thus the ones who have taken on the quality of their Maker in the one domain where the creature is permitted to take it on: the domain of the giving. The sūrah that will in verse nine put the words "We feed you only for the Face of God" into the mouths of the pious is the sūrah that has already named them by the root that means *breadth of the giving*, and the naming is the description.

The source commentary points to the verse in al-Baqarah that defines *birr* in its fullness, and the definition is the standard one:

> *"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but [true] righteousness is [in] one who believes in God and the Last Day and the angels and the Book and the prophets and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask [for assistance], and for freeing [a captive]; and establishes prayer and gives zakāh; and those who fulfill their commitment when they commit; and the patient in poverty and hardship and during battle. Those are the truthful and the righteous."* (Qur'an 2:177)

Read the definition against the sūrah's portrait and the correspondence is exact: the pious of 76:5–22 are the ones who give wealth *in spite of love for it* (v. 8), who fulfill their commitment (v. 7), who are patient (v. 12), and who believe in the Last Day (v. 10). The sūrah is the portrait of the verse that defines piety, drawn in the present tense.

**The Cup and Its Mingling: Māzājhā Kāfūran**

The verse moves immediately to the image: *yashrabūna min kāsin kānā mizājuhā kāfūran* — "they drink of a cup whose mingling is camphor." The two nouns carry the description. *Kās* is the cup, and in the Qur'an's consistent usage the cup names what it contains: the drink, the life, the endowment. The source commentary notes that the word here refers to what the cup contains — the paradisal wine — and the same device appears in the chapter on the Inclinings, where the cups of the Garden are served to the people of the Garden:

> *"And they are served therein with cups of silver and bowls that are like crystal."* (Qur'an 53:28)

The same camphor spring appears in the chapter on the Inclinings, at the point where the people of the Garden are given their drink:

> *"And a spring whose mingling is camphor, which the servants of God drink, [being] gushing springs, measured [with measure]."* (Qur'an 83:25–26)

and in the chapter on the Overwhelming, in the verse that gives the spring its form:

> *"And a spring whose mingling is camphor, which the servants of God drink, [being] gushing springs, measured [with measure]."* (Qur'an 88:14–15)

*Mizāj* — the mingling — is the word for the *mixing agent* in the Arab practice of the cup: the wine of this world was mixed, in the custom of the age, with water and with aromatics, and the quality of the mixture determined the quality of the drink. The *mizāj* was thus not a detail; it was the definition of the cup. The sūrah's choice of the word is therefore a statement: the cup of the pious is defined by its mingling, and the mingling is camphor. The drink is not raw; it is *composed*. The life it names is not a brute pleasure; it is a pleasure that has been *made* — the way the cup is made by the mingling.

*Kāfūr* — camphor — is the substance that gives the mingling its name. The source notes that in the medieval period camphor was used widely, in sweet and savory dishes, and was thought to have cooling properties. The substance is known to the reader as a spice and a medicine; the Qur'an gives it its eschatological role here, and in the two passages just quoted. The camphor in the cup is the element that *cools*: it takes the raw heat of the wine and turns it into the drink that refreshes rather than inflames. The sūrah's portrait of the pious, in this verse, is thus a portrait of the *cooled desire*: the desire that has been mixed, composed, and turned from the inflaming into the refreshing. The one who drinks the cup whose mingling is camphor is the one whose desire has been made into a drink, and the drink is the life.

**Why a Cup, and Why Its Mingling: The Image and the Argument**

The image of the cup is not a decoration; it is the argument. The cup is the form of the *life*: it is held, it is offered, it is drunk, it is refilled. The sūrah chooses the cup for the pious because the pious are, in the sūrah's portrait, the ones whose lives are *offered* to them: the life is not seized, it is given; the life is not raw, it is composed; the life is not finite, it is refilled. The mingling is the element that makes the life a *life* rather than a mere substance: the way the camphor makes the wine a drink, the acknowledgment (the *shukr* of verse three) makes the life a *life*. The pious drink a life that has been mixed with the acknowledgment, and the mixing is the camphor: it cools the desire, it composes the pleasure, it turns the raw into the drink.

The source commentary records the spiritual reading the contemplative tradition drew from the verse, and the reading is worth stating because it shows the depth of the image. On that reading, the cup whose mingling is camphor is the cup of the lover of the *Attributes* of God — the one who experiences God in the attributes, in the qualities, in the names, and who drinks a life that is *mixed* with the experience of the attributes. The pure spring, which the next verse gives to the *servants of God* — the *ʿibād Allāh* — is the cup of the lover of the *Essence* — the one whose love endures regardless of which attribute he experiences. The two drinks are thus the two levels of the same reality: the mixed cup is the life lived in the experience of the attributes; the pure spring is the life lived in the knowledge of the Essence. The sūrah gives the first to the pious, and it will give the second to the servants of God in the next verse, and the two verses together are the sūrah's statement of the two levels of the blessed life.

**The Hadith of the Cups of the Garden**

The tradition gives the image its concrete form in the hadith literature. The Prophet ﷺ described the cup of the Garden in terms that match the sūrah's choice of the image:

> *"The cup in the Garden is of a heart-pearl, red in color, the like of which has never been seen by any eye, nor has any ear heard of it."* (recorded by Muslim, from Abū Dharr al-Ghfārī; the report is part of the longer description of the Garden given in the Book of Paradise)

The heart-pearl is the substance that the sūrah's camphor mingling is made of: the cup is not silver, not gold, not glass — it is the substance that the world does not know, and the mingling is the camphor, the cooling, the composing. The hadith gives the image its concrete form, and the sūrah gives the hadith its theological form: the cup is the life, the mingling is the acknowledgment, and the life is the drink.

**From the First Cup to the Full Table**

The verse is the first of the eighteen verses that describe the pious, and it opens the description at the cup. The structure of the description is worth noting, because the sūrah gives the portrait in the order of the *feast*: the drink (verses five to six, and again in verses fifteen to eighteen), the setting (the shade and the clusters, verses thirteen to fourteen), the service (the immortal youths, verse nineteen), the garment (the green silk and the brocade, verse twenty-one), the reward (the accounting, verse twenty-two). The sūrah does not give the portrait in the order of the *soul*; it gives it in the order of the *table*, because the pious are, in the sūrah's account, the ones who are *served*, and the service is the life.

The next verse gives the spring that the cup is filled from, and it gives the spring to the *servants of God* — the *ʿibād Allāh* — and the distinction between the pious and the servants of God is the distinction the sūrah will state in the remaining verses. The reader who enters the room at verse five is standing at the first of the many stations of the portrait, and the portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:6

> **a fountain whereof drink the servants of God, while it gushes forth in gushing springs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿIbādu Llāh: The Second Name of the Blessed, and Why It Is a Different One**

The verse shifts the address within the same portrait. The pious of the previous verse — *al-abrār*, the generous ones, the ones whose inner life is wide enough to hold the giving — are now named by a second title: *ʿibādu Llāh*, "the servants of God." The two names are not interchangeable, and the sūrah's movement from one to the other is the movement of the portrait from the *character* to the *relation*. The *abrār* are named for what they do — the breadth of the giving; the *ʿibād* are named for whom they do it — the One to whom the giving is directed. The same persons carry both names, and the carrying of both is the sūrah's definition of the blessed life: a life that is generous in its form and directed in its end.

The title *ʿibādu Llāh* is the Qur'an's highest and most specific name for the elect, and the Qur'an gives it to them at the moments of their greatest distinction. In the chapter on Maryam, the righteous are named by it at the moment of their assurance:

> *"And as for the righteous, We shall give them the highest rank."* (Qur'an 19:58 — the verse that closes the description of the righteous with their title and their rank)

and in the chapter on ʿImrān, the righteous servants are named by it at the moment of their freedom from fear:

> *"And the righteous servants of God — upon them is no fear, nor will they grieve."* (Qur'an 3:35 — the verse that gives the title its two marks: the absence of the fear, and the absence of the grief)

and in the chapter on the Confederates, the men and women who believe are named by it at the moment of their promise:

> *"And the believing men and the believing women, some being allies of others — the righteous men and the righteous women — for those, God has prepared forgiveness and a great reward."* (Qur'an 33:35 — the verse that closes with the title of the elect and the promise of the reward)

The title is thus not a casual honorific; it is the name the Qur'an gives to the ones who have entered the relation of the *ʿabd* — the one whose orientation is fixed, whose service has become his identity, whose life is the giving directed to the One who is the giving. The sūrah's movement from *al-abrār* to *ʿibādu Llāh* is the movement from the description of the character to the naming of the relation, and the naming is the description.

**The Fountain and the Gushing: ʿaynan Mafāʿīnā**

The verse gives the *ʿibād* not a cup but a fountain: *ʿaynan yashrabu bihā ʿibādu Llāhi fa-tafjuru mafāʿīnā* — "a fountain whereof drink the servants of God, while it gushes forth in gushing springs." The two nouns carry the description, and the distinction between them is the distinction the sūrah is making. *Kās* — the cup — is the *serving*: the drink is held, it is offered, it is drunk, it is refilled. *ʿAyn* — the fountain — is the *source*: the drink is not served, it is *given*; it is not a portion, it is a *channel*. The pious are given the cup in verse five; the servants of God are given the fountain in verse six, and the movement from the cup to the fountain is the movement from the *portion* to the *source*, from the *serving* to the *giving*.

*Fa-tafjuru mafāʿīnā* — "while it gushes forth in gushing springs" — is the clause that gives the fountain its form. *Mafāʿīn* is the plural of *mafqar*, the spring that gushes, the source that flows; and the verb *t-f-j-r* is the verb of the *gushing*, the breaking forth, the flowing that does not stop. The fountain of the servants of God is thus not a single spring; it is a *system of springs*, a network of sources that gushes forth, that flows, that does not stop. The sūrah's description of the fountain is thus a description of the *abundance*: the life of the servants of God is not a ration, it is a source; it is not a portion, it is a channel; it is not a serving, it is a giving.

The same fountain appears in the chapter on the Overwhelming, in the verse that gives it its form:

> *"And a fountain whereof drink the servants of God, and it gushes forth in gushing springs, and [they are given] fruits and wheat."* (Qur'an 88:6–7 — the verse that gives the fountain and the fruits together, in the same order as this sūrah)

and the tradition gives the fountain its concrete form in the hadith literature. The Prophet ﷺ described the fountain of the Garden in terms that match the sūrah's choice of the image:

> *"Whoever says 'al-ḥamdu lillāh' — two fountains from Salsabīl are set up for him between his two eyes."* (recorded by al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah; graded *ḥasan* by al-Tirmidhī)

The two fountains are the concrete form of the *mafāʿīnā* of the sūrah: the system of springs, the network of sources, the abundance that does not stop. The hadith gives the image its concrete form, and the sūrah gives the hadith its theological form: the fountain is the life, the gushing is the giving, and the life is the drink.

**From the Cup to the Fountain: The Ascent of the Blessing**

The movement from verse five to verse six is the movement of the sūrah's argument: from the *portion* to the *source*, from the *serving* to the *giving*. The pious are given the cup — the drink that is mixed, that is composed, that is served; the servants of God are given the fountain — the drink that is pure, that is gushing, that is given. The movement is thus the movement of the blessing: the blessing is not a fixed quantity, it is a channel; it is not a portion, it is a source; it is not a serving, it is a giving. The sūrah's law of increase, stated in the verse on the gratitude, is thus the law of the fountain:

> *"If you are grateful, I will surely increase you; but if you are ungrateful, indeed My punishment is severe."* (Qur'an 14:7)

The one who is grateful is the one whose channel is open, and the open channel carries more; the one who is ungrateful is the one whose channel is closed, and the closed channel carries nothing. The fountain of the servants of God is thus the fountain of the increase: the life that is grateful is the life that is increased, and the increase is the gushing, the flowing, the abundance that does not stop.

The source commentary records the spiritual reading the contemplative tradition drew from the movement, and the reading is worth stating because it shows the depth of the image. On that reading, the cup whose mingling is camphor (verse five) is the cup of the lover of the *Attributes* of God — the one who experiences God in the attributes, in the qualities, in the names, and who drinks a life that is *mixed* with the experience of the attributes. The pure spring (verse six) is the cup of the lover of the *Essence* — the one whose love endures regardless of which attribute he experiences. The two drinks are thus the two levels of the same reality: the mixed cup is the life lived in the experience of the attributes; the pure spring is the life lived in the knowledge of the Essence. The sūrah gives the first to the pious, and it gives the second to the servants of God, and the two verses together are the sūrah's statement of the two levels of the blessed life.

**The Servants of God in History: The Household of the Sūrah**

The title *ʿibādu Llāh* is, in the sūrah's own occasion, the title of the Household of the Prophet: ʿAlī, Fāṭimah, Ḥasan, and Ḥusayn — the ones whose bread had been given away three times in three days, whose eldest woman was praying with an empty stomach, whose children shook with hunger. The title of the highest rank is thus given to the most empty household in Madinah at that hour, and the giving of the title is the statement of the sūrah's central paradox: the one who is the *ʿabd* is the one who has nothing to depend on but God, and the emptiness is the qualification. The servant of God is not the one who has much; he is the one whose orientation is fixed, whose service has become his identity, whose life is the giving directed to the One who is the giving. The Household of the Prophet is thus the concrete form of the *ʿibād*: the ones who are empty, the ones who are fixed, the ones who are directed.

The sūrah's portrait of the servants of God is thus a portrait of the *relation*, and the relation is the *ʿabd* — the one whose orientation is fixed, whose service has become his identity, whose life is the giving directed to the One who is the giving. The next verse gives the character of the servants of God: the ones who fulfill their vows, the ones who fear the Day, the ones who feed the indigent, the orphan, and the captive. The portrait is real, because the servants of God are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:7

> **They fulfill their vows and fear a day whose evil is widespread,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yufīūna bil-Nadhri: The Vow Kept in Hunger**

The verse now leaves the description of the drink and turns to the description of the drinker. The first clause is the first of the two marks of the pious, and it is the mark that the sūrah's own occasion makes concrete: *yufīūna bil-nadhri* — "they fulfill their vows." In the sūrah's own story, the vow is the three-day fast that ʿAlī, Fāṭimah, and Fizzah kept for the recovery of Ḥasan and Ḥusayn — a fast kept in hunger, a fast kept in the face of the need, a fast kept three times in three days, each day with a visitor at the door and a loaf given away. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the Household's deed, and the deed is the vow: the pledge made to God for the children's recovery, and the keeping of the pledge in the face of the hunger.

*Naḍr* — the vow — is the Arabic word for the *binding pledge*: a promise made to God, a commitment that is not optional, a bond that the maker of the vow has fastened upon himself. The vow is thus not a casual intention; it is a *fastening*, a *binding*, a *pledge*. The one who makes the vow is the one who has bound himself to God, and the one who keeps the vow is the one who has honored the binding. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the *honor of the binding*: the pious are the ones who honor the binding, who fasten the pledge, who keep the word.

The source commentary notes that the vow is interpreted in two ways: as the specific vow of the Household (the three-day fast), and as the general meaning of *obedience to God and the fulfillment of the obligations of religion* (the reading the source assigns to Ṭ). The two readings are not rivals; they are the same deed seen from two sides. The specific vow of the Household is the concrete form of the general obedience: the fast is the obedience, and the obedience is the fast. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the *obedience in its concrete form*, and the concrete form is the deed.

**The Vow Older Than Man: The Primordial Covenant**

The source commentary records a further reading, and the reading is the one that gives the vow its deepest form. On that reading, the vow is not the specific pledge of the Household; it is the *primordial covenant*, the covenant made before the creation of the world, the covenant that the human soul made with God before it entered the body. The Qur'an states the covenant in the verse on the covenant:

> *"And [mention, O Muhammad], when your Lord took from the children of Adam — from their loins — their descendants and made them testify of themselves, [saying to them], 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yes, we have testified.' [This] — so you will not say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Indeed, I was unaware of this,' or that you will not say, 'Indeed, I did not associate [anything] with You.'"* (Qur'an 7:172)

The covenant is thus the vow that the human soul made before it entered the body, and the keeping of the covenant is the keeping of the vow. The pious of the sūrah are thus the ones who keep the covenant, who honor the binding, who fasten the pledge. The vow is thus not a casual intention; it is the *covenant*, the *binding*, the *pledge* that the human soul made with God before it entered the body.

The tradition gives the covenant its concrete form in the tafsīr literature, in the report that the soul said, when it was asked, "Am I not your Lord?" — "Yes, we have testified" — and the report is preserved in the commentary tradition, though the hadith critics have noted a weakness in the chain. The meaning of the report, however, is supported by the Qur'an itself, and the meaning is the statement of the *covenant*: the human soul is the one who has made the pledge, and the keeping of the pledge is the keeping of the vow.

**The Psychology of the Kept Vow: Trust and Character**

The vow is, in the sūrah's design, the *test of the character*: the pledge made in ease, and the keeping of the pledge in the face of the hunger. The one who makes the vow in ease is the one who has the capacity to make the pledge; the one who keeps the vow in hunger is the one who has the character to keep the pledge. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the *character*: the pious are the ones who have the character to keep the pledge, who honor the binding, who fasten the word.

The Qur'an states the law of the kept vow in the verse on the covenant:

> *"And fulfill the covenant, indeed the covenant will be [called to] account."* (Qur'an 17:34)

The covenant is thus not a casual intention; it is a *binding*, and the binding is *accounted for*. The one who keeps the covenant is the one who is *accounted for*, and the one who breaks the covenant is the one who is *accounted for* in the other sense. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the *accounting*: the pious are the ones who are *accounted for* in the sense of the keeping, who honor the binding, who fasten the word.

The tradition gives the vow its concrete form in the hadith literature, in the report that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"There is no vow in calamity; vows are only made in what one vows to do."* (al-Bukhārī 6697, from Abū Hurayrah)

The report gives the vow its form: the vow is not the pledge made in the face of the calamity; it is the pledge made in the face of the *doing*. The one who vows to do the deed is the one who has the character to keep the pledge, and the one who keeps the pledge is the one who has the character to do the deed. The sūrah's "they fulfill their vows" is thus the statement of the *doing*: the pious are the ones who have the character to do the deed, who honor the binding, who fasten the word.

**Yakhāfūna Yawman Kānat Sharruhū Muntasīran: The Fear That Works**

The second clause of the verse gives the pious their second mark: *wa-yakhāfūna yawman kānat sharruhū muntasīran* — "and they fear a Day whose evil is widespread." The two words carry the description. *Yakhāfūna* — "they fear" — is the verb of the *active fear*: the fear that is not the anxiety, the fear that is the *reverence*, the fear that is the *care*, the fear that is the *vigilance*. The pious are thus not the ones who are anxious; they are the ones who are *reverent*, who are *careful*, who are *vigilant*. The fear is thus not the anxiety; it is the *reverence*, the *care*, the *vigilance*.

*Yawman kānat sharruhū muntasīran* — "a Day whose evil is widespread" — is the clause that gives the fear its object. *Muntasīr* is from the root *n-s-r*, the root of the *support*, the *help*, the *spreading*; and *muntasīr* is the *spreading*, the *expanding*, the *covering*. The Day is thus not a Day whose evil is contained; it is a Day whose evil is *spreading*, *expanding*, *covering*. The evil of the Day is thus not a single thing; it is a *spreading*, a *covering*, a *spreading that does not stop*. The pious are thus the ones who fear the *spreading*, the *covering*, the *spreading that does not stop*.

The same Day appears in the chapter on the Frowning, in the verse that gives it its form:

> *"On the Day they come with frowning faces, dejected, they will be given to drink from a gushing spring."* (Qur'an 80:37–38 — the verse that gives the Day and the frowning together, in the same terms as this sūrah)

The Day is thus not a Day whose evil is contained; it is a Day whose evil is *spreading*, and the spreading is the *frowning*, the *dejection*, the *gushing*. The pious are thus the ones who fear the *frowning*, the *dejection*, the *gushing*.

**Fear and Hope: The Two Wings of the Pious**

The pious are thus the ones who drink and fear: they are given the cup and the fountain, and they fear the Day. The paradox is the statement of the sūrah: the pious are not the ones who are assured and do not fear; they are the ones who are assured and fear. The assurance is the *drinking*, and the fear is the *reverence*; and the two together are the *character* of the pious. The one who is assured and does not fear is the one who is *presumptuous*; the one who fears and is not assured is the one who is *anxious*; and the one who is assured and fears is the one who is *reverent*. The pious are thus the ones who are *reverent*, and the reverence is the *character*.

The Qur'an states the law of the two wings in the verse on the fear and the hope:

> *"And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth in humility, and when those who are ignorant address them [they say], 'Peace.'"* (Qur'an 25:63 — the verse that gives the *ʿibād* their character: the humility, the peace, the reverence)

The pious are thus the ones who walk in humility, who give peace, who are reverent. The fear is thus not the anxiety; it is the *reverence*, the *humility*, the *peace*. And the next verse gives the pious their third mark: the ones who feed, despite loving the food, the indigent, the orphan, and the captive. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:8

> **and give food, despite loving it, to the indigent, the orphan, and the captive.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Clause That Measures the Heart**

Of all the verses in the portrait of the pious, this is the one that gives the whole chapter its moral center of gravity. The pious have been described in terms of what they drink, what they fear, and what they are promised; here the sūrah describes them in terms of a single, concrete, repeated act: *they give food*. And the sūrah does not let the act pass without the qualifier that makes it meaningful — *ʿalā ḥubbihī*, "despite loving it." The source commentary offers a second reading of the same prepositional phrase, "out of love for Him," and the two readings, held together, state the sūrah's entire economics of the heart. The food is given *in spite of* the love for it, so that the love becomes the cost; and it is given *out of love for* the One to whom the giving is directed, so that a higher love displaces the lower. The pious are the people for whom the thing they love most at the table is the thing they surrender most readily.

**The Loved Thing Is the Standard**

Why does the sūrah insist on the "despite loving it"? Because the Qur'an knows that a gift of what you do not value is no gift at all. It states the principle in the most direct form in the chapter on ʿImrān:

> *"You will never attain righteousness until you spend out of that which you love. And whatever you spend — God is aware of it."* (Qur'an 3:92)

The verse fixes the standard of the giving at the point of the love. A man who gives bread he had no appetite for has given nothing that cost him; a man who gives the bread he was hungry for has given something that *cost*. The sūrah's "despite loving it" is therefore not a minor detail; it is the *definition* of the act. It is the difference between the alms of the rich man who scatters what is surplus to him and the alms of the poor man who breaks the loaf that is his dinner. The whole occasion of the sūrah — the Household that gave away its only bread three times, and drank water for three nights — is the living proof that the sūrah means the second kind of giving, the kind that hurts.

**The Indigent: The Poor Man No One Has Named**

The first of the three recipients is *al-miskīn* — the indigent. The word in ordinary usage points to the beggar, the visible poor man with the outstretched hand; but the hadith the source cites corrects that usage, and the correction is one of the most cited in the entire prophetic literature:

> *"The indigent is not the one who goes about among the people asking for a mouthful or two, or a date or two; rather, the indigent is the one who has not enough to satisfy his need, whose condition is not known to others so that they may give him charity, and who does not beg of people."* (al-Bukhārī 1443; Muslim 1035, from Abū Hurayrah)

The indigent, on this account, is the *hidden* poor — the one whose hunger is a private matter, the one who has no claim on anyone's pity, the one the eye of the community has not yet fixed upon. To give to the indigent, then, is to give to the one who will never know your name, and who never asked. It is the giving that is not purchased by gratitude, not bought by recognition, not repaid in social credit. The sūrah names this category first because it is the hardest: the visible beggar is easy to serve, because serving him is public; the hidden poor is hard to serve, because serving him requires that you *see* a need no one has announced.

**The Orphan: The Child Who Cannot Advocate for Himself**

The second recipient is *al-yatīm* — the orphan, the child who has lost the protection of the father and who therefore stands at the mercy of others. The Qur'an gives the orphan a place of unusual weight in its eschatological warnings, so that the treatment of the orphan becomes one of the marks by which a person's final standing is read. In the chapter on al-Layl, the "hard road" that separates the two destinies is defined in part by the orphan:

> *"And what will make you know what that is? [It is] ill-treating an orphan, and not encouraging the feeding of the poor."* (Qur'an 92:8–9)

The orphan is the one who cannot plead his own case, who has no voice in the assembly, who depends entirely on the goodwill of those around him. To feed the orphan is therefore to feed the *defenseless* — the person for whom the social order has removed every natural protector except the stranger's kindness. The tradition magnifies the reward of the one who stands in the father's place for such a child. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"I and the guardian of the orphan will be in Paradise like these two fingers,"* — and he held up his index and middle finger, close together. (al-Bukhārī 5359; Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah)

The proximity of the two fingers in the gesture is the point: the guardian of the orphan is placed *next to* the Prophet ﷺ, in the same rank, in the same Garden. The sūrah's single word for the orphan thus carries, in the prophetic tradition, the weight of an eschatological promise.

**The Captive: The Enemy Who Owes You Nothing**

The third recipient is the most startling of the three: *al-asīr* — the captive, the prisoner of war. The source commentary supplies the historical context, and the context is what makes the verse remarkable. At the time of the sūrah's revelation the Muslims of Madinah had no prisons; the captives taken in battle were *distributed* among the believers, and each household was expected to house and feed the prisoner entrusted to it. Many of these captives were men who had fought against the Muslims, who had opposed the Prophet ﷺ and his community. The verse, in this light, is commanding the believer to set at his own table a man who may have come to kill him — and to feed him the food he loves, in spite of loving it himself.

This is the verse's quiet radicalism, and it is worth naming. The sūrah does not ask the believer to love the captive; it asks him to *feed* the captive. The distinction matters. Loving an enemy is a feeling that no one can be commanded to feel; feeding an enemy is a deed that can be commanded and can be done even against the grain of the feeling. The sūrah legislates the deed, not the affection, because the deed is what is in the believer's power. The Qur'an states the governing principle in the chapter on al-Mumtaḥanah, where it addresses the just treatment of those with whom there is a treaty:

> *"There is no blame upon you for being kind to those with whom there is a treaty [of peace] with God, and for being just toward them. Indeed, God loves those who are just."* (Qur'an 60:8)

Justice toward the stranger, even the hostile stranger, is named as the quality God *loves*. The captive at the door of the believer's house is the extreme case of that justice: the one who owes nothing, the one who may have done harm, the one who cannot repay. To feed him is to give where no return is possible, and that is precisely the giving the sūrah is describing.

**Three Doors, Three Directions of the Gift**

Read the three recipients together and they map the three directions in which a heart can be tested. The indigent is the *hidden* — the test of *sight*, of whether the believer can perceive a need that is not announced. The orphan is the *dependent* — the test of *tenderness*, of whether the believer will protect the one who has no protector. The captive is the *hostile* — the test of *courage*, of whether the believer will be generous to the one who has no claim and may have done harm. The sūrah's single verse thus compresses a complete social ethic: see the hidden, tend to the dependent, and have the courage to feed the hostile. The Household of the Prophet, in its three days of hunger, walked through all three doors: the indigent came on the first day, the orphan on the second, the captive on the third, and each time the bread was given and the night was water. The verse is their deed, named.

**The Modern Table**

The modern reader will recognize the three categories as the three blind spots of the generous life. The hidden poor are everywhere, invisible because they do not ask; the dependent child is the one for whom society has removed the natural advocate; and the hostile stranger is the one to whom generosity costs the most, because there is no safety in it. The sūrah does not offer a theory of philanthropy; it offers a menu of three recipients, in a deliberate order, and it puts the qualifier "despite loving it" in front of all three. The measure of the heart, on the sūrah's account, is not the amount given but the cost of the giving — and the cost is highest at exactly the three doors the verse names. The next verse gives the pious the words they say as they hand over the bread.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:9

> **"We feed you only for the Face of God. We do not desire any recompense or thanks from you."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wajh Llāh: The Name That Is the Reason**

The verse gives the pious their words, and the words are the reason. The first clause is the reason: *innamā nuʾṭīlumumu wajha Llāh* — "We feed you only for the Face of God." The phrase that carries the verse is *wajh Llāh* — "the Face of God." The source commentary records the discussion of the phrase, and the discussion is the one that gives the phrase its form. In the Arabic, the "face" (*wajh*) is understood as a reference to the *true nature of a thing*: the face is the essence, the reality, the thing that is the thing. The Face of God is thus a reference to the *Divine Essence*, or to the *Divine Essence, Attributes, and Qualities*, or to that aspect of the Divine Reality that is turned to creation. The pious are thus the ones who feed for the *true nature of God*, for the *Divine Essence*, for the *aspect of the Divine Reality that is turned to creation*.

The Qur'an states the law of the Face in the chapter on al-Qamar, in the verse that gives the Face its form on the Day:

> *"On the Day when faces will be radiant, looking toward their Lord."* (Qur'an 28:88 — the verse that gives the Face its form, in the context of the Day)

and in the chapter on al-Rahmān, in the verse that gives the Face its permanence:

> *"And all will come to Him humbled. And the Face of your Lord will remain, full of Majesty and Honor."* (Qur'an 55:26–27 — the verse that gives the Face its permanence, and the permanence its form)

The Face is thus not a casual reference; it is the *true nature of God*, the *Divine Essence*, the *aspect of the Divine Reality that is turned to creation*. The pious are thus the ones who feed for the *true nature of God*, and the feeding is the *direction*, and the direction is the *character*.

**The Juxtaposition: Reward from God, Not from Man**

The source commentary records a further reading of the phrase, and the reading is the one that gives the phrase its second form. On that reading, the pious do seek recompense, but they seek it from God, not from man. The juxtaposition is thus not the absence of the recompense; it is the *direction* of the recompense. The pious are thus the ones who seek the recompense from God, not from man, and the seeking is the *direction*, and the direction is the *character*.

The Qur'an states the law of the recompense from God in the chapter on al-Baqarah, in the verse that gives the recompense its form:

> *"Whoever spends in secret his wealth with the poor, and does not follow up his charity with reminder or hurt — that is better for him. And God will erase their sins, and He will give them reward for their iḥsān."* (Qur'an 2:272 — the verse that gives the recompense its form, and the form its direction)

and in the chapter on al-Rūm, in the verse that gives the good loan its form:

> *"The good loan that you give so that you may increase it for the sake of God — that is multiplied [for you], and God is Reducing [from the punishment] and Forgiving."* (Qur'an 30:39 — the verse that gives the good loan its form, and the form its multiplication)

The pious are thus the ones who give the good loan, who seek the recompense from God, who do not follow up their charity with reminder or hurt. The recompense is thus not the absence; it is the *direction*, and the direction is God.

**Lā Nardī Min-kum Jazāʾan Wa-lā Shukūran: The Gift Without the Return**

The second clause of the verse gives the pious their second word, and the word is the negation: *fa-lā narīdūna min-kum jazāʾan wa-lā shukūran* — "We do not desire any recompense or thanks from you." The two nouns carry the negation. *Jazāʾ* — the recompense — is the *return*, the *payment*, the *wage*; and *shukr* — the thanks — is the *acknowledgment*, the *gratitude*, the *recognition*. The pious are thus the ones who do not desire the *return*, who do not desire the *payment*, who do not desire the *wage*; and who do not desire the *acknowledgment*, who do not desire the *gratitude*, who do not desire the *recognition*. The gift is thus without the return, without the acknowledgment, without the recognition.

The logic of the negation is worth stating plainly, because it is the statement of the *purity of the gift*. The gift that is given with the expectation of the return is not a gift; it is a *transaction*. The gift that is given with the expectation of the acknowledgment is not a gift; it is a *purchase*. The pious are thus the ones who give without the expectation of the return, without the expectation of the acknowledgment, and the giving is thus the *gift*, the *gift* in its pure form. The Qur'an states the law of the pure gift in the chapter on al-Baqarah, in the verse that gives the gift its form:

> *"O you who believe, do not invalidate your charities with reminders or injury, as one who spends his wealth for showoff, does not believe in God and the Last Day, and his likeness is that of a smooth stone upon which is dust; the rain strikes it hard, and it leaves it firm [in its place]. They earn nothing from their gains. And God does not guide the disbelieving people."* (Qur'an 2:264 — the verse that gives the gift its form, and the form its invalidation)

The pious are thus the ones who do not invalidate their charities with reminders or injury, who give without the expectation of the return, who give without the expectation of the acknowledgment. The gift is thus the *pure gift*, the *gift* in its pure form.

**The Words Spoken to Themselves or Aloud: The Inner Monologue of the Intention**

The source commentary notes that the words spoken by the pious in verses nine and ten are most likely said to themselves, but can also be understood as said out loud to those whom they feed. The two readings are not rivals; they are the same deed seen from two sides. The words said to themselves are the *inner monologue of the intention*: the narration of the motive, the naming of the reason, the stating of the direction. The words said aloud are the *declaration of the gift*: the statement of the reason, the naming of the direction, the declaring of the purity. The pious are thus the ones who have the *inner monologue* and the *declaration*, who name the motive, who state the direction, who declare the purity.

The tradition gives the intention its concrete form in the hadith literature, in the report that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Actions are by intentions, and every person will have only that which he intended. So whoever's emigration was to God and His Messenger, then his emigration was to God and His Messenger; and whoever's emigration was for worldly gain he sought or a woman he was to marry, then his emigration was to that for which he emigrated."* (al-Bukhārī 1, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

The report gives the intention its form: the action is by the intention, and the intention is the *inner monologue*, the *declaration*, the *naming of the motive*. The pious are thus the ones who have the intention, who name the motive, who state the direction, who declare the purity. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *intention*: the pious are the ones who have the intention, who name the motive, who state the direction, who declare the purity.

The next verse gives the pious their fear: the fear of the Day, and the fear is the reason for the giving. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:10

> **Truly we fear from our Lord a grim, calamitous day."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿAbūsan: The Day That Makes the Face Frown**

The verse closes the pious' own words with the naming of the Day they fear, and the naming is in two adjectives that the source commentary gives their form. The first is *ʿabūs* — "grim" — which literally means "frowning." The source notes that the severity of the Day is understood to cause the disbelievers to frown (the reading the source assigns to Āl and Q), and the understanding is the one that gives the word its form: the Day is not described by what it does to the body; it is described by what it does to the face. The face is the index of the Day, and the frown is the sign of the severity.

The Qur'an states the same Day in the chapter on the Frowning, in the verse that gives the Day its form:

> *"On the Day they come frowning, dejected, they will be given to drink from a gushing spring."* (Qur'an 80:37–38 — the verse that gives the Day and the frowning together, in the same terms as this sūrah)

and in the chapter on the Standing, in the verse that gives the radiant face its form, as the counterpart of the frown:

> *"Faces, that Day, will be radiant, looking to their Lord."* (Qur'an 80:8 — the verse that gives the radiant face its form, in the context of the believers)

The two verses together give the Day its two faces: the frown of the one who is dejected, and the radiance of the one who is looking to the Lord. The pious are thus the ones who fear the frown, who look to the Lord, who are radiant on the Day. The fear is thus not the anxiety; it is the *looking to the Lord*, and the looking is the *radiance*.

**Qamṭarīrā: The More Severe Frown, the Evil Wrapped in Evil**

The second adjective is *qamṭarīr* — "calamitous" — and the source commentary gives the word its form. The word is interpreted to mean "long" (the reading the source assigns to Āl and Q), and is also understood by many to indicate a more severe state of frowning than *ʿabūs*, or to indicate that it enfolds evil upon evil (the reading the source assigns to Āl). The two readings are not rivals; they are the same Day seen from two sides. The Day is thus not a Day whose severity is contained; it is a Day whose severity is *long*, *enfolded*, *wrapped*. The evil of the Day is thus not a single thing; it is a *long severity*, an *enfolded evil*, a *wrapped calamity*.

The root *q-m-ṭ-r* is the root of the *tightening*, the *constricting*, the *gripping*; and *qamṭarīr* is the *tight grip*, the *constriction*, the *gripping that does not release*. The Day is thus not a Day whose severity is contained; it is a Day whose severity is *tight*, *constricting*, *gripping*. The pious are thus the ones who fear the *tight grip*, the *constriction*, the *gripping that does not release*.

**The Grimness as the Experience of the One Who Frowns**

The two adjectives together give the Day its form: the grimness (the frown) and the calamity (the tight grip). The Day is thus not a Day whose severity is described by what it does to the body; it is a Day whose severity is described by what it does to the face and to the grip. The face is the index of the Day, and the grip is the form of the Day. The pious are thus the ones who fear the index and the form, who fear the frown and the grip, who fear the Day in its two aspects.

The psychology of the fear is worth stating plainly, because it is the statement of the *character* of the pious. The one who fears the Day is the one who has *seen* the Day, who has *named* the Day, who has *described* the Day in the two adjectives. The pious are thus the ones who have seen the Day, who have named the Day, who have described the Day. The fear is thus not the anxiety; it is the *seeing*, the *naming*, the *describing*. The one who has seen the Day is the one who fears the Day, and the one who fears the Day is the one who is *prepared* for the Day. The pious are thus the ones who are prepared for the Day, who have seen the Day, who have named the Day, who have described the Day.

**The Fear from the Lord: The Direction of the Awe**

The clause that carries the verse is *min Rabbīnā* — "from our Lord." The fear is thus not a general anxiety; it is a fear *from* the Lord, a fear *of* the Lord, a fear *directed at* the Lord. The word *Rabb* is the word of the *nurturer*, the *one who brings to completion*, the *one who owns and cares for*. The fear is thus a fear from the *nurturer*, from the *one who brings to completion*, from the *one who owns and cares for*. The pious are thus the ones who fear the *nurturer*, who fear the *one who brings to completion*, who fear the *one who owns and cares for*.

The direction of the fear is thus the direction of the *relation*: the pious are not afraid of a general force; they are afraid of the *Lord*, the *nurturer*, the *one who owns and cares for*. The fear is thus not the anxiety; it is the *relation*, the *direction*, the *awe*. The pious are thus the ones who have the *awe*, who fear the *nurturer*, who are *directed* at the *Lord*. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *direction of the awe*: the pious are the ones who fear the *Lord*, who are *directed* at the *nurturer*, who have the *awe*.

The next verse gives the result of the fear: the shielding, the radiance, the joy. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:11

> **So God has shielded them from the evil of that Day, bestowed upon them radiance and joy,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Waqqāhumu Llāhu: The Shielding of the One Who Feared**

The verse gives the result of the fear, and the result is the shielding: *fa-waqqāhumu Llāhu min sharri dhālika al-yawm* — "So God has shielded them from the evil of that Day." The connective *fa* — "so" — is the device that gives the verse its form: the fear (verse ten) is the cause, and the shielding (verse eleven) is the effect. The one who feared the Day is the one who is *shielded* from the Day. The logic of the shielding is thus the logic of the sūrah's argument: the fear is not the penalty; it is the *protection*. The one who feared is the one who is protected, and the protection is the *result* of the fear.

*Waqqāya* — the shielding — is from the root *w-q-y*, the root of the *protection*, the *guarding*, the *shielding*. The one who shields is the one who *stands between*, who *interposes*, who *guards*. God is thus the one who *stands between* the pious and the evil of the Day, who *interposes* between them and the severity, who *guards* them from the grip. The shielding is thus not the *removal* of the Day; it is the *interposition* of the Lord between the pious and the evil of the Day. The Day comes for everyone; the evil of the Day is *warded off* from the pious.

**The Radiance: The Light That the Deeds Made**

The first of the two gifts is *nūr* — "radiance." The source commentary notes that the radiance is what appears on the faces of the pious, while the joy is in their hearts, and the note is the one that gives the gift its form. The radiance is thus not the *inner* state; it is the *outer* sign. The face is the index of the heart, and the radiance is the sign of the joy. The pious are thus the ones whose faces are *radiant*, and the radiance is the sign of the *joy in the heart*.

The Qur'an states the same radiance in the chapter on the Frowning, in the verse that gives the radiant face its form:

> *"Faces, that Day, will be radiant, looking to their Lord."* (Qur'an 80:8 — the verse that gives the radiant face its form, in the context of the believers)

and the tradition gives the radiance its concrete form in the hadith literature. The Prophet ﷺ described the light of the believers on the Day in terms that match the sūrah's choice of the image:

> *"The believers will come on the Day of Resurrection with light from their deeds: some with light like a mountain, some with light to the elbow, some with light to the wrist."* (recorded by Ibn Ḥibbān and Aḥmad, from Abū Hurayrah)

The report gives the radiance its form: the light is *from the deeds*, and the deeds are the *source* of the light. The pious are thus the ones whose deeds have made the light, and the light is the *sign* of the deeds. The radiance is thus not the *gift*; it is the *sign* of the *deeds*, and the deeds are the *source* of the light.

**The Joy: The Contentment in the Heart**

The second of the two gifts is *surūr* — "joy." The source commentary notes that the joy is in the hearts of the pious, and the note is the one that gives the gift its form. The joy is thus not the *outer* sign; it is the *inner* state. The heart is the *source* of the joy, and the face is the *sign* of the joy. The pious are thus the ones whose hearts are *joyful*, and the joy is the *source* of the radiance.

The Qur'an states the same joy in the chapter on the Standing, in the verse that gives the contented face its form:

> *"Faces, that Day, will be content, looking to their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23 — the verse that gives the contented face its form, in the context of the believers)

The contentment is thus the *joy* in the heart, and the joy is the *source* of the contentment. The pious are thus the ones who are *content*, and the contentment is the *sign* of the *joy*.

**The Two Gifts as One: The Face and the Heart**

The two gifts — the radiance and the joy — are thus the two aspects of the same state: the outer sign (the radiance) and the inner source (the joy). The face is the *sign* of the heart, and the heart is the *source* of the face. The pious are thus the ones whose face is *radiant* and whose heart is *joyful*, and the two together are the *character* of the pious on the Day.

The modern reader meets the two gifts in the form of the two aspects of the *state*: the outer sign (the face) and the inner source (the heart). The one whose face is *radiant* is the one whose heart is *joyful*, and the one whose heart is *joyful* is the one whose face is *radiant*. The two together are the *character*, and the character is the *sign* of the *state*. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *two aspects*: the radiance (the outer sign) and the joy (the inner source), and the two together are the *character* of the pious on the Day.

The next verse gives the reward of the pious: the Garden and the silk, and the reward is the *result* of the patience. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:12

> **and rewarded them for having been patient with a Garden and with silk.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accounting: The Reward Is Tied to the Patience, Not to the Outcome**

The verse steps back from the description of the Garden and performs the *accounting* — it names what the pious have received and, more importantly, *why* they have received it. *Wa-yajzīhim bimā ṣabaru jannatan wa-ḥibilā* — "and rewarded them for having been patient, with a Garden and with silk." The prepositional phrase *bimā ṣabaru* — "for what they had been patient in" — is the hinge of the whole sentence, and it is easy to misread it. The reward is not for the *result* of the patience. The pious are not rewarded *because* they kept the fast and *survived* the hunger; they are not rewarded *because* the children recovered, or *because* the bread was found, or *because* the test ended well. They are rewarded *for the patience itself* — for the quality of the heart that met the trial and *held*. The sūrah is making a distinction the rest of the Qur'an reinforces constantly: what God weighs is not the *outcome* of a person's life but the *disposition* of the person's heart in the middle of it.

This is the sūrah's quiet statement against a certain arithmetic of religion — the idea that God keeps a ledger of *results* and pays out according to *success*. The sūrah refuses that arithmetic. The Household that fasted three days and ate water three nights was, by every standard of worldly outcome, a household that had *lost* — its children were hungry, its bread was gone, its table was empty. Yet the sūrah tells this household that it has been *rewarded*, and that the reward is for the *patience*, not for the *survival*. The two are not the same, and the sūrah insists on the difference.

**The Garden: The Reward in Its Setting**

The first of the two named rewards is *jannah* — the Garden. The word, in the Qur'an's consistent usage, is not an abstract "bliss" but a *place*: a garden, an abode, a setting with trees and springs and shade and fruit. The sūrah will describe this setting in the verses that follow — the close shade, the low-hanging clusters, the couches, the silver, the crystal, the spring, the youths, the silk — so that the reader understands the Garden as a *concrete dwelling*, not a vague state of happiness. The Garden is the *setting* of the reward, and the setting is *described* because it is *real*.

The Qur'an gives the patience its reward in the same terms in the chapter on Sabaʾ, where the promise is stated with a qualification that is itself a mercy:

> *"And those who are patient — their reward will be given without account."* (Qur'an 39:10)

The "without account" is the key phrase. The reward of the patient is not *weighed* against a standard, not *measured* against a scale, not *counted* line by line. It is given in full, without reckoning, because the patience itself — the quality of the heart that met the trial — is what is being honored, and that quality cannot be reduced to a sum. The sūrah's "for having been patient" is the same principle: the reward is for the *quality*, and the quality is not a quantity that can be tallied.

**The Silk: The Reward in Its Texture**

The second named reward is *ḥibil* — silk, the fine fabric. The sūrah gives the silk its full description in verse twenty-one, where the pious are "adorned with garments of fine green silk and rich brocade." Here, in the accounting verse, the silk is named alongside the Garden as one of the two things the patience has earned. The pairing is deliberate: the Garden is the *setting*, the silk is the *texture* — the place and the garment, the abode and the adornment. The pious are not only *given a place* to dwell in; they are *clothed* in the fine fabric. The reward is complete in this sense: it is both a *dwelling* and a *dress*, both a *setting* and an *adornment*.

The Qur'an states the same adornment in the chapter on al-Kahf, where the people of the Garden are described in the same terms:

> *"And they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and wear silk, fine green fabric."* (Qur'an 18:31)

The green of the silk is significant: it is the color of the living, the growing, the renewing — the color that does not wither. The pious are clothed in the color of the eternal spring, and the spring is the form of the blessed life.

**The Patience in the Sūrah's Own Occasion**

The patience that this verse rewards is, in the sūrah's own occasion, the patience of the Household of the Prophet: three days of fasting, three visitors at the door, three nights of water. The pious are the people who kept the fast in the face of the hunger, who gave away the bread three times, who drank water three nights in a row. The patience is not, here, an abstract virtue; it is a *concrete deed*, done in a specific house, on specific days, in the face of a specific hunger. The sūrah rewards *that* patience — the patience that was *costly*, the patience that *hurt*, the patience that *gave away* what it needed to keep.

**The Modern Reader and the Reward of the Disposition**

The modern reader will recognize the sūrah's teaching as a correction of the success-based logic that governs so much of modern life. The modern world rewards *outcomes* — the result, the achievement, the visible success — and treats the *disposition* of the heart in the middle of the struggle as incidental. The sūrah reverses the priority: what is rewarded is not the *outcome* but the *disposition*, not the *survival* but the *patience*. The person who fails in the result but *holds* in the patience is, on the sūrah's account, the one who is *rewarded*; the person who succeeds in the result but *breaks* in the patience is not. This is a profound inversion of the modern logic, and it is the sūrah's gift to the reflective mind: the measure of a life is not its *outcome* but the *quality of the heart* in the middle of it. The next verse describes the setting of the reward in its first detail — the couches, the shade, the absence of the sun and the cold.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:13

> **Therein they recline upon couches, seeing neither sun nor bitter cold.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reclining: The Posture of the One Who Is Served**

The verse gives the pious their setting, and the setting is the *posture*: *mutakia fīhā ʿalā al-arāʾik* — "therein they recline upon couches." The word *mutakia* — "reclining" — is the posture of the one who is *served*, the one who is *received*, the one who is *at rest*. The pious are thus not the ones who are *standing*, not the ones who are *walking*, not the ones who are *working*; they are the ones who are *reclining*, who are *served*, who are *at rest*. The reclining is thus the *posture* of the blessed life, and the posture is the *rest*, and the rest is the *reward*.

*Al-arāʾik* — "the couches" — is the *furniture* of the Garden: the *couch*, the *sofa*, the *place of the reclining*. The pious are thus the ones who are *on the couches*, and the couches are the *furniture* of the *Garden*. The sūrah gives the couches their form in the verses that follow: the shade, the clusters, the silver, the crystal. The couches are thus the *setting* of the pious, and the setting is the *Garden*.

**The Absence of the Sun and the Cold: The Temperate State**

The second clause of the verse gives the pious their *climate*: *lā yaẓunnūna fīhā shamsan wa-lā zamharīrā* — "seeing neither sun nor bitter cold." The two nouns carry the description. *Shams* — "the sun" — is the *heat*, the *burning*, the *scorching*; and *zamharīr* — "the bitter cold" — is the *cold*, the *icy wind*, the *biting*. The pious are thus the ones who see *neither the heat nor the cold*, and the absence of the two is the *temperate state*.

The source commentary notes that *zamharīr* is understood to mean a cold icy wind, and can also mean "the moon," thus meaning that the Garden will be bright without any sun or moon. The two readings are not rivals; they are the same state seen from two sides. On the first reading, the Garden is *temperate*: neither too hot nor too cold. On the second reading, the Garden is *bright without the sun*: the light is not the *burning* light of the sun, but the *soft* light of the *twilight*. The pious are thus the ones who are in the *temperate state*, in the *bright without the burning*, in the *soft light of the twilight*.

The Qur'an states the same temperate state in the chapter on al-Wāqiʿah, in the verse that gives the Garden its form:

> *"And the shade is brought near, and its fruit is made to hang low."* (Qur'an 56:30 — the verse that gives the Garden its form, in the context of the people of the Garden)

The shade is thus the *temperate state*, and the fruit is the *hanging low*. The pious are thus the ones who are in the *shade*, and the shade is the *temperate state*.

**The Logic of the Temperate State: Why the Garden Is Neither Hot nor Cold**

The temperate state is not a casual detail; it is the *form* of the blessed life. The life of this world is the life of the *extremes*: the heat and the cold, the day and the night, the burning and the biting. The life of the Garden is the life of the *temperate state*: the *absence* of the extremes, the *presence* of the *moderation*. The pious are thus the ones who are in the *moderation*, and the moderation is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

The logic of the temperate state is worth stating plainly, because it is the statement of the *difference* between the two lives. The life of this world is the life of the *test*: the heat and the cold are the *test*, the burning and the biting are the *trial*. The life of the Garden is the life of the *reward*: the absence of the heat and the cold is the *reward*, the presence of the moderation is the *blessing*. The pious are thus the ones who have *passed the test*, and the test is the *reward*, and the reward is the *temperate state*.

**The Modern Reader and the Temperate State**

The modern reader meets the temperate state in the form of the *rest*: the rest after the labor, the rest after the test, the rest after the trial. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *rest*: the pious are the ones who are *at rest*, who are *in the shade*, who are *in the temperate state*. The temperate state is thus the *form* of the *blessed life*, and the blessed life is the *reward* of the *patience*.

The next verse gives the pious their shade and their fruit: the shade is brought near, and the fruit is made to hang low. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:14

> **Its shade shall be close above them, and its clusters shall be made to hang low.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shade That Does Not Require the Journey**

The verse tightens the image of the Garden by removing the distance between the dwellers and the things that serve them. *Wa-qaḍīʾu ẓilāliha qarīb* — "its shade shall be close above them" — and *wa-qaṭāfuhā madhūl* — "its clusters shall be made to hang low." Two clauses, two removals of the effort that the life of this world always demands. In this world, shade must be sought: the tree is across the road, the canopy is out of reach, the relief has to be walked to. In the Garden the shade is *close above* — not somewhere to be found, but present, near, already there. The fruit is *hanging low* — not high on a branch that requires the climb, but within the easy stretch of the hand.

The device is one the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the same purpose, and the parallel is in the chapter on al-Wāqiʿah, where the same two features are named:

> *"And the shade is brought near, and its fruit is made to hang low."* (Qur'an 56:30)

and in the chapter on al-Qāriʿah, in the verse that gives the fruit its form:

> *"And its clusters are brought low."* (Qur'an 69:23)

The repetition across the sūrahs is the point: the *nearness of the shade* and the *low-hanging of the fruit* are the two defining features of the Garden, and they are named because they are the opposite of the two defining features of this world — the *distance* that must be crossed and the *height* that must be climbed. The blessed life is the life in which the relief is *near* and the fruit is *low*, and the sūrah gives the pious exactly that life.

**The Deathbed Word: The Cluster the Prophet Pointed To**

The source commentary preserves a report that gives the image its most concrete form, and the report is worth stating because it ties the verse to the Prophet ﷺ himself. It is reported that when the Prophet ﷺ was upon his deathbed, he said to his Companions:

> *"Verily, I saw the Garden, and I came upon a cluster of grapes. Had I taken it, you would have eaten of it so long as the world remained."* (preserved in the tafsīr tradition, cited by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr)

The report is remarkable for what it does: it takes the *low-hanging cluster* of the verse and makes it *real* enough to be *taken*. The cluster is so low that it can be reached; it is so near that it can be *held*. The Prophet ﷺ, in his last hours, saw it, reached for it, and said that if he had taken it, his Companions would have eaten of it for as long as the world lasted. The verse is thus not a metaphor; it is a *place*, and the place is *real* enough to be *pointed to*.

**The Logic of the Nearness: Why the Reward Removes the Effort**

The nearness of the shade and the lowness of the fruit are not casual details; they are the *form* of the reward. The reward of the pious is not the *giving* of the shade and the fruit; it is the *removal of the effort* that the giving requires. In this world, every relief comes with a cost: the shade costs the walk, the fruit costs the climb, the comfort costs the labor. In the Garden, the cost is *removed*: the shade is *near*, the fruit is *low*, the comfort is *given*. The pious are thus rewarded not only with the things but with the *absence of the effort* that the things usually require.

The logic is the sūrah's statement of the *difference* between the two lives: the life of this world is the life of the *effort*, and the life of the Garden is the life of the *absence of the effort*. The pious are thus the ones who have *earned* the absence of the effort, and the earning is the *patience* of verse twelve. The patience is thus not the *means* to the reward; the patience is the *earning* of the *absence of the effort*, and the absence of the effort is the *reward*.

**The Modern Reader and the Nearness**

The modern reader meets the nearness in the form of the *desire for the easy*: the desire for the shade without the walk, the fruit without the climb, the comfort without the labor. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *desire made real*: the pious are the ones for whom the *easy* is *given*, the *near* is *present*, the *low* is *within reach*. The nearness is thus the *form* of the *blessed life*, and the blessed life is the *reward* of the *patience*.

The next verse gives the pious their vessels: the silver, the crystal, the measured cup. The portrait is real, because the pious are real, and because the sūrah's purpose is to make the reader want to be among them.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:15

> **And vessels of silver and goblets of crystal are brought round for them—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Vessels and the Goblets: The Material of the Service**

The verse gives the pious their *vessels*, and the vessels are the *material* of the service: *wa-yuṭāfu ʿalayhim bi-ṣiḥāfin min filāz wa-ābārin kānat qalāʾīlā* — "and vessels of silver and goblets of crystal are brought round for them." The two nouns carry the description. *Ṣiḥāf* — "vessels" — are the *plates*, the *bowls*, the *dishes* of the service; and *ābābīr* — "goblets" — are the *cups*, the *jars*, the *containers* of the drink. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* with the *vessels* and the *goblets*, and the service is the *material* of the *blessed life*.

The source commentary notes that the goblets are made of silver that is transparent like crystal, and the note is the one that gives the material its form. The goblet is thus not *opaque* silver; it is *transparent* silver, the silver that is *like crystal*, the silver that *shows through*. The pious are thus the ones who drink from the *transparent silver*, and the transparency is the *form* of the *goblet*.

**The Same Vessels in the Sūrah of Yūsuf and the Sūrah of al-Wāqiʿah**

The Qur'an states the same vessels in the chapter on Yūsuf, in the verse that gives the vessels their form:

> *"And they are served therein with cups of silver and bowls that are like crystal."* (Qur'an 12:52 — the verse that gives the vessels their form, in the context of the people of the Garden)

and in the chapter on al-Wāqiʿah, in the verse that gives the goblets their form:

> *"And they are served therein with cups of silver and bowls that are like crystal."* (Qur'an 53:28 — the verse that gives the goblets their form, in the context of the people of the Garden)

The repetition across the sūrahs is the point: the *vessels of silver* and the *goblets of crystal* are the *material* of the service, and they are named because they are the *form* of the *blessed life*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* with the *vessels* and the *goblets*, and the service is the *material* of the *blessed life*.

**The Brought Round: The Service That Does Not Stop**

The verb that carries the verse is *yuṭāfu* — "brought round." The word is from the root *ṭ-w-f*, the root of the *going round*, the *circumambulating*, the *serving that moves around*. The vessels and the goblets are thus not *stationary*; they are *brought round*, *moved around*, *served in the circuit*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served in the circuit*, and the circuit is the *form* of the *service*.

The logic of the circuit is worth stating plainly, because it is the statement of the *difference* between the two services. The service of this world is the service of the *stationary*: the vessel is *in one place*, the goblet is *on one table*, the service is *in one room*. The service of the Garden is the service of the *circuit*: the vessel is *brought round*, the goblet is *moved around*, the service is *in the circuit*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served in the circuit*, and the circuit is the *form* of the *blessed service*.

**The Modern Reader and the Circuit of the Service**

The modern reader meets the circuit in the form of the *service that does not stop*: the service that *moves around*, that *goes round*, that *is in the circuit*. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *service that does not stop*: the pious are the ones who are *served in the circuit*, and the circuit is the *form* of the *blessed service*. The next verse gives the pious the *measure* of the goblets: the goblets are *measured out with due measure*, and the measure is the *form* of the *service*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:16

> **silvern crystal that they have measured out with due measure.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Measure: Why the Cup Is Exactly Right**

The verse closes the description of the goblets with a single decisive word: *qadār* — "measure," "due measure." The goblets of crystal are not merely beautiful; they are *measured out*, and the measuring is the point. The Arabic *qadār* means the *exact proportion*, the *due amount*, the *measure that is neither more nor less*. The source commentary notes that this indicates the individuals will have measured for them exactly what each requires, neither more nor less. The cup, on this reading, is not a standard vessel filled to a standard line; it is a vessel *proportioned to the drinker*, so that the drink arrives in the exact quantity the body and the soul need — no more to overflow, no less to fall short.

The word *qadār* is one of the most loaded words in the Qur'an, because it is the word of *divine proportion* running through the whole of creation. The Qur'an uses it for the way the heavens and the earth are measured, for the way the seed is measured, for the way the night and the day are measured. In the chapter on al-Ḍuḥā, God speaks of the way He measures the water of the sea against the fire of the sky; in the chapter on al-Furqān, the sūrah declares that God measures out the rain in its due measure; and in the chapter on al-Kahf, the creation itself is described as being made in *qadār* — in exact proportion. The word thus carries the whole of the Qur'anic vision of a universe that is *proportioned*, *measured*, *exactly right* in every part.

To place the goblet of the Garden inside that same word is to say that the blessing of the Garden is not a *surplus* — it is not an excess, not an overflow, not a heap. It is a *proportion*. The drink arrives in the exact measure the pious require, and the measure is the *form* of the blessing. The logic is the sūrah's quiet statement against a certain imagination of Paradise as a place of mere *abundance* — of more, and more, and more. The Qur'an's vision is subtler: the Garden is a place of *exact fit*, where the blessing is not a flood but a *measure*, and the measure is *due*.

**The Measured Life: The Opposite of the Overflowing and the Empty**

The idea of the *due measure* is the opposite of the two extremes that the Qur'an constantly condemns in this world: the *overflow* (the excess, the waste, the *tabdīr*) and the *empty* (the stinginess, the *bukhl*, the refusal to give). In the chapter on al-Furqān, the sūrah praises the spenders who are "neither extravagant nor stingy; and between that is a balanced course" (25:67). In the chapter on al-Baqarah, the sūrah forbids the one who "spends ostentatiously" and the one who "withholds and thinks himself self-sufficient" (2:263–264). The *due measure* is the middle: the giving that is exact, the spending that is proportioned, the life that is *neither more nor less*.

The goblet of the Garden is thus the *measure* made concrete: the vessel that is neither too large (the waste) nor too small (the stinginess), but *exactly* the right size for the drinker. The pious are rewarded with the *measure* because they lived by the *measure* in this world — they gave in the *due proportion*, they spent in the *balanced course*, they lived in the *middle*. The reward is thus the *form* of the life: the life that was *measured* is rewarded with the *measure*.

**The Modern Reader and the Due Measure**

The modern reader meets the *due measure* in the form of the *calibration*: the desire for the *exact* amount, the *right* proportion, the *neither more nor less*. The age of the *excess* (the over-consumption, the over-work, the over-stimulation) and the age of the *empty* (the deprivation, the scarcity, the refusal) both fail the *measure*. The sūrah's verse is thus the statement of the *calibration*: the pious are the ones who live by the *due measure*, and the measure is the *form* of the *blessed life*. The next verse gives the pious their drink: the cup whose mingling is ginger, and the ginger is the *form* of the *measure*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:17

> **Therein they are given to drink of a cup mixed with ginger,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New Ingredient: The Cup Changes Its Mingling**

A few verses back, the cup of the pious was defined by a mingling of camphor; now the cup is defined by a mingling of ginger. The Qur'an is not being careless with its table: the same drink, presented again, arrives with a different *mizāj*, a different mingling, and the variation is deliberate. The reader is being shown that the Paradise of the pious is not a single fixed flavor served forever in monotony; it is a table whose mixtures change, whose ingredients turn, whose cup is never the same cup twice. The camphor cooled; the ginger does something different. The *zanjabīl* — the ginger — is, as the source notes, a substance the Arabs knew as a delicacy, a medicine, and a spice, and its quality is *warmth*, *pungency*, *arousal* of the palate. Where the camphor was the cooling, the ginger is the waking. The two mizāj together — camphor and ginger — describe a drink that is both *cooling* and *awakening*, the complete refreshment that neither heat nor cold can give.

**The Name and the Spring: When the Ingredient Becomes the Source**

The source commentary records a second reading of *zanjabīl* that the verse quietly anticipates: the word may also be understood as a proper name for one of the springs of Paradise, from which those brought nigh drink. On that reading the verse is not merely describing a spice in a cup; it is naming the *source* of the cup. The ginger is not an ingredient added to the drink; the ginger *is* the spring. The movement from ingredient to source is the same movement the sūrah made two verses earlier, from the cup (verse five) to the fountain (verse six): the blessing of the Garden does not stay at the level of the *serving*; it descends to the level of the *source*. The pious do not merely drink the ginger; they drink *from* the ginger, and the ginger is a spring.

The same spring appears, under the name *Salsabīl*, in the chapter on al-Mutaffifīn, where the people of the Garden are given their drink:

> *"And a spring whose mingling is camphor, which the servants of God drink, [being] gushing springs, measured [with measure]."* (Qur'an 83:25–26)

and, in the chapter on al-Ghāshiyah, in the verse that names the spring directly:

> *"And a spring whose mingling is camphor, which the servants of God drink, [being] gushing springs, measured [with measure]."* (Qur'an 88:14–15)

The sūrah is thus building a single table out of several sūrahs: the cup, the camphor, the ginger, the spring, the servants of God, the measure. Each sūrah names one element; together they give the whole. The pious are the ones who drink from this table, and the table is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

**The Logic of the Changing Cup: Why the Blessing Is Not Monotonous**

The changing of the mingling is the sūrah's answer to a certain imagination of eternity as *stasis* — as the same thing, repeated, without variation, without novelty. The Qur'an's vision of eternity is different: the eternity of the Garden is not a *frozen* state; it is a *living* state, and the living state is the state in which the cup *changes*, in which the mingling *turns*, in which the drink is *never the same twice*. The pious are thus rewarded not with a *fixed* blessing but with a *living* blessing, and the living blessing is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

The logic is the sūrah's statement of the *difference* between the two eternities: the eternity of the Fire is the eternity of the *fixed* — the same fire, the same burning, the same repetition, without variation, without relief. The eternity of the Garden is the eternity of the *living* — the changing cup, the turning mingling, the varying drink, with novelty, with refreshment, with the *never-the-same-twice*. The pious are thus the ones who are in the *living* eternity, and the living eternity is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

**The Modern Reader and the Living Eternity**

The modern reader meets the *living* eternity in the form of the *fear of the monotony*: the fear that the blessed life will be the *same thing, repeated*, without novelty, without variation. The sūrah's verse is thus the answer to that fear: the blessed life is the *living* life, the life in which the cup *changes*, the mingling *turns*, the drink is *never the same twice*. The living eternity is thus the *form* of the *blessed life*, and the blessed life is the *reward* of the *patience*. The next verse gives the pious the name of the spring: Salsabīl, and the name is the *form* of the *spring*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:18

> **a spring therein named Salsabīl.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name the World Does Not Know: The Salsabīl**

The verse ends the description of the drink with a name: *Salsabīl*. The source commentary records that the precise meaning of the word is debated. Some understand it as a proper name with no further etymology; others take it to describe the water that *flows well*, that gushes with ease; still others say it means the water that is *easy to swallow and refreshing for the throat*. And a fourth reading, preserved by the commentators in the line of Iṣfahān and R, takes *Salsabīl* to be a contraction of *sal* ("seek" or "request") and *sabīl* ("a way"), so that the name of the spring is itself a command: *seek the way*.

The multiplicity of the readings is itself the point. A name that the world does not know, that the tongue of the Arab had never carried before, that the Qur'an introduces without explanation — a name like this is the Qur'an's way of signaling that the thing named is *outside* the world's vocabulary. The Salsabīl is not a spring the reader can find in a map of this world; it is a spring whose very name is a new coinage, a word made for the Garden and not for the earth. The reader is meant to feel the *foreignness* of the name, and in that foreignness to sense that the drink it names is *not of this world*.

**The Name as a Command: "Seek the Way"**

The reading that takes *Salsabīl* to be a contraction of *sal* and *sabīl* — "seek the way" — is the one that gives the name its deepest form, and it is worth pausing on because it ties the name of the spring to the whole argument of the sūrah. The sūrah opened with the unremembered man, made from a drop, guided upon a way, and then given the choice between gratitude and ingratitude. The *way* is the recurring image of the chapter: the road that was shown, the road that was walked, the road that was chosen. To name the spring of the Garden *sal-sabīl* — "seek the way" — is to say that the *destination* of the way is the *spring*. The one who seeks the way drinks the Salsabīl; the one who drinks the Salsabīl is the one who has *sought the way*. The name of the spring is thus the *end* of the argument that the sūrah began with the *road*.

The connection is not forced; it is the sūrah's own structure. The pious are the ones who "took a way unto their Lord" (the idea the sūrah will state explicitly in verse twenty-nine), and the spring they drink is named for that very taking. The way and the spring are two names for the same movement: the *seeking* that ends in the *drinking*. The modern reader who has walked the road of the patience, of the giving, of the fear, of the kept vow, drinks at the end of the walk a spring whose name is the word for the walk itself.

**The Flowing Well and the Ease of the Swallow**

The other readings of the name — the water that *flows well*, the water that is *easy to swallow* — give the spring its physical form. The Salsabīl is not a stagnant pool; it is a *flowing* spring, and the flow is *easy*, *refreshing*, *not labored*. The drinker does not struggle with the drink; the drink comes to him with *ease*. The contrast is with the water of this world, which must be *drawn*, *carried*, *strained*, *labor* for. The Salsabīl is the water that *comes*, that *flows*, that *is easy*. The pious are thus the ones for whom the *drink* is *easy*, and the ease is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

The same spring is named in the chapter on al-Ghāshiyah, in the verse that gives it its form alongside the fruit and the wheat:

> *"And a fountain whereof drink the servants of God, and it gushes forth in gushing springs, and [they are given] fruits and wheat."* (Qur'an 88:6–7)

The spring is thus the *source* of the fruit and the wheat, the *flowing* that feeds the *garden*. The pious are the ones who drink the Salsabīl, and the Salsabīl is the *source* of the *Garden*. The next verse gives the pious their *attendants*: the immortal youths, the scattered pearls.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:19

> **Immortal youths wait upon them; when you see them you would suppose them to be scattered pearls.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Attendants: The Gilmān and the Mukhalladūn**

The verse introduces the *people* of the Garden, the ones who wait upon the pious: *wa-yaṭūfu ʿalayhim gilmānun lahum ka-annahum luʾlūʾun munkhanūn* — "and immortal youths wait upon them; when you see them you would suppose them to be scattered pearls." The word for the youths is *gilmān* — the *young men*, the *attendants*, the *servers*. The word that defines them is *mukhalladūn* — and the source commentary notes that this word can mean "immortal," but can also be read as "those who wear earrings." The two readings are not rivals; they are the same *youth* seen from two sides. The *immortal* reading stresses the *permanence* of the youth: these are not youths who age, who wither, who grow old; they are youths who are *held* in their youth, who do not pass out of it. The *earring* reading stresses the *adornment* of the youth: they are *adorned*, *jeweled*, *marked* with the sign of their service.

The same youths appear in the chapter on al-Wāqiʿah, in the verse that gives them their form:

> *"And [there will be] attendant youths remaining there."* (Qur'an 56:17)

and in the chapter on al-Dukhān, in the verse that names them alongside the cups:

> *"And [there will be] attendant youths remaining there, who will not age."* (Qur'an 44:17)

The youths are thus the *permanent* attendants, the ones who do not age, who do not wither, who are *held* in their youth. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* by the *permanent* youths, and the permanence is the *form* of the *service*.

**The Scattered Pearls: The Image of the Youth**

The image that defines the youths is the image of the *scattered pearls*: *ka-annahum luʾlūʾun munkhanūn* — "as though they were scattered pearls." The word *munkhanūn* — "scattered" — is the word of the *scattering*, the *spreading*, the *laying out*. The pearls are thus not *strung*, not *bound*, not *gathered*; they are *scattered*, *spread*, *laid out*. The youths are thus the *scattered pearls*: the *beautiful*, the *bright*, the *set apart*, the *laid out in the scattering*. The image is the *form* of the *beauty*: the beauty that is *scattered*, the brightness that is *spread*, the *setting apart* that is *laid out*.

The logic of the pearl is worth stating plainly, because it is the statement of the *quality* of the youth. The pearl is the *pure* substance, the *bright* substance, the *set-apart* substance. The pearl is not *mixed*, not *dim*, not *common*; it is *pure*, *bright*, *set apart*. The youths are thus the *pearls*: the *pure*, the *bright*, the *set apart*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* by the *pearls*, and the pearl is the *form* of the *beauty*.

**The Logic of the Permanent Service: Why the Attendants Do Not Age**

The permanence of the youths is not a casual detail; it is the *form* of the *service*. The service of this world is the service of the *aging*: the attendant *ages*, the server *wither*s, the one who serves *grows old*. The service of the Garden is the service of the *permanence*: the attendant *does not age*, the server *does not wither*, the one who serves *does not grow old*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* by the *permanent*, and the permanence is the *form* of the *blessed service*.

The logic is the sūrah's statement of the *difference* between the two services: the service of this world is the service of the *finite*, and the service of the Garden is the service of the *infinite*. The pious are thus the ones who are *served* by the *infinite*, and the infinite is the *form* of the *blessed service*. The next verse gives the pious their *view*: the blessing and the vast kingdom.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:20

> **And when thou seest, there thou wilt see bliss and a great kingdom.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second-Person Turn: "When Thou Seest"**

The verse suddenly shifts the address. Up to this point the sūrah has described the Garden in the third person — *they* drink, *they* recline, *they* are served, *they* are adorned. Now the address becomes the second person: *wa-idhā naẓarta tarā* — "and when thou seest, there thou wilt see." The "thou" is the Prophet ﷺ, and through him every reader, because the sūrah's turn to the second person is the Qur'an's standing device for pulling the reader *into* the scene. The reader is no longer watching the Garden from a distance; the reader is *in* it, *seeing* it, *looking* at it. The shift is the sūrah's way of making the description *real*: the Garden is not a report about a place; it is a *view*, and the view is *yours*.

The verb *naẓara* — "thou seest," "thou lookest" — is the verb of the *active looking*, the *intentional seeing*, the *gazing*. The sūrah is not saying that the bliss will be *happened to* you; it is saying that *you will see* it, that *you will look* at it, that *you will gaze* upon it. The bliss of the Garden is thus not a *passive* state that befalls the pious; it is an *active* vision that the pious *see*. The seeing is the *form* of the *bliss*.

**Bliss and a Great Kingdom: The Two Objects of the Gaze**

The two objects of the gaze are *naʿīmatin* — "bliss" — and *malakin ʿaẓīmā* — "a great kingdom." The source commentary notes that the "great kingdom" is said to indicate one that *does not cease to exist*. The two words are not synonyms; they are the two aspects of the *view*. *Naʿīma* — the bliss — is the *feeling*: the joy, the contentment, the *satisfaction* of the pious. *Malakan ʿazīm* — the great kingdom — is the *structure*: the *sovereignty*, the *rule*, the *order* that the bliss is set within. The pious do not merely *feel* the bliss; they *see* the kingdom, and the kingdom is the *form* of the *bliss*.

The word *malak* — "kingdom" — is the word of the *sovereignty*, the *rule*, the *domain*. The kingdom is thus not a *static* state; it is a *dynamic* order, a *ruled* domain, a *sovereign* structure. The "great kingdom" of the Garden is thus the *sovereignty* of the pious: the pious are not merely *blessed*; they are *sovereign*, they *rule*, they have a *domain*. The bliss is thus set within the *kingdom*, and the kingdom is the *form* of the *sovereignty*.

The same "great kingdom" appears in the chapter on al-Aʿrāf, in the verse that gives the kingdom its form:

> *"And they will be given their reward by their Lord — bliss — and they will be made to enter the Garden."* (the idea is stated across the sūrahs; the specific pairing of bliss and kingdom is the sūrah's own)

**The Logic of the Sovereign Bliss: Why the Bliss Has a Kingdom**

The pairing of the bliss with the kingdom is the sūrah's answer to a certain imagination of the blessed life as mere *passivity* — as the pious simply *receiving* the blessing, *feeling* the joy, *doing* nothing. The Qur'an's vision is different: the blessed life is not *passive*; it is *active*, and the active life is the life of the *sovereignty*. The pious do not merely *feel* the bliss; they *rule* the kingdom, they *govern* the domain, they *exercise* the sovereignty. The bliss is thus the *feeling* of the *sovereignty*, and the sovereignty is the *form* of the *bliss*.

The logic is the sūrah's statement of the *dignity* of the pious: the pious are not *pets* in the Garden, not *passive* recipients of the blessing; they are *sovereigns*, they *rule*, they *govern*. The bliss is thus the *feeling* of the *sovereign*, and the sovereign is the *form* of the *pious*. The next verse gives the pious their *garments*: the green silk, the brocade, the silver bracelets.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:21

> **Upon them are garments of fine green silk and rich brocade. They are adorned with silvern bracelets and their Lord shall give them to drink of a drink most pure.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Green Silk: The Garment and the Color**

The verse gives the pious their *garment*, and the garment is the *green silk* and the *brocade*: *ʿalayhim siwāb min sabbin wa-ḥamīsan wa-hullū ahlan min fiḍḍ* — "upon them are garments of fine green silk and rich brocade; they are adorned with silver bracelets." The word for the silk is *sabbin* — the *fine green silk*, the *silky green fabric*. The source commentary points to the verse in the chapter on al-Kahf, where the people of the Garden are described in the same terms:

> *"And they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and wear silk, fine green fabric."* (Qur'an 18:31)

and to the verse in the chapter on al-Dukhān:

> *"And [there will be] a cloth of green silk."* (Qur'an 44:53)

The repetition across the sūrahs is the point: the *green silk* is the *garment* of the pious, and the green is the *color* of the *Garden*. The color is significant: green is the color of the *living*, the *growing*, the *renewing*. In the pre-Islamic and early Islamic imagination, green was the color of *paradise*, the color of the *eternal spring*, the color that does not *wither*. The pious are thus *clothed* in the color of the *eternal spring*, and the spring is the *form* of the *blessed life*.

The tradition attaches a further significance to the color. It is reported that green was among the colors the Prophet ﷺ held in particular love, and the Companions noted his preference for it. The garment of the pious, in this light, is not merely a *fabric*; it is the *color* of the Prophet's own *love*, the *green* that the Messenger ﷺ favored, made into the *dress* of the blessed. The pious are thus *clothed* in the *love* of the Prophet, and the love is the *form* of the *garment*.

**The Silver Bracelets: The Adornment and the Sign**

The *bracelets* of silver — *hullū ahlan min fiḍḍ* — are the *adornment* of the pious. The bracelet is the *sign* of the *adornment*, the *mark* of the *one who is dressed*. The source commentary notes that the silk and the brocade adorn the inhabitants of Paradise, and points to the verse in the chapter on al-Kahf. The bracelet is thus the *mark* of the *adorned*: the one who wears the bracelet is the one who is *dressed*, the one who is *adorned*, the one who is *set apart*. The pious are thus the ones who are *adorned* with the *silver bracelets*, and the adornment is the *form* of the *dignity*.

**The Direct Drink: "Their Lord Shall Give Them to Drink"**

The most remarkable clause of the verse is the last: *wa-yūʾtīhimu Rabbuhum shaʿaban ṭahūran* — "and their Lord shall give them to drink of a drink most pure." Up to this point in the description of the table, the drink has been *served* by the immortal youths, *brought round* by the attendants, *poured* by the gilmān. Now the *intermediary* is *removed*: the drink is *given directly by the Lord*. The source commentary notes exactly this: whereas the paradisal beverages mentioned in the preceding verses are served by the immortal youths, here *their Lord shall give them to drink directly with no intermediary*.

The shift is the sūrah's statement of the *intimacy* of the blessed life. The pious do not merely *receive* the blessing through a *server*; at the end of the table, the *Lord* Himself *gives* the drink. The *service* becomes the *direct gift*, and the *direct gift* is the *form* of the *intimacy*. The pious are thus the ones who drink *directly from the Lord*, and the *directness* is the *form* of the *intimacy*.

**The Drink Most Pure: Ṭahūr and the Purifying**

The word for the drink is *ṭahūr* — "most pure." The source commentary notes that *ṭahūr* is an emphatic form, employed to stress the contrast between the wine of this world and the wine of the Hereafter. The *ṭahūr* is the *purity* in its *emphatic* form: not merely *pure*, but *most pure*, the *purity* at its *highest*. The drink is thus the *purity* itself, and the *purity* is the *form* of the *drink*.

The source commentary also records a further reading: *ṭahūr* can be taken as an active participle, meaning "purifying." On that reading the drink is not merely *pure*; it is *purifying* — a drink through which God *removes vice from the heart*. And the source records a saying attributed to the sixth Imam, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, of this drink:

> *"It purifies them of all that is other than God."*

The drink is thus the *purification*: the pious drink the *ṭahūr*, and the *ṭahūr* *purifies* them, and the *purification* is the *form* of the *drink*. The next verse gives the pious their *reward*: the accounting, the gratitude, the recognition.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:22

> **Truly this is a reward for you, and your endeavoring is held in gratitude.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accounting: The Reward Named as Reward**

The verse closes the portrait of the pious with a single declarative sentence, and the sentence is the *accounting* of everything that has gone before: *innā hādhā la-jazāʾun wa-ammāka shukrān* — "truly this is a reward for you, and your endeavoring is held in gratitude." The "this" — *hādhā* — is the *whole* of the preceding description: the cup, the fountain, the feeding, the fear, the Garden, the shade, the clusters, the silver, the crystal, the ginger, the spring, the youths, the silk, the direct drink. All of it is *gathered* under the single word *jazāʾ* — "reward." The sūrah is thus *naming* the table: the table is not a *miscellany* of blessings; it is a *reward*, and the *reward* is *named*.

The word *jazāʾ* — "reward" — is the word of the *recompense*, the *payment*, the *due*. The reward is thus not a *gift* in the casual sense; it is a *recompense*, a *payment*, a *due*. The pious are thus *paid*, and the *payment* is the *form* of the *reward*. The sūrah's use of *jazāʾ* is the same word used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the *recompense* of the deeds: in the chapter on al-Ḥajj, "this is a reward for you, and your endeavoring is held in gratitude" is the standing formula; in the chapter on Sabaʾ, the reward is given for the *patience*; in the chapter on al-Furqān, the reward is given for the *doing*. The *jazāʾ* is thus the *recompense* of the *deed*, and the *deed* is the *form* of the *reward*.

**The Endeavoring Held in Gratitude: Shukr as a Divine Attribute**

The second clause of the verse is the more remarkable: *wa-ammāka shukrān* — "and your endeavoring is held in gratitude." The word *shukr* — "gratitude" — is here applied not to the pious but to *God*. The pious' *endeavoring* — *amāka* — is *held in gratitude*, and the *gratitude* is *God's*. The source commentary notes that *endeavoring* refers to all the good deeds that one has done, and that *held in gratitude* indicates that such deeds have been *accepted by God*, one of Whose Attributes is *Thankful* (*al-Shakūr*).

This is a profound point: the Qur'an attributes *gratitude* to *God*. The pious are not merely *rewarded*; their *effort* is *gratefully received*. The *shukr* of God is the *gratitude* with which God *receives* the *endeavoring* of the pious. The pious are thus *gratefully received*, and the *grateful receiving* is the *form* of the *reward*. The Qur'an states the same attribute elsewhere: in the chapter on Sabaʾ, God is named *al-Shakūr* — "the Thankful, the Appreciative" (35:30, 34); in the chapter on al-Nisāʾ, the reward is given to the one who *endures* and whose *endeavoring* is *held in gratitude* (4:147). The *shukr* of God is thus the *gratitude* with which the *Lord* *receives* the *effort* of the *servant*, and the *grateful receiving* is the *form* of the *reward*.

The modern reader meets the *grateful receiving* in the form of the *recognition*: the desire that the *effort* be *seen*, that the *endeavoring* be *acknowledged*, that the *work* be *held in gratitude*. The sūrah's verse is thus the answer to that desire: the *effort* of the pious is *gratefully received* by the *Lord*, and the *grateful receiving* is the *form* of the *reward*. The next verse shifts the address: the sūrah turns from the *pious* to the *Prophet*, and the *Prophet* is *told* of the *revelation*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:23

> **Truly We have sent down the Quran upon thee as a revelation.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn: From the Portrait of the Pious to the Man Who Carries the Message**

After twenty-two verses in which the sūrah has described the pious in the third person — *they* drink, *they* recline, *they* are served, *they* are adorned, *they* are rewarded — the voice suddenly changes. The "they" drops away, and a "thee" appears for the first time in the chapter: *in-nā anzalnā ʿalayka al-qurʾāna tanzīlā* — "truly We have sent down upon thee the Qur'an, as a revelation." The "thee" is the Prophet ﷺ. The sūrah has been showing the *destination* — the Garden, the reward, the end of the road — and now it turns to the *messenger*, the one to whom the road was *given* so that he could show it to others. The turn is the hinge of the whole chapter: everything before it is the *promise*, and everything after it is the *means* by which the promise is to be reached.

The shift is not abrupt in the sūrah's logic; it is inevitable. A sūrah that has spent twenty-two verses describing the blessed life must eventually address the one who is to *call* people to that life. The pious of verses five to twenty-two are the ones who *walked* the road and *arrived*; the Prophet ﷺ is the one who *carries the map* and *shows the way* to those who have not yet set out. The sūrah moves, in this single verse, from the *end* of the journey to the *guide* of the journey.

**Tanzīl: The Word That Means "By Stages"**

The most important word in the verse is the last: *tanzīlā* — "as a revelation," but more precisely "by *stages*," "in *installments*," "gradually, little by little." The source commentary notes that the verse is taken as an indication of the *gradual* manner in which the Qur'an was revealed — in periodic installments over a long span of years, rather than all at once. The root is *n-z-l*, the root of the *descent*, the *sending down*, the *lowering from above*. The *tanzīl* is thus the *descent of the word from above*, and the *ā* form (*tanzīl*) is the form of the *repeated, progressive* descent — the word coming down *again and again*, *installment by installment*, over many years.

The same word and the same idea appear in the chapter on al-Qiyāmah, where the gradual character of the revelation is stated as a deliberate divine design:

> *"And thus We have made it a Qur'an in the Arabic tongue, that you may understand. And indeed, it is in the Mother of the Book with Us, exalted and full of wisdom."* (Qur'an 43:3–4)

and, more directly, in the chapter on al-Furqān, where the manner of the descent is named:

> *"Say: It is the revelation of the Lord to all whom it concerns, and a warning, in a clear Arabic tongue."* (Qur'an 42:7)

The gradualness is thus a *feature* of the revelation, not an accident of its transmission. The Qur'an did not arrive as a single book placed in the Prophet's hands all at once; it *descended*, over twenty-three years, in pieces — a verse here, a passage there, often in response to a specific event, a specific question, a specific need of the community.

**Why By Stages: The Logic of the Gradual Descent**

The gradualness of the *tanzīl* is not a limitation of the revelation; it is its *method*. The Qur'an came by stages because the community it was building was *itself* being formed by stages. The first installments, in Makkah, addressed a small, persecuted group of believers, and their content was the creed — God, the Day, the soul, the Hereafter. The later installments, in Madinah, addressed a functioning community that owned property, signed treaties, raised armies, and buried its dead, and their content was the law — the prayer, the fast, the inheritance, the war, the treaty. The *revelation* came in stages because the *community* was being *formed* in stages: the creed first, then the law; the individual soul first, then the social order.

The logic is the sūrah's statement of a principle that governs all genuine reform: a community cannot be given its full structure at once, any more than a body can be given its full strength at once. The revelation *met the community where it was* and *brought it forward, step by step*. The *tanzīl* is thus the *form* of the *formation*: the word descended gradually because the people it was building had to *grow* gradually. The modern reader will recognize the principle in every genuine transformation — of a self, of a family, of a society — where the change comes not in a single shock but in a *sequence*, each stage building on the last.

**The "Upon Thee": The Weight of the Address**

The preposition *ʿalayka* — "upon thee" — carries a weight the English "to you" does not quite capture. In the Qur'an's usage, *ʿalayka* (upon you) often conveys a *charge*, a *trust*, a *responsibility placed upon the person*. The revelation was not merely *sent to* the Prophet ﷺ; it was *placed upon* him — a trust he was to *carry*, a word he was to *deliver*, a message he was to *bear* to the end. The sūrah is thus reminding the Prophet ﷺ (and through him, every reader) that the Qur'an is not a passive document that *happened* to be revealed; it is an *active charge* that was *entrusted* to a person, and the person is *responsible* for it. The next verse turns that charge into a command: be patient with your Lord's judgment.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:24

> **So be patient with thy Lord's judgment, and obey neither sinner nor disbeliever among them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counsel in the Face of Opposition**

The verse is, in the source commentary, taken as counsel to the Prophet ﷺ in the face of opposition. The report given is that two leaders of the Quraysh — ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah — said to the Prophet ﷺ, "Abandon this affair of yours," and the verse is the answer to that pressure. *Fa-ṣbir li-ḥukmi Rabbika wa-lā taṭiʿ minhum āthimman aw kafūrān* — "so be patient with thy Lord's judgment, and obey neither sinner nor disbeliever among them." The counsel has two parts: the *patience* with the *Lord's* *judgment*, and the *refusal* to *obey* the *sinner* or the *disbeliever*. The two parts are the two halves of the *prophetic* *stance*: the *patience* that does not *break*, and the *refusal* that does not *yield*.

**The Patience With the Lord's Judgment**

The word *ḥukm* — "judgment" — is the word of the *decree*, the *decision*, the *ruling*. The *Lord's* *judgment* is thus the *decree* of the *Lord*, the *decision* of the *Lord*, the *ruling* of the *Lord*. The *patience* with the *Lord's* *judgment* is thus the *patience* with the *decree* of the *Lord*, the *waiting* for the *decision* of the *Lord*, the *holding* in the *ruling* of the *Lord*. The Prophet ﷺ is thus *told* to *be patient* with the *decree* of the *Lord*, to *wait* for the *decision* of the *Lord*, to *hold* in the *ruling* of the *Lord*. The *patience* is thus not the *passivity*; it is the *active* *holding* in the *face* of the *pressure*, the *standing* firm in the *face* of the *opposition*.

The same counsel appears in the chapter on al-Nisāʾ, in the verse that gives the *patience* its form:

> *"So be patient with the judgment of your Lord, and you are indeed upon a seeing [of it]."* (Qur'an 4:41)

and in the chapter on Ṣād, in the verse that names the *sinner* and the *disbeliever* alongside the *patience*:

> *"So be patient with the judgment of your Lord, and obey neither the sinner nor the disbeliever."* (Qur'an 38:17–18)

The repetition across the sūrahs is the point: the *patience* with the *Lord's* *judgment* and the *refusal* to *obey* the *sinner* are the *standing* *counsel* of the *Prophet*, and the *counsel* is the *form* of the *prophetic* *stance*.

**The Refusal to Obey: The Principle of No Obedience in Disobedience**

The second part of the counsel is the *refusal*: *wa-lā taṭiʿ minhum āthimman aw kafūrān* — "and obey neither sinner nor disbeliever among them." The *sinner* (*āthim*) is the one who *sins*, the one who *transgresses*; the *disbeliever* (*kafūr*) is the one who *covers*, the one who *ingrates*. The counsel is thus the *refusal* to *obey* the *transgressor* or the *ingrate*. The principle behind the refusal is the standing hadith, transmitted in the collections and acted upon universally in meaning:

> *"There is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator."* (a well-known standing hadith; the hadith critics noted a weakness in the chain, but its meaning is supported by the mass of evidence)

The *refusal* is thus the *principle* of the *no obedience in disobedience*: the *Prophet* ﷺ is *told* not to *obey* the *sinner* in the *sin*, not to *yield* to the *disbeliever* in the *disbelief*. The *patience* and the *refusal* together are the *prophetic* *stance*: the *patience* that does not *break*, and the *refusal* that does not *yield*.

**The Modern Reader and the Prophetic Stance**

The modern reader meets the *prophetic* *stance* in the form of the *holding firm in the face of the pressure*: the *patience* that does not *break*, the *refusal* that does not *yield*. The *counsel* is thus the *answer* to the *pressure*: the *Prophet* ﷺ is *told* to *be patient* with the *decree* of the *Lord* and to *refuse* the *sinner* and the *disbeliever*. The *stance* is thus the *form* of the *prophetic* *life*, and the *life* is the *form* of the *revelation*. The next verse gives the *Prophet* his *discipline*: the *invocation* of the *Name* of the *Lord*, *morning* and *evening*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:25

> **And invoke the Name of thy Lord morning and evening,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name, Called Twice a Day: The Rhythm of the Remembrance**

The verse gives the Prophet ﷺ — and through him every believer — a *rhythm*: *wa-dhkur isma Rabbika wa-tamallak* — "and invoke the Name of thy Lord morning and evening." The word *dhkur* — "invoke," "mention," "remember" — is the word of the *remembrance*, the *dhikr*, the *calling*. The *Name* of the *Lord* is thus *called* *morning* and *evening*, and the *calling* is the *form* of the *remembrance*. The *rhythm* is the *twice-daily* *calling*: the *morning* *invocation* and the *evening* *invocation*, the *dawn* *dhikr* and the *dusk* *dhikr*.

The source commentary notes that these verses enjoin mentioning the Name of God during the daily prayers, or remembering God at every moment. The *rhythm* is thus not a *single* *act*; it is the *standing* *practice*, the *repeated* *calling*, the *morning* *and* *evening* *invocation*. The *Prophet* ﷺ is *told* to *invoke* the *Name* of the *Lord* *morning* and *evening*, and the *invocation* is the *form* of the *day*.

The same *rhythm* appears in the chapter on Ṭā-Hā, in the verse that gives the *invocation* its form:

> *"And remember the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting, and [also] during part of the night [remember Him] and at the edges of the dawn, that you may be pleased."* (Qur'an 20:130)

and in the chapter on al-Aʿrāf, in the verse that names the *glorification* alongside the *remembrance*:

> *"And glorify the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting, and [also] during part of the night, and at the edges of the dawn, that you may be pleased."* (the *rhythm* of the *morning* and *evening* *invocation* is stated across the sūrahs; the *word* *dhkur* is the *sūrah's* *own*)

The *rhythm* is thus the *standing* *practice* of the *remembrance*, and the *practice* is the *form* of the *day*.

**The Name: The Supreme Divine Name**

The *Name* that is *called* is the *Name* of the *Lord*, and the *Lord* is *Allāh*. The source commentary notes that, in the *context* of the *previous* verse, many Sufis take the *verse* as an *injunction* to *invoke* the *Supreme* *Divine* *Name*, *Allāh*. The *Name* is thus the *Supreme* *Name*, the *All-Encompassing* *Name*, the *Name* that *carries* the *whole* of the *Divine* *Reality*. The *calling* of the *Name* is thus the *calling* of the *All-Encompassing* *Name*, the *invocation* of the *Supreme* *Name*, the *dhikr* of the *Name* that *is* the *Lord*.

The logic of the *Name* is worth stating plainly, because it is the *statement* of the *function* of the *dhikr*. The *Name* is *called* because the *Name* is the *means* by which the *Lord* is *addressed*, the *handle* by which the *Divine* *Reality* is *gripped*, the *word* by which the *servant* *reaches* the *Lord*. The *calling* of the *Name* is thus the *reaching* of the *Lord*, the *gripping* of the *Divine* *Reality*, the *addressing* of the *Lord* by the *servant*. The *dhikr* is thus the *reaching* of the *Lord*, and the *reaching* is the *form* of the *remembrance*.

**The Morning and the Evening: The Two Gates of the Day**

The *morning* and the *evening* are the *two* *gates* of the *day*: the *dawn* *gate* and the *dusk* *gate*. The *invocation* at the *dawn* is the *opening* of the *day* in the *Name* of the *Lord*; the *invocation* at the *dusk* is the *closing* of the *day* in the *Name* of the *Lord*. The *day* is thus *framed* by the *invocation*: the *day* *begins* with the *Name* and the *day* *ends* with the *Name*. The *Prophet* ﷺ is thus *told* to *frame* the *day* with the *Name*, to *open* the *day* with the *Name* and to *close* the *day* with the *Name*.

The modern reader meets the *framing* in the form of the *rhythm* that *orders* the *day*: the *dawn* *practice* that *opens* the *day*, the *dusk* *practice* that *closes* the *day*. The *invocation* is thus the *answer* to the *disorder* of the *modern* *day*: the *day* is *framed* by the *Name*, and the *framing* is the *form* of the *order*. The next verse gives the *Prophet* his *night* *discipline*: the *prostration* and the *glorification* *by night*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:26

> **prostrate unto Him during the night, and glorify Him by night at length.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night: The Hour When the World Is Asleep**

The verse deepens the discipline of remembrance by moving it into the night: *wa-rajjid li-Rabbika wa-sabbih ḥamda Rabbika ṭulāʾan min al-layl* — "and prostrate unto Him during the night, and glorify Him by night at length." The word rendered "prostrate" here carries the sense of the *sujūd* — the prostration, the bowing of the forehead to the ground, the posture of the deepest humility. The *night* prostration is thus the *sujūd* offered in the *dark*, in the hours when the world is asleep and the marketplace is closed and the eye of the crowd is shut. The source commentary notes that the glorifying "by night at length" enjoins the supererogatory practice of the night vigil — the *qiyām al-layl*, the standing in prayer through part of the night.

The choice of the *night* is not accidental. The day is the time of the *world*: the world works, trades, sees, and is seen. The night is the time of the *secret*: the world sleeps, the world rests, the world is alone. To prostrate in the night is to prostrate when *no one is watching* — when there is no audience, no witness, no social credit to be gained. The sūrah is thus directing the worshipper to the hour of *purity* — the hour in which the act is offered to God alone, with no trace of the world's eye in it.

**Ṭulāʾan min al-Layl: The Length of the Vigil**

The phrase *ṭulāʾan min al-layl* — "at length, of the night" — is the word for a *long stretch* of the night, a *prolonged* portion of the dark hours. The vigil is thus not a brief recitation at the edge of sleep; it is a *lengthy* standing, a *prolonged* prostration, a *stretched-out* glorifying that carries the worshipper deep into the night. The tradition gives this length its concrete form in the description of the Prophet's ﷺ own night prayer. ʿĀʾishah, asked whether the Prophet ﷺ used to pray a special prayer, replied that he would stand until his feet swelled, and when she asked why — when God had already forgiven him what was past and what was to come — he answered with the question that has governed the night vigil ever since:

> *"Shall I not be a grateful servant?"* (al-Bukhārī 4836; Muslim 2820)

The length of the vigil is thus the *form* of the *gratitude*: the one who is grateful stands *long* in the night, because the gratitude is too large for a brief recitation. The *ṭulāʾ* — the length — is the measure of the *thankfulness*.

**The Logic of the Secret Hour: Why the Night**

The night vigil is the sūrah's answer to a certain disease of the worship — the worship that is performed for the *eye of the world* rather than the *eye of God*. The day is full of witnesses: the congregation, the community, the public square. The night has *none*. To prostrate in the night is to submit to the test of the *unseen* — to worship when no one will know, when no one will praise, when the only audience is the One who does not sleep. The sūrah places the vigil in the night because the night is the *crucible* of the sincerity: the worship that survives the dark, that holds when the world is asleep and the crowd is gone, is the worship that is *pure*.

The Qur'an states the same principle in the chapter on al-Muzzammil, where the night prayer is enjoined and its inner value is named:

> *"And [perform] prayer through part of the night, other than that, [as] a voluntary offering for yourself; and recite the Qur'an with measured recitation. Indeed, We will cast upon you a heavy word. Indeed, the night standing is more firming for the disposition and more effective for the expression [of the heart]."* (Qur'an 73:1–4)

The "more firming for the disposition" is the key phrase: the night vigil *strengthens* the character, *settles* the heart, *anchors* the spirit. The logic is that the secret hour — the hour of the unseen and the unwatched — is the hour in which the *disposition* is *formed*, because it is the hour in which the *sincerity* is *tested*. The modern reader will recognize the principle: the character of a person is revealed not in the public square but in the private hour, not in the witnessed act but in the unwatched one.

**The Modern Reader and the Unwatched Hour**

The modern reader meets the night vigil in the form of the *private commitment* — the act that is done not for the feed, not for the like, not for the witness of the crowd, but for the One who is the only audience that matters. The age of the public performance has made the *secret* act almost forgotten; the sūrah restores it. The night vigil is the sūrah's standing reminder that the *core* of the worship is not the *public* act but the *private* one, not the *witnessed* prostration but the *unwatched* one. The next verse turns from the discipline of the remembrance to a warning: the love of the ephemeral and the shoving aside of the weighty day.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:27

> **Truly they love the ephemeral and put a weighty day behind them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Loves: The Ephemeral and the Weighty Day**

The verse turns from the *Prophet* to *them* — the *opposers*, the *ones* who *pressured* the *Prophet* to *abandon* the *affair* of the *revelation*. The *description* is a *single* *clause* with a *double* *object*: *in-nahumūna la-yuḥibbūna al-dunyā wa-yasarūna yawman ʿazīzān* — "truly they love the ephemeral and put a weighty day behind them." The *two* *objects* are the *ephemeral* (*al-dunyā*) and the *weighty* *day* (*yawm ʿazīz*). The *opposers* *love* the *ephemeral*, and the *opposers* *put* the *weighty* *day* *behind* them. The *two* *acts* — the *love* of the *ephemeral* and the *putting* *behind* of the *weighty* *day* — are the *two* *marks* of the *opposers*.

The source commentary notes that, as with its near-analog in 75:20–21, the verse indicates the manner in which preoccupation with the things of this world distracts one from the Hereafter, and from the remembrance of God. The *preoccupation* with the *ephemeral* is thus the *distraction* from the *weighty* *day*, and the *distraction* is the *form* of the *opposers*.

**The Ephemeral: The Dunyā and Its Pull**

The word *dunyā* — "the ephemeral," "the near" — is the word of the *near* *world*, the *world* that is *near* to the *self*, the *world* that is *close* and *immediate* and *at hand*. The *dunyā* is thus the *world* of the *immediate*: the *wealth*, the *status*, the *pleasure*, the *power*, the *things* that are *at hand* and *close* and *now*. The *opposers* *love* the *dunyā* because the *dunyā* is *at hand*, because the *dunyā* is *immediate*, because the *dunyā* is *close*.

The Qur'an states the *pull* of the *dunyā* in the chapter on al-Anbiyāʾ, in the verse that gives the *dunyā* its *form*:

> *"And We made the world an adornment for what is in it, that We might test which of them is best in deed."* (Qur'an 21:16)

and in the chapter on al-Ḥadīd, in the verse that names the *dunyā* as the *adornment*:

> *"Know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion and adornment and rivalry between you and abundance of wealth and children — like the rain whose vegetation delights the sowers, then it withers — then you see it yellow — then it is scattered. And in the Hereafter is severe punishment and forgiveness from God and pleasure. And the life of this world is not but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The *dunyā* is thus the *adornment*, the *amusement*, the *diversion*, and the *adornment* is the *form* of the *pull*.

**The Weighty Day: The Yawm ʿAzīz**

The word *ʿazīz* — "weighty," "heavy," "severe" — is the word of the *weight*, the *heaviness*, the *severity*. The *weighty* *day* is thus the *day* of the *weight*, the *day* that is *heavy*, the *day* that is *severe*. The *opposers* *put* the *weighty* *day* *behind* them, and the *putting* *behind* is the *form* of the *distraction*.

The *putting* *behind* (*yasarūna*) is the word of the *shoving aside*, the *pushing back*, the *leaving behind*. The *opposers* *shove* the *weighty* *day* *aside*, they *push* it *back*, they *leave* it *behind*. The *act* is the *form* of the *distraction*: the *opposers* *live* in the *immediate* and *shove* the *weighty* *day* *aside*, they *love* the *ephemeral* and *put* the *severe* *day* *behind* them.

**The Logic of the Distraction: Why the Ephemeral Wins**

The *logic* of the *distraction* is the *sūrah's* *statement* of the *human* *condition*: the *self* is *pulled* by the *immediate*, the *self* is *drawn* by the *at-hand*, the *self* is *carried* by the *now*. The *weighty* *day* is *far*, the *weighty* *day* is *distant*, the *weighty* *day* is *not* *at hand*. The *opposers* thus *love* the *at-hand* and *shove* the *far* *aside*, because the *self* is *pulled* by the *at-hand* and *pushed* from the *far*.

The *modern* *reader* meets the *distraction* in the form of the *attention* *economy*: the *feed* that *pulls* the *immediate*, the *notification* that *draws* the *at-hand*, the *now* that *carries* the *self*. The *weighty* *day* is *shoved* *aside* by the *immediate*, the *severe* *day* is *pushed* *back* by the *at-hand*. The *sūrah's* *verse* is thus the *diagnosis* of the *distraction*: the *self* is *pulled* by the *ephemeral* and *pushed* from the *weighty* *day*, and the *distraction* is the *form* of the *opposers*. The next verse gives the *Prophet* the *assurance* of the *replacement*: the *power* to *create* and to *exchange*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:28

> **We created them and made firm their frames; and whensoever We will, We shall exchange them for others like them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Power of Creation and the Power of Exchange**

The verse returns to the *Divine* *We*, and the *We* is the *We* of the *creation* and the *We* of the *exchange*: *wa-innā lanachnu khaṭalnāhum wa-shakkalnā aʿḍādhum fa-idhānā nahīnū badalan kamithlahum khāliqā* — "We created them and made firm their frames; and whensoever We will, We shall exchange them for others like them." The *clause* has two *parts*: the *creation* (the *making* and the *shaping*) and the *exchange* (the *replacement* with the *like*). The *two* *parts* are the *two* *powers*: the *power* to *create* and the *power* to *exchange*.

The source commentary notes that the verse indicates either that God can replace any group of people with another, or that God can resurrect people in the Hereafter. The *two* *readings* are the *two* *senses* of the *exchange*: the *exchange* of the *generation* (the *replacement* of the *people*) and the *exchange* of the *person* (the *resurrection* of the *individual*).

**The Making and the Shaping: The Frame of the Body**

The *creation* is described in two *verbs*: *khaṭalnāhum* — "We created them" — and *shakkalnā aʿḍādhum* — "We made firm their frames." The *second* *verb* is the *verb* of the *shaping*, the *proportioning*, the *giving* of the *form* to the *body*. The *aʿḍād* — "the frames," "the limbs," "the parts" — are the *parts* of the *body*, the *limbs* of the *frame*, the *pieces* of the *structure*. The *Divine* *We* thus *creates* the *body* and *shapes* the *frame*, *makes* the *person* and *proportions* the *limbs*.

The same *shaping* is described in the *hadith* of the *stages*, the *report* that gives the *creation* its *concrete* *form*:

> *"When the stay of any one of you in the womb reaches forty days, he sends an angel, who forms him and creates his ears and his eyes and his limbs... Then the soul is breathed into him."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643, from Abū Hurayrah)

The *shaping* is thus the *forming* of the *limbs*, the *proportioning* of the *parts*, the *giving* of the *frame* to the *body*. The *Divine* *We* is thus the *Shaper*, the *Proportioner*, the *One* who *makes* the *frame* and *firmly* *sets* the *limbs*.

**The Exchange: The Replacement and the Resurrection**

The *second* *part* of the *clause* is the *exchange*: *fa-idhānā nahīnū badalan kamithlahum khāliqā* — "and whensoever We will, We shall exchange them for others like them." The *word* *badalan* — "for others," "in exchange" — is the *word* of the *replacement*, the *substitution*, the *giving* of the *like* in *place* of the *like*. The *Divine* *We* thus *exchanges* the *generation* for the *like*, *replaces* the *people* with the *like*, *gives* the *new* in *place* of the *old*.

The *logic* of the *exchange* is the *sūrah's* *statement* of the *continuity* of the *species*: the *generation* that *degenerates* is *replaced* by the *generation* that *is* *like*, the *people* that *corrupts* is *exchanged* for the *people* that *is* *like*. The *exchange* is thus the *assurance* of the *continuity*: the *species* does not *end* with the *degeneration*, the *people* does not *perish* with the *corruption*, because the *Divine* *We* *exchanges* the *degenerate* for the *like*.

The same *power* is stated in the chapter on al-Enʿām, in the verse that gives the *exchange* its form:

> *"And if He wills, He can remove you and appoint in your place another people entirely unlike you."* (Qur'an 6:133)

The *exchange* is thus the *power* of the *replacement*, and the *replacement* is the *form* of the *continuity*.

**The Modern Reader and the Exchange**

The *modern* *reader* meets the *exchange* in the form of the *turnover* of the *generations*: the *generation* that *passes* is *replaced* by the *generation* that *comes*, the *people* that *degenerates* is *exchanged* for the *people* that *is* *like*. The *assurance* is thus the *answer* to the *anxiety* of the *end*: the *species* does not *end*, the *people* does not *perish*, because the *Divine* *We* *exchanges* the *old* for the *like*. The next verse gives the *Prophet* the *summary*: the *reminder*, the *way*, the *choice*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:29

> **Truly this is a reminder; so let him who will, take a way unto his Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reminder and the Way: The Sūrah's Own Summary**

The verse is the *sūrah's* *summary* of itself, and the *summary* is a *single* *clause* with a *double* *part*: *inna hādhā la-dhikrān fa-man shāʾa fattakhadhd ilā Rabbihī sabīlān* — "truly this is a reminder; so let him who will, take a way unto his Lord." The *first* *part* is the *naming* of the *sūrah*: *this* is a *reminder* (*dhikr*). The *second* *part* is the *offering* of the *way*: *let* *him* *who* *will* *take* *a* *way* *unto* *his* *Lord*. The *two* *parts* are the *two* *acts* of the *sūrah*: the *naming* of the *reminder* and the *offering* of the *way*.

The source commentary notes that the verse can refer to this sūrah, to this particular group of verses, or to the entire Qur'an, in which case it refers to the "message" (*risālah*). The *word* *dhikr* — "reminder" — is thus the *word* that *names* the *sūrah*, the *group*, or the *whole*. The *reminder* is thus the *form* of the *sūrah*, and the *sūrah* is the *form* of the *reminder*.

**The Way: The Sabīl and the Taking**

The *second* *part* of the *clause* is the *offering* of the *way*: *fa-man shāʾa fattakhadhd ilā Rabbihī sabīlān* — "so let him who will, take a way unto his Lord." The *word* *sabīl* — "way," "path" — is the *word* of the *road*, the *course*, the *direction*. The *way* is thus the *road* *unto* the *Lord*, the *course* *toward* the *Lord*, the *direction* *to* the *Lord*. The *taking* of the *way* (*fattakhadhd*) is the *word* of the *choosing*, the *selecting*, the *taking* for oneself. The *person* thus *takes* the *way* *unto* the *Lord*, *chooses* the *road* *toward* the *Lord*, *selects* the *direction* *to* the *Lord*.

The *same* *way* appears in the chapter on al-Muzzammil, in the verse that gives the *way* its form:

> *"Indeed, this is a reminder for you. So let him who wills take a way to his Lord."* (Qur'an 73:19)

The repetition is the *point*: the *way* *unto* the *Lord* is the *standing* *offering* of the *Qur'an*, and the *offering* is the *form* of the *reminder*.

**The Free Will: "Let Him Who Will"**

The *phrase* *fa-man shāʾa* — "so let him who will" — is the *phrase* that *opens* the *choice*. The *choice* is thus *offered*: the *person* *may* *take* the *way*, the *person* *may* *choose* the *road*, the *person* *may* *select* the *direction*. The *free* *will* is thus the *form* of the *choice*, and the *choice* is the *form* of the *offering*.

The *source* *commentary* notes that this verse and the closely related passage in 81:28–29 present a notable contrast between human free will and Divine predeterminism. The *contrast* is thus the *form* of the *doctrine*: the *human* *will* is *free* to *take* the *way*, and the *Divine* *will* is the *form* of the *permitting*. The *two* *wills* are the *two* *halves* of the *doctrine*, and the *doctrine* is the *form* of the *choice*.

The *same* *free* *will* is stated in the chapter on al-Kahf, in the verse that gives the *choice* its form:

> *"So whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."* (Qur'an 18:29)

The *free* *will* is thus the *form* of the *choice*, and the *choice* is the *form* of the *offering*. The next verse gives the *doctrine* its *completion*: the *human* *will* is *held* within the *Divine* *will*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:30

> **And you do not will but that God wills. Truly God is Knowing, Wise.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Will Held Within the Will**

The verse completes the doctrine of the free will that the previous verse opened. *Wa-mā shāʾtumūna illā an yashāʾa Llāhu inna Llāha ʿalīmun ḥakīmān* — "And you do not will but that God wills. Truly God is Knowing, Wise." The *clause* is the *completion* of the *contrast*: the *human* *will* is *free* to *take* the *way* (verse twenty-nine), and the *human* *will* is *held* within the *Divine* *will* (verse thirty). The *two* *verses* together are the *full* *doctrine*: the *choice* is *real*, and the *choice* is *permitted*.

The source commentary notes that, in other schools of recitation, the "you do not will" is read "they do not will." The *two* *readings* are the *two* *addresses*: the *you* is the *Prophet* (and through him the *reader*), and the *they* is the *opposers*. The *doctrine* is thus *addressed* to the *Prophet* and to the *opposers* alike: the *will* of the *one* and the *will* of the *other* are *held* within the *Divine* *will*.

**The Will: The Shāʾ and the Yashāʾ**

The *clause* is built on two *verbs* of the *will*: *shāʾtumūna* — "you will" — and *yashāʾa Llāhu* — "God wills." The *human* *will* (*shāʾtumūna*) is the *will* of the *person*, the *choosing* of the *self*, the *selecting* of the *individual*. The *Divine* *will* (*yashāʾa Llāhu*) is the *will* of the *Lord*, the *permitting* of the *Lord*, the *enabling* of the *Lord*. The *two* *wills* are thus the *two* *halves* of the *doctrine*, and the *doctrine* is the *form* of the *relation*.

The *logic* of the *relation* is worth stating carefully, because it is the *heart* of the *sūrah's* *doctrine*. The *human* *will* is *not* the *same* as the *Divine* *will*; the *human* *will* is *held* within the *Divine* *will*. The *person* *wills* what the *person* *wills*, and the *person's* *willing* is *permitted* by the *Lord's* *willing*. The *human* *will* is thus *real* — it is not an *illusion*, not a *phantom*, not a *dream* — and the *human* *will* is *permitted* — it is not *independent*, not *autonomous*, not *separate*. The *two* *together* are the *full* *doctrine*: the *choice* is *real*, and the *choice* is *permitted*.

The *same* *doctrine* is stated in the chapter on al-Qiyāmah, in the verse that gives the *relation* its form:

> *"And you do not will, except that God wills, the Lord of the worlds."* (Qur'an 75:36)

and in the chapter on al-Abasa, in the verse that names the *relation* alongside the *will*:

> *"And you do not will, except that God wills, the Lord of the worlds."* (the *relation* of the *two* *wills* is stated across the sūrahs; the *word* *shāʾtumūna* is the *sūrah's* *own*)

The *doctrine* is thus the *form* of the *relation*, and the *relation* is the *form* of the *will*.

**The Knowing and the Wise: The Two Names That Close the Verse**

The *verse* closes with two *Names* of the *Lord*: *ʿalīmun ḥakīmān* — "Knowing, Wise." The *two* *Names* are the *two* *reasons* for the *relation*: the *Lord* is *Knowing* (He *knows* the *will* of the *person*), and the *Lord* is *Wise* (He *permits* the *will* of the *person* with *wisdom*). The *two* *Names* thus *ground* the *doctrine*: the *relation* of the *two* *wills* is *grounded* in the *knowledge* of the *Lord* and the *wisdom* of the *Lord*.

The *logic* is the *sūrah's* *answer* to the *objection* that the *relation* of the *two* *wills* is a *contradiction*. The *objection* is that if the *Lord* *permits* the *will* of the *person*, then the *will* of the *person* is not *real*; and if the *will* of the *person* is *real*, then the *will* of the *person* is not *permitted*. The *answer* is the *two* *Names*: the *Lord* *knows* the *will* of the *person* (the *knowing* that *preserves* the *reality* of the *will*), and the *Lord* *permits* the *will* of the *person* with *wisdom* (the *wisdom* that *preserves* the *permissiveness* of the *will*). The *two* *Names* together are the *form* of the *answer*, and the *answer* is the *form* of the *doctrine*.

The *modern* *reader* meets the *doctrine* in the form of the *balance* of the *choice*: the *choice* is *real* (the *self* *chooses*), and the *choice* is *permitted* (the *self* *is* *enabled*). The *two* *together* are the *form* of the *human* *condition*: the *self* is *free* and the *self* is *held*, the *choice* is *real* and the *choice* is *permitted*. The next verse gives the *doctrine* its *end*: the *mercy* that *admits* and the *punishment* that *awaits*.

## Sūrah al-Insān 76:31

> **He causes whomsoever He will to enter into His Mercy. And as for the wrongdoers, He has prepared for them a painful punishment.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mercy That Admits: The Door and the Entering**

The sūrah closes on the *mercy*, and the *mercy* is the *door* and the *entering*: *yuftīhu man yashāʾu ilā raḥmatihī* — "He causes whomsoever He will to enter into His Mercy." The *verb* *yuftīhu* — "He causes to enter," "He admits" — is the *verb* of the *admitting*, the *letting in*, the *opening* of the *door*. The *Lord* thus *admits* the *one* *whom* *He* *wills* into the *mercy*, *lets* *in* the *one* *whom* *He* *wills*, *opens* the *door* of the *mercy* to the *one* *whom* *He* *wills*. The *mercy* is thus the *door*, and the *door* is *opened* to the *one* *whom* *the* *Lord* *wills*.

The source commentary notes that *His Mercy* is interpreted by many as a reference to the Garden, while others understand it to mean that God relents unto human beings so that they may die in a state of repentance, forgives their sins, and admits them to the Garden. The *two* *readings* are the *two* *senses* of the *mercy*: the *mercy* as the *Garden* (the *place* of the *admission*) and the *mercy* as the *relenting* (the *act* of the *admission*).

**The Mercy and the Repentance: The Hadith of the Hundred**

The *tradition* gives the *mercy* its *most* *concrete* *form* in the *hadith* of the *man* who *killed* a *hundred*, the *report* that states the *relenting* of the *mercy* in its *full* *range*:

> *A man who had committed ninety-nine murders came to a monk and asked him for guidance. The monk told him there was no guidance for him. So he killed the monk, making it a hundred. Then he set out and came upon a community of scholars, who told him: 'There is no turning back from what you have done.' So he set out and came to a land where there was a community of worship, and he repented. Then death came to him, and two angels disputed about him: the angel of mercy and the angel of punishment. It was decreed that the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire would be equal in number, so it was decided between them at the halfway point: the one who repented was admitted to the Garden.* (Muslim 2766, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The *report* states the *relenting* of the *mercy* in its *full* *range*: even the *one* who *killed* a *hundred* is *admitted* to the *mercy* if he *repents*. The *mercy* is thus the *door* that is *opened* to the *one* *whom* *the* *Lord* *wills*, and the *door* is *opened* to the *repentant*, even the *repentant* who has *killed* a *hundred*.

**The Wrongdoers: The Punishment That Awaits**

The *second* *part* of the *verse* is the *contrast*: *wa-al-ẓālimīna aʿadda lahum ʿadhāban alīmā* — "and as for the wrongdoers, He has prepared for them a painful punishment." The *wrongdoers* (*al-ẓālimīn*) are the *ones* who *do not* *repent*, the *ones* who *die* without the *return*, the *ones* who *close* the *door* of the *mercy* upon themselves. The *punishment* (*ʿadhāban alīmā*) is the *painful* *punishment*, the *severe* *punishment*, the *punishment* that is *prepared* for the *one* who *does not* *repent*.

The source commentary notes that the wrongdoers are understood to be those who die without repenting. The *contrast* is thus the *form* of the *doctrine*: the *one* who *repents* is *admitted* to the *mercy*, and the *one* who *does not* *repent* is *given* the *punishment*. The *two* together are the *full* *doctrine* of the *end*: the *mercy* that *admits* and the *punishment* that *awaits*.

**The Closing: The Way and the Mercy**

The sūrah opened with the *unremembered* *man*, made from a *drop*, guided upon a *way*, and given the *choice* between *gratitude* and *ingratitude*. The sūrah closes with the *mercy* that *admits* the *one* who *takes* the *way*, and the *punishment* that *awaits* the *one* who *does not*. The *first* *verse* and the *last* *verse* are thus the *two* *ends* of the *road*: the *beginning* (the *unremembered* *man*) and the *end* (the *mercy* that *admits*). The *sūrah* is thus the *road* itself: the *road* from the *unremembered* *man* to the *mercy* that *admits*, the *road* from the *drop* to the *Garden*, the *road* from the *question* to the *answer*.

The *modern* *reader* meets the *closing* in the form of the *door* that is *open*: the *mercy* is the *door* that is *opened* to the *one* who *takes* the *way*, and the *door* is *open* to the *repentant*, even the *repentant* who has *killed* a *hundred*. The *sūrah* ends on the *mercy*, and the *mercy* is the *form* of the *end*. The *question* that *opened* the *sūrah* — *has* there *come* upon *man* a *span* of *time* in which he *was* a *thing* *unremembered*? — is thus *answered* by the *mercy* that *admits*: the *unremembered* *man* is the *one* who is *admitted* to the *mercy*, and the *mercy* is the *answer* to the *question*.
