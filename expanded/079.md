# Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt (Chapter 79) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Named for a Pull**

Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt, "Those That Wrest Violently," takes its name from the opening word of its first verse — *al-nāziʿāt*, the plural of a participle built from the root *n-z-ʿ*, which carries the sense of pulling, wrenching, or extracting something with force, as one draws a spear from a wound or a stake from hard ground. The sūrah is named after a single verb-form, and in that naming the whole chapter is already summarised: something is being pulled out. What is being pulled? The commentators answer: souls from bodies at the moment of death, and human beings from the earth at the moment of resurrection. A sūrah that opens with the image of a violent extraction ends with the image of a lifetime shrinking into an evening. Between those two images lies the entire argument of the chapter about the Day of Judgment.

The chapter has two other names preserved in the early literature. It is called al-Sāhirah, "The Wide Expanse," after verse 14, where the resurrected are said to find themselves upon a vast, featureless plain. It is also called al-Ṭāmmah, "The Calamity," after verse 34, which names the Day of Judgment by a word derived from a verb meaning to overflow or to rise above everything, the way a flood rises above the banks. A sūrah thus has three titles drawn from three of its own verses — a pull, a plain, and a flood — and each title is one scene of the same event.

**Where It Stands in the Order of Revelation**

Al-Nāziʿāt is a Makkan sūrah from the later Makkan period, and the soundest opinion places it close to Sūrah 78, al-Nabaʾ, immediately before or after it. That adjacency is not accidental. Al-Nabaʾ closes with the warning:

> *"Truly We have warned you of a punishment nigh — the Day when man shall see that which his hands have forwarded, and the disbeliever shall say, 'Would that I were dust!'"* (Qur'an 78:40)

Al-Nāziʿāt then opens by swearing, in five successive oaths, on the very agents and forces through which that "nigh punishment" arrives. The connection the classical commentators noted is that the second sūrah takes up precisely where the first left off: al-Nabaʾ announced that a warning had been given; al-Nāziʿāt shows the machinery of the warning in motion. Read together, the two chapters form one continuous discourse on the Hereafter — the announcement, and then the execution.

In the standard muṣḥaf it is the seventy-ninth chapter and contains forty-six verses. In the Indo-Pak arrangement of the text into *rukūʿs* (thematic sections marked in the margin) it is divided into two: verses 1–26, which move from the oaths through the story of Moses and Pharaoh; and verses 27–46, which move from the argument from creation to the two destinies and the closing question about the Hour.

**The Five Oaths and the Grammar of Divine Swearing**

The sūrah opens with five oaths — *wa'l-nāziʿāt gharqā*, *wa'l-nāshiṭāt nashṭā*, *wa'l-sābiḥāti sabḥā*, *fa'l-sābiqāti sabqā*, *fa'l-mudabbirāti amrā* — and this pattern of successive oaths is one of the distinguishing marks of the short Makkan revelation. Why does God swear at all? The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī, explain that an oath serves three functions at once: it magnifies the object sworn by, it magnifies what is sworn about, and it seizes the listener's attention at the opening of a discourse. When the Qur'an swears by a created thing — by the sun, by the pen, by the horse's breath, by time — it is simultaneously telling you that this thing is worth your attention and telling you that what follows is worth your life.

There is a deliberate asymmetry here that later jurists drew out with care. God may swear by whatever He has created, because the Creator swearing by His creature is an act of honouring it. Human beings may not, because for a creature to swear by a creature is to raise that creature to the rank of the Ultimate. The Prophet ﷺ fixed the rule in terms that appear in the most authoritative collections:

> *"Whoever swears, let him swear by God, or else let him remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 3836; Muslim 1646)

and, in a wording transmitted by al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd:

> *"Whoever swears by other than God has committed an act of association."* (al-Tirmidhī 1535; Abū Dāwūd 3251)

The theological logic is plain. An oath is an appeal to the highest authority you acknowledge as guarantor of your word. To swear by something is, functionally, to declare it the most binding thing in your universe. The Qur'an's own oaths therefore are not merely rhetorical ornaments; they are a curriculum in what deserves awe. And the five oaths of al-Nāziʿāt are a curriculum in the unseen administration of the world — the angels who extract souls, who descend with commands, who race ahead, who manage affairs — followed immediately by the Day toward which all that administration is moving.

**The Argument of the Chapter in Five Movements**

The sūrah's structure is unusually clean, and once seen it governs everything that follows.

The first movement (vv. 1–5) swears by the unseen workforce of God. The second (vv. 6–9) describes the Day itself: the quaking, the successor that follows it, the trembling hearts, the humbled eyes. The third (vv. 10–14) stages the objection of the deniers — *"Are we to be restored as we were before? What! When we have become decayed bones?"* — and answers it not with a metaphysical treatise but with a scene: one cry, and behold, they are standing on the plain. The fourth (vv. 15–26) turns to history and tells the story of Moses and Pharaoh, the first account of Moses in the chronological order of revelation, offered here as a warning to the Makkan notables who behaved exactly as Pharaoh had. The fifth (vv. 27–33) turns to cosmology: *Are you more difficult to create or the sky that He has built?* The sixth (vv. 34–41) returns to the Day and divides humanity into two — the rebel who preferred this world, and the one who feared standing before his Lord and forbade his soul its caprice. The seventh (vv. 42–46) closes with the question the Makkans actually asked — *when shall it set in?* — and refuses the question while confirming the fact.

Notice what this structure accomplishes. The chapter does not argue for the Resurrection from one direction but from four independent directions at once: the administration of the unseen (which implies a Governor who does not abandon His governance), the testimony of history (which shows that arrogant power is destroyed), the testimony of the greater creation (which shows that the lesser creation is no difficulty), and the testimony of moral consciousness (which shows that a world without a reckoning would be incoherent). A person may resist one of these arguments; the chapter supplies four.

**The First Moses of the Qur'an**

Verses 15–26 carry a distinction that is easy to miss: according to the standard chronological arrangements of the revelation, this is the *first* place in the Qur'an where the story of Moses is narrated. The later, longer accounts in Sūrahs 7, 20, 26, 28, and 44 had not yet been revealed. The sūrah assumes its audience already knows the outline — the Arabs of Makkah lived alongside Jewish and Christian communities and were familiar in broad terms with the story of Moses and Pharaoh — and it uses that shared knowledge economically: a prophet is sent to the most arrogant ruler on earth; the ruler is invited gently, offered a sign, refuses, escalates, declares himself a god, and is destroyed.

That economy is itself the point. The Qur'an is not retelling a biography; it is drawing a parallel. The Makkans who mocked Muhammad ﷺ were not being asked to accept a strange new doctrine about a distant people. They were being shown their own behaviour in an older mirror. Pharaoh said, *"I am your lord most high"* — and every human being who demands absolute obedience, who makes himself the final court of appeal in his own life or in his society, is repeating a sentence that was already answered at the bottom of a sea.

**The Two Destinations, and the Definition of the Human**

If one wanted the moral centre of the chapter, it would be verses 37–41, which define two human beings by two inner acts. The first *rebelled* and *preferred the life of this world*. The second *feared the standing before his Lord* and *forbade his soul from caprice*. Neither definition mentions wealth, lineage, nationality, or even the number of rituals performed. Both definitions are about the direction of a person's fear and the direction of a person's desire.

This is the deepest psychological insight of the sūrah, and it anticipates the closing verses about time. A human being is, in the end, a set of preferences about where ultimate weight lies. Verse 46 then supplies the measure: on the Day they see it, the whole of earthly time — childhood, ambition, grief, triumph, old age — will have the felt length of an evening, or of the morning that precedes it. The chapter that began with souls being wrenched out of bodies ends with a lifetime compressed into a few hours of light. The reader is left with a single practical question, and the Qur'an poses such questions in order to be answered by conduct rather than by comment: what are you preferring, and what is it worth in the currency of an evening?

**How to Read What Follows**

What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary. Each of the forty-six verses is treated as an independent, complete discourse, as the verse-by-verse method requires, drawing on the Arabic roots and morphology, on the cross-references within the Qur'an with their actual wording, on the authenticated sayings of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, on the classical exegetes — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAbbās and the early authorities he transmits, Ibn Taymiyyah where his legal and doctrinal reasoning bears on the verse — and on the reasoning that connects a late Makkan revelation to the ordinary experience of a modern human being: the death of a parent, the arrogance of power, the shrinking of a career, the fear of standing before someone who has read the whole file.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:1

> **By those that wrest violently,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath, Not a Statement**

The Qur'an does not begin this chapter with a claim; it begins with a swear. The opening particle *wa-* is the particle of the oath (*wāw al-qasam*), and what follows is not an argument to be assessed but an appeal to be felt: *wa'l-nāziʿāt gharqā* — "by those that wrest violently." Something is about to be said of such weight that the Speaker introduces it by invoking the most powerful forces in the unseen world. The classical commentators explain the logic of this: when a person swears by a thing, he does so either because the thing sworn by is itself great, or because what is sworn about is great, or both. Here both apply. The agents invoked are the angels entrusted with the most terrifying operation in the created order — the separation of a soul from its body — and what will be sworn about is the Day on which every one of those separations is finally settled.

Al-Ṭabarī records the interpretive range with his characteristic breadth: the words may refer to different categories of angels, to different modes of death, to the stars and other celestial bodies in their various phases, or to all of these together, because God is swearing by everything that exhibits the characteristics the verses describe. Al-Rāzī adds a methodological caution that deserves to be remembered throughout this commentary: we cannot assert with certainty which of these interpretations is what God intended; we can say that they are all possible, and that the oath's power does not depend on selecting one. The verse is deliberately capacious, and the reverence that belongs to the Qur'an includes not forcing it into a single box.

**The Root That Means to Pull Something Out by Force**

The word *nāziʿāt* is the feminine plural of an active participle from the root *n-z-ʿ*, and this root is one of the most physically concrete in the Arabic language. It means to pull, to pluck, to extract, to wrench away — the motion of drawing a spear out of a wound, a stake out of packed earth, a tooth out of a jaw. Arabic uses the same root for drawing a bow to its full extent, for stripping something off a body, and for pulling a thing out of the place it does not want to leave.

The Qur'an employs this root elsewhere with the same sense of extraction, and each occurrence throws light on this one. God says of the Day of Judgment:

> *"And on the Day when We raise up from every community a witness against them from among themselves..."* (Qur'an 16:84)

— using the verb *nazaʿnā*, "We pluck out," for the summoning of a community's own witness from within it. It says of Satan at the moment of the first sin:

> *"...he stripped them of their clothing that he might show them their shameful parts."* (Qur'an 7:27)

— *yanziʿu ʿanhumā libāsahumā*, "he wrenches off from them their covering." And it says of God's absolute sovereignty:

> *"Say: O God, Master of the dominion, You give the dominion to whom You will and You wrest the dominion from whom You will."* (Qur'an 3:26)

— *wa tanziʿu al-mulk mimman tashāʾ*, "and You pluck kingship away from whom You will." Read these three together and a pattern emerges: *n-z-ʿ* is the verb of removal from a place of attachment, and it is used for garments, for authority, and for people. What the first verse of al-Nāziʿāt adds is the removal of the soul.

**"Gharqā": Drawn to the Utmost**

The second word, *gharqā*, is a verbal noun in the accusative describing the manner of the wresting, and its root is the same as the word for drowning. Its classical sense is intensity carried to the limit: the archer who draws the bowstring all the way to the arrowhead is said to have drawn it *gharqan*, "to the drowning point." So the phrase means wrestling with the utmost force, pulling until nothing is left in the hand. There is no gentleness in the word and none is intended. The commentators explain why: these are the angels who extract the souls of those who die in a state of rejection, and the extraction is violent because the soul does not wish to go. It has grown attached to the body as its whole world; being torn from it is like being torn from the only home it ever knew.

Ibn ʿAbbās, through Mujāhid and the other early authorities, understood the verse in exactly these terms, and al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī all transmit it as the primary reading. Al-Zamakhsharī sharpens it: the souls of the disbelievers are drawn out as the thorn is drawn from damp wool, catching and tearing at every fibre on the way.

**The Authentic Description of That Extraction**

This interpretation is not a pious guess. It corresponds to one of the longest and most detailed authenticated reports about the moment of death, narrated by al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib and recorded by Abū Dāwūd (no. 4753), by Aḥmad in his *Musnad*, and by al-Ḥākim, who declared its chain sound. The Prophet ﷺ was sitting at a grave in the cemetery of al-Baqīʿ while the diggers worked, and he described what happens when the disbeliever dies:

> *"…the angel of death comes and sits at his head and says: 'O evil soul, come out to the anger of God and His displeasure.' So it scatters throughout his body, and he extracts it as a many-pronged skewer is extracted from wet wool, tearing with it the veins and sinews."* (Abū Dāwūd 4753; Aḥmad; al-Ḥākim — ṣaḥīḥ)

The image of *al-saffūd* — the iron skewer with several prongs — being pulled out of soaked wool is precise: the wool resists, the fibres cling, and the extraction is ruinous to what it passes through. This is precisely *n-z-ʿ gharqan*. The report continues to describe the soul being carried upward, the gates of heaven not opening for it, and its being cast back down — the Qur'an's own summary of which is:

> *"…the gates of heaven shall not be opened for them, nor shall they enter the Garden until the camel passes through the eye of the needle."* (Qur'an 7:40)

**Why the Qur'an Opens a Chapter About the Last Day with the Moment of Death**

There is a rhetorical reason and a psychological reason, and both matter. Rhetorically, death is the first instalment of the Last Day. The denier who mocks the Resurrection is not confronting a distant abstraction; he is confronting an event that has already happened to every human being who has ever lived, and that will happen to him. By opening with the extraction of the soul, the sūrah moves the Hereafter from the far horizon to the bedside. Psychologically, the human being is constitutionally incapable of feeling the Last Day, because it lies beyond experience; but he is entirely capable of feeling death, because he has watched it. The Qur'an's method is to use the felt event as the bridge to the unfelt one.

Consider what happens in a hospital room. A person who spent forty years accumulating — property, titles, disputes, grudges, unfinished schemes — reaches a point at which every one of those possessions becomes, in a single afternoon, irrelevant to him personally. Nothing is transferred to him; everything is transferred from him. That is a small rehearsal of the wresting. The verse teaches that the rehearsal and the performance use the same verb.

**Not Yours to Keep: The Soul as a Loan**

Underneath the violence of the image lies a doctrinal fact that the Qur'an states flatly elsewhere:

> *"God takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died [He takes] in their sleep. Then He keeps those upon which He has decreed death and releases the others until an appointed term."* (Qur'an 39:42)

The soul is not property; it is a deposit. This is why the violent extraction is not merely a physical description but a verdict on a life. A person who has lived as though his soul, his time, his body and his faculties were his own private estate experiences their recall as confiscation. A person who has lived as a steward experiences it as a return. The same hand performs the same motion in both cases; what differs is the direction of the will being pulled. That is why the next verse describes an extraction that is quick rather than wrenching — not because a different angel is sent, but because a different soul is being taken.

**The Modern Reader at the Same Bedside**

We live in the first civilization in history that has systematically removed death from view. The vast majority of deaths now occur in institutions, behind curtains, in hours when the family is elsewhere, and the body is then handled by professionals. The cultural effect is not that people stopped dying but that they stopped practising for it. The Qur'an's opening oath here is a deliberate reversal of that arrangement. It forces the reader, in the very first words of the chapter, to imagine the specific, physical, non-negotiable moment at which the thing he calls "me" is separated from the thing he calls "my body" — and to notice that he has no technique whatsoever for negotiating it.

This is not morbidity. The Prophet ﷺ described the practice in a single sentence that has been acted upon by ascetics and scholars for fourteen centuries:

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī; graded ḥasan)

— *akthirū dhikra hādim al-ladhdhāt*, death being the thing that demolishes every pleasure. The purpose of remembering it is not sadness but ordering: a person who remembers that his lease on the body is revocable at any moment without notice stops postponing his apologies, stops hoarding what he cannot keep, and stops treating the opinion of strangers as the final measure of his worth. The oath at the head of this sūrah is the first of forty-six such reminders, and it is the harshest because it is the first.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:2

> **by those that draw out quickly,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Verb, and the Difference It Makes**

The second oath changes one letter of the moral universe. The first verse spoke of wrenching (*n-z-ʿ*) with maximum force (*gharqan*); this verse speaks of drawing out (*n-sh-ṭ*) with *nashṭan* — and the difference between the two verbal nouns is the whole difference between two ways of dying. Arabic is exceptionally fine-grained about the manner of an action, and here the Qur'an exploits that fineness: the same event, the departure of a soul, is described twice with two different textures. One is a tearing; the other is a sliding.

The root *n-sh-ṭ* carries two related senses that both apply. The first is to draw something out quickly and nimbly — the way one pulls a bucket up from a well with a single practised heave, or draws an arrow from a quiver without looking. The second sense, from the same root, is liveliness and alacrity: *nashāṭ* in Arabic means the energy of someone who acts promptly and cheerfully, and a person described as *nashīṭ* is one who moves briskly and without reluctance. So the word simultaneously describes the motion of the angel and the disposition of the soul being taken. It comes out quickly because it is not resisting. It is not resisting because it is lively, willing, eager.

**Who These Are, According to the Commentators**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī transmit the primary reading: these are the angels who draw out the souls of the believers, and the drawing is quick because the believer goes willingly into what lies beyond. Some commentators, however, hold the opposite view, noting that *nashiṭa* can also imply a thing that must be pulled with considerable effort — as a rope that has snagged — in which case this verse continues the description of the disbelievers and hypocrites begun in verse 1, describing the difficulty the angels meet rather than any willingness in the soul. Others take verses 1–2 together as a reference to death itself, since the exit of the soul is violent for some and gentle for others.

A third line of interpretation, also recorded by al-Ṭabarī, reads the oaths as describing the stars and celestial bodies as they travel swiftly from one horizon to another, in which case *nashṭan* captures the speed of the traverse. And there is the reading of Mujāhid, transmitted by al-Rāzī, that divides the first three verses among three kinds of death: verse 1 the death of the disbelievers, verse 2 the death of the believers, and verse 3 the death of the elect among the believers. This last reading is the one most exegetes find rhetorically satisfying, because it explains why the Qur'an would bother to swear three times by death: it is not repeating itself, it is grading.

**The Bucket and the Drop: The Other Half of the Same Report**

The report of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib that described the violent extraction in the previous verse continues by describing the gentle one, and the two halves together are the commentary's own best gloss on verses 1 and 2. When the believer dies, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"…angels descend from heaven with faces as though they were the sun, and they sit from him as far as the eye can see, and the angel of death comes and sits at his head and says: 'O good soul, come out to the forgiveness of God and His good pleasure.' So it flows out and comes forth as a drop flows from the mouth of a waterskin, and he takes it."* (Abū Dāwūd 4753; Aḥmad; al-Ḥākim — ṣaḥīḥ)

Compare the two images with care, because the Qur'an's choice of words depends on them. In the first case a many-pronged iron skewer is dragged out of wet wool; in the second a drop of water slides from the mouth of a leather waterskin. Nothing is torn in the second image. There is no resistance, no catching, no damage. The soul does not have to be persuaded, because it has been expecting the invitation. The Qur'an's *nashṭan* — a swift, clean pull — is the exact equivalent of the drop leaving the waterskin.

The Qur'an makes the same contrast in its own idiom elsewhere. Of those who die in a state of purity it says:

> *"Those whom the angels take while they are pure, saying: 'Peace be upon you. Enter the Garden for what you used to do.'"* (Qur'an 16:32)

And of the process itself:

> *"Say: The angel of death who is entrusted with you will take you; then unto your Lord you shall be returned."* (Qur'an 32:11)

The verb in 32:11 is *yatawaffākum* — "he will receive you in full" — and the commentators note that the same verb is used for receiving a debt in full. Death is not a loss of part of you; it is the collection of the whole of you by the One who owns it.

**Why the Disposition of the Soul Determines the Manner of Its Exit**

The deepest question this verse raises is not what the angels do but why the same operation produces two different experiences. The answer the tradition gives is that the manner of death is a function of the manner of life, and the reason is entirely intelligible.

A soul that has spent decades attached to the body and its appetites has, in effect, built its identity out of that attachment. It has no independent centre. When the body is withdrawn, the soul experiences the withdrawal as annihilation, and it clings — which is why the extraction must be forced. A soul that has spent decades practising detachment — through prayer, through charity, through restraint, through the daily rehearsal of standing before God — has already learned to hold the body lightly. It has already made, five times a day, the small separations that prepare for the large one. When the summons comes it recognises it, and it goes.

The analogy is not far to find. Consider two people leaving a house at the end of a tenancy. One has never packed a box, has let the place accumulate for years, and has refused to think about the date; when the landlord arrives the removal is chaotic, painful, and involves things being dragged out by force. The other has been packing gradually for months, has already sent the important things ahead, and walks out on the day with a bag. The landlord's action is identical. The experience is not.

This is precisely the logic behind the Prophet's ﷺ instruction to be in the world as a resident who expects to leave:

> *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveller."* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar)

The stranger keeps few possessions, stays unentangled, and can depart on short notice. The command is not to hate the world; it is to arrange one's attachments so that departure does not require a wresting.

**"Nashāṭ": The Ethics of Promptness Hidden Inside the Word**

There is a second gift in this verse, and it is easy to miss. Because the root *n-sh-ṭ* is also the root of *nashāṭ* — liveliness, alacrity, briskness — the verse plants, in the middle of a passage about death, an ethical principle about how to live. The soul that is taken *nashṭan* is a soul that lived *nashīṭan*: promptly, energetically, without dragging its feet.

The Qur'an and the Sunnah treat promptness in good as a virtue in its own right and treat its opposite, *kasal* (laziness, lethargy), as a genuine spiritual disease from which the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge daily:

> *"O God, I seek refuge in You from anxiety and grief, from weakness and laziness, from miserliness and cowardice, and from being overcome by debt and overpowered by men."* (al-Bukhārī 6369; Muslim 2706)

He also gave the reason for urgency in a report recorded by al-Tirmidhī:

> *"Hasten to do good deeds before seven. Are you waiting for anything but poverty that makes you forget, or wealth that makes you transgress, or illness that corrupts, or old age that weakens, or death that overtakes, or the Dajjāl — and the Dajjāl is the worst thing awaited — or the Hour, and the Hour is more bitter and more calamitous?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2306; graded ḥasan)

The list is a diagnosis of the seven ways a human being talks himself out of doing what he knows he should do: not enough resources, too many resources, poor health, declining energy, running out of time, and finally the two great unknowns. Every one of them is a postponement strategy. The verse's answer is that the angel who comes for you will come quickly, and it would be fitting if the deeds you send ahead of you were done quickly too.

**A Word on the Grammar, and Why It Matters**

Both this verse and the last end in an accusative verbal noun — *gharqā*, *nashṭā* — and the same pattern continues in the next two verses (*sabḥā*, *sabqā*). Arabic grammarians explain that these accusatives describe the manner or degree of the action, so the oaths are not simply "by those who wrest and those who draw out" but "by those who wrest *with the utmost wrenching* and those who draw out *with the swiftest drawing*." The oath is on the intensity, not on the bare fact.

That detail is theologically significant. God is not swearing by the existence of angels in a general way; He is swearing by the extremity of their operation. The point being established is that the boundary between this world and the next is not a vague dissolving but a precise, forceful, expertly executed transition, carried out by agents who are never late and never gentle by accident. Anyone who imagines death as a fading, a dimming, a slow release of vapour has misread the register of these verses. The Qur'an's death is an event with an agent, an address, and a manner.

**Two Ways Out, One Door**

Read together, verses 1 and 2 present a complete and unsentimental picture of the human exit. There is one door and there are two ways of going through it. Nothing about the door is negotiable; everything about the manner is determined long before the arrival. The believer is not exempted from death — the same angel of death comes to the saint and to the tyrant — but he is exempted from the tearing, because he spent his life loosening the knots that would otherwise have to be cut.

The practical conclusion is available to anyone at any age and in any condition. The way to make one's exit *nashṭan* rather than *gharqan* is not a deathbed formula but a lifetime of small releases: forgiving the person you have been nursing a grievance against, giving away the money you have been afraid to give, praying the prayer you have been deferring, saying the apology you have been avoiding. Each of these is a loosened fibre. When the pull comes, the difference between wool that clings and wool that lets go will have been decided a thousand times before, in ordinary Tuesdays, by ordinary choices, none of which looked like a rehearsal for anything.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:3

> **by those that glide serenely,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Motion: Swimming**

The third oath introduces a completely different kind of movement. The first was a wrench, the second a swift pull; this one is *sabḥ* — swimming, gliding, floating. The root *s-b-ḥ* is the ordinary Arabic verb for swimming in water, and by extension for any smooth, sustained, unresisting motion through a medium. A swimmer is one who has learned to move *with* the water rather than against it; his effort is real but it produces no violence, no tearing, no noise. The verbal noun *sabḥan* therefore describes motion that is at once continuous, effortless in appearance, and perfectly adapted to the element it moves through.

The Qur'an uses this same verb for the celestial bodies, and that usage is the key to one of the main interpretations here:

> *"And He it is Who created the night and the day, and the sun and the moon — each swimming in an orbit."* (Qur'an 21:33)

> *"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day — each swimming in an orbit."* (Qur'an 36:40)

The choice of *yasbaḥūn* for the planets is remarkable. The ancient mind naturally imagined the heavens as a machine of interlocking wheels or as bodies nailed to crystal spheres. The Qur'an's image is instead of bodies *swimming* — a word that implies a medium, buoyancy, and a motion governed from within rather than driven from without. Whether the oath here refers to angels, to the stars, or to ships on the sea, the shared quality is the same: a serene, purposeful traversal of a vast domain.

**The Three Interpretations, and What They Share**

Al-Ṭabarī records three principal readings. The first: these are the angels who glide down bearing God's provision and sustenance, moving through the realms of creation as a swimmer moves through water. The second: they are the stars in their courses. The third: they are ships that sail gently upon the sea — a reading that connects the verse to the Qur'an's frequent use of the sailing ship as a sign:

> *"And you see the ships cleaving through it that you may seek of His bounty, and that perhaps you may be grateful."* (Qur'an 16:14)

> *"And among His signs are the ships in the sea, like mountains."* (Qur'an 42:32)

Some commentators also connect the verse to the early morning, reading *sābiḥāt* in relation to the coming of dawn, which would tie it to the morning prayer and to the Qur'an's statement:

> *"...and [establish] the recitation of the dawn. Truly the recitation of the dawn is witnessed."* (Qur'an 17:78)

And there is the reading of Mujāhid, mentioned earlier, that this is the third grade of death — the death of the elect among the believers, which is so unresisted that it is barely a departure at all.

Al-Rāzī offers the spiritual counterpart to all of this. In his scheme of the heart's return to God, "those that glide serenely" describes spirits that, having struggled and been disciplined, now move through the realm of divine sovereignty as a swimmer crosses an ocean — no longer fighting, but traversing. The three readings are not in competition; they are three registers of one reality: there is a domain of God's rule, and there are beings who move through it smoothly because they are entirely aligned with it.

**The Angels Descending: What the Authentic Reports Say**

If the verse refers to angels, its claim is not vague, and the Sunnah supplies the detail. The angels are not occasional visitors to this world; they are in continuous descent and ascent around human beings, and the Prophet ﷺ described the schedule:

> *"Angels take turns among you — angels of the night and angels of the day — and they gather at the dawn prayer and the afternoon prayer. Then those who spent the night among you ascend, and He asks them, though He knows best: 'In what state did you leave My servants?' They say: 'We left them praying, and we came to them praying.'"* (al-Bukhārī 7429; Muslim 632, from Abū Hurayrah)

The image of two shifts of angels reporting on the same human being, and finding him in the same posture of worship at both handovers, is one of the most economical descriptions of a faithful life ever given. And the descent is not limited to reporting:

> *"There is no group of people who sit remembering God except that the angels surround them, mercy covers them, tranquillity descends upon them, and God mentions them to those who are with Him."* (Muslim 2700)

The angels descend on the night of decree in a specific mission:

> *"The angels and the Spirit descend therein by the permission of their Lord, for every affair."* (Qur'an 97:4)

Notice the phrase "for every affair" — *min kulli amr* — which will be echoed almost exactly in the fifth verse of this sūrah, *al-mudabbirāti amrā*, "those that govern affairs." The two verses belong together.

**The Provision That Comes Down Without Being Earned**

The reading of these as angels bearing provision touches something that the modern mind finds difficult and the Qur'an states as elementary fact:

> *"And there is no creature moving on the earth but that its provision is upon God. And He knows its place of dwelling and its place of storage. All is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 11:6)

The provision of every living thing is guaranteed, registered, and delivered — and the tradition understands that delivery to occur through the agency of the angels entrusted with it. This is why the classical commentators so often pair Michael with rain and vegetation. The theological implication is not that the human being is relieved of work — the Qur'an commands travel through the earth and the seeking of bounty (67:15; 62:10) — but that the *result* of the work is not produced by the work. A farmer ploughs; he does not make the seed germinate. A trader travels; he does not create the buyer's desire. Between the effort and the outcome stands an administration that is not his, and that is what these gliding beings are doing.

The psychological consequence of believing this is enormous, and it is one of the few doctrines that reliably lowers anxiety rather than raising it. A person who believes that the outcome is entirely his own production must either become arrogant when things go well or collapse when they go badly. A person who believes that the outcome is delivered by a governance that is not his can work with full seriousness and sleep without full control.

**Gliding as a Description of the Faithful Life**

There is a further reason this verse sits between the wrenching and the racing. The order of the three motions — wrench, pull, glide — traces a progression, and the commentators who read all three as grades of death are pointing at a general law: the more a being is aligned with the medium it moves through, the less resistance it meets.

The analogy is available in ordinary experience. A person who has never learned to swim panics in deep water; his flailing exhausts him and the water defeats him. A trained swimmer in the same water covers the same distance at a fraction of the cost, because he has made himself a body the water can carry. Nothing about the water changed. The change was in his relation to it.

The Qur'an repeatedly presents the religious life in exactly these terms. It calls its law a *sharīʿah* — a path to water — and describes its obligations as a burden that is deliberately light:

> *"God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you."* (Qur'an 2:185)

> *"And He has not placed upon you in the religion any hardship."* (Qur'an 22:78)

The person who has made the practices of the religion habitual — the prayer at its time, the fast in its month, the charity at its threshold — finds that those practices carry him through hardship the way water carries a swimmer. The person who has made them foreign to himself finds that the same practices are an ordeal, and he experiences religion as a series of drowning incidents. The difference is not in the law; it is in whether he learned to move inside it.

**Ships on the Sea: The Verse's Own Best Picture**

If we take the reading of the ships — and it is transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and it fits the word precisely — the verse becomes a small miracle of observation. A ship gliding serenely is the paradigm of motion that is both enormous in scale and silent in execution: thousands of tons of hull and cargo moving across an ocean, and what the eye registers is not effort but grace. The Qur'an returns to ships again and again precisely because they are the strongest image it has of sustained movement that depends entirely on forces outside the vessel: the wind in the sail, the buoyancy of the water, the skill of the helmsman who can do nothing except cooperate with both.

That is the position of the human being in the administration of the world. He is not the ocean and he is not the wind; he is a hull, and his task is to keep himself seaworthy, to set his sail correctly, and to stop pretending that he generated the breeze. The gliding ones of this verse are the beings who never forgot this. They move through the whole breadth of creation — from the throne to the bedside of a dying person — without ever once mistaking the medium for themselves.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:4

> **by those that race to the fore, outstripping,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Shift in the Particles: From Addition to Sequence**

Something changes grammatically at this verse, and the change is meaningful. The first three oaths are introduced by *wa-*, the simple particle of conjunction — "and by those that wrest, and by those that draw out, and by those that glide." But the fourth and fifth oaths are introduced by *fāʾ*, which in Arabic carries the sense of sequence, consequence, or immediacy: "and then," "and so." The grammarians note this shift and explain it: the first three describe parallel categories, while the last two describe what follows from them — the ones who race ahead are the consequence of the gliding, and the ones who govern affairs are the consequence of the racing. The sūrah is describing a hierarchy, not a list.

The word itself, *al-sābiqāt sabqā*, is built on the root *s-b-q*, to precede, to outstrip, to arrive first. *Sabq* is the verbal noun of the race, and the same root gives the Arabic for the prize of a race and for a competitive contest. So the oath is on the ones who are ahead in a competition — and the Qur'an has told us elsewhere, repeatedly, that there is a competition and that it has winners.

**The Race the Qur'an Announces**

The Qur'an speaks of *al-sābiqūn*, "the foremost," as a named category of the saved:

> *"And the foremost, the foremost — those are the ones brought near, in Gardens of delight."* (Qur'an 56:10–12)

It then explains how that rank is earned and who may hold it:

> *"Then We caused to inherit the Book those We chose of Our servants; and among them are some who wrong themselves, and among them are some who are moderate, and among them are some who are foremost in good deeds by God's permission. That is the great bounty."* (Qur'an 35:32)

And it issues the invitation in the imperative, in the grammatical form of a race:

> *"Race toward forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden whose breadth is as the breadth of the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 57:21)

> *"So race to good deeds. Unto God is your return, all of you, and He will inform you of that wherein you differed."* (Qur'an 2:148)

The Qur'an's choice of athletic language is deliberate and it is not softened anywhere. Good deeds are not merely encouraged; they are to be *competed for*. The imagery presumes scarcity of time, an irreversible finish line, and the fact that not everyone will finish in the same position. This is a striking feature of Qur'anic ethics, because it refuses the modern sentimental assumption that all sincere effort is equally rewarded regardless of what it produced. The Qur'an says the opposite:

> *"And for all there are ranks from what they did, that He may repay them in full for their deeds, and they will not be wronged."* (Qur'an 46:19)

**Who the Racers Are**

Al-Ṭabarī records the interpretations. The primary reading is that these are the angels who race ahead carrying the souls of the believers toward Paradise — the swift escort of the report of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, who describes the soul of the believer being taken upward through the heavens with the angels calling out to one another as they pass each gate. Others take the verse to refer to the angels who race to record good deeds and ascend with them before the recording of bad ones, a reading grounded in the Prophet's ﷺ description of the two recording angels:

> *"God has pardoned for my community what the soul whispers, so long as it does not act or speak upon it."* (al-Bukhārī 5269; Muslim 127)

— the principle being that good is written immediately and multiplied, while evil is held back, suspended, and given every opportunity to be erased:

> *"…whoever intends a good deed and does not do it, God writes it with Him as a complete good deed; and if he intends it and does it, God writes it with Him as ten good deeds up to seven hundred times, and many times over. And whoever intends an evil deed and does not do it, God writes it with Him as a complete good deed…"* (al-Bukhārī 6491; Muslim 131)

Others read the verse as referring to the souls that outstrip, or to the horses of the warriors, or to the stars that outstrip one another in their courses. Al-Rāzī, in his spiritual reading, takes the verse as an allusion to the variation among spirits in the degrees of their journeying toward God — some souls cover in a year what others cover in a lifetime, and the distance travelled is not measured in time but in sincerity.

**The Deeds That Keep Running After the Runner Stops**

There is a dimension of *sabq* that the tradition developed with great precision, and it is the most practical part of this verse. The race does not end at death; the deeds a person launched continue to run. The Prophet ﷺ stated the rule:

> *"When a person dies, his deeds are cut off except three: an ongoing charity, knowledge from which people benefit, or a righteous child who prays for him."* (Muslim 1631)

The three exceptions share one structure: each is an act whose effect outlives the actor. A well dug, a school endowed, a book written, a student taught, a child raised who now prays — these keep accumulating for a person lying in the ground who is doing nothing at all. The commentators on this verse connect it to the same principle: the *sābiqūn* are not merely those who were fast in life, but those whose good is still travelling after them.

The practical implication is a change of strategy. Most people optimise for what they can accomplish within their own lifetime — the career, the house, the visible achievement. The verse points at a different optimisation: invest in what has momentum, in what will still be moving when you are not. A person who spends a decade building an institution that trains others has done less in his own person than a person who built a monument to himself, and far more in the accounting that survives him.

**Why Racing Is Not Anxiety**

It would be easy to misread this verse as an exhortation to frantic urgency, and the tradition is careful to prevent that. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak believer, and in both there is good. Be eager for what benefits you, seek help from God, and do not feel helpless."* (Muslim 2664)

Note the components: eagerness, reliance, and the refusal of helplessness — but not haste. Haste, *ʿajalah*, is elsewhere condemned; the same root's opposite, deliberation, is praised. What the verse demands is not speed of motion but priority of direction. The one who outstrips in this race is not the one who moves most frantically; he is the one who started earliest, wasted least, and kept the correct bearing.

Consider two people beginning the same journey at different times. The first sets out in his youth, moves at a walking pace, and never deviates. The second waits until he is old, then sprints, and arrives exhausted behind the walker. The Qur'an's praise for *al-sābiqūn* is praise for the walker who started when he was young — and its repeated warnings against postponement are addressed to the person who keeps telling himself he will begin later:

> *"Until, when death comes to one of them, he says: 'My Lord, send me back, that I may do good in what I left behind.' Never! It is only a word he is saying."* (Qur'an 23:99–100)

**The Modern Version of the Same Race**

Every human being is already in a competition whether or not he thinks he is. The modern world organises one relentlessly: comparison. Social platforms have made the ranking of lives continuous, public, and quantified — followers, photographs, achievements, milestones — and the anxiety this produces is not manufactured by the technology; the technology has merely made visible a competition the Qur'an already assumed and correctly located.

The verse's contribution is to name the competition correctly and thereby to change what winning means. In the race the Qur'an describes, the metric is not visibility but effect, not intensity but constancy, and not what you accumulated but what you set in motion. The Prophet ﷺ answered the question of which deed is best with an answer that has nothing to do with spectacle:

> *"The most beloved deeds to God are those that are most regular, even if they are few."* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783)

Consistency beats intensity. The one who outstrips is the one who did not stop. That is the entire secret of the *sābiqūn*, and it is available to the person with the least impressive résumé in the room.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:5

> **and by those that govern affairs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summit of the Five Oaths**

The fifth oath is the conclusion of the sequence, and the particle *fāʾ* that introduces it signals that it follows from the four before it. The word is *al-mudabbirāt amrā* — "those that govern affairs," from the root *d-b-r*, which carries the sense of the back or the rear of a thing, and hence of looking at a matter from its end. *Tadbīr* in Arabic is therefore not merely managing; it is managing with the outcome in view — arranging a matter by first standing, in the imagination, at its conclusion. The one who *yudabbir* an affair is one who has already thought through where it will land.

So the oath is on beings who administer the affairs of the world with perfect foresight. Al-Qurṭubī records the dominant reading: these are the angels charged with the governance of the created order. Some commentators specify the four great archangels — Gabriel, entrusted with revelation; Michael, entrusted with provision and rain; the Angel of Death, entrusted with souls; and Isrāfīl, entrusted with the Trumpet — each responsible for a particular domain. Others apply the verse to all angels who carry administrative responsibility, including those mentioned in the previous verses. A minority reads it as referring to the planets, in the astrological sense of the governing bodies.

**The Names of the Four, in the Prophet's Own Prayer**

The specificity of the archangelic reading is not later speculation; it is anchored in authenticated supplication. The Prophet ﷺ would open his night prayer by addressing God through these very names, as ʿĀʾishah reported:

> *"O God, Lord of Gabriel, Michael and Isrāfīl, Originator of the heavens and the earth, Knower of the Unseen and the Witnessed — You judge between Your servants in that wherein they differ. Guide me by Your permission to the truth in that wherein they differ. Truly You guide whom You will to a straight path."* (Muslim 770)

That the Prophet ﷺ chose these three names as the frame for his request for guidance is itself instructive. Gabriel is the angel of knowledge, Michael the angel of sustenance, Isrāfīl the angel of the final summons. Between knowledge, provision and the end of the world lies the whole of human need, and the prayer asks God — who is Lord of the administrators — for the one thing that makes the other two useful.

The Qur'an states the same administrative structure directly:

> *"He arranges the affair from the heaven to the earth; then it ascends to Him in a Day whose measure is a thousand years of what you count."* (Qur'an 32:5)

> *"The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years."* (Qur'an 70:4)

The word in 32:5 is *yudabbiru al-amr* — the same verbal form as in this verse. The arrangement of the affair is God's act, executed through His angels; and 32:5 makes explicit that the arrangement descends and returns, that nothing is delegated permanently and nothing is lost in the delegation.

**Angels Are Not Gods: The Line the Verse Draws**

The verse's most important doctrinal work is negative, and it would have been perfectly understood by its first audience. The pagan Arabs did not deny that there were beings who administered the world; that was precisely the core of their religion. They believed in intermediaries who held domains, who could be petitioned, who stood between them and the High God:

> *"And they worship besides God that which harms them not nor benefits them, and they say: 'These are our intercessors with God.'"* (Qur'an 10:18)

Against this the Qur'an performs a precise operation. It affirms the administration — the angels exist, they act, they govern affairs — while stripping the administrators of any independent authority. They are executors, not principals. Every act they perform is by permission, on command, and within limits:

> *"And they say: 'The Compassionate has taken a son.' Glory be to Him! Rather, they are honoured servants. They do not precede Him in speech, and they act only by His command."* (Qur'an 21:26–27)

> *"…they do not intercede except for one with whom He is pleased, and they stand in awe of Him."* (Qur'an 21:28)

This is why the Qur'an can swear by the governing angels in the same breath as it insists that only God is to be worshipped. The administration of the world is real; it is also entirely derived. Ibn Taymiyyah made this the organizing principle of his critique of intercession cults: to acknowledge an agent is not to worship him, and to worship an agent is to mistake a servant for a master. The angels themselves, on this account, are the most rigorous monotheists in existence, since they know better than anyone how little of their power is their own.

**The Human Being Inside an Administered Universe**

The verse has an immediate and slightly unsettling implication for the reader: you are, at this moment, inside an administration. Your provision is being delivered, your deeds are being recorded, your lifespan is registered, and the eventual summoning of your soul has been assigned to an agent. The Qur'an states the recording plainly:

> *"And indeed, there are over you watchers, noble recording angels, who know whatever you do."* (Qur'an 82:10–12)

Modern life is arranged so that this fact is easy to ignore, and the modern arrangement has a name: the assumption of self-ownership. The default posture of contemporary culture is that a person is the author of his own life, answerable to no one, and that any claim of oversight is an infringement. The verse does not argue with that posture; it simply describes a different order of things and leaves the reader to notice which description matches his experience. Anyone who has ever tried to control an outcome — a medical result, a market, another person's heart, his own mortality — has already discovered the limits of self-ownership by experiment.

There is, however, a second and far more consoling implication. Because the universe is administered, it is not arbitrary. Things are not happening to you at random. The doctrine of divine decree (*qadar*) in Islam is precisely the belief that the affairs of the world are governed by a wisdom, even when that wisdom is inaccessible:

> *"No calamity befalls on the earth or in yourselves but that it is in a register before We bring it into being. Truly that is easy for God — that you may not grieve over what has escaped you nor exult over what He has given you."* (Qur'an 57:22–23)

The stated purpose of believing in the register is therapeutic, and it is stated with unusual candour: the point of believing that affairs are governed is so that you neither collapse over loss nor become insufferable over gain. Both extremes are errors of attribution, and both are corrected by the same fact.

**Tadbīr as a Human Virtue, Not a Human Claim**

Because *tadbīr* is here an attribute of God's administration, the tradition was careful to make it a human virtue as well — but a subordinate one. The believer is to plan, arrange, and take precautions, while knowing that the outcome is not in his plan. The Prophet ﷺ exemplified this at the Battle of the Trench, where he accepted Salmān al-Fārisī's Persian suggestion to dig a defensive trench around Madinah — a piece of military engineering unknown to the Arabs — and combined it with prayer and reliance. He taught the same balance in a single sentence:

> *"Tie your camel, then place your trust in God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2517)

And he warned against the opposite error, haste, which is *tadbīr* without foresight:

> *"Deliberation is from God, and haste is from the devil."* (al-Tirmidhī 2012; graded ḥasan)

The connection between this verse and that teaching is exact. The angels *yudabbirūn al-amr* — they arrange affairs by seeing their ends. A human being who arranges his affairs without seeing their ends is not managing; he is reacting. The person who borrows money he cannot repay, who marries for an impulse, who speaks in anger, who postpones repentance on the assumption that he will live another decade, has not practised *tadbīr* at all. He has performed the motions of management while refusing the only thing that makes management real: standing at the end of the matter and looking back.

**The Oath's Payoff**

Five oaths, then, and their combined force is now visible. The Qur'an has sworn by the agents who extract souls at death, who draw them out swiftly, who glide with provision, who race ahead with good, and who govern the affairs of the world — and everything it has sworn by points in the same direction. There is an administration. It is staffed. It is precise. It is not indifferent. And it is moving toward a day.

The reader who reaches verse 6 has been prepared for what comes next, because he now knows that the Day of Judgment will not be a catastrophe that overtakes an ungoverned universe. It will be the scheduled conclusion of an administration that has been running since the first moment of creation, executed by the same agents who are running it now, in the same manner — precisely, swiftly, and without the possibility of an appeal to a higher office, because there is no higher office. That is the oath. The next verse is the thing sworn about.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:6

> **on the Day when the quaker quakes**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Thing Sworn About Is Finally Named**

The five oaths were suspended, and now they land. In Arabic, a series of oaths requires an answer — the *jawāb al-qasam* — the statement whose truth the swearing was meant to guarantee. Verse 6 supplies the frame for it: *yawma tarjufu al-rājifah*, "on the Day when the quaker quakes." Grammatically the word *yawma* is an accusative of time setting the scene for what is coming; the actual sworn statement will be completed across the following verses, and some grammarians note that the Qur'an's suspension of the answer is itself a device — the listener is held, waiting, exactly as the world will be held.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī record that some commentators see the omission of an explicit response to the conditional in this passage as a deliberate affirmation: what is going to happen must happen, and the sentence does not need to finish to make that clear. The rhetorical effect on the listener is not unlike the effect of a sentence that stops mid-clause in an emergency announcement. The unfinished form conveys urgency better than the finished one would.

**The Root of Shaking: *R-J-F***

*Al-rājifah* is a feminine active participle from *r-j-f*, a root denoting violent agitation — the shaking of ground, the trembling of a body, the convulsion of something massive. It is a stronger word than the ordinary verb for movement; it describes a shaking in which the thing shaken loses its composure. The Qur'an uses the same root of Mount Sinai at the moment of the theophany:

> *"And when his Lord appeared to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell unconscious."* (Qur'an 7:143)

and of the earth in the description of the Last Day:

> *"When the earth is shaken with its [final] shaking, and the earth discharges its burdens."* (Qur'an 99:1–2)

and of the Hour itself:

> *"O mankind, fear your Lord. Truly the convulsion of the Hour is a tremendous thing — the Day you see it, every nursing mother will forget the one she nursed, and every pregnant woman will abort her pregnancy, and you will see mankind drunk though they are not drunk; but the punishment of God is severe."* (Qur'an 22:1–2)

Note the structure of that last passage. It does not describe the geology of the event; it describes the consequences for a nursing mother and a pregnant woman — the two strongest attachments the human body knows, both of which will be overridden. The Qur'an consistently measures the magnitude of the shaking not in physical units but in what it displaces from the human heart.

**The Two Blasts: The Dominant Reading**

The majority of the classical commentators read *al-rājifah* as the first of the two blasts of the Trumpet, blown by Isrāfīl. The Qur'an describes both blasts explicitly:

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth will swoon, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown a second time, and behold, they will be standing, looking on."* (Qur'an 39:68)

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown with one blast, and the earth and the mountains will be lifted and crushed with a single crushing. On that Day the Event will come to pass."* (Qur'an 69:13–15)

> *"The Day when the Trumpet is blown, and you will come in crowds."* (Qur'an 78:18)

The first blast ends the world; the second begins the next one. Between them lies the interval in which the Trumpet is silent and nothing that was alive remains alive. The Prophet ﷺ described his own position relative to that interval in a report recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *"The people will swoon on the Day of Resurrection, and I shall be the first to raise his head — and behold, Moses is holding one of the pillars of the Throne. I do not know whether he awoke before me, or whether he was exempted on account of his swooning at the mountain."* (al-Bukhārī 2411; Muslim 2374)

The report is remarkable for two reasons. It confirms that the swooning is universal in scope, and it shows the Prophet ﷺ declining to claim superiority even in that moment, offering instead an explanation that honours Moses. Even in describing the Last Day, the Prophetic manner refuses self-aggrandisement.

**The Angel Waiting With the Trumpet at His Lips**

There is a report that belongs more closely to this verse than to any other, and it changes the verse from a prediction into a present condition. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"How can I be at ease when the one in charge of the Trumpet has already put it to his lips, inclined his head, and is listening, waiting to be commanded to blow?"* The Companions said: "What should we say, Messenger of God?" He said: *"Say: 'God is sufficient for us, and what an excellent trustee He is. Upon God we have placed our trust.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 2431; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

The theological content is startling and it is easy to skim past. According to this report, the first verse's shaking is not a future possibility being announced; it is a state of readiness already in place. The instrument is at the angel's mouth. The only thing missing is the command. The Prophet ﷺ — who is guaranteed Paradise, who is the most honoured of creation — describes himself as unable to be at ease about it, and prescribes not an explanation but a supplication.

That prescription is worth dwelling on. When confronted with a fact that cannot be changed and cannot be predicted, the Prophet ﷺ does not offer a theory that dissolves the fear. He offers words of reliance. This is the mature religious response to the unalterable: not the elimination of concern but the correct placement of it. Fear of an event you cannot influence is useless; reliance upon the One who controls it is not.

**Other Readings of the Verse**

The exegetical tradition is characteristically broader than the dominant reading. Some maintain that *al-rājifah* is the earth and the mountains, which will shake on that Day, while *al-rādifah* is the heaven and the planets. Others say *al-rājifah* is the first shaking of the earth and *al-rādifah* the second, when it is split apart and fades away. Others read *al-rājifah* as "the Event" (*al-wāqiʿah*) in which the earth quakes, and others as the Hour itself, which is said to "quake" in 22:1. A minority takes both verses as an allusion to death and all that follows it — the shaking of one's own world at the moment the soul is pulled out, and the resurrection that follows.

Al-Rāzī's methodological principle applies here as it did to the oaths: these readings are possibilities, not certainties, and the verse's force does not depend on adjudicating between them. What all of them agree on is the substance: there is a Day on which the settled order of things will lose its composure, and it will not be a local event.

**Why the Image of Shaking Rather Than Fire or Darkness**

The Qur'an has many images available for catastrophe — fire, flood, darkness, drowning — and it chooses shaking for the Hour more often than any other. The reason is available to anyone who has lived through an earthquake, and it is this: shaking attacks the one assumption on which every other human activity depends.

We are prepared, psychologically, for a great many calamities. We insure against fire. We build levees against floods. We keep torches against darkness. But the ground beneath us is not treated as a risk at all; it is treated as the precondition of risk assessment. Every plan a human being makes — where to sleep, where to store things, where to stand — assumes that the floor will be there and will be level. When the floor moves, the mind does not know how to process it, because the framework within which it processes everything else has itself become unreliable.

That is precisely the Qur'an's point about the Last Day, and it is why the image is chosen. The Day is not merely a large disaster inside the existing order; it is the dissolution of the order. The categories by which a person has organised his life — safe and dangerous, near and far, mine and yours, before and after — will all be shaking. The verse is telling the reader that what is coming cannot be prepared for the way a flood is prepared for, because it removes the ground on which preparation stands.

**The Small Rehearsals of the Shaking**

This is why the verse has such force in ordinary life. Every human being has experienced a small version of *al-rājifah*: the phone call that rearranges a life, the diagnosis, the betrayal by someone trusted absolutely, the collapse of a business built over decades, the day a country discovers that its institutions are not solid. In each of these, the sensation is identical and unmistakable — not sadness, but the discovery that the floor was never what one thought.

The Qur'an's method with such experiences is not to deny their severity. It is to relocate them. The person who has understood that the stability of this world is provisional — that it is a shaking thing in a temporary arrangement — is not spared the earthquake, but he is not destroyed by it, because his centre of gravity was never placed there. This is the practical function of eschatology in the Islamic tradition, and it is why the short Makkan sūrahs spend so much of their length on the Last Day: they are training the heart in advance, in peacetime, so that the training holds when the shaking starts.

The verse, then, is the pivot of the sūrah. Everything before it was an oath; everything after it is a consequence. And it begins by taking away the one thing the reader had not noticed he was relying on.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:7

> **and the successor follows upon it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Al-Rādifah": The One Who Rides Behind**

The word is built on the root *r-d-f*, whose concrete image is unmistakable: the person who sits behind a rider on the same mount. The *ridf* is the pillion passenger; *irtadafa* means to mount behind someone; *ridāfah* came to denote succession and deputyship. The root carries with it, therefore, not mere following but following *immediately and inseparably* — so close that the two form a single unit of motion.

Applied to the Hour, *al-rādifah* is the second blast of the Trumpet, following the first with no gap that human language can measure. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī all record this as the primary reading, and it is confirmed by the Qur'an's own description of the sequence in 39:68: the first blast, the universal swooning, and then the second blow, after which "behold, they are standing, looking on." The word *idhā* — "behold," "suddenly" — is important. There is no gradual recovery, no waking up slowly. The transition from universal death to universal standing is instantaneous.

The Qur'an uses the same root in another verse to make a different but related point about imminence:

> *"Say: 'Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close behind you.'"* (Qur'an 27:72)

— *ʿasā an yakūna radifa lakum baʿḍu alladhī tastaʿjilūn*, "perhaps some of that which you are trying to hasten is already riding at your heels." The disbelievers mocked the Prophet ﷺ by demanding that the punishment be brought immediately, and the verse replies that the thing they are mocking is already mounted behind them, travelling at their own speed. The image is exactly the one in this verse: the second event is not far off; it is attached to the first.

**The Two Blasts as a Single Act**

The commentators observed something subtle about the pairing of verses 6 and 7. The first blast destroys and the second restores; the first ends the world and the second begins the reckoning. Yet they are performed by the same angel, on the same instrument, in one continuous assignment. The destruction and the resurrection are not two policies; they are two halves of one act.

This matters theologically, because a common objection to the Last Day is that it represents a change of divine intention — as though God had given up on the world and decided to start again. The Qur'an's presentation refuses that reading. The same hand that formed the world dissolves it and re-forms it, and the two operations are as continuous as the two notes of a single phrase:

> *"It is He Who originates creation, then He brings it back again, and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

The phrase "and that is easier for Him" is a concession to human reasoning, not a statement about divine effort, since nothing is difficult for God; the commentators explain that the Qur'an speaks in terms the listener can grasp. If you can make something once, remaking it from its own material is, in your terms, the lesser task.

**The Sequence Within the Sequence**

Between the two blasts, the tradition places a series of events that the Qur'an describes in various places and that this verse's word *tatbaʿuhā* — "follows upon it" — compresses into a single motion. The earth will be replaced:

> *"On the Day when the earth will be replaced by another earth, and the heavens [as well], and they will come forth before God, the One, the Prevailing."* (Qur'an 14:48)

The mountains will be removed:

> *"And they ask you about the mountains. Say: 'My Lord will blast them and scatter them as dust, and He will leave it a level plain. You will see therein neither crookedness nor curve.'"* (Qur'an 20:105–107)

The seas will be set ablaze and the graves will be turned inside out:

> *"And when the seas are set ablaze…"* (Qur'an 81:6)

> *"And when the graves are turned inside out…"* (Qur'an 82:4)

And the human being will come forward individually:

> *"And every soul will come, with it a driver and a witness."* (Qur'an 50:21)

The sūrah you are reading will arrive at the plain in verse 14 — *ʿalā al-sāhirah*, "upon the wide expanse" — and the connection between verse 7 and verse 14 is direct: the following event deposits everyone, without exception, in one place.

**Why the Second Event Is More Serious Than the First**

It is worth pausing on an asymmetry. The first blast is the end of the world, which sounds like the worst thing that could happen; but in the structure of the Qur'an, the second is the more consequential. The first removes you from a place where you had options. The second places you in a place where you have none.

The Prophet ﷺ described the standing that follows with a detail that has been quoted by preachers for fourteen centuries, from ʿĀʾishah:

> *"You will be gathered barefoot, naked, uncircumcised, and uncircumcised."* I said: "O Messenger of God, men and women, all looking at one another?" He said: *"The matter is too grave for them to look at one another."* (al-Bukhārī 3349; Muslim 2859)

The final sentence does the real work. Nakedness is ordinarily the ultimate exposure, and the Prophet ﷺ says that on that Day no one will even notice it, because the gravity of the situation will have abolished the categories of shame and desire that ordinarily govern the body. This is what "following upon it" means in practice: the second event does not merely continue the first, it introduces an entirely different order of reality in which the rules of the previous one no longer apply.

**The Rider Behind: An Image for Every Human Life**

There is a further resonance in the word *rādifah* that the tradition did not miss. If the second event rides behind the first, then the second event is already present, invisible, in everything that happens now. Every death is a first blast with a second attached to it. Every choice has a consequence mounted behind it. Nothing in the moral order is a closed event; everything is followed by its rider.

The Qur'an states this in the most compact form possible:

> *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (Qur'an 99:7–8)

The image is of a scale so sensitive that it registers a single speck of dust. The reason the Qur'an can promise this is that no act is ever finished and filed away; each one has something riding behind it that will arrive at the appointed place and time. The person who lied in a meeting twenty years ago and no one noticed has not performed a completed action; he has performed an action with a passenger.

**Living With a Rider Behind You**

The practical effect of this verse, when it is taken seriously, is a change in the relationship to time. Most people live as though their deeds ended when the moment passed — as though the argument, the transaction, the glance, the neglected obligation, simply ceased to exist once it was over. The verse denies this. Nothing ceases; everything is followed.

And the reverse is equally true, which is the consoling half of the doctrine. The prayer said alone in a room with no witnesses has a rider. The charity given without being seen has a rider. The anger swallowed that no one knew about has a rider. The verse is therefore not only a warning; it is the strongest possible argument against the despair that comes from believing that a good act performed in private was wasted.

The Prophet ﷺ described the outcome of one such private act with an economy that has made the report famous:

> *"A man was walking along a road and felt extreme thirst. He found a well, went down into it, drank, and came out — and there was a dog panting, licking the dust from thirst. He said: 'This dog is suffering from thirst as I was.' So he went back down, filled his shoe with water, held it in his mouth, climbed up, and gave the dog to drink. God thanked him for that and forgave him."* They said: "Messenger of God, is there a reward for us in these animals?" He said: *"In every living being with a moist liver there is a reward."* (al-Bukhārī 2363; Muslim 2244)

No one saw. Nothing was recorded publicly. The act was, by every worldly measure, inconsequential — and it was followed by a rider large enough to erase a lifetime. That is the second blast in miniature, and it is the reason this verse is not, in the end, a verse about catastrophe. It is a verse about consequence.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:8

> **Hearts that Day shall tremble,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Organ That Cannot Be Trained to Lie**

The verse names a specific organ and a specific involuntary movement: *qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifah* — "hearts, on that Day, beating violently." The word *wājifah* is a participle from *w-j-f*, which describes the heart's pounding — not its ordinary beating but the hard, visible, uncontrollable hammering of fear. It is the movement a person feels in his throat when bad news arrives.

The choice is precise, and it exposes the theme of these verses: the collapse of composure. In this life a human being has a great many instruments of self-presentation. He can steady his voice, arrange his expression, choose his words, cite his credentials, and construct a persona that bears little relation to his interior state. Every social institution depends on this capacity — the courtroom, the interview, the negotiation, the funeral, the first date. What verse 8 announces is a day on which the instruments stop working, and the first instrument to fail is the one that was never fully under control anyway.

You can hold your face still. You cannot hold your heart still. The verse begins there.

**The Qur'an's Parallel Descriptions**

The Qur'an describes the same physiological fact in several places, and the details accumulate into a portrait.

> *"So warn them of the Day of the Imminent Event, when hearts will be at the throats, choking in agony. The wrongdoers will have no friend nor any intercessor obeyed."* (Qur'an 40:18)

The image of the heart rising into the throat is anatomically evocative: the sensation everyone knows of the chest constricting under terror, described as the heart climbing toward the mouth.

> *"...men whom neither trade nor sale distracts from the remembrance of God, and the establishment of prayer, and the giving of zakāh — they fear a Day in which hearts and eyes will be turned about."* (Qur'an 24:37)

Here the turning is of both hearts and eyes, and the verse pairs them exactly as al-Nāziʿāt does in verses 8 and 9.

> *"You will see them presented [to the Fire], humble from humiliation, looking from a stolen glance."* (Qur'an 42:45)

— *yankhurūna ilayhā bi'baṣarin khaṣṣī*, "looking at it with a furtive, contracted gaze." The Qur'an is describing people trying to maintain dignity in a situation that abolishes it, and failing.

**Why "Hearts" Is Indefinite, and What Al-Rāzī Made of It**

There is a grammatical detail in this verse that al-Rāzī turned into an interpretation. The word *qulūbun* is indefinite — it carries the nunation, the *-un* ending, that marks an unspecified noun — and the commentators observe that not every heart is said to tremble. Some hearts, on that Day, will be steady. The Qur'an promises exactly this:

> *"God will keep firm those who believe with the firm word, in the life of this world and in the Hereafter."* (Qur'an 14:27)

> *"They will not be grieved by the greatest terror, and the angels will meet them: 'This is your Day which you were promised.'"* (Qur'an 21:103)

So the trembling of verse 8 belongs to a specific category of people, and the indefiniteness of the word signals that the category is real but not universal. This is one of the small technical devices by which the Qur'an makes general statements that are not actually general — and it matters practically, because a reader who assumes that everyone trembles will conclude that trembling is simply the human condition on that Day and that nothing can be done about it. The verse's grammar leaves the door open, and the rest of the sūrah will tell you what is on the other side of it.

**The Qur'anic Heart Is Not the Modern Heart**

Modern English uses "heart" for emotion and "mind" for cognition, and this division makes the Qur'anic vocabulary difficult to read. The Arabic *qalb* is neither. It is the organ of apprehension — of knowing, judging, and deciding — and it is also the seat of feeling, because in the Qur'anic understanding knowing and feeling are not two separate faculties but one faculty operating in different modes.

> *"Have they not travelled through the land, so that they might have hearts by which to understand, and ears by which to hear? For truly it is not the eyes that are blinded, but the hearts within the breasts are blinded."* (Qur'an 22:46)

The verse says the heart is what understands. Blindness is located there, not in the eye. This is why the Qur'an's diagnosis of unbelief is never primarily intellectual; it never says that disbelievers failed to obtain sufficient information. It says their hearts were sealed, hardened, covered, veiled — conditions of the knowing organ rather than deficits of data:

> *"No! Rather, what they used to do has covered over their hearts."* (Qur'an 83:14)

The Prophet ﷺ explained this covering in a report recorded by al-Tirmidhī:

> *"When the servant commits a sin, a black spot is placed on his heart. If he desists, seeks forgiveness and repents, his heart is polished. But if he increases, it increases until it covers his heart — and that is the covering which God mentioned: 'No! Rather, what they used to do has covered over their hearts.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 3334; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The doctrine here is that the heart's condition is manufactured by its owner's repeated acts. It is not a fixed endowment; it is a sediment. Every act leaves a deposit, and the deposits accumulate into a character. This is the deepest psychological claim in Islamic ethics, and it is available to verification in ordinary life: the person who lies repeatedly does not merely acquire a habit of lying, he acquires an inability to feel the lie. The organ of moral perception becomes calloused in exactly the place where it was used wrongly.

**The Heart as the Only Thing God Examines**

If the heart is the organ of knowing and character, it is also the only thing that will be inspected. The Prophet ﷺ stated the criterion in a sentence that has become the standard reference for the entire subject:

> *"Truly God does not look at your bodies or your appearances, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

The report's exclusion is as important as its inclusion: neither physique nor presentation counts in the assessment. Everything a modern person invests in — appearance, image, curation, impression management — is excluded from the examination by name. And the Prophet ﷺ located the whole of a person's integrity in a single organ:

> *"Truly in the body there is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Truly it is the heart."* (al-Bukhārī 52; Muslim 1599)

Verse 8 should therefore be read not as a description of a physiological symptom but as the exposure of the organ that has been under examination the entire time. The heart trembles on that Day because it is, at last, in a situation it cannot manage. It spent a lifetime presenting itself; now it is being read.

**The Heart Is Movable: The Consoling Half of the Doctrine**

There is a corollary that the tradition drew with great care, and it is the reason this verse is not simply frightening. If the heart is manufactured by repeated acts, it can also be repaired by repeated acts; and if God examines the heart, God also turns it. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The hearts of the children of Adam are all between two fingers of the fingers of the Compassionate, as one heart; He turns them as He wills."* Then he said: *"O God, Turner of hearts, make our hearts firm upon Your religion."* (Muslim 2654)

His most frequent supplication, according to the reports of Anas and Umm Salamah, was:

> *"O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion."* (al-Tirmidhī 2140; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The fact that the Prophet ﷺ — whose heart was the firmest in creation — asked repeatedly for firmness establishes a rule for everyone else: no one's heart is stable by nature. Stability is requested, not possessed. And the doctrine of free will is preserved intact within it, because the turning is described as a response: God turns the heart of the one who turns toward Him, as the Qur'an states plainly:

> *"And as for those who are guided, He increases them in guidance and gives them their righteousness."* (Qur'an 47:17)

**The Trembling That Begins Now**

The final observation belongs to practice. The Qur'an does not describe the trembling of the heart on the Last Day merely to frighten; it describes it because the same organ is trembling now, in this life, about the wrong objects. A person's heart pounds before a presentation, before a financial loss, before the opinion of a person he does not even like, before a medical result. It does not pound when he lies, or when he neglects his parents, or when he delays the prayer, or when he looks at what he should not look at.

That distribution is a diagnosis. It tells you what the heart currently believes is real. And the correction the tradition prescribes is not to stop feeling fear but to redirect it — to make the heart tremble before the One it will actually stand before, so that it stops trembling before things that cannot help or harm it:

> *"...so do not fear them, but fear Me, if you are believers."* (Qur'an 3:175)

A person who has learned to fear God has, in a real sense, been vaccinated against every other fear. Not because the other fears disappear — they will still rise in the throat — but because they have been demoted from ultimates to inconveniences. The trembling of verse 8 is, on this reading, the final exposure of a heart that spent a lifetime trembling before the wrong things, and it is avoidable. The avoidance is what the rest of this sūrah is about.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:9

> **with eyes humbled.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heart's State Written on the Face**

Verse 8 described the heart; verse 9 describes what the heart's condition does to the eyes. The Arabic reads *abṣāruhā khāshiʿah* — "their eyes humbled" — and the pronoun in *abṣāruhā* ("their," literally "its") refers back to the hearts of the previous verse, binding the two verses into a single psychosomatic fact. The eyes belong to those hearts. What is happening inside is visible outside, and it is visible in the organ least capable of disguise.

The word *khāshiʿah* comes from *kh-sh-ʿ*, a root denoting a humility that expresses itself physically: the lowering of the head, the dropping of the gaze, the stillness of a person who has stopped asserting himself. It is not fear in the abstract; it is fear made visible in posture. When a person enters the presence of someone who holds absolute power over him, the first thing that changes is his eyes. He cannot hold the gaze. The verse describes billions of people doing this at once.

**The Same Phrase in Three Other Sūrahs**

The Qur'an repeats this description in terms that are nearly identical, which suggests it is a fixed image in its eschatological vocabulary.

> *"...their eyes humbled, abasement shall overcome them."* (Qur'an 68:43, describing the disbelievers on the Day)

> *"Their eyes humbled, abasement shall overcome them — and they were called to prostrate while they were sound [but did not]."* (Qur'an 68:43 in context; cf. 70:44)

The second of these is devastating in its construction. The humiliation of the Day is explicitly connected to a refusal in this life: they were invited to prostrate when they were healthy, able-bodied, and free — and they declined. The eyes that would not lower themselves voluntarily in worship will be lowered involuntarily in exposure. The verse does not state a new punishment; it describes the completion of an act that was already begun.

> *"...their eyes humbled, they will come forth from the graves as if they were scattered locusts."* (Qur'an 54:7)

The locust simile is worth pausing over. Locusts in the Qur'an are the image of overwhelming, undirected multiplicity:

> *"…humbled, their eyes, they will come forth from the graves as if they were scattered locusts."* (Qur'an 54:7)

and elsewhere:

> *"...they will emerge from the graves as if they were locusts scattered about."* (Qur'an 54:7; cf. 7:133, where locusts are among the plagues sent upon Pharaoh's people)

The comparison strips the resurrected of individuality in the eye of the observer: an immense swarm, indistinguishable, directionless, moving as one. The person who spent his life insisting on his own distinctness — his name, his rank, his reputation, his audience — arrives in a condition in which none of those markers are legible.

**Khushūʿ as a Practice Before It Is a Condition**

The word *khushūʿ* is not exclusively eschatological. The Qur'an uses it of the highest state of the believer in this life, and this is the key to understanding the verse's practical function.

> *"Successful indeed are the believers who are humble in their prayers."* (Qur'an 23:1–2)

> *"And seek help in patience and prayer — and truly it is heavy, except for the humble."* (Qur'an 2:45)

> *"Has not the time come for those who believe that their hearts should be humble at the remembrance of God and at what has come down of the truth?"* (Qur'an 57:16)

The contrast between the two uses is exact and it is the whole moral of these two verses. The same organ, the eye, will be humbled either by choice or by force. There are only these two options, and the person who chooses the first will not experience the second in the same way. This is why the classical tradition treated *khushūʿ* in prayer not as a decorative refinement but as the substance of the act. The Prophet ﷺ warned against prayer performed without it in the strongest terms available to him:

> *"Truly a person prays, and nothing is recorded for him but a tenth of his prayer, or a ninth, or an eighth, or a seventh, or a sixth, or a fifth, or a quarter, or a third, or a half of it."* (Abū Dāwūd 796; Aḥmad)

The arithmetic is the point. The prayer is not a binary transaction that either happened or did not; it is measured by the presence of the heart within it.

**The Eye as the Organ One Must Train**

The Islamic tradition developed an unusually detailed discipline around the gaze, precisely because it identified the eye as the point at which the heart's condition enters and leaves the person. The Prophet ﷺ instructed that in prayer the gaze should rest at the place of prostration, and he rebuked those who looked upward:

> *"What is wrong with people who raise their eyes to the heaven in their prayer?"* His voice was stern. *"Let them stop, or their eyesight will be snatched away."* (al-Bukhārī 750)

And he promised a specific reward for the controlled gaze:

> *"Whoever lowers his gaze from the beauty of a woman for the sake of God, God will make him taste the sweetness of faith in his heart."* (Aḥmad; al-Ḥākim, who declared it sound)

Notice the mechanism described in that report: an act of the eye produces a taste in the heart. The tradition's psychology is a closed loop — the gaze shapes the interior, and the interior then expresses itself in the gaze. This is why verse 9 can predict the eyes of the Last Day with such confidence: the eyes on that Day will simply be showing, in public, what they have been practising in private for seventy years.

The Prophet ﷺ also promised a specific exemption for one kind of eye:

> *"Two eyes the Fire will not touch: an eye that wept from the fear of God, and an eye that stood guard in the way of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 1639; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The pairing is instructive. One eye is humbled inwardly, by tears in private; the other is disciplined outwardly, by wakefulness on a border at night. Both are acts of the eye performed with no audience, and both are said to purchase immunity. The logic of verse 9 is thus reversible: the eye that lowers itself voluntarily will not be lowered involuntarily.

**The Culture of the Gaze, and the Verse Against It**

There is a specific modern context in which this verse acquires unusual sharpness. Contemporary life has made being looked at the primary measure of a person's worth. Platforms exist whose entire function is the accumulation of gazes; careers, self-esteem and income are now indexed to attention; and a generation has been trained from childhood to evaluate its own existence by the number of eyes upon it. The result is a psychological arrangement in which the humbled eye is understood as failure and the watched eye as success.

The Qur'an inverts this entirely. In its ordering, the humbled eye is the achievement and the watched eye is the trap:

> *"And do not strain your eyes toward that with which We have given enjoyment to some groups of them — the splendour of the life of this world — that We may test them therein. And the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring."* (Qur'an 20:131)

The verse addresses the eyes directly and tells them where not to go. It is a discipline of attention, and attention — as anyone who has tried to concentrate for ten minutes knows — is the hardest faculty a modern person possesses to govern. The person who cannot control his gaze for the length of a prayer will find the gaze controlled for him on the Day described in verse 9, and the experience will not be pleasant.

**Why the Eyes, and Not the Words**

A final observation. The Qur'an describes the Day in terms of bodies: trembling hearts, humbled eyes, voices reduced to whispers, faces that day covered in dust. It rarely describes it in terms of speech, and when it does, speech is failing — the tongue being sealed and the limbs testifying instead (36:65). The reason is that the Day is a day of evidence, and bodies are better evidence than words.

Words can be arranged. A competent person can construct a defence, offer an interpretation, express regret in eloquent terms. Bodies cannot. The heart that pounds and the eye that drops are reports that cannot be edited. Verse 9 is therefore the completion of verse 8's argument: the human being's capacity for self-presentation — the great achievement of his social life, the thing that allowed him to be a liar, a diplomat, a performer, a fraud, or a saint who concealed his sainthood — reaches its limit. What is left is the organ he never fully controlled, showing to everyone, forever, exactly what he was.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:10

> **They will say, "Are we to be restored as we were before?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection Arrives in the Voice of Sarcasm**

The verse reproduces speech, and the Arabic preserves its tone. *Qālū a-innā la-mardūdūna fī al-ḥāfirah* — literally, "What! Are *we* really to be returned to the former state?" The sentence is loaded with emphatic particles: the interrogative *a-* prefixed to *innā*, which already means "indeed we," and the emphatic *la-* in *la-mardūdūna*. In Arabic this construction is not a request for information. It is the grammar of incredulity — the way a person says "You can't seriously be telling me that *I* have to go back."

The commentators noted this and drew the correct conclusion. Al-Ālūsī and al-Qurṭubī both observe that the objectors are not asking in order to learn; they are asking in order to mock. That distinction is essential to the verse, because it changes what kind of answer is required. A genuine question deserves evidence. A mocking question needs something else: it needs to be exposed as a question that was never actually a question.

This is why the sūrah does not respond to verse 10 with an argument. It responds with a scene.

**"Al-Ḥāfirah": The Path You Came By**

The key word is *al-ḥāfirah*. It is built on the root *ḥ-f-r*, to dig, and the classical Arabic idiom *rajaʿa fī ḥāfiratihi* — "he returned in his *ḥāfirah*" — means to retrace one's steps, to go back the way one came. The image is of a path worn or dug out by repeated passage: an animal's track cut into the ground, a route made visible by the passage of feet. To return "in the *ḥāfirah*" is to go back along the exact furrow you came out of.

Applied to resurrection, the word is exact in a way that English translation loses. The objectors are not asking whether they will exist again in some general sense; they are asking whether they will come back along the identical track — the same self, the same body, the same personal continuity, retraced in reverse. That is a much more specific and much harder question than "will we survive death," and the Qur'an's choice of the idiom shows that it understood precisely what was being denied.

The commentators record three readings of the word. The first: it means the original state of life, so the question is "shall we be brought back to life after death?" The second: it refers to the earth, since the human being came out of the earth and will return into it — and this connects the word directly to its root, digging, and to the grave as the hole that was dug. The third: it means the first affair or the original condition, i.e. the condition in which they were first created.

All three converge on the same point, which is that the objection is about *identity*, not about existence. The disbelievers are not saying "we will not continue to exist." They are saying "we will not come back as *us*."

**The Same Objection in Six Other Places**

The Qur'an records this objection more often than almost any other, and each recording adds a piece of the answer. Reading them together is the best commentary on this verse.

> *"And they say: 'What! When we are bones and dust, shall we indeed be resurrected as a new creation?' Say: 'Be stones or iron, or any created thing that is great in your breasts.' And they will say: 'Who will restore us?' Say: 'He Who originated you the first time.'"* (Qur'an 17:49–51)

> *"And man strikes for Us a similitude and forgets his own creation. He says: 'Who will give life to bones when they are rotten?' Say: 'He will give them life Who first brought them into being, and He knows every creation.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

> *"Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes indeed — We are able to reconstruct his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

> *"And they said: 'When we are dead and become dust and bones, shall we indeed be raised? We and our forefathers were promised this before — this is nothing but legends of the ancients.'"* (Qur'an 23:82–83)

> *"And if you wonder, wondrous is their saying: 'When we are dust, shall we indeed be [recreated] in a new creation?' Those are the ones who disbelieved in their Lord, and those will have shackles upon their necks."* (Qur'an 13:5)

The most revealing of these is 75:4, and the commentators have dwelt on it at length. Why the fingertips? Because the fingertip is the part of the human body that carries no duplicates: the ridges are unique to the individual and were unique long before anyone had a word for it. The verse answers the objection about identity with the single most identity-specific feature of the human form. It is as though the Qur'an anticipated the objection in its precise form — "you cannot reconstitute *me*, since matter is interchangeable" — and answered with the part of the body that is not interchangeable.

**The Argument the Qur'an Actually Uses**

There is a pattern in the Qur'anic replies, and it is worth isolating because it is unusually rational. The Qur'an does not respond to the resurrection objection with a demand for faith. It responds with an inference from what the objector already accepts.

The objector accepts that he was created. He cannot deny it, because he exists. The Qur'an's argument is then a comparison of two operations: the origination of something from nothing, and the reassembly of something from its own remains. The first is manifestly the greater.

> *"It is He Who originates creation, then He brings it back again, and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

> *"Is not He Who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? Yes — and He is the Creator, the All-Knowing."* (Qur'an 36:81)

> *"And He it is Who originates creation, then He brings it back, and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

The phrase "and that is easier for Him" has been much discussed, because nothing is easier or harder for God; His act is a single word, *"Be," and it is* (2:117). Al-Rāzī and others explain that the comparison is addressed to human reason in its own terms: in your scale of difficulty, remaking a thing from its remains is less than making it from nothing. The Qur'an is arguing *ad hominem* in the technical, non-pejorative sense — from the premises the interlocutor already holds.

The occasion of revelation transmitted in the exegetical literature makes the confrontation concrete. Ibn Kathīr records, in his commentary on 36:78, that Ubayy ibn Khalaf came to the Prophet ﷺ with a decayed bone, crumbled it in his hand, blew it into the air, and said: "O Muhammad, do you claim that God will resurrect this after it has become like this?" The reply, revealed on the spot, was the verse: *"Who will give life to bones when they are rotten? Say: He will give them life Who first brought them into being."* The gesture — crumbling a bone and scattering it — is the disbeliever's entire argument in physical form: *look how dispersed this is*. And the reply does not dispute the dispersal. It disputes the relevance of dispersal to a Creator who does not need to gather what He never lost.

**The Modern Form of the Same Question**

The objection in verse 10 is not obsolete; it has merely changed its vocabulary. The contemporary version does not say "decayed bones"; it says "the brain is the mind." If consciousness is wholly a function of a particular arrangement of neurons, then when that arrangement dissolves there is nothing to reconstitute — the person is not somewhere else, he is nowhere, and resurrection becomes not difficult but incoherent.

The Qur'an's argument survives this reformulation intact, and it does so because it never depended on the persistence of matter. The verse in Sūrah Qāf states the actual mechanism:

> *"We know best what the earth diminishes of them, and with Us is a recording book."* (Qur'an 50:4)

The claim is that the specification of the person is preserved, not the particles. And this is a position that modern reasoning has independently arrived at from a different direction. The atoms in a human body are replaced continuously; within a few years almost none of the matter that composes you today is the matter that composed you a decade ago, and yet no one says you have ceased to exist. Personal identity, in other words, has never been a function of material persistence — it is a function of pattern, information, and continuity of history. The person who objects that resurrection is impossible because matter disperses is making an assumption about identity that his own continued existence already refutes.

The Qur'an, four thousand years before this argument was articulated in a laboratory, made the same move in four words: *We know what the earth diminishes of them.*

**Why the Question Is Really About Something Else**

The most important observation about verse 10 is the one the following two verses will confirm: this is not a metaphysical question. It is a question about consequences.

A person who genuinely doubted the resurrection would ask about evidence. The objectors do not ask about evidence; they ask whether *they* — emphatically, *a-innā*, "is it really *us*" — will be restored as they were. The emphasis is on themselves, and it betrays what is actually at stake. To be restored as one was is to be restored with the debts, the injustices, the betrayals and the neglects one committed. The question "shall we be restored?" is, in the mouth of a person who has wronged others, the question "shall I be held to it?"

This is a permanent feature of human reasoning, and it is one of the most important things the Qur'an teaches about unbelief. People rarely reject a proposition because they have examined it; they reject it because they have priced it. The verse records the price being calculated out loud. And verse 12 will show the calculation completed.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:11

> **What! When we have become decayed bones?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Bone That Whistles**

The word translated "decayed" is *nakhirah*, and it is more specific than the English suggests. The root *n-kh-r* describes a bone that has become hollow and porous with age — the bone through which the wind passes with a whistling sound. Classical lexicographers define *al-ʿaẓm al-nakhir* as the bone that has rotted until it is empty inside, so that when air moves through it, it produces a sound. The image is of something not merely dead but structurally annihilated: a framework with nothing in it, a shape that has lost its substance.

The disbeliever's argument is thus stated with a physical demonstration in mind. He is not saying "we will have died." He is saying: consider what remains. Not a body, not even a skeleton — a porous tube through which the wind whistles. Consider how far that is from a living, thinking, accountable person.

**The Other Reading, and What It Adds**

There is a second canonical reading of this verse, transmitted from Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr among the seven reciters, in which the word is *nākirah* rather than *nakhirah*. *Nākirah* is from a different root — *n-k-r*, the root of *munkar*, the denied or the unrecognisable — and it means "unknown," "unfamiliar," "that which is not recognised." So on this reading the verse says: "What! When we have become bones that are unrecognisable?"

The two readings are not in conflict; they are two halves of the same objection. *Nakhirah* addresses the physical claim: the bones have lost their structure. *Nākirah* addresses the identity claim: even the remains cannot be identified as anyone. Between them they state the objection in its complete form — the material is gone and the identification is gone, so there is nothing to reassemble and no one to reassemble it as.

The fact that both readings were preserved in the canonical transmission, and that the exegetes discussed both rather than choosing one, is characteristic of the tradition's treatment of such variants. They are treated as complementary expansions of meaning, not as competing claims.

**The Qur'an's Answer, Given in Full**

The reply to this specific objection appears in the Qur'an several times, and it is worth setting out completely because it is the most sustained rational argument in the Book.

First, the argument from origination:

> *"And he strikes for Us a similitude and forgets his own creation. He says: 'Who will give life to bones when they are rotten?' Say: 'He will give them life Who originated them the first time, and He knows every creation.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

The phrase "and forgets his own creation" is the pivot. The objector is invited to remember a fact about himself that he cannot dispute: he was nothing, and then he was. Whatever produced that transition cannot be defeated by the lesser problem of arranging matter that already exists.

Second, the argument from the transformation already occurring in nature:

> *"He Who made for you, from the green tree, fire — and behold, from it you kindle."* (Qur'an 36:80)

This verse has been much discussed, and its force is often missed. The green tree is the paradigm of wetness and life; fire is the paradigm of dryness and consumption. Yet fire is produced from the green wood. The argument is that the Qur'an's audience had daily experience of a transformation between opposites that they considered impossible — and that a God who performs that transformation continuously in front of them cannot be limited by their intuitions about which transformations are permitted.

Third, the argument from the greater creation:

> *"Surely the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind — but most of mankind know not."* (Qur'an 40:57)

This is the argument the sūrah will itself deploy in verse 27, and it is the argument that does the most work in the Qur'an's case. The objector accepts that God built the heavens. He is then asked whether the reassembly of a human body is a larger task than the construction of the cosmos. If the answer is no — and it obviously is no — then the objection collapses.

Fourth, the argument from the precision of God's knowledge, already quoted: *"We know best what the earth diminishes of them, and with Us is a recording book"* (50:4).

Fifth, the argument from God's own statement of His power over the exact case in question:

> *"And they said: 'When we are bones and fragments, shall we indeed be raised as a new creation?' Say: 'Call them stones or iron, or a creation greater in your minds.' …And they will say: 'Who will bring us back?' Say: 'He Who originated you the first time.'"* (Qur'an 17:50–51)

Note the escalation God invites: *make the material as intractable as you like*. Stones. Iron. Something harder than iron, if you can think of one. The challenge is deliberately open-ended, because the answer does not depend on the material.

**The Argument in the Language of Craft**

An analogy makes the logic accessible, and the tradition uses analogies of this kind constantly. Consider a craftsman who builds a complex machine — a clock, say — from raw ore. He smelts, casts, files, assembles, and adjusts until the machine keeps time. Then the machine is smashed and its parts scattered into a landfill where they rust for a century.

Now ask: is it harder to build the clock the first time from ore, or to rebuild it from the scattered parts? The answer is obvious, and it is not even close. The first act required the invention of the design, the discovery of the principles, the creation of tools that did not exist. The second act requires only knowledge of the design — which the maker has, since he made it — and the ability to gather the pieces.

The objector in verse 11 has inverted the difficulty. He treats the reassembly as harder than the origination because he sees the scattering but cannot see the specification. He is like a man who watches a building being demolished and concludes that no one could ever build it again, having never considered that the architect still holds the drawings.

The Qur'an says exactly this in a single verse:

> *"And it is He Who originates creation, then brings it back, and that is easier for Him. And His is the highest example in the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 30:27)

**Why the Objector Cannot See It**

There remains the question of why a person who accepts the greater cannot accept the lesser. The Qur'an's diagnosis is uncomfortable and it is stated flatly:

> *"And they denied them wrongfully and arrogantly, though their souls were certain of them. So see how was the end of the corrupters."* (Qur'an 27:14)

The phrase *wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum* — "though their souls were certain of them" — is the crucial detail. The Qur'an states that the denial of Pharaoh's people was not caused by insufficient conviction. Their souls were certain. The denial was caused by *ẓulm* (wrongdoing) and *ʿulūw* (self-elevation). This is a claim about human psychology that the modern study of motivated reasoning has largely confirmed: belief is not produced by evidence alone but by evidence filtered through interest, and where the interest is strong enough, the filter is nearly absolute.

Ibn Taymiyyah made this the foundation of his account of unbelief. Kufr, in his analysis, is very often not a failure of knowledge but a failure of submission — the refusal to accept a truth whose acceptance would require a change of life. This is why the Qur'an so often pairs unbelief with arrogance rather than with ignorance, and why its remedy for unbelief so often begins with the breaking of pride rather than with the addition of arguments.

The objector holding the crumbled bone was not, on this account, confused. He was unwilling. And unwillingness is not cured by more information; it is cured by a change of heart, which is why the Qur'an's invitation is addressed to the heart before it is addressed to the intellect.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:12

> **They say, "This, then, would be a ruinous return!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Confession Inside the Mockery**

The three verses 10–12 form a single utterance, and the third of them is the most revealing. *Qālū tilka idhan karratun khāsirah* — "They say: 'That, then, would be a return of loss.'" The word *khāsirah* is the feminine active participle of *kh-s-r*, to lose, to suffer loss, to be ruined. Applied to a *karrah* — a return, a repeated act, a second turn — it means a return that ends in loss for the one who makes it.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both read this as a confession rather than a further mockery, and their reading is the key to the passage. The objectors are saying: *if this were true, we would be ruined.* They know it. The sentence is not a denial of the return; it is an assessment of what the return would cost them.

That distinction is worth holding on to, because it dismantles the modern assumption that the opponents of the Qur'an were simply ignorant people who lacked evidence. They were not making an epistemological claim. They were making a cost-benefit calculation, and they were making it out loud.

**The Structure of the Admission**

Consider what the sentence requires in order to be uttered. A person who believes the resurrection is impossible does not say "that would be a ruinous return." He says "that cannot happen." The conditional form of the sentence — *tilka idhan*, "that, then," with *idhan* marking a consequence — betrays that the speaker has, at least momentarily, entertained the possibility and immediately priced it.

The sequence in verses 10–12 is thus a complete psychological record:

Verse 10: the incredulous question, with the emphasis on the self — *are we really to be restored?*

Verse 11: the physical argument — *when we are rotten bones?*

Verse 12: the real reason — *that would be a disaster for us.*

The physical argument in verse 11 is doing rhetorical work, not evidential work. It is the respectable-sounding reason offered in support of a conclusion reached on other grounds entirely. This is a pattern visible in every era and every dispute: the objection that is stated is almost never the objection that is felt.

**What Specifically Would Be Ruined**

It is worth asking what the loss would consist of, because the answer explains the resistance. The Makkans being addressed by this sūrah were not ordinary people. They were the custodians of the Kaʿbah, the arbiters of Arabian pilgrimage, the holders of hereditary offices — the giving of water to pilgrims (*siqāyah*), the providing of food (*rifādah*), the keeping of the keys (*ḥijābah*), the carrying of the banner (*liwāʾ*). Their authority was religious, economic, and tribal simultaneously, and it rested on a religious order in which no one would be held to account for what they did with it.

The resurrection would not merely be an inconvenient metaphysical addition to that order. It would be its abolition. Every injustice committed under the cover of religious office, every orphan's property absorbed, every caravan's profits concealed, every poor man's debt enforced, every mockery of a poor preacher in the courtyard — all of it would be entered into a record and audited by a party who could not be bribed, flattered, or out-negotiated.

That is the *karrah khāsirah*. Not the loss of life — they already had that — but the loss of a position that was comfortable precisely because it was unaudited.

**The Same Sentence, Said Differently, in Every Age**

The verse describes a permanent human manoeuvre, and recognising it is one of the most useful things a reader can take from this sūrah. When a truth threatens a person's arrangements, he will rarely say "this truth threatens my arrangements." He will say "this truth is implausible." The implausibility is genuine to him — motivated reasoning produces real conviction, not feigned conviction — but its origin is in the cost.

The modern instances are legion and they are structurally identical. The executive who finds the evidence for climate change inconclusive while his compensation depends on the outcome. The physician who disputes a diagnosis that would require him to change his practice. The scholar who discovers methodological flaws in a study whose conclusion he finds objectionable, and none in a study whose conclusion he likes. The man who decides that the religious obligations he has been avoiding are probably not obligatory.

In each case the stated objection is evidential and the actual objection is that acceptance would require payment. The Qur'an names this with a precision that no amount of modern vocabulary improves upon:

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?" God knew, so He sealed his hearing and his heart and placed a covering over his sight. So who will guide him after God? Will you not remember?"* (Qur'an 45:23)

The phrase "takes his caprice as his god" is the diagnosis in full. The person who has made his desire the object of his ultimate concern will find his intellect recruited into the defence of it, and he will experience the resulting convictions as reasoned.

**Why the Qur'an Reports the Admission Rather Than Refuting It**

There is a striking feature of the Qur'an's method here. It could have answered verse 12 with a threat. Instead it simply records it, and lets the admission stand in the text forever, in the objectors' own words, without comment.

This is a rhetorical choice with a purpose. The reader is left alone with the sentence and is thereby forced to notice what it reveals. No refutation is needed, because the sentence refutes itself: a person who says "if that were true I would be ruined" has already conceded that he has something to lose, and everything he has to lose was acquired at someone's expense.

The tradition records the same manoeuvre in the confrontation between the Prophet ﷺ and the Makkans, and it is worth noting that he never once accused them of stupidity. He accused them of knowing. When they offered him wealth, kingship, and marriage in exchange for silence — the terms transmitted in the reports of the negotiation with ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah — the offer itself was the proof that they understood the message perfectly. They were not trying to disprove it; they were trying to buy it off.

**The Return That Is Not a Loss**

The verse leaves the reader with an unspoken alternative, and the sūrah supplies it thirty verses later. There is a return that is not a *karrah khāsirah*, and it is available to the person who has nothing to lose — or, more precisely, to the person who has already given away what would otherwise be counted against him.

> *"And whoever is saved from his own greed — those are the successful."* (Qur'an 59:9)

> *"So as for one who gives and fears God, and affirms the best [reward], We will ease him toward ease. And as for one who withholds and considers himself free of need, and denies the best [reward], We will ease him toward hardship."* (Qur'an 92:5–10)

The two trajectories described in Sūrah 92 are the same two trajectories described here. The person who has been generous, who has feared, and who has believed in the good outcome finds that the return is not ruinous; he arrives with a record he is not afraid of. The person who withheld, who considered himself self-sufficient, and who denied the outcome finds the return exactly as ruinous as he feared.

That is the deepest irony of verse 12, and it is the irony the Qur'an intends. The objectors were correct. It *would* be a ruinous return — for them, as they were. The error was not in their assessment of the return; it was in their assumption that the assessment was fixed. It was a prediction about themselves, and it was within their power, at that moment, to make it false.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:13

> **Yet it shall be but a single cry,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Disproportion That Is the Whole Point**

The verse consists of four Arabic words and it contains the most startling disproportion in the chapter. *Fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidah* — "It will be nothing but a single cry." On one side: the entire human race, from the first person to the last, every generation, every civilisation, every unmarked grave in every desert and every seabed. On the other side: one shout.

The particle *innamā* is restrictive; it means "only," "nothing but," and its function here is to strip the event of everything the listener's imagination will automatically supply. The listener expects an effort — a gathering, a procession, an operation of some duration involving agents and stages. The verse removes all of it. There is no operation. There is a sound.

**"Zajrah": The Shout That Drives**

The word is built on the root *z-j-r*, which means to drive or restrain by shouting — the cry a herdsman uses to move animals, the shout that both propels and controls. It is not a neutral sound; it is a command delivered at volume, with an audience that must respond and no capacity to respond otherwise. The English "cry" is too soft for it. It is closer to a bellow of command, the kind of shout that ends discussion.

The Qur'an uses this same word for the resurrection in a verse that is nearly identical to this one:

> *"It will be but a single shout, and behold, they will be seeing."* (Qur'an 37:19)

> *"And there will be but one shout, and behold, they will all be brought before Us."* (Qur'an 36:53)

> *"And the matter of Our Hour is but as a single shout — and behold, they are extinguished."* (Qur'an 36:29, of the destruction of a people)

> *"And these have but one shout."* (Qur'an 38:15)

The repetition of the formula across Sūrahs 36, 37, 38 and 79 shows that it is a fixed expression in the Qur'an's eschatology. The resurrection is, in the Qur'an's own consistent idiom, a single vocalisation.

**One Cry, or Two Blasts?**

The exegetes addressed an apparent difficulty. Sūrah al-Zumar describes two blasts of the Trumpet — the first at which all creation swoons, the second at which all rise:

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth will swoon, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown a second time, and behold, they will be standing, looking on."* (Qur'an 39:68)

How then can this verse speak of a single cry? The commentators — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī — explain that the verse refers specifically to the second blast, the one at which humanity is gathered. Each blast is single; the verse is describing the second, and it is describing it in relation to the gathering rather than in relation to the whole sequence. Some also read the reference as being to the summoning after the rising, the call that assembles the resurrected on the plain — again a single command.

There is also a report about the interval between the two blasts that the tradition preserves with characteristic honesty about the limits of its knowledge. Abū Hurayrah narrated:

> *"Between the two blasts there will be forty."* They said: "Forty days?" He said: *"I refuse [to specify]."* They said: "Forty months?" He said: *"I refuse."* They said: "Forty years?" He said: *"I refuse."* (al-Bukhārī 4814; Muslim 2955)

The narrator's refusal to speculate about a number the Prophet ﷺ left unquantified is itself a lesson in exegetical discipline. The tradition transmits what was said and declines to complete what was not.

**"Single" Means Instantaneous**

The commentators make a second point about the word *wāḥidah*. Its function is not only numerical but temporal: a single cry means no duration. The gathering will not unfold over time; it will occur. The Qur'an states this directly in two verses that the exegetes quote on this passage:

> *"And Our command is but one, like the twinkling of an eye."* (Qur'an 54:50)

> *"And to God belongs the Unseen of the heavens and the earth, and the matter of the Hour is but as the twinkling of an eye, or nearer still."* (Qur'an 16:77)

The phrase "or nearer still" — *aw aqrab* — is significant. The comparison with the blink is itself presented as an overestimate. The event is faster than the fastest thing human beings can measure by their own bodies.

The Sunnah supplies a picture of this suddenness that has been quoted by preachers for fourteen centuries, because it is so concrete:

> *"The Hour will be established while two men are spreading out a garment between them, and they will neither fold it up nor trade in it; and the Hour will be established while a man is lifting a morsel to his mouth, and he will not eat it."* (al-Bukhārī 7121; Muslim 2954)

The two images — a transaction interrupted mid-negotiation, a mouthful of food interrupted mid-air — are chosen because they are the most ordinary human moments imaginable. The point is not merely that the Hour will come suddenly; it is that it will come in the middle of something trivial, and that the trivial thing will never be completed.

**The Theology of Effortless Power**

Underneath the verse lies a doctrine about the nature of divine action, and it is one of the sharpest distinctions between the Qur'anic God and the gods of the surrounding mythologies. In the mythologies known to the Arabs, and in most religious systems of the ancient world, the gods labour. They struggle, they assemble, they exhaust themselves, they require assistants, they rest afterwards. The Qur'an denies all of this in terms that could not be plainer:

> *"And We certainly created the heavens and the earth and all between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us."* (Qur'an 50:38)

> *"Do they not see that God, Who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation, is able to give life to the dead? Yes — He is over all things Powerful."* (Qur'an 46:33)

> *"His command, when He intends a thing, is only to say to it: 'Be' — and it is."* (Qur'an 36:82)

The resurrection of the entire human race, in this framework, is not a project. It is an utterance. The disproportion between the scale of the event and the triviality of its cause is not a rhetorical exaggeration to be discounted; it is the doctrine of divine omnipotence stated in operational terms.

**An Analogy for the Disproportion**

Human experience contains one analogy that helps, and it is a modern one. Consider a city at night. To light every building, every street, every room in that city by hand would require an army working for years. To light it all at once requires one person to close one switch. The disproportion between the effect and the action is not a matter of the effect being smaller; it is a matter of the system being designed so that all the effort was spent in the building of it, and nothing is left to spend at the moment of activation.

The Qur'an's picture of creation is exactly this. The effort — if the word may be used at all of God — was in the origination. Everything subsequent, including the recall of every soul that ever lived, is activation. This is why the Qur'an insists so persistently that the second creation is *easier* than the first (30:27), not because God's power varies but because the work that had to be done was done.

**Why the Suddenness Matters Practically**

The verse is not a metaphysical curiosity; it has a practical edge, and the edge is this: if the transition is instantaneous, then there is no interval in which to prepare.

Every human being operates with an implicit assumption of a grace period. When a deadline approaches, we assume there will be a warning. When a relationship deteriorates, we assume there will be a conversation. When health fails, we assume there will be symptoms in time to act. The assumption is often correct, and its frequent correctness is precisely what makes it dangerous, because it trains the person to postpone.

Verse 13 removes the assumption at the level where it matters most. There will be no warning phase, no transition period, no final opportunity. The garment will be in mid-air. The morsel will be in mid-air. This is why the Qur'an's repeated command about the Hereafter is not "believe" but "hasten":

> *"And hasten to forgiveness from your Lord, and a Garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 3:133)

The verb is *sāriʿū* — race. And the Prophet ﷺ explained the reasoning:

> *"Hasten to good deeds before the trials come, like pieces of a dark night: a man wakes a believer and becomes a disbeliever by evening, or is a believer in the evening and becomes a disbeliever by morning — selling his religion for a trifling portion of this world."* (Muslim 118)

The image of trials arriving like fragments of a dark night — so thick that they cannot be distinguished from one another — is the moral equivalent of the single cry. Both describe a situation in which the window closes without announcing itself.

**The Cry That Has Already Been Heard**

There is a final dimension to this verse, and the tradition developed it in the practice of remembrance. The single cry is the ultimate summons, but it is not the only one. The call to prayer is a summons five times a day; the summons of the Qur'an is a summons every time it is recited; the death of someone close is a summons that arrives with a name on it. The believer's practice is to treat each of these as a small version of the cry, so that the great one does not arrive as an interruption.

This is why the Islamic tradition has no separate "spiritual" sphere that can be switched on and off. The prayer times are fixed by the position of the sun, which means they cannot be scheduled around convenience. The fast is fixed by the month, not by readiness. The alms are fixed by the amount, not by mood. The whole structure is designed to produce a person for whom summons are normal, and who therefore does not require a warning when the final one comes.

The man who has spent seventy years answering small calls will not be disoriented by a large one. The man who has spent seventy years ignoring them will find that the single cry of verse 13 is not the first he has heard — it is only the first he could not ignore.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:14

> **then, behold, they will be upon a wide expanse.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plain With Nothing On It**

The word is *al-sāhirah*, and the sūrah is named after it in one of its alternate titles. Its root is *s-h-r*, the root of sleeplessness — *sahar* is the state of being awake through the night, unable to sleep. Applied to a place, the word carries a double meaning that the commentators drew out: it is the open, exposed surface of the earth, and it is the place where no one sleeps. Both senses are active in the verse, and both are exact. There will be no sleep on that plain, and there will be nothing on it.

Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr record the primary interpretation: *al-sāhirah* is the surface of the earth, renewed and levelled for the gathering. Others connect it to the descriptions of the plain elsewhere in the Qur'an:

> *"And We will remove the mountains, and you will see the earth a level plain, and We will gather them and leave out none of them."* (Qur'an 18:47)

> *"And they ask you about the mountains. Say: 'My Lord will blast them and scatter them as particles of dust, and leave it a level plain — you will see therein neither crookedness nor curve.'"* (Qur'an 20:105–107)

Still others held that it refers to a specific location — a plain near Jerusalem, in the vicinity of Mount Arīḥā, where some traditions say the Resurrection will take place. And al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī preserve the linguistic reading that *sāhirah* means simply that they will be *awake*, roused by the intensity of their fear, since sleep is impossible in that condition.

**The Authentic Description of the Gathering Place**

The Prophet ﷺ described this plain in a report that is among the most quoted in the eschatological literature. Sahl ibn Saʿd al-Sāʿidī narrated:

> *"The people will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection upon a white land, reddish-white, like a pure loaf of bread, in which there is no landmark for anyone."* (al-Bukhārī 6521; Muslim 2790)

Every element of that description is doing work. *White and reddish-white* — a surface of uniform, unvaried colour. *Like a pure loaf* — the Arabic *qurṣat al-naqī* refers to a flat round of fine flour bread, smooth and featureless; the comparison is to a surface with no texture, no elevation, no variation. And then the sentence that matters most: *laysa fīhā ʿalam li-aḥad* — "there is no landmark in it for anyone."

The word *ʿalam* means a marker, a sign, a distinguishing feature — the same word used for a mountain that serves as a navigational mark, for a banner, and for a person famous enough to be a landmark in his field. On that plain there is no banner, no mountain, no boundary stone, no address, no flag. There is nothing by which anyone can be identified or by which anyone can orient himself.

**The Removal of Every Marker by Which a Person Is Known**

This is where the verse's psychological force lies. Human beings do not locate themselves by coordinates. They locate themselves by markers: the house they grew up in, the school they attended, the city on their identification, the title on their card, the family name that opens doors, the neighbourhood, the accent, the car, the clothes. Strip these away and most people cannot say who they are.

The plain of verse 14 is the systematic removal of all of them. No landmark for anyone. The king and the porter stand on the same featureless surface with nothing to distinguish them, and neither has any means of establishing his former position, because the markers are gone and the people who recognised them are gone and the institutions that conferred them are gone.

The Qur'an states the same principle in a verse about the dissolution of lineage:

> *"And when the Trumpet is blown, there will be no lineage between them that Day, nor will they ask one another."* (Qur'an 23:101)

> *"And they will come forth to Him humble, and wealth will not avail you this Day."* (Qur'an 57:12, in context)

The Prophet ﷺ made the point with a specific and uncomfortable bluntness:

> *"God will gather the first and the last on the Day of Resurrection, and a caller will call out: 'Whoever used to associate a partner with God, let him seek his reward from other than God — for God is the least in need of any partner.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 3158; Ibn Mājah, in similar wording)

And he destroyed the tribal basis of Arabian identity in one sentence:

> *"O people, your Lord is one and your father is one. Truly there is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except by righteousness."* (Aḥmad; declared sound by the hadith critics — delivered in the Farewell Sermon)

The plain of verse 14 is the physical realisation of that sermon. It is not a metaphor for equality; it is equality enforced by the removal of everything that inequality was built from.

**How the Standing Will Feel**

The tradition supplies details of the experience on that plain that make the verse concrete rather than abstract.

> *"The people will be gathered barefoot, naked, uncircumcised."* (al-Bukhārī 3349; Muslim 2859)

> *"People will sweat on the Day of Resurrection until their sweat goes seventy cubits into the earth, and it will bridle them until it reaches their ears."* (al-Bukhārī 6532; Muslim 2863)

The second report adds a distinction that the commentators emphasised:

> *"The sun will be brought near the creation on the Day of Resurrection until it is a mile from them, and people will be [immersed] in their deeds — so among them will be one whose sweat reaches his ankles, among them one whose sweat reaches his knees, among them one whose sweat reaches his waist, and among them one whom the sweat will bridle."* The narrator said: I saw the Messenger of God ﷺ indicating this with his hand toward his mouth. (Muslim 2862)

The sweat is described as varying with the deeds. It is not a uniform environment experienced differently by different temperaments; it is a physical state produced by the person's own record. The same sun, the same plain, the same crowd — and one man to the ankles, another to the mouth.

And there is an exemption, which is the reason the passage is not merely terrible:

> *"Seven will God shade in His shade on the Day when there is no shade but His shade: a just ruler; a youth who grew up in the worship of God; a man whose heart was attached to the mosques; two men who loved each other for God's sake, meeting and parting upon it; a man called by a woman of position and beauty who said, 'I fear God'; a man who gave charity so secretly that his left hand did not know what his right hand spent; and a man who remembered God in seclusion and his eyes overflowed."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

The seven are all defined by acts performed without an audience — secret charity, private tears, a refusal no one knew about, a loyalty maintained with no benefit. The shade is purchased by the things the plain cannot show, which is precisely the point: on a surface with no landmarks, the only distinctions that survive are the ones that were never visible.

**What Travels Onto the Plain**

The verse invites a practical question that the tradition answers with a famous report. Anas ibn Mālik narrated:

> *"The deceased is followed by three: his family, his wealth, and his deeds. Two return and one remains with him — his family and his wealth return, and his deeds remain."* (al-Bukhārī 6514; Muslim 2960)

The report is a complete inventory. Everything a person spends his life accumulating falls into three categories, and two of the three have an escort that turns around at the grave. The Qur'an says the same thing about wealth with a precision that has made the report unforgettable:

> *"The son of Adam says: 'My wealth, my wealth!' But is there anything of your wealth except what you ate and consumed, or wore and wore out, or gave in charity and sent ahead? The rest is left for the people."* (Muslim 2958)

The three permitted uses of wealth — consumption, wear, and charity — are the only three in which the wealth ever becomes *yours* in any sense that survives you. Everything else is storage on behalf of heirs. On the plain of verse 14, the stored portion is not present at all. Only the charity is.

**Why the Plain Is Featureless: The Logic of a Fair Hearing**

There is a rational explanation for the featurelessness of the plain that the commentators implied and that deserves to be stated. A courtroom is deliberately stripped of markers. There is no defendant's office, no plaintiff's home, no neighbourhood, no rank displayed on the wall — because every one of those things would bias the proceeding. The bareness of the room is not austerity; it is a condition of the fairness of the hearing.

The plain of verse 14 is the same design applied at scale. The removal of the landmarks is not a cruelty added to the punishment; it is the removal of everything that would have corrupted the judgment. The man who was powerful in this world cannot be advantaged by his power, because the power has no infrastructure. The man who was despised cannot be prejudiced against, because no one can tell. The hearing is finally conducted under conditions in which the only evidence admitted is the record.

That is what the verse means by *sāhirah* in its deepest sense: an open place, with nothing in it, where nothing can be hidden and nothing can be leveraged. The person who spent his life building markers will arrive to find that they were admissible nowhere. The person who spent his life building a record will arrive to find that it was the only thing that ever travelled.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:15

> **Hast thou heard tell of Moses,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Has the News Come to You?": The Formula of Attention**

The verse opens with a question that appears several times in the Qur'an and never as a genuine inquiry. *Hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā* — "Has the account of Moses come to you?" The same formula introduces other narratives:

> *"Has the account of the hosts come to you — Pharaoh and Thamūd?"* (Qur'an 85:17–18)

> *"Has the account of the Overwhelming Event come to you?"* (Qur'an 88:1)

> *"Has the account of the honoured guests of Abraham come to you?"* (Qur'an 51:24)

> *"And has the account of Moses come to you?"* (Qur'an 20:9)

The function of the formula is rhetorical and it is precise. It performs three operations at once. It announces that what follows is *news* — something not previously known, therefore deserving of attention. It puts the listener in the position of one who is about to be informed, which is a position of receptivity rather than debate. And it implies that the news matters, because no one prefaces trivial information with "have you heard?"

The word *ḥadīth* is itself significant. It means news, report, discourse — and it is one of the words the Qur'an uses for itself:

> *"This is a noble Qur'an, in a guarded tablet"* — and elsewhere — *"In what discourse after it will they believe?"* (Qur'an 77:50)

> *"So leave Me with those who deny this discourse."* (Qur'an 68:44)

The story of Moses is therefore described in the same word used for revelation as a whole. It is not an illustration appended to doctrine; it is itself revelation, carrying authority in its own right.

**The Argument Hidden in the Question**

There is a dimension to this verse that its first audience would have felt immediately and that a modern reader may miss. The Prophet ﷺ was an unlettered man in a city without libraries, and he was reciting detailed narratives of the ancient prophets. The Qur'an addresses the implication directly and repeatedly, and it does so precisely in connection with the story of Moses:

> *"And you were not on the western side when We decreed to Moses the command, nor were you among the witnesses."* (Qur'an 28:44)

> *"And you were not a resident among the people of Madyan, reciting to them Our verses — but We were senders."* (Qur'an 28:45)

> *"And you were not beside the mount when We called. But [it is] a mercy from your Lord, that you may warn a people to whom no warner came before you, that perhaps they may remember."* (Qur'an 28:46)

The three verses name the three specific locations and moments in the Moses story — the western slope of the mountain where the command was given, the land of Madyan where Moses spent a decade, and the mount where he was called — and state flatly that Muhammad ﷺ was at none of them. The argument is the argument of an eyewitness account delivered by someone who was not present. Either he invented it, which would require a source, or he received it.

> *"That is from the news of the Unseen which We reveal to you. You were not with them when they resolved upon their affair, while they were scheming."* (Qur'an 12:102)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt stands at the head of the first chronological narration of Moses in the entire revelation, and the question "has the account come to you?" therefore carries, for the Prophet's own community, an additional edge: this is an account that did not come from you.

**Why Moses, and Why Here**

According to the standard chronological arrangements of the revelation, this is the first place in the Qur'an where the story of Moses is narrated at length. The later accounts in Sūrahs 7, 20, 26, 28, and 44 had not yet been revealed. The sūrah assumes a basic familiarity — the Arabs of Makkah lived alongside Jewish tribes and Christian communities and knew the broad outline — and it uses that familiarity economically, in eleven verses, to make a single point.

Moses is the most frequently mentioned individual in the Qur'an, named far more often than any other prophet, including Muhammad ﷺ. The reason is not biographical but structural. Moses is the Qur'an's paradigm case of a prophet confronting a political power that claims divinity. Every other prophet in the Qur'an faces a community; Moses faces a state. Abraham confronts his father and his people; Noah confronts his generation; Ṣāliḥ confronts a tribe; but Moses confronts an institutionalised tyranny with an army, a bureaucracy, a priesthood, a propaganda apparatus, and a claim to be a god.

That is why the story appears here, in a Makkan sūrah addressed to a persecuted community. The Muslims of Makkah were not facing an army. They were facing a commercial aristocracy with religious authority, social leverage, and a monopoly on the sanctuary — a power far smaller than Pharaoh's but structurally identical. The sūrah is telling them: this has happened before, and it ended a specific way.

**Consolation and Warning in the Same Story**

The classical commentators noted that the Moses narrative in the Makkan sūrahs performs a double function, and both functions are visible in this single verse.

For the Prophet ﷺ and his followers, it is consolation. The man who will be sent to the most powerful ruler on earth was, at the time of his calling, a fugitive — wanted for a killing, living in exile, tending sheep for his father-in-law, walking home in the cold with his family. The Qur'an's choice to begin the story at the moment of his calling rather than at the moment of his victory is deliberate: the reader is shown that the eventual outcome belongs to a man who began in a worse position than the Muslims of Makkah.

For the Quraysh, it is a warning with a precise target. The Makkans who mocked the Prophet ﷺ were not being asked to evaluate an unfamiliar doctrine. They were being shown their own behaviour in an older mirror, with the ending already filled in.

The Qur'an states this purpose explicitly when it summarises the function of prophetic narrative:

> *"And all that We relate to you of the news of the messengers is that by which We make firm your heart. And in this has come to you the truth, and an exhortation, and a reminder for the believers."* (Qur'an 11:120)

Three functions in one verse: firmness for the believer, truth for the enquirer, and reminder for the one who already knows. The story of Moses is not entertainment and it is not history in the academic sense. It is instrumentation.

**The Modern Reader and the Question**

The question "has the account of Moses come to you?" arrives at a modern reader differently than it arrived at a seventh-century Arab, but not less forcibly. Most people in the contemporary world have heard *of* Moses. Very few have heard *the account* — the specific structure of the story, the sequence of its confrontations, the mechanism of its outcome. The narrative has been flattened into cultural reference: a film, a phrase, a name.

The verse's question is therefore still a question. And the answer it invites is not knowledge but attention. The Qur'an does not ask whether you have heard the name; it asks whether the *account* has come to you — whether the thing itself has reached you, in its detail, with its claims intact.

There is a practical version of this that applies to everything worth knowing. Most people have "heard of" the great texts, the great events, the great arguments, in the sense that the names are familiar. Almost no one has had the account come to them — read in full, in order, with the difficulty left in. The difference between the two states is the difference between having an opinion about a thing and having encountered it. The verse opens a story that will require eleven verses to tell, and it opens it with a question that the honest reader must answer no.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:16

> **when his Lord called out to him in the holy valley of Ṭuwā?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of Calling From a Distance**

The word is *nādāhu* — "He called him" — from *n-d-w*, a root denoting a call made from a distance, with the voice raised. It is not the word for a whispered instruction or a private thought; it is the word for a summons across a space. The Qur'an uses it of God's address to several of His prophets, and in Moses's case it names a mode of communication that the tradition identified as unique:

> *"...and God spoke to Moses directly."* (Qur'an 4:164)

The word *taklīman* is a verbal noun of emphasis; the construction states that the speech was direct, without intermediary. The Qur'an then sets out the three possible modes of divine communication and places Moses in the middle one:

> *"And it is not for a mortal that God should speak to him except by revelation, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger who reveals by His permission what He wills."* (Qur'an 42:51)

Moses received the second: from behind a veil. He heard the speech without seeing the Speaker — which is why the subsequent request to see God, and the mountain's crumbling at the moment of the manifestation (7:143), forms such a powerful contrast. The tradition's title for Moses, *Kalīm Allāh*, "the one to whom God spoke," derives from this verse and this event.

**Ṭuwā: The Name and Its Meanings**

The valley is described twice in one phrase: *al-wādi al-muqaddas* — "the holy valley" — and then named: *Ṭuwā*. The commentators offer several readings of the name. It may be simply a proper name for the valley at the foot of Mount Sinai. It may derive from *ṭawā/ṭayya*, "to fold" or "to roll up," in which case the valley is described as wrapped in sanctity. And there is the reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās that it means "sanctified twice" — *ṭuwā* being read as a dual, so that the phrase describes a place given a double consecration.

The Qur'an describes the same location in Sūrah Ṭāhā:

> *"Truly I am your Lord, so remove your sandals. Truly you are in the holy valley of Ṭuwā."* (Qur'an 20:12)

And in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ it gives the precise geography of the moment:

> *"And when he came to it, he was called from the right slope of the valley, in the blessed ground, from the tree: 'O Moses, truly I am God, Lord of the worlds.'"* (Qur'an 28:30)

Three coordinates in one verse: the right slope, the blessed ground, the tree. The tradition preserved these details with the care it always gives to the physical settings of revelation, because the setting is part of the meaning.

**Why God Sanctifies a Place**

The designation of a valley as holy raises a question that the Islamic tradition answered with unusual clarity. What does it mean for a patch of ground to be sacred? Ground is ground. The atoms in the valley of Ṭuwā were not different from the atoms in the valley next to it.

The answer given by the exegetes, and developed systematically by Ibn Taymiyyah in his discussions of sacred places and times, is that sanctity in Islam is conferred, not intrinsic. A place is holy because God designated it, a time is holy because God appointed it, and the holiness consists in what is to be done there rather than in any physical property of the site. This is why the Kaʿbah is called the first house appointed for mankind (3:96) — *appointed*, not discovered — and why the sanctity of the sacred months is a matter of decree (9:36).

The practical consequence is important and it distinguishes Islam sharply from the religious geography of the surrounding world. There is no sacred soil that transfers holiness by contact, no spring whose water has intrinsic power, no stone that grants blessing to the one who touches it. The Prophet ﷺ removed the possibility of the entire category in the most emphatic terms. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb narrated:

> *"I saw the Messenger of God ﷺ kiss the Black Stone and say: 'Truly I know that you are a stone that neither harms nor benefits. Had I not seen the Messenger of God ﷺ kiss you, I would not have kissed you.'"* (al-Bukhārī 1597; Muslim 1270)

The statement is a deliberate demystification performed at the most sacred physical object in Islam. The act is an act of obedience to a command, not an extraction of power from a stone. The valley of Ṭuwā is holy in exactly the same sense: it is the place where God chose to speak, and its honour is entirely derived.

**The Man in the Valley: What Moses Was Doing**

The Qur'an's accounts supply the context of this moment in detail, and the detail is the most humanising part of the story. Moses was not on a spiritual retreat. He was cold, in the dark, with his family, and he had left them to fetch fire.

> *"When Moses said to his family: 'Truly I perceive a fire. I will bring you news from it, or a brand of fire, that you may warm yourselves.'"* (Qur'an 28:29)

> *"And when Moses said to his family: 'Stay here — truly I perceive a fire. Perhaps I shall bring you news from it, or a burning brand, that you may warm yourselves.'"* (Qur'an 20:10)

The picture is complete and unglamorous: a man walking through a cold desert night, away from his family, on an errand. He is a fugitive — he had left Egypt after killing a man, and he was wanted. He is poor — he had spent a decade as a hired shepherd in Madyan. He is unaccompanied, uncertain of the route, and his immediate concern is that his family is cold.

The revelation arrives into that situation. This is not incidental to the story; it is the pattern of the story, and it is one of the Qur'an's most consistent teachings about how God's initiative reaches human beings. Moses was not seeking prophethood. He had no plan for it, no ambition for it, and no preparation for it. He was doing an ordinary, difficult, necessary thing, and the call found him there.

The same pattern runs through the Qur'an's other callings. Mary was in a place apart, attending to her own affairs, when the angel came to her (19:16–17). Muhammad ﷺ was in a cave, in a practice of withdrawal, when the first revelation came (96:1). None of the three was performing a career move.

**The Valley as a Description of the Human Situation**

The commentators on the spiritual tradition read this setting as a description rather than a location, and the reading is defensible on the text's own terms. The valley is low. It is night. It is cold. The man in it is lost, and he is moving toward a light he can see but cannot reach, on behalf of people he loves.

The Qur'an uses precisely this image of a person in darkness moving toward a distant light as its central metaphor for the difference between guidance and misguidance:

> *"Or like a rainstorm from the heaven, in which is darkness, thunder and lightning — they put their fingers in their ears against the thunderclaps, in fear of death; and God encompasses the disbelievers. The lightning all but snatches away their sight..."* (Qur'an 2:19–20)

And it describes the unguided life as:

> *"...darknesses upon a deep sea, covered by waves above which are waves, above which are clouds — darknesses, one upon another. When he puts out his hand, he can barely see it..."* (Qur'an 24:40)

Against these, the valley of Ṭuwā is the opposite configuration: a man in darkness who sees a fire and walks toward it, and finds that the fire is a voice. That is the structure of every genuine conversion in the Qur'an's presentation — the seeker moves toward what he thinks is warmth and discovers that it is speech.

**What the Verse Teaches About Where God Speaks**

There is a practical conclusion here that has been drawn by ascetics and ordinary believers alike for fourteen centuries. God spoke to Moses in a valley, at night, in the cold, on an errand. He did not speak to him in the palace he had been raised in, or in the court he had fled, or in any place of status.

The tradition generalised this observation into a rule about the conditions of receptivity. The human being is most open to guidance when his resources are exhausted — when the money has run out, the connections have failed, the body has aged, the plan has collapsed. Not because suffering is intrinsically valuable, but because the ordinary mechanisms of self-sufficiency have stopped functioning and the person has, for the first time in years, nothing to offer. The Qur'an describes exactly this transition in the story of Jonah:

> *"And [mention] the man of the fish, when he went off in anger and thought that We would not constrain him. Then he called out in the darknesses: 'There is no god but You — glory be to You; truly I was among the wrongdoers.' So We answered him and saved him from grief. And thus We save the believers."* (Qur'an 21:87–88)

The three darknesses — the night, the sea, and the belly of the fish — are the valley of Ṭuwā in another key. And the closing generalisation is the important part: *"And thus We save the believers."* The pattern is not unique to Jonah; it is the pattern.

The verse, then, places the reader in the valley before it introduces the mission. Before Moses is sent to Pharaoh, he is shown cold and uncertain, on an errand for his family. The man who will confront the greatest power of his age begins as a man looking for a fire. That ordering is not accidental, and it is the first thing the story has to teach.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:17

> **"Go unto Pharaoh! Truly he has rebelled!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Commission in Six Words**

The entire prophetic commission is delivered in a single sentence, and its economy is remarkable. *Idhhab ilā Firʿawna innahū ṭaghā* — "Go to Pharaoh; truly he has transgressed." There is no army, no alliance, no strategy, no leverage, and no condition. There is one man, one journey, and one diagnosis.

Notice what the verse does and does not say. It does not say "go to Pharaoh because he is powerful," though he was the most powerful man alive. It does not say "go to Pharaoh because he oppresses the Children of Israel," though he did. It gives one reason: *innahū ṭaghā* — he has exceeded his limit. The mission is addressed to a moral fact, not a political one.

That distinction is the whole theology of prophethood in a sentence. The prophets are not sent to rebalance power. They are sent to correct transgression — and the transgression of the tyrant is not that he is strong but that he has passed a boundary set for him.

**"Ṭaghā": The Word for Crossing a Line**

The root *ṭ-gh-y* (and its variant *ṭ-gh-w*) denotes exceeding a set limit, and Arabic uses it for exactly that in physical contexts. The Qur'an itself uses it of water:

> *"Truly, when the water rose, We carried you in the flowing [ark]."* (Qur'an 69:11)

— *ṭaghā al-māʾ*, the water exceeded its banks. It uses it of the destruction of Thamūd:

> *"Thamūd denied by their transgression."* (Qur'an 91:11)

— *bi-ṭughwāhā*, by their exceeding. And it derives from the same root the word *ṭāghūt*, used throughout the Qur'an for whatever is worshipped or obeyed in place of God — the false authority, the idol, the tyrant:

> *"And We certainly sent to every community a messenger: 'Worship God and avoid the ṭāghūt.'"* (Qur'an 16:36)

The semantic field is consistent and it is precise. A *ṭāghī* is not simply a bad or cruel person. He is a person who has moved past a limit that was fixed for him, and the term implies the existence of the limit as clearly as it implies the violation. You cannot transgress a boundary that does not exist. The word therefore contains, within itself, the affirmation that Pharaoh had a limit — that he was a creature with a ceiling, whatever he believed.

**The Mechanism of Transgression, Stated in the Earliest Revelation**

The Qur'an gives the psychology of *ṭughyān* in two verses, and they belong to the earliest revelation received by Muhammad ﷺ:

> *"No — truly man transgresses, because he considers himself self-sufficient."* (Qur'an 96:6–7)

The structure of the sentence is causal and it is the most important causal claim in the Qur'an's moral psychology: *an raʾāhu istaghnā* — "because he sees himself as having no need." Transgression is not caused by wickedness; it is caused by a perception of sufficiency. The man who believes he needs nothing from anyone will eventually behave as though the rules do not apply to him, and the sequence is not a moral accident but a mechanism.

This is why the Qur'an so consistently pairs wealth with transgression, and poverty with receptivity. It is not that wealth is evil; it is that wealth produces the *feeling* of self-sufficiency, and that feeling is the precondition of *ṭughyān*:

> *"But as for one who withholds and considers himself free of need, and denies the best [reward], We will ease him toward hardship."* (Qur'an 92:8–10)

The three elements are chained: withholding, self-sufficiency, denial. And Pharaoh is the case study — a man whose kingdom produced in him the conviction that he required nothing.

**Why One Man Was Sent Rather Than an Army**

The commission raises an obvious strategic question, and the answer to it is the whole point of the story. God had the power to destroy Pharaoh directly; He did so eventually. He could have sent an army, or a plague, or an earthquake, and the Qur'an records that He sent plagues — but only after the mission had been delivered and refused.

What God sent first was a man with a message. This establishes a principle that the tradition drew out as a rule of justice: no community is punished until it has been warned by a messenger. The Qur'an states it repeatedly:

> *"And your Lord never destroys towns until He sends to their mother city a messenger reciting to them Our signs."* (Qur'an 28:59)

> *"And never did We destroy a town but that it had warners."* (Qur'an 26:208)

> *"…that We may not destroy the towns save that their people are wrongdoers."* (Qur'an 28:59, in context)

> *"…and We never punish until We send a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

The principle is the foundation of the Islamic understanding of divine justice. Punishment without prior warning would be arbitrary; punishment after a rejected warning is a consequence. The mission of Moses to Pharaoh is therefore not a preliminary to the destruction; it is the condition of its justice.

**"A Word of Truth Before a Tyrant"**

The commission in this verse established a category of action that the Islamic tradition identified as the highest form of courage, and it named it explicitly. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī narrated:

> *"The best of jihad is a word of justice before a tyrannical ruler."* (Abū Dāwūd 4344; al-Tirmidhī 2174; Ibn Mājah 4011 — declared ṣaḥīḥ)

In another wording transmitted by al-Ḥākim and others:

> *"The chief of martyrs is Ḥamzah ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and a man who stood before an unjust ruler, commanded him [to good] and forbade him [from evil], and he killed him for it."* (al-Ḥākim; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

The two reports together define the category precisely. The best jihad is not the battlefield; it is the sentence spoken to a person who can kill you for saying it. And the highest rank of martyrdom after Ḥamzah — who died at Uḥud — is given to the man who does what Moses did in this verse.

Ibn Taymiyyah developed the jurisprudence of this category at length, distinguishing it from both silence and from reckless provocation. The obligation of commanding good and forbidding evil applies to the ruler as it applies to anyone else; but it is to be performed with the intention of correcting, not of humiliating, and with a realistic assessment of consequence. The Prophet ﷺ gave the general instruction:

> *"Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."* (Muslim 49)

The descending order is not a ranking of persons but of capacities. When the hand is unavailable — when one has no institutional power — the tongue remains, and when even that is impossible, the heart's refusal is still counted as faith. What is never permitted is the heart's approval.

**The Modern Form of This Verse**

The commission in verse 17 is not confined to prophets. It has an ordinary, daily form, and recognising it is the practical use of the verse.

Every human being operates inside structures in which someone has *ṭaghā* — exceeded a limit. A supervisor who humiliates subordinates because no one has ever objected. A family member whose temper has become an accepted feature of the household. An institution that has normalised a fraud. A community that has agreed, silently, not to mention a particular injustice. In each of these, the transgression is sustained not by the tyrant's strength but by everyone else's calculation.

The verse's answer is uncomfortable and it is not softened anywhere in the tradition. Someone has to go. Not a delegation, not a committee, not a person with leverage — one person, with a sentence, at personal cost. This is why the report calls it the *best* of jihad: because it is the form of courage that cannot be performed for an audience, cannot be rewarded by a promotion, and cannot be done by anyone other than the person who is standing there.

There is also a psychological truth in the structure that the tradition understood well. Tyranny expands to fill the space nobody defends. The tyrant rarely begins by claiming divinity; Pharaoh began, according to the reports cited by al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī, with the more modest statement *"O notables, I know of no god for you other than myself"* (28:38), and only later reached *"I am your lord most high"* (79:24). Between those two sentences lay the silence of everyone who could have objected to the first.

The commission of verse 17 exists because that silence is never neutral. The man who goes to Pharaoh is not performing an act of heroism beyond the ordinary; he is performing the ordinary act that everyone else declined, and the reason the story is told in every age is to make that plain.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:18

> **"And say, 'Dost thou wish to be purified"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Polite Sentence Ever Addressed to a Tyrant**

The sentence Moses is instructed to deliver is extraordinary, and it is worth reading slowly. The man before him has just been described by God as having transgressed. He rules the greatest empire on earth. He enslaves a people, kills their sons, and is on a trajectory toward claiming divinity. And the address Moses is given is a question: *hal laka ilā an tazakkā* — "do you have [an inclination] to purify yourself?"

Not "you must purify yourself." Not "purify yourself, or else." A question, framed as an offer, leaving the decision entirely with the addressee. The particle *hal laka* literally means "is there for you [a way/inclination] to…" — it hands the initiative over. It is the linguistic form of an open door.

The Qur'an gives the same instruction in Sūrah Ṭāhā with equal explicitness:

> *"Go, you and your brother, with My signs, and do not slacken in My remembrance. Go, both of you, to Pharaoh — truly he has transgressed. And speak to him a gentle word, that perhaps he may remember or fear."* (Qur'an 20:42–44)

The command to use a *qawlan layyinā* — a soft, gentle word — is addressed to two men who had just been told, in the same passage, that their adversary has transgressed. The gentleness is not a concession to weakness; it is a deliberate methodology commanded by God in full knowledge of what Pharaoh is.

**Why Gentleness, Given What Is at Stake**

The question deserves a real answer, because it is often raised as an objection: why be gentle with someone who is committing atrocities?

The first reason is practical and the Qur'an states it in the verse itself: *"that perhaps he may remember or fear."* The purpose of the address is transformation, not condemnation. An accusation produces defence; a question produces thought. A person who is attacked closes; a person who is offered something examines it. The tradition understood this with perfect clarity, and the Prophet ﷺ stated it as a general law:

> *"Gentleness is not found in anything except that it adorns it, and it is not removed from anything except that it disgraces it."* (Muslim 2594)

> *"Truly God is gentle and loves gentleness, and He gives for gentleness what He does not give for harshness, and what He does not give for anything else."* (Muslim 2593)

The second reason is theological. If the offer were delivered as a compulsion, its acceptance would not be meaningful. Faith that is coerced is not faith; it is compliance, and the Qur'an distinguishes them explicitly:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion. The right course has become distinct from error."* (Qur'an 2:256)

The offer must be genuinely open for the acceptance to be genuine. This is why the Qur'an's missionaries are given questions rather than ultimatums.

The third reason is that the alternative was available and was declined. God could have sent Moses with the plagues first. The plagues came, but they came after the refusal — the sequence in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf is explicit: the demand, the refusal, then the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, and the blood (7:133). The gentleness was not a softness in the system; it was a stage in the justice of the system.

**"Tazakkā": The Word That Means Purification and Growth at Once**

The word is built on *z-k-w*, and this root is one of the most important in the Qur'an because it holds two meanings that most languages separate. *Zakā* means to be pure and to grow. The same root gives *zakāh*, the obligatory alms — so called, the commentators explain, because giving purifies the wealth and increases it. Purification in Arabic is therefore not a subtractive operation. It is not the removal of something from a static object; it is the condition in which a thing grows.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the economic half of this principle in a report that has been the foundation of Islamic charity for fourteen centuries:

> *"Charity does not decrease wealth."* (Muslim 2588)

The verse's offer to Pharaoh uses the same word, and the implication is precise: you are not being asked to give something up. You are being offered growth. The purification on offer is the condition in which a human being develops.

The Qur'an makes this the criterion of success in two separate verses:

> *"Successful indeed is the one who purifies himself."* (Qur'an 87:14)

> *"Successful is the one who purifies it — and failed is the one who corrupts it."* (Qur'an 91:9–10)

And it locates the responsibility for the operation with the individual while affirming that its success is not from him:

> *"And whoever purifies himself, purifies himself only for himself. And to God is the final return."* (Qur'an 35:18)

> *"And had it not been for the grace of God upon you and His mercy, not one of you would have been pure ever. But God purifies whom He wills."* (Qur'an 24:21)

The two verses are not in tension; they describe the two halves of the Islamic understanding of moral improvement. The effort is genuinely yours — no one else can perform it for you, and its benefit accrues to you alone. The result is genuinely God's — no amount of effort produces purity without grace. The person who grasps only the first half becomes self-righteous; the person who grasps only the second becomes passive.

**What Purification Actually Involves**

The offer is general — *tazakkā*, "that you become pure" — and its content is supplied elsewhere. Purification in the Qur'an is not ritual cleanliness, though it includes it. It is the correction of three things: what one believes, what one wants, and what one does.

Of belief:

> *"It is He Who sent among the unlettered a messenger from among themselves, reciting to them His verses, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom."* (Qur'an 62:2)

Of desire:

> *"And as for one who feared the standing before his Lord and forbade the soul from caprice — truly the Garden is the refuge."* (Qur'an 79:40–41, later in this very sūrah)

Of conduct:

> *"Take from their wealth a charity by which you purify them and sanctify them."* (Qur'an 9:103)

The three are ordered, and the order is not arbitrary. A person cannot purify his conduct while his desires remain disordered, and he cannot order his desires while his beliefs about what is real remain wrong. The offer to Pharaoh — purify yourself, and I will guide you to your Lord — contains the whole sequence in its correct order.

**The Methodology of Invitation**

This verse established, for the Islamic tradition, the grammar of religious invitation, and the grammar has specific features that later jurists codified.

The invitation is offered, not imposed. It begins with a benefit to the listener, not with his faults. It is phrased in terms the listener can accept. It leaves the decision open. And it is delivered without insult, because insult destroys the possibility of acceptance and thereby defeats the purpose.

The Prophet ﷺ embodied this so consistently that ʿĀʾishah's description of him became the standard reference:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ was not given a choice between two matters except that he chose the easier of them, so long as it was not a sin; and if it was a sin, he was the furthest of people from it. And the Messenger of God ﷺ never took revenge for himself for anything, unless the sanctities of God were violated, in which case he took revenge for God."* (al-Bukhārī 3560; Muslim 2327)

The distinction in the final sentence is the operative one. The Prophet ﷺ did not retaliate for personal insult — ever — but he did not remain silent when a divine sanctity was violated. The two are frequently confused, and the confusion produces either a religion of perpetual offence or a religion of no convictions.

**The Question That Was Refused**

The tragedy of the verse is that it ends in a question that was never answered. Pharaoh did not engage with the offer. The subsequent verses will describe denial, disobedience, turning away, and finally a public claim to divinity. What he never did was consider the question.

That is a familiar human pattern and it is worth naming. When a person is offered something that would require him to change, the most common response is not refusal; it is non-engagement. He changes the subject. He attacks the messenger. He raises an irrelevant objection. He asks for time. The offer is not rejected so much as it is never permitted to become a real option.

The Qur'an records this behaviour in Pharaoh's own words in Sūrah al-Zukhruf:

> *"Then when Our signs came to them with clarity, they said: 'This is manifest sorcery.'"* (Qur'an 43:49, in context)

The classification of the sign as sorcery is the non-engagement in its purest form. It does not examine the evidence; it assigns the evidence to a category that has already been dismissed. And once the category is assigned, no further argument is possible, because the argument has been defined as irrelevant.

This is the mechanism by which most people decline most invitations, in every age. The question of verse 18 — *would you like to be purified?* — is put to every human being at various points by various means, and the ordinary response is not "no." It is a change of subject.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:19

> **and that I guide thee to thy Lord that thou mightest have fear?'**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thy Lord": The Whole Argument in Two Words**

The second half of the invitation contains the entire theological claim in the smallest possible space. *Wa ahdiyaka ilā rabbika* — "and that I guide you to *your* Lord."

The possessive pronoun does the work. Moses does not say "to God" in the abstract; he says "to *your* Lord" — *rabbika*. The statement is a direct, unanswerable correction of Pharaoh's self-understanding. You are not the highest. You have a Lord. The word *rabb* in Arabic denotes the one who nurtures, sustains, educates, and brings a thing to its completion — the owner who provides for what he owns. To tell a man that he has a *rabb* is to tell him that he is provided for, and therefore that he is not self-sufficient, and therefore that he is not divine.

The Qur'an records Pharaoh's reaction to precisely this formulation, and his reaction reveals that he understood it perfectly:

> *"[Pharaoh] said: 'And who is your Lord, O Moses?' He said: 'Our Lord is He Who gave everything its creation, then guided.'"* (Qur'an 20:49–50)

Note that Pharaoh does not deny the existence of a Lord. He asks for identification — which is what a person does when the category is not in question but the referent is. And Moses's answer defines the Lord by two acts: creating and guiding. Both are things Pharaoh does not do.

**Two Kinds of Guidance, and Why Moses Could Offer Only One**

The word *ahdiyaka* — "I will guide you" — requires a distinction that the Islamic tradition drew with great care, because confusing the two kinds of guidance has produced a great deal of error.

The first is *hidāyat al-irshād* — the guidance of showing the way. This is pointing: explaining the road, marking the route, removing confusion. Every prophet has it. Every teacher has it. Moses has it, and he offers it here.

The second is *hidāyat al-tawfīq* — the guidance of enabling the person to actually walk the road. This is not information; it is the inclination of the heart to act on the information. Only God has it. The Qur'an states the distinction with total clarity in a verse addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself about his beloved uncle:

> *"Truly you do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills, and He knows best the guided."* (Qur'an 28:56)

The occasion of this verse was the death of Abū Ṭālib, the Prophet's ﷺ uncle and protector, who declined to profess faith on his deathbed despite the Prophet's ﷺ pleading. The most earnest desire of the most sincere guide could not produce the guidance — because the second kind is not his to give. The Qur'an states the principle again:

> *"Not upon you is their guidance, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 2:272)

This doctrine has two practical consequences that are constantly relevant. For the one who invites: he is responsible for the delivery, not for the outcome. This removes both the arrogance of the successful preacher and the despair of the unsuccessful one. For the one who is invited: the fact that a messenger cannot produce guidance in him does not excuse him, because the information has been delivered and the capacity to act on it is his. The guidance of showing the way is offered to everyone; the guidance of the heart follows the use of what was shown.

**"Fa-Takhshā": Fear That Comes From Knowing**

The verse ends with the purpose of the guidance: *fa-takhshā* — "so that you may have *khushyah*." The word is not the ordinary word for fear, and the difference matters.

*Khawf* is fear of harm — the reaction to danger. Any animal has it. *Khushyah* is fear that contains awe and is produced by knowledge of the object feared. It is the fear a person feels before something vast and good, not merely something dangerous. The Qur'an reserves this word for the fear of the knowledgeable:

> *"…and only those among His servants who have knowledge fear God. Truly God is Almighty, Forgiving."* (Qur'an 35:28)

The sequence in the verse is therefore epistemic before it is emotional. Guidance produces knowledge; knowledge produces *khushyah*. The verse does not say "fear me and then you will learn." It says "learn, and then you will fear." Reverence is the output of understanding, not the precondition of it.

This is a distinctive feature of the Qur'anic understanding of religion and it distinguishes Islam from every system that begins with terror. The fear that the Qur'an cultivates is not the fear of a child before a violent parent; it is the fear of an astronomer before the scale of the cosmos — an awe that increases with knowledge rather than decreasing. The person who knows more about God fears Him more, not less, and this is stated as a diagnostic:

> *"Do you not see that God sends down water from the heaven, and We bring forth with it fruits of varying colours? And among the mountains are tracts, white and red, of varying shades, and [some] intensely black. And among people and beasts and grazing animals are those of varying colours likewise. Only those among His servants who have knowledge fear God."* (Qur'an 35:27–28)

The verse moves from rain to fruit to geology to zoology to human variation — a survey of the natural world — and then concludes with the statement about knowledge and fear. The connection is explicit: the more you know about the creation, the more you fear the Creator.

**The Order of the Two Verses**

Read together, verses 18 and 19 contain a complete programme in three steps, and the order is the point.

First: purification. The person is offered the improvement of himself. Second: guidance to his Lord. He is offered knowledge of the One who made him. Third: *khushyah*. He arrives at reverence.

Notice what is absent. There is no threat, no fire, no punishment, no ultimatum. The threats appear later in the story, and they appear only after the offer has been refused. The invitation itself consists entirely of benefits.

This ordering is not a softening of the message; it is the message's psychology. A person who is frightened into compliance has not changed; he has merely calculated. A person who has been shown that purification is available, that his Lord is knowable, and that knowing Him produces awe — such a person has been offered an actual alternative to his current state. The Qur'an consistently offers the alternative before it announces the consequence, and this verse is the clearest example of the practice.

**What the Verse Assumes About the Human Being**

Underneath the three steps lies an assumption about human nature that the verse takes for granted: that Pharaoh is capable of purification, that he is capable of knowing his Lord, and that he is capable of awe. The invitation would be meaningless if he were not.

This is the Islamic doctrine of *fiṭrah*, stated most directly in a report that has been central to the tradition's anthropology:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian — as an animal gives birth to a whole offspring. Do you perceive anything mutilated in it?"* (al-Bukhārī 1385; Muslim 2658)

The human being is not born corrupt; he is born oriented. The orientation can be buried under habit, culture, interest, and pride, but it is not destroyed — and the invitation works because it addresses something still present. This is why the Qur'an's prophets are always sent *to* their people rather than *at* them, and why the address is always in the language of the people (14:4). There is something in the listener that the message is aimed at.

Pharaoh had it too. That is what makes his story a warning rather than a curiosity. The man who was offered purification, guidance, and awe — in the gentlest possible terms, by a prophet, with a miracle to follow — was not a different species. He was a human being with the same *fiṭrah* as the reader, who made a sequence of choices that are available to anyone.

**The Verse Applied**

The invitation of verses 18 and 19 is repeated, in substance, to every human being who has ever lived, and it is worth hearing it in its own terms rather than in the terms of the story.

*Would you like to be purified?* — not punished, not shamed, not reminded of your faults; offered an improvement.

*And shall I show you your Lord?* — not a doctrine to defend, not a set of obligations to negotiate, but an introduction to the One who is already sustaining you.

*So that you may stand in awe?* — the outcome being not terror but the specific, enlarging reverence that comes from finally knowing what is actually there.

This is the offer. The story's next verses record what happened when it was made to the most powerful man on earth, and the reason the story is told in every age is not that Pharaoh was exceptional. It is that he was not.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:20

> **Then he showed him the greatest sign.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign Comes After the Invitation, Not Before**

The order of these verses is a deliberate theological statement. Verse 18–19 contain the offer: purification, guidance, reverence. Verse 20 contains the evidence: *fa-arāhu al-āyata al-kubrā* — "then he showed him the greatest sign."

The sign follows the invitation. This matters, because the alternative order — overwhelming demonstration first, invitation second — would produce compliance rather than faith. A man who bows before a miracle has not necessarily changed; he has adjusted his behaviour in the presence of superior force. A man who is offered purification and then shown evidence for the offer has been given everything required for a genuine decision.

The Qur'an is explicit that God withholds overwhelming signs precisely for this reason:

> *"And they swear by God with their most solemn oaths that if a sign came to them they would surely believe in it. Say: 'The signs are only with God.' And what will make you realise that when it comes they will not believe? And We turn their hearts and their eyes as they did not believe in it the first time, and We leave them in their transgression, wandering blindly."* (Qur'an 6:109–110)

The verse reveals something that most people assume and the Qur'an denies: that more evidence would produce more belief. The Makkans swore that a sign would convince them. God responds that He knows otherwise — a sign of overwhelming magnitude does not convert the unwilling; it merely eliminates their excuse. The evidence is already sufficient. What is missing is not information.

**What "The Greatest Sign" Was**

The Qur'an states that Moses was given nine clear signs:

> *"And We certainly gave Moses nine clear signs. So ask the Children of Israel — when he came to them, Pharaoh said to him: 'Truly I consider you, O Moses, to be bewitched.'"* (Qur'an 17:101)

The nine are traditionally enumerated as the staff, the white hand, the years of famine, the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood, and the parting of the sea — though the enumerations vary among the commentators, and the Qur'an itself does not list them in one place. What this verse adds is that one of them was *the greatest*, and the exegetes propose several candidates.

The staff is the most commonly cited. Its transformation is described at length:

> *"And what is that in your right hand?" He said: "It is my staff; I lean on it and I bring down leaves with it for my sheep, and I have other uses for it." He said: "Throw it down, O Moses." So he threw it down — and behold, it was a serpent, moving swiftly."* (Qur'an 20:17–20)

> *"So Moses threw his staff, and behold, it was a manifest serpent."* (Qur'an 26:32)

The white hand is the second candidate:

> *"Insert your hand into your bosom — it will come out white, without harm."* (Qur'an 27:12)

> *"And press your hand to your side — it will come out white without evil. That is another sign, that We may show you some of Our greatest signs."* (Qur'an 20:22–23)

Others hold that the greatest sign was the parting of the sea, since it was the sign that ended Pharaoh. And al-Rāzī records the view that the greatest sign was all of them together, since no single one is identified in the text.

The exegetical caution here is instructive. The verse says "the greatest sign" and does not specify; the tradition offers possibilities without pretending to certainty. This is characteristic of the classical method: where the text is general, the commentary remains general.

**Why the Signs Matched the Culture's Claim to Excellence**

There is a pattern in the Qur'anic miracles that the tradition identified early and that makes the choice of the staff intelligible. Each prophet's sign confronts the domain in which his society claims mastery.

Egypt's claim was magic. Its priests were the acknowledged experts of the ancient world in occult art, and Pharaoh's power rested partly on the religious apparatus that produced and certified that expertise. Moses's sign was therefore a staff that outdid every magician in Egypt — in their own field, at their own game, in front of an audience of their own professionals.

The Israelite claim in the time of Jesus was medicine and healing, and his signs were healings. The Arabian claim in the time of Muhammad ﷺ was eloquence — poetry was the highest art form, and the market of ʿUkāẓ was a national institution — and his sign was a text.

The pattern is not arbitrary. A sign delivered in a domain the audience does not respect proves nothing to them; a sign delivered in the domain they consider supreme removes their excuse completely. This is why the magicians' reaction matters so much, and it is recorded in detail:

> *"And the magicians fell down prostrate. They said: 'We believe in the Lord of Hārūn and Moses.'"* (Qur'an 20:70)

> *"And the magicians were thrown down prostrate, saying: 'We believe in the Lord of the worlds, the Lord of Moses and Hārūn.'"* (Qur'an 7:120–122)

The experts — the people best qualified to distinguish a trick from a miracle — prostrated immediately. They did not deliberate. They recognised in the first instant that what they were watching was outside their discipline entirely. This is the most powerful evidence in the story that the sign was sufficient, and it makes Pharaoh's refusal a datum about Pharaoh rather than about the evidence.

**What Pharaoh Did With the Evidence**

The Qur'an records his response in detail, and it is the study of a man protecting his position:

> *"[Pharaoh] said: 'You believed in him before I gave you permission? Truly this is a plot that you have plotted in the city, to drive out its people. But you will know.'"* (Qur'an 7:123)

> *"Truly he is your chief who taught you magic. So I will cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, and I will crucify you on the trunks of palm trees, and you will know which of us is more severe and more lasting in punishment."* (Qur'an 20:71)

Note the argument. He does not address the evidence. He does not claim the magicians were deceived. He offers a conspiracy theory — this was arranged in advance — and then proceeds to threats. The transition from evidence to threat is the point at which a position admits that it has no argument.

The magicians' reply is one of the great sentences in the Qur'an:

> *"They said: 'We will never prefer you over what has come to us of clear signs and over He Who created us. So decree whatever you wish to decree — you can only decree concerning the life of this world.'"* (Qur'an 20:72)

The final clause contains the whole answer: *you can only decree concerning this life*. The magicians had, in a single moment, correctly identified the exact boundary of Pharaoh's power. He could kill them. That was the extent of it. And once the boundary was visible, the threats stopped working.

This is the moment the story turns, and it happens offstage. The verse in al-Nāziʿāt says only *"Then he showed him the greatest sign"* — and the next verse says *"But he denied and disobeyed."* Between the showing and the denial, the magicians had already believed. Pharaoh was the only person in the room who did not.

**The Limits of Evidence, and the Modern Version**

The verse teaches something that the modern world has largely forgotten: evidence does not compel belief, because belief is not produced by evidence alone. It is produced by evidence as received by a will that is willing to be changed by it.

The magicians received the same evidence as Pharaoh. They were better qualified to evaluate it than he was — it was their profession. They believed within seconds. He denied. The difference between them was not access to data.

This is why the Islamic tradition is remarkably untroubled by the absence of coercive proofs. It does not offer a demonstration that forces assent, because it knows that no such thing exists for a person who is unwilling. What it offers is the same thing Moses offered: an invitation, an explanation, and a sign sufficient for the one who is prepared to look.

The practical consequence for a believer in the modern world is a certain freedom. He is not obliged to produce arguments that would compel a hostile interlocutor, because the Qur'an itself denies that such arguments exist for a hostile interlocutor. His obligation is clarity, gentleness, and evidence — and then, as the verse in Sūrah al-Kahf puts it:

> *"And say: 'The truth is from your Lord. So whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve.'"* (Qur'an 18:29)

The sign has been shown. What happens next belongs to the one who is looking at it.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:21

> **But he denied and disobeyed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs, Two Refusals**

The verse consists of two words in Arabic: *fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā* — "so he denied and disobeyed." Each verb names a different kind of refusal, and together they cover the whole of the response.

*Kadhdhaba* is the intensive form of "to lie" or "to declare false" — he called the truth a lie. This is the refusal of belief, the rejection of the proposition. It is an act of the intellect and the tongue, and in the intensive form it carries the sense of doing so emphatically, repeatedly, and publicly.

*ʿAṣā* is from *ʿ-ṣ-y*, to disobey, to rebel against a command. This is the refusal of action, the rejection of the obligation. It is an act of the will.

The pairing is not decorative. Islamic theology has always distinguished between these two, and the distinction has practical consequences. A person may accept a truth and fail to act on it — the ordinary human condition, which the tradition calls sin. And a person may reject the truth itself, which is a different order of failure and one that makes the subsequent sin systemic rather than incidental. Pharaoh did both, and the order in the verse is the order of causation: the denial produced the disobedience.

Both verbs are used without objects. He did not merely deny *something*; he denied. He did not disobey *someone*; he disobeyed. The objects are understood from the context — the sign and the messenger — and the ellipsis generalises the statement. This is not a report about a particular transaction; it is a portrait.

**The Qur'an's Own Commentary on the Second Verb**

The Qur'an uses the same word for Pharaoh's disobedience in another Makkan sūrah, and adds the consequence:

> *"Truly We sent to you a messenger as a witness over you, as We sent to Pharaoh a messenger. But Pharaoh disobeyed the messenger, so We seized him with a severe seizing."* (Qur'an 73:15–16)

The two verses, in Sūrah al-Muzzammil and here in al-Nāziʿāt, are close neighbours in the revelation and close in content. Both describe the sending, the disobedience, and the seizing. The verse in al-Muzzammil supplies the adjective that al-Nāziʿāt leaves to the reader: the seizing was *wazīl* — heavy, crushing, severe.

The word *ʿaṣā* is also used in the Qur'an for the disobedience of communities, and it carries a specific weight:

> *"And they disobeyed His messengers and followed the command of every obstinate tyrant."* (Qur'an 11:59)

> *"So he [Pharaoh] will go before his people on the Day of Resurrection and lead them to the Fire — and wretched is the place to which they are led."* (Qur'an 11:98)

That image — the leader walking ahead of his followers into the Fire, performing at the end exactly the function he performed in life — is the Qur'an's answer to the political theory of the tyrant. He led them; the leading did not stop at the grave.

**Denial Was Not a Conclusion**

The most important thing this verse teaches is the mechanism of the denial, and the Qur'an states it with an explicitness that leaves no room for a charitable reconstruction. Pharaoh's people — and by extension Pharaoh — did not deny for lack of conviction:

> *"And they denied them wrongfully and arrogantly, though their souls were certain of them. So see how was the end of the corrupters."* (Qur'an 27:14)

*Wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum* — "though their souls were certain of them." The denial occurred alongside certainty. The cause was *ẓulm* and *ʿulūw* — wrongdoing and self-elevation.

This is the Qur'an's consistent diagnosis, and it is a claim about human nature that any honest observer can verify. People do not usually reject inconvenient truths because they have examined them and found them wanting. They reject them because the examination would produce a result they cannot afford. The denial is not the output of reasoning; it is the input to it. Reasoning is then recruited, in good faith, to defend a conclusion that was reached before the reasoning began.

Ibn Taymiyyah made this the centre of his account of unbelief. Kufr, in his analysis, is most often not ignorance but *kibr* — the unwillingness to accept a truth that would require the person to be less than he thinks he is. This is why the Qur'an so frequently pairs unbelief with arrogance rather than with confusion, and why the remedy it prescribes begins with the breaking of pride.

**The Contrast With the Magicians**

The verse's placement makes a comparison unavoidable. In the preceding verses, Moses showed the greatest sign. In the fuller accounts, the magicians — the professional experts, the ones whose livelihood depended on distinguishing real magic from false — prostrated within seconds and declared their belief, knowing that the declaration would cost them their hands, their feet, and their lives (20:70–72; 7:120–126).

The same sign produced two opposite results in the same room. This is the single most important datum in the story, and it destroys the assumption that the disbeliever's problem is evidential. If the evidence were insufficient, the magicians would have been deceived too — and they were the least likely people on earth to be deceived, because it was their profession.

The verse therefore describes not a failure of proof but a choice. And it is a choice available to anyone, which is why the story is told.

**The Modern Form of "He Denied and Disobeyed"**

The two verbs describe a condition that is extremely common and almost never diagnosed. A person learns that something he is doing is wrong. He does not dispute it — in fact he agrees, privately. And he continues.

The modern vocabulary for this is weak. We call it "knowing better," and we treat it as a lapse. The Qur'an treats it as a state with a name and a trajectory. The denial is not the initial scepticism; it is the internal narrative constructed to permit the continued disobedience — the reasons found afterwards, the exceptions claimed, the special circumstances invoked. And the disobedience feeds the denial: each repetition of the act makes the narrative more necessary and more elaborated.

The Prophet ﷺ described the terminal point of this process in a report about the heart:

> *"When the servant commits a sin, a black spot is placed on his heart. If he desists, seeks forgiveness and repents, his heart is polished. But if he increases, it increases until it covers his heart."* (al-Tirmidhī 3334; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The covering is not a punishment imposed from outside; it is the sediment of the choices. And the Qur'an names the resulting state:

> *"Then was the end of those who did evil the worst, because they denied the signs of God and used to ridicule them."* (Qur'an 30:10)

The verse's two words — he denied and he disobeyed — are therefore not a historical note. They are a diagnosis available to anyone who has continued doing something he knows he should not, and who has begun, without noticing, to construct reasons.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:22

> **Then turned his back, moving swiftly.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Posture and the Speed**

The verse is two words again, and both are physical. *Thumma adbara yasʿā* — "then he turned his back, striving." The first verb gives the posture; the second gives the energy.

*Adbara* is from *d-b-r*, the back. To turn one's back is, in Arabic as in English, the universal gesture of rejection — not merely withdrawal but withdrawal with the body oriented away. The Qur'an uses the same word for the refusal of the Makkans:

> *"But he denied and turned away, then went to his family, walking proudly."* (Qur'an 75:32–33)

The parallel is exact. In both cases the refusal is followed immediately by a return to the comforts of ordinary life and a display of confidence. The man has decided; now he can go home.

*Yasʿā* is from *s-ʿ-y*, to strive, to move with effort and purpose, to work at something. It is the same root used for the ritual walking between Ṣafā and Marwah — the *saʿy* of the pilgrimage — and for honest labour:

> *"And whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it with its due striving, while he is a believer — those are the ones whose striving is appreciated."* (Qur'an 17:19)

The word therefore carries no negative valence in itself. Striving is praiseworthy. What the verse gives us is a man applying full, genuine, energetic effort — to the wrong thing.

**Two Readings of What He Was Doing**

Al-Rāzī records two interpretations. The first: Pharaoh turned and fled when he saw the sign — the staff becoming a serpent — and the "striving" describes his haste in getting away. The second, and the one most commentators prefer: he turned away from Moses and set immediately to work devising a plan against him. The "striving" is then political effort — the summoning of advisers, the organisation of the counter-demonstration, the marshalling of the magicians.

The second reading fits the wider Qur'anic account better, since Pharaoh clearly did not simply flee; he organised. And it is the reading that makes the verse most instructive, because it describes something far more common than cowardice: the conversion of a man's full capacity into resistance.

**The Tyrant's Energy**

This is the verse's central insight, and it deserves to be stated plainly. Pharaoh was not lazy, not indifferent, and not stupid. He was energetic. The moment he was confronted with a claim that threatened him, he mobilised — he gathered, he planned, he assembled the specialists, he staged a public event. The Qur'an shows him working.

And the verse pairs this energy with the turning of the back. The combination is the diagnosis: the whole of his capacity, applied in the direction away from the truth.

Human beings do this constantly and in far smaller matters. A person confronted with a fact he does not want to accept does not usually go idle; he becomes very busy. He commissions research that will confirm his position. He finds flaws in the source. He occupies his schedule so thoroughly that the question never arises in a quiet moment. The energy is real; the direction is the problem.

The Qur'an describes the same dynamic in a verse about wealth:

> *"Truly man is ungrateful to his Lord, and truly he is a witness to that, and truly he is, in the love of wealth, intense."* (Qur'an 100:6–8)

The word translated "intense" — *la-shadīd* — describes the ferocity of his attachment. And Sūrah al-Layl contrasts two kinds of striving directly:

> *"As for one who gives and fears God, and affirms the best [reward], We will ease him toward ease. And as for one who withholds and considers himself free of need, and denies the best [reward], We will ease him toward hardship."* (Qur'an 92:5–10)

The two men in that passage differ not in the amount of effort but in its orientation. The sūrah you are reading will make the same contrast in verses 37–41, between the one who *rebelled* and the one who *feared and forbade his soul*.

**The Back Turned: A Qur'anic Motif**

The image of turning away recurs throughout the Qur'an, and it is always a moral diagnosis rather than a physical description.

> *"And when it is said to them: 'Come to what God has revealed and to the Messenger,' you see the hypocrites turning away from you in aversion."* (Qur'an 4:61)

> *"Perhaps, if you turned away, you would cause corruption in the land and sever your ties of kinship."* (Qur'an 47:22)

That second verse is remarkable in its implication: the Qur'an suggests that turning away from revelation does not produce a neutral state but an active one — corruption in the land and the breaking of family bonds. The turning of the back is not the absence of a direction; it is a direction, and it has consequences.

Pharaoh's turned back produced exactly this. The verses that follow describe an assembly, a proclamation, and a claim to divinity — and behind all of it, the enslavement of a people and the killing of their sons. The corruption was not incidental to the refusal; it was its output.

**The Busyness That Follows a Refusal**

There is a psychological observation here that the tradition developed carefully and that modern life confirms at every turn. When a person has decided against a truth that would require him to change, the most effective way to maintain the decision is to eliminate the conditions in which the truth could be reconsidered. Those conditions are quiet, unstructured, and unoccupied.

The modern economy is extraordinarily good at producing the opposite. Attention is now a commodity that is harvested continuously; there is no queue, no commute, no waiting room, and no walk that is not filled with input. The person who wishes not to think about something has never had so many tools available to him.

The Islamic tradition understood this mechanism and legislated against it. The five daily prayers are distributed across the day at intervals that cannot be consolidated, precisely so that the day contains fixed points at which activity stops. The fast removes food and drink for a month, removing another continuous stream of comfort. The retreat (*iʿtikāf*) removes the person from the world entirely for ten days. The pilgrimage removes him for weeks. The entire structure is designed to guarantee the existence of intervals in which the turned back might turn around.

The Prophet ﷺ identified the same danger in a sentence that has been quoted by ascetics for fourteen centuries:

> *"Two blessings that many people are deprived of: health and free time."* (al-Bukhārī 6412)

The word translated "deprived of" — *maghbūn fīhimā* — is the language of a bad bargain: people are being cheated in these two, and they do not notice. Free time is the condition in which a human being can be reached; the elimination of free time is therefore the most effective defence against being reached. Pharaoh's *yasʿā* — his swift, purposeful activity immediately after turning his back — is the ancient form of the same defence.

**What the Verse Says About the Direction of Effort**

The verse is, in the end, a warning addressed to energetic people, and that is the group least likely to think it applies to them. The lazy person is not in danger of becoming Pharaoh. The idle person is not in danger of the assembly and the proclamation. The danger belongs to the capable: the man with resources, energy, connections, and the habit of getting things done.

The Qur'an's question to such a person is not "are you working?" but "in which direction?" The verse supplies the two options in a single line — turning the back while striving — and the rest of the sūrah supplies the alternative, which is not idleness but a different orientation:

> *"And whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it with its due striving, while he is a believer — those are the ones whose striving is appreciated."* (Qur'an 17:19)

*Saʿyahā* — the striving for it — is the same root as Pharaoh's *yasʿā*. The word is not the problem. The direction is.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:23

> **Then he gathered and proclaimed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parody of the Gathering**

The word *fa-ḥashara* — "he gathered" — is the same word the Qur'an uses, throughout, for the gathering of humanity on the Last Day. *Al-ḥashr* is the technical term for the assembling of all creation for judgment, and it gives its name to an entire sūrah.

> *"And [mention] the Day when We gather them all together, then We say to those who associated partners: 'Where are your partners whom you used to claim?'"* (Qur'an 6:22)

> *"And the Day when the Trumpet is blown and We gather the criminals that Day, blue-eyed."* (Qur'an 20:102)

> *"And We will gather them and leave out none of them."* (Qur'an 18:47)

The choice of this verb for Pharaoh's assembly is therefore loaded. It presents the tyrant performing, in miniature and in parody, the act that belongs to God alone. He gathers. He convenes. He summons. And the sūrah will arrive, eleven verses later, at the real gathering — *fa-idhā hum bi'l-sāhirah* — where the convener is not a king.

**What the Gathering Was For**

Al-Ṭabarī records the majority reading: Pharaoh gathered the people in order to make a proclamation of his own sovereignty. Others connect the verse to the summoning of the pursuit of the Israelites:

> *"Then Pharaoh sent heralds to the cities: 'Truly these are a small band, and they have certainly enraged us, and we are all vigilant.'"* (Qur'an 26:53–56)

The fuller accounts show both gatherings — the assembly at which the confrontation with the magicians took place, held on a public festival day when the population would be present (20:58–59), and the later mobilisation. In both cases the structure is identical: a crowd is assembled so that something can be declared in front of it.

**Why the Tyrant Needs an Audience**

There is a point here about the nature of political power that the verse makes in two words. Pharaoh did not merely hold power; he announced it. And the announcement required an assembly.

Power that is not acknowledged does not function. A ruler's authority exists in the recognition of the ruled, and recognition must be produced, renewed, and displayed. This is why every tyranny in history has invested so heavily in spectacle — the procession, the anniversary, the rally, the broadcast — and why the spectacle is not a waste of resources from the tyrant's point of view but the actual maintenance of his position.

The Qur'an shows Pharaoh addressing his court in exactly these terms:

> *"And Pharaoh proclaimed among his people: 'O my people, is not the dominion of Egypt mine, and these rivers flowing beneath me? Do you not see? Or am I not better than this one who is contemptible and can scarcely speak?'"* (Qur'an 43:51–52)

The argument is entirely comparative and entirely public. It consists of what he has, what flows beneath him, and how he compares to his rival — and it ends with the demand that constitutes the whole of it: *do you not see?* The tyrant's claim requires witnesses. It cannot be made alone in a room.

**The Proclamation That Follows**

The verse ends on the verb of proclamation, *fa-nādā*, and the next verse supplies the content: *"I am your lord most high."* The pairing of gathering and proclamation is the structure of every assertion of absolute authority, and it is worth noting what it consists of.

First: assemble the population. Second: address them. Third: state a claim about yourself in the second person — *your* lord. Not "I am great." Not "I am divine." But "I am *your* lord" — a claim of jurisdiction over specific people, expressed as a relationship.

This is the anatomy of tyranny, and it is more precise than the usual description. The tyrant does not primarily want to be worshipped in the abstract. He wants to be obeyed by you. The claim is not metaphysical; it is jurisdictional. It is a claim about who has the final say in your life.

**The Modern Assembly**

The verse's structure has acquired a technology that Pharaoh could not have imagined, and the modern reader should not assume that the phenomenon belongs to antiquity. The gathering has been industrialised and the proclamation has been democratised.

Every platform is a *ḥashr* — an assembly — and every post is a *nidāʾ*, a call. The difference from Pharaoh's assembly is one of scale and access, not of structure. A person with a following assembles an audience and addresses it with a statement about himself, and the statement is measured by the response it produces. The mechanic is identical: power asserted in front of witnesses, renewed daily, decaying without repetition.

The Islamic tradition's attitude toward this mechanic is worth recovering, because it is the exact inverse. The Prophet ﷺ was offered kingship, wealth, and the position of supreme arbiter in Makkah, in the negotiation with ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah preserved in the early sources, and he declined all of it. His authority was built on the opposite foundation — not on being proclaimed but on being believed, and it required no assembly because it did not depend on recognition.

He also warned specifically against the desire for the proclamation:

> *"Whoever wears the garment of fame, God will clothe him in a garment of humiliation on the Day of Resurrection."* (Abū Dāwūd 4029; Ibn Mājah 3607)

And:

> *"You will see the people driven to the gathering; the worst of them are those who are double-faced — those who come to these with one face and to those with another."* (al-Bukhārī 7179; Muslim 2526)

The concern in both reports is with the person whose self-presentation is engineered for an audience. The verse in al-Nāziʿāt shows where that engineering ends: in an assembly convened by the presenter himself, at which he announces that he is the highest authority present.

**The Two Gatherings**

The verse's placement within the sūrah makes it a hinge. Behind it lies the small gathering in Egypt, at which one man proclaimed himself the highest lord. Ahead of it, in verse 14, lies the great gathering on the plain without landmarks, at which every proclamation ever made will be irrelevant.

The Qur'an intends the reader to hold both at once. Every assembly convened by human authority — the court, the parliament, the boardroom, the platform, the rally — is a rehearsal of nothing. It produces no record that survives it. The only gathering at which what was said matters is the one that no human being convenes, on a surface with no markers, where the only thing a person has brought is what he did.

The verse is not primarily about Pharaoh. It is about the assembly, and every reader has attended several.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:24

> **and said, "I am your lord most high."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Named a Category**

Four Arabic words — *anā rabbukumu al-aʿlā* — and they have done more work in Islamic political and theological vocabulary than almost any other phrase in the Qur'an. "I am your lord, the most high." The sentence became the paradigm of tyranny, the shorthand by which the tradition identifies any claim of ultimate authority by a human being. When later Muslim writers wanted to condemn a ruler who had made himself unaccountable, they did not need to describe him; they needed only to quote this verse.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī record the sequence of Pharaoh's claims, and the sequence matters. According to the reports, he began with the more limited statement:

> *"And Pharaoh said: 'O notables, I know of no god for you other than myself.'"* (Qur'an 28:38)

And only later arrived at the sentence in this verse. The progression is from the denial of rivals to the assertion of supremacy — from *there is no other* to *I am the highest*. The commentators note that this is how such claims develop: the elimination of competitors is the first stage, and the declaration of absolute rank is the second.

**"Rabbuka al-Aʿlā": A Divine Formula, Stolen**

There is a linguistic fact here that is easy to miss and that makes the claim far more specific than it appears. The phrase *rabbuka al-aʿlā* — "your Lord, the Most High" — is a formula the Qur'an itself uses of God:

> *"Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High."* (Qur'an 87:1)

The entire sūrah that follows al-Nāziʿāt in the muṣḥaf — Sūrah al-Aʿlā, "The Most High" — takes its title from this phrase. It is a divine designation, and Pharaoh's sentence is not a vague boast; it is the appropriation of a specific Name.

The Qur'an elsewhere uses the same pairing in a context of explicit contrast:

> *"So exalted is God, the True King. There is no god except Him, Lord of the Noble Throne."* (Qur'an 23:116)

> *"And say: 'Praise be to God, Who has taken no son, and has no partner in dominion, and has no [need of a] protector out of weakness' — and magnify Him with [great] magnification."* (Qur'an 17:111)

The word *al-aʿlā* — the Most High — is one of the designations the Qur'an reserves for God, and it appears in a sūrah named after it. Pharaoh's claim is therefore not "I am powerful" or "I am great." It is a direct usurpation of a divine title, made in public, to a subject population.

**The Claim Is About You, Not About Him**

The most important word in the sentence is the possessive pronoun. He did not say "I am the most high." He said "I am *your* lord most high."

The distinction is the whole nature of the crime. A man who says "I am the greatest being in existence" has made a metaphysical error. A man who says "I am *your* lord" has made a jurisdictional claim — a claim about who holds final authority over specific human beings. Tyranny is not a delusion about the universe; it is an assertion about other people's lives.

This is why the Qur'an's response to Pharaoh is not a cosmological argument. It is the declaration that there is no god but God, and that declaration is aimed precisely at the possessive: *your* Lord is not Pharaoh, whoever Pharaoh says he is.

The two testimonies of Islam are structured as a direct answer to this sentence. *Lā ilāha illā Allāh* — "there is no god but God" — negates every claim of the kind Pharaoh made. *Muḥammadun rasūl Allāh* — "Muhammad is the Messenger of God" — establishes that the content of the religion comes from outside the ruler and is not his to define. Ibn Taymiyyah analysed the two testimonies in exactly these terms: the first denies the legitimacy of any competing ultimate; the second denies the ruler's authority over what is true.

**The Mechanism, Restated**

The verse completes a causal chain that the Qur'an began in the earliest revelation:

> *"No — truly man transgresses, because he considers himself self-sufficient."* (Qur'an 96:6–7)

Self-sufficiency produces transgression; transgression, unchecked, produces the claim. Pharaoh's sentence is not the beginning of his condition but its endpoint. It was preceded by decades of unchallenged power, unlimited wealth, a priesthood that certified his divinity, and a population that had never seen him contradicted.

The Prophet ﷺ described the divine perspective on such a claim in a report that has become the standard reference for the theology of pride:

> *"God says: 'Pride is My cloak and greatness is My garment. Whoever competes with Me in either of them, I will punish him.'"* (Muslim 2620)

The imagery is precise: a cloak and a garment are what a person wears — the visible manifestation of who he is. Pride and greatness are, as it were, God's clothing, and to wear them is to impersonate. The report does not describe the punishment as an external penalty; the impersonation *is* the offence, and the punishment follows from the nature of the act.

**Namrūd, the Quraysh, and the Universality of the Sentence**

Pharaoh is not the only person in the Qur'an to make a claim of this kind, and the presence of others establishes that the sentence is a category rather than an individual quirk.

Abraham confronted a king — identified in the tradition as Namrūd — who made a functional version of the same claim:

> *"Have you not considered the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord, because God had given him kingship? Abraham said: 'My Lord gives life and causes death.' He said: 'I give life and cause death.'"* (Qur'an 2:258)

The claim here is not to divinity in the metaphysical sense but to the power of life and death over subjects — which, practically, is the same claim. And the Quraysh, addressing the Prophet ﷺ, made the operational version: they claimed the right to determine what was true in religious matters because they held the sanctuary and the hereditary offices.

The Qur'an generalises the diagnosis:

> *"And among people is he who disputes about God without knowledge and follows every rebellious devil."* (Qur'an 22:3)

> *"Then We made you a successor in the land after them, that We may see how you will act."* (Qur'an 10:14)

The last of these is the verse that turns the story into a mirror. Every generation is a successor, and the test is the same.

**The Sufi Reading, and What It Turns On**

The classical commentary preserves an interpretation that deserves mention because it draws a distinction of real subtlety. Al-Qurṭubī and others record that ʿAlāʾ al-Dawlā al-Simnānī (d. 736/1336) reported seeing the soul of the Sufi martyr al-Ḥallāj in an exalted state, and he prayed to God asking how this could be — al-Ḥallāj had said "I am the Real" (*anā al-Ḥaqq*), and Pharaoh had said "I am your lord most high," yet one was in the highest Paradise and the other in Hell. The answer given, as the report has it, was that Pharaoh saw only himself and forgot God, while al-Ḥallāj saw only God and forgot himself.

The distinction the interpretation rests upon is genuine and it is doctrinally important, whatever one makes of the report's transmission. The formal similarity of the two sentences conceals opposite contents. Pharaoh's sentence is an assertion of self: *I am the highest over you.* The words attributed to al-Ḥallāj, in the ecstatic register in which the Sufis used such language, were understood by his defenders as an effacement — a statement that nothing remains in the speaker but the Real, not that the speaker is the Real.

The tradition did not treat the question lightly. Al-Ḥallāj was executed in 309/922, and the jurists who condemned him did so on the grounds that such statements, whatever the speaker's state, are impermissible in public because they cannot be distinguished by the hearer from the claim they resemble. Ibn Taymiyyah discussed the case at length and concluded that the speaker's inward state is known to God, but that the utterance in the public sphere remains subject to judgment on its apparent meaning. The two positions — that the state excuses the speaker before God, and that the apparent meaning governs in this world — are not contradictory, and holding both is characteristic of the tradition's method.

**The Secular Form of the Sentence**

A modern reader may be tempted to think that no one says "I am your lord most high" any more. The observation is true and irrelevant, because the sentence has a functional equivalent that is spoken constantly.

The functional equivalent is the claim of final authority over one's own moral universe: that there is no standard outside the self to which the self is answerable, that no command binds except the one the individual issues to himself, and that any external claim to authority is an infringement. This is not a philosophical position held by academics; it is the unexamined default of contemporary culture, taught from childhood and enforced by the entire structure of consumer life, in which the individual's preference is the only acknowledged source of obligation.

The Qur'an identifies this position precisely and gives it a name:

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god? God knew, so He sealed his hearing and his heart and placed a covering over his sight. So who will guide him after God?"* (Qur'an 45:23)

*Takes his caprice as his god* — *ittakhadha ilāhahu hawāhu*. The phrase is not metaphorical decoration. It describes the same structure as Pharaoh's sentence, relocated from the palace to the individual: the highest authority in the person's universe is the person's own desire.

Pharaoh required an assembly to make his proclamation. The modern version requires none, which is why it is more widespread and less visible. But it is the same claim, and this verse is addressed to it.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:25

> **So God seized him with a punishment exemplary in the Hereafter and in this world.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Akhadhahu": The Seizing**

The verb is *akhadha* — to take, to seize, to grasp. It is an ordinary word, and the Qur'an uses it for God's punishment with a consistency that makes it a technical term:

> *"Truly the seizing of your Lord is severe."* (Qur'an 85:12)

> *"And thus is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are wronging. Truly His seizing is painful, severe."* (Qur'an 11:102)

> *"And We seized them with the punishment, that perhaps they would return."* (Qur'an 43:48)

The word's force lies in its simplicity. It does not describe a process, a negotiation, or an appeal. It describes a hand closing. The Qur'an's theology of punishment is that it is not a system of graduated sanctions administered by a bureaucracy; it is an act, and when it occurs, it is complete.

Notice also what the verse does not say. It does not say that Pharaoh was defeated in battle, or overthrown by rivals, or killed by an assassin. It says God seized him. The Qur'an consistently attributes the destruction of the tyrants directly to divine action, bypassing the historical mechanics — not because the mechanics did not operate, but because the verse is stating what the mechanics were an instrument of.

**"Nakāl": The Punishment That Teaches**

The word *nakālan* is the key term of the verse, and it is untranslatable in a single English word. It is built on the root *n-k-l*, which means to refrain or to prevent, and it gives Arabic the word for a shackle — *nikl* — the thing that restrains. A *nakāl* is therefore a punishment whose function is restraint: a penalty so exemplary that it deters others.

The verse is saying that Pharaoh's destruction was designed. It was not merely the removal of a problem; it was the production of a lesson. The punishment had an audience, and the audience was everyone after him.

The Qur'an states this purpose explicitly at the moment of Pharaoh's death:

> *"So today We will save you in your body, that you may be a sign for those after you. And truly many among mankind are heedless of Our signs."* (Qur'an 10:92)

The phrase *fa'l-yawma nunajjīka bi-badanika* — "today We will save you in your body" — has been read by the commentators as referring to the preservation of Pharaoh's corpse, which was cast up on the shore rather than lost at sea. The stated purpose is not honour but instruction: the body is kept as a sign. The final clause is the sting — *and truly many among mankind are heedless of Our signs* — which is a direct address to the reader.

**Two Readings of "The Last and the First"**

The phrase *nakāla al-ākhirati wa'l-ūlā* — "the exemplary punishment of the last and the first" — has two principal readings in the classical commentary.

The first, and the most common: the punishment of the Hereafter and of this world. Pharaoh was drowned in the sea and he is punished in the Fire; he received both. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī record this reading, connecting it to the plagues sent upon his people and the drowning (7:135–137; 20:78–79).

The second, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī and others: "the punishment of the last [utterance] and the first [utterance]" — that is, the punishment for the final claim, *"I am your lord most high,"* and for the earlier claim, *"I know of no god for you other than myself"* (28:38). On this reading the verse is a precise accounting: each of the two blasphemous statements received its corresponding penalty, and nothing was overlooked.

Some commentators combine the readings: "the first" is the punishment for denying Moses, and "the last" for the claim to lordship. The multiplicity is characteristic of the exegetical method — where the Arabic admits several senses and none contradicts the others, the tradition preserves all of them.

**The Sequence of the Destruction**

The Qur'an narrates the end in several places, and the sequence is worth setting out because it answers the objection that Pharaoh repented.

The plagues came first, each announced and each followed by a promise that was broken:

> *"So We sent upon them the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, and the blood — distinct signs — but they were arrogant and were a criminal people. And when the punishment fell upon them, they said: 'O Moses, pray for us to your Lord by the covenant He has made with you. If you remove the punishment from us, we will surely believe you and send the Children of Israel with you.' But when We removed the punishment from them for a term they were to reach, behold, they broke their promise."* (Qur'an 7:133–135)

The pursuit followed, and then the sea:

> *"So We inspired Moses: 'Strike the sea with your staff.' And it parted, and each part was like a great mountain. And We brought the others near. And We saved Moses and those with him, all of them. Then We drowned the others."* (Qur'an 26:63–66)

And the moment of death, with its rejected profession:

> *"And We brought the Children of Israel across the sea, and Pharaoh and his hosts pursued them in tyranny and enmity — until, when the drowning overtook him, he said: 'I believe that there is no god except the One in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am of those who submit.'"* (Qur'an 10:90)

The reply is one of the most severe in the Qur'an:

> *"Now? And you had disobeyed before, and were of the corrupters?"* (Qur'an 10:91)

The commentators explain the principle with care. The profession of faith at the moment of certain death, when the unseen has become visible, is not faith — it is recognition. Faith requires the possibility of disbelief; it is a choice made while the outcome is uncertain. A man drowning who says "I believe" is not making a choice; he is reporting an observation. This is why the Qur'an states as a general rule:

> *"But the acceptance of repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says: 'Now I repent.'"* (Qur'an 4:18)

**Why the Punishment Was Delayed**

There is a question the verse raises implicitly: Pharaoh made his claims over a long period, and the seizure came at the end. Why the interval?

The Qur'an answers this in a verse that has been quoted by preachers for fourteen centuries:

> *"And let not those who disbelieve think that Our respite to them is good for themselves. We respite them only that they may increase in sin, and for them is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

And:

> *"And how many a town did We respite while it was wronging, then We seized it — and to Me is the destination."* (Qur'an 22:44)

The respite is not an acquittal; it is an extension of the opportunity, and the extension is itself part of the accounting. The tyrant who is given forty years has been given forty years of chances — the magicians' conversion, the plagues, the repeated offers, the warnings of Moses — and each one refused adds to the final reckoning rather than subtracting from it.

This doctrine has a practical function that is easy to miss. The apparent immunity of the powerful is one of the oldest reasons for the loss of faith: the wicked prosper, the righteous suffer, and no correction arrives. The Qur'an does not dispute the observation. It disputes the inference — that the absence of immediate punishment means the absence of judgment.

**The Lesson Function of the Punishment**

Because the punishment was *nakāl* — exemplary, deterrent — its primary beneficiary is not Pharaoh's contemporaries but every subsequent reader. The verse is addressed to an audience that will never meet Pharaoh, and it asks that audience to take something from the story.

That is the subject of the next verse, and the connection is direct: the punishment was designed as a lesson, and the following verse identifies who is capable of receiving it.

There is also a historical dimension that the tradition noted. Pharaoh's name became, in Islamic usage, a common noun. Muslim writers did not need to explain a reference to "the Pharaoh"; the word had entered the language as the name of a category. This is the *nakāl* operating exactly as the word describes — the punishment continuing to restrain, through the persistence of the story, long after the punished man is dust.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:26

> **Truly in that is a lesson for whosoever fears.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"ʿIbrah": The Thing That Carries You Across**

The word for "lesson" here is *ʿibrah*, from the root *ʿ-b-r*, to cross over. The same root gives Arabic the word for a ford — *maʿbar*, the place where a river is crossed — and the word for the interpretation of a dream, *taʿbīr*, which is the crossing from the image to its meaning.

An *ʿibrah* is therefore not a moral appended to a story. It is the vehicle by which the reader crosses from the particular to the general — from "this happened to a king in Egypt three thousand years ago" to "this is what happens to the human soul under these conditions, including mine." The word contains the whole method of Qur'anic narrative in a single noun.

The Qur'an uses the same word of the prophetic stories generally:

> *"There was certainly in their stories a lesson for those of understanding."* (Qur'an 12:111)

> *"He causes the night to pass into the day and the day into the night… Truly in that is a lesson for those of sight."* (Qur'an 24:44)

> *"There is certainly for you in them [the grazing animals] a lesson."* (Qur'an 16:66)

And it issues the command directly:

> *"So take a lesson, O you of insight."* (Qur'an 59:2)

**Why the Story of Moses Is Called a Lesson Here**

The verse closes the Moses section of the sūrah, and its placement is significant. Eleven verses have narrated the confrontation — the call, the invitation, the sign, the denial, the turning away, the assembly, the proclamation, the seizure. The verse then tells the reader what all of that was for.

It was not a history lesson. It was not a demonstration of God's power in the abstract. It was an *ʿibrah* — a crossing point, a thing designed to move the reader from where he is to somewhere else.

The Qur'an states this function of narrative explicitly and denies the alternative:

> *"We relate to you the best of narratives in what We have revealed to you of this Qur'an, though before it you were among the unaware."* (Qur'an 12:3)

> *"And all that We relate to you of the news of the messengers is that by which We make firm your heart."* (Qur'an 11:120)

The stories are instrumentation. They are designed to produce a specific effect in a specific reader, and the effect is not information.

**"For Whosoever Fears": The Condition of Reception**

The most important phrase in the verse is the last one: *li-man yakhshā* — "for the one who fears." The lesson is not available to everyone. It has a precondition, and the precondition is named.

This is a striking claim about knowledge, and it is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an with equal directness:

> *"…and only those among His servants who have knowledge fear God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

> *"And remind — truly the reminder benefits the believers."* (Qur'an 51:55)

> *"Truly in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or listens attentively while he is present [in mind]."* (Qur'an 50:37)

The last of these specifies the condition with unusual precision: a heart, and attention while present. Not intelligence, not education, not access to information. A heart that is functioning, and attention that is actually there.

Why is fear the condition? Because the lesson of Pharaoh's story is a lesson about pride, and the proud person cannot receive it. To learn from Pharaoh, a person must be capable of seeing himself in Pharaoh — must be able to recognise, in his own smaller register, the same impulse to be unaccountable, the same irritation at correction, the same preference for his own judgment over a command that costs him something. The person who cannot imagine himself in that position will hear the story as a report about someone else's bad character, and he will learn nothing.

*Khushyah* — the reverential fear produced by knowledge — is precisely the capacity to see oneself inside a moral category rather than outside it. It is the opposite of the self-exemption that made Pharaoh what he was. The verse is therefore saying that the antidote to the disease described in verses 17–25 is the same quality whose absence caused the disease.

**The Blindness That Prevents the Lesson**

The Qur'an describes the failure to take the lesson as a specific kind of blindness, and locates it not in the eyes:

> *"Have they not travelled through the land, so that they might have hearts by which to understand and ears by which to hear? Truly it is not the eyes that are blinded, but the hearts within the breasts are blinded."* (Qur'an 22:46)

The verse describes travel — the actual visiting of the ruins of destroyed peoples, which the Arabs did constantly on their caravan routes past Thamūd and Madyan — and says that travel without a functioning heart produces nothing. The evidence is available; the organ for processing it is not.

This is why the Qur'an so often prefaces its historical references with the question "have they not travelled?" (22:46; 30:9; 35:44; 40:21; 47:10). The invitation is not to archaeology. It is to the observation that the ruins are there, that the people who built them were not weaker than the present inhabitants, and that the conclusion follows.

**The Modern Condition of the Lesson**

There is a specific modern difficulty with this verse that deserves to be named. The contemporary world has an unprecedented supply of *information* about history and an unprecedented scarcity of *ʿibrah*. The ruins have been photographed, catalogued, and made available to anyone with a connection; the tyrannies of the last century have been documented in exhaustive detail; and the lessons drawn from them are almost always drawn in a way that excludes the reader.

The mechanism is subtle. When a person learns about a historical atrocity, the ordinary response is to identify with its victims — to feel horror at the perpetrators and solidarity with the sufferers. This response feels morally serious and it produces no change, because the reader has placed himself in the category that requires no correction. The *ʿibrah* requires the opposite placement: the recognition that under the right conditions, with the right amount of power and the right absence of accountability, he would have been in the other category.

This is precisely what *khushyah* makes possible and what its absence prevents. The person who fears God knows that he is capable of anything, because he knows that his goodness is not a permanent possession but a state maintained by grace and by choice. The person who does not fear believes his own virtue is a settled fact, and he therefore cannot learn from the fall of anyone.

The verse's final phrase is thus not a restriction but a diagnosis. It is not saying that the lesson is reserved for an elite. It is saying that the lesson requires a specific inner condition, and that the condition is available — and is in fact the only thing standing between the reader and the lesson.

**The Transition the Verse Makes**

The verse also performs a structural function within the sūrah. It closes the historical argument and opens the cosmological one. Having established that the destruction of the arrogant is a matter of recorded fact, the sūrah turns, in the very next verse, to a different kind of evidence:

> *"Are you more difficult to create, or the sky that He has built?"* (Qur'an 79:27)

The movement is from history to cosmology, and both are directed at the same question. History answers the objection "would God really intervene?" — He has, repeatedly, and the record is public. Cosmology answers the objection "could God really resurrect the dead?" — He built something much larger, and you can see it.

The *ʿibrah* of verse 26 is the bridge between them. It is the reader's own movement from the story of a dead king to the question of his own resurrection, and the sūrah will not let him make that movement passively. The next verse is a question addressed to him directly.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:27

> **Are you more difficult to create or the sky that He has built?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument From the Greater to the Lesser**

The sūrah changes register here. It has just finished with history — the story of a tyrant who was destroyed — and it now turns to a question, addressed directly to the reader, about the physical universe. *A-antum ashaddu khalqan am al-samāʾ banāhā* — "Are you more difficult to create, or the sky that He built?"

The argument is a formal inference, and it is one of the most common logical forms in the Qur'an. The commentators call it *qiyās al-awlā*, the argument from the greater to the lesser: if the greater is established, the lesser follows necessarily. Ibn Taymiyyah discussed this form at length and identified it as the Qur'an's preferred mode of reasoning, precisely because it does not require the interlocutor to accept any contested premise — only to accept what he already accepts and draw the obvious consequence.

The structure here is exact:

1. You accept that God created the heavens. (The pagan Arabs accepted this; the Qur'an records their acceptance repeatedly.)
2. The heavens are greater than you.
3. Therefore, your creation — and by extension your re-creation — is not a difficulty for Him.

The Qur'an states the same argument explicitly in three other places:

> *"Surely the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind — but most of mankind know not."* (Qur'an 40:57)

> *"Is not He Who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? Yes — and He is the Creator, the All-Knowing."* (Qur'an 36:81)

> *"Do they not see that God, Who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation, is able to give life to the dead? Yes — He is over all things Powerful."* (Qur'an 46:33)

The first of these ends with a diagnosis: *but most of mankind know not*. The failure is not a failure of the argument; it is a failure to perform it.

**"Banāhā": The Sky as a Building**

The word used for the sky's creation is *banāhā* — "He built it" — and the choice is deliberate. The Qur'an consistently describes the heavens in architectural terms, and the imagery is developed across several sūrahs:

> *"Have you not considered how God created seven heavens in layers, and made the moon therein a light and made the sun a lamp?"* (Qur'an 71:15–16)

> *"And We built above you seven strong [heavens], and made a burning lamp."* (Qur'an 78:12–13)

> *"And We made the heaven a protected ceiling, and they are from its signs turning away."* (Qur'an 21:32)

> *"It is God Who raised the heavens without pillars that you see."* (Qur'an 13:2)

The architectural vocabulary — building, layers, ceiling, structure, raising without supports — is not poetic decoration. It carries a specific claim: the sky is not a spontaneous phenomenon but a construction, and construction implies a builder, a plan, and a purpose. The Qur'an uses the same word for God's making of the human being:

> *"…and [the creation of] your Lord, the Most High, Who created and fashioned…"* (Qur'an 87:1–2)

**What "More Difficult to Create" Actually Claims**

The phrase *ashaddu khalqan* — "harder in creation," "more intense in creation" — is the pivot, and it is worth noticing what it does *not* claim. It does not claim that the human being is insignificant. It does not claim that human creation is trivial. It claims only that in the comparison of difficulty, the heavens are greater.

This is a rhetorical move of some sophistication, because it concedes what the objector would want conceded — that a human being is a complex, remarkable thing — while establishing that the concession does not help him. Yes, you are marvellous. The sky is more marvellous. He made the sky.

The Qur'an uses the same comparative elsewhere and in each case it produces the same conclusion:

> *"…or a creation greater than that in your breasts."* (Qur'an 17:51)

> *"Are we more difficult to create, or those We created before?"* (Qur'an 37:11)

The second of these is addressed to the generation that had already seen the process work. The first is an open invitation to escalate the comparison as far as the listener wishes.

**The Modern Reader and the Scale of the Comparison**

This verse was addressed to people whose knowledge of the heavens consisted of what could be seen with the naked eye: a dome, some lights, a daily rotation. The comparison was already decisive for them. For a modern reader the comparison has become almost incomprehensible in its magnitude.

The observable universe contains, by current estimates, on the order of a hundred billion galaxies, each containing on the order of a hundred billion stars, and it has been expanding for something under fourteen billion years. The distances involved are such that light — the fastest thing known to exist — requires years to cross the gap to the nearest star and hundreds of thousands of years to cross our own galaxy.

The Qur'an does not state these figures, and it would be a mistake to claim that it does. But the argument it makes is not damaged by them; it is amplified. The question *"are you more difficult to create, or the sky?"* was decisive when the sky meant a dome with visible lights. It is decisive beyond reckoning now.

And there is a further observation that the verse makes available. The human being who finds his own resurrection difficult to imagine is a creature of roughly seventy kilograms standing on a planet of roughly six thousand trillion trillion kilograms, orbiting a star that will outlast him by billions of years, in a galaxy containing a hundred billion such stars, in a universe containing a hundred billion such galaxies. The disproportion is not a theological argument invented by the Qur'an; it is an observation about the reader's own situation, which the Qur'an invites him to make.

**Why the Objector Cannot See It**

The verse ends with a comparison, and the Qur'an elsewhere explains why the comparison fails to persuade:

> *"Surely the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind — but most of mankind know not."* (Qur'an 40:57)

The diagnosis is ignorance, but not the ignorance of facts. It is the ignorance produced by proximity. A person is surrounded by his own concerns — his body, his income, his relationships, his plans — and these occupy the whole of his attention. The sky is above him constantly and he never looks at it. The argument is available at any moment of any day, in any location on earth, at no cost, and almost no one performs it.

This is why the Qur'an so often phrases its invitation as an instruction to look:

> *"Do they not look at the camels — how they are created? And at the sky — how it is raised? And at the mountains — how they are set up? And at the earth — how it is spread out?"* (Qur'an 88:17–20)

Four objects, all of them available to a seventh-century Arab within a hundred metres of where he is standing. The verse is not asking for research. It is asking for attention. And the reason attention is required is that the evidence is so familiar that it has become invisible.

The Prophet ﷺ described the state of the person who does not look:

> *"Wretched is the servant of the dinar, wretched is the servant of the dirham, wretched is the servant of the fine garment — if he is given he is pleased, and if he is not given he is displeased."* (al-Bukhārī 2887)

The diagnosis is not that such a person is wicked; it is that he is *rushed* — a servant of a currency and a garment. A person in that condition will not perform the comparison in verse 27, not because he is incapable of it but because his attention has been sold.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:28

> **He raised high its canopy and fashioned it,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of Construction**

The verse describes the sky's making in two operations. *Rafaʿa samkahā* — "He raised its height." *Fa-sawwāhā* — "and He fashioned it."

*Samk* is the vertical dimension — the height, the elevation, the upward extent of a structure. The Qur'an uses the same word in Sūrah al-Shams of the heaven's construction:

> *"And the sky and He Who built it."* (Qur'an 91:5)

and the notion of raising appears throughout:

> *"It is God Who raised the heavens without pillars that you see."* (Qur'an 13:2)

The emphasis on raising is significant. The sky's defining feature, from the human standpoint, is its height — the fact that it is above, that it encloses, that it does not come down. The verse names that feature and attributes it to an act.

*Sawwāhā* is from *s-w-y*, a root denoting making a thing level, proportioned, complete, without defect. It is the word used for God's making of the human being:

> *"O man, what has deceived you concerning your Lord, the Generous, Who created you and fashioned you and balanced you?"* (Qur'an 82:6–7)

> *"And [by] the soul and He Who fashioned it."* (Qur'an 91:7)

Applied to the sky, it means that the structure is not merely tall but proportioned — internally consistent, correctly arranged, without irregularity.

**The Invitation to Falsify**

The Qur'an does not merely assert that the heaven is without flaw. It issues an open challenge to find one, and the challenge is repeated:

> *"[He] Who created seven heavens in layers. You see no flaw in the creation of the Compassionate. So return your gaze — do you see any fissure? Then return your gaze again and again — your gaze will return to you humbled and exhausted."* (Qur'an 67:3–4)

This is a remarkable epistemological posture. Most religious texts make claims that are not testable. This one specifies the test — look for a defect — and predicts the outcome of the test. The invitation is not restricted to believers, to the learned, or to any particular era. *Return your gaze again and again.*

The tradition's understanding of the challenge has been vindicated in an interesting way. Fourteen centuries of increasingly sophisticated observation — the naked eye, the telescope, the photographic plate, the radio array, the space observatory — have produced an enormous body of knowledge about the structure of the heavens, and have not produced a single instance of the kind of defect the verse denies. What observation has produced is a picture of extraordinary regularity: the same physical constants operating at every scale, the same elements in every star, the same laws at every distance. The *sawwāhā* — the fashioning, the proportioning — is precisely what the observations show.

It should be said plainly that the Qur'an is not a scientific text and does not claim to be, and Ibn Taymiyyah warned against the exegetical habit of tying Qur'anic statements to the current scientific consensus, on the ground that the consensus changes and the text does not. The point here is narrower and safer: the text invites inspection, and inspection has not embarrassed it.

**"Sawwā": The Same Word for the Human Being**

There is a detail in the verse that carries the sūrah's argument forward. The verb used for the fashioning of the sky — *sawwāhā* — is the same verb used, elsewhere in the Qur'an, for the fashioning of the human being (82:7; 87:2; 91:7). The two are described with the same word.

This is not a coincidence of vocabulary; it is the argument of verse 27 restated in verse 28. The One who fashioned the sky fashioned you, by the same act of fashioning, with the same precision. The comparison is embedded in the grammar.

And the Qur'an draws the conclusion in a verse that is often quoted and rarely followed to its end:

> *"And in yourselves — do you not see?"* (Qur'an 51:21)

The two loci of evidence — the sky and the self — are named together, and the question is left hanging. The person who examines either finds the same thing: a structure of such intricacy and regularity that the alternatives to design become progressively harder to state.

**The Protected Ceiling**

The description of the sky as a raised canopy is completed elsewhere in the Qur'an with a statement about its function:

> *"And We made the heaven a protected ceiling, and they are from its signs turning away."* (Qur'an 21:32)

The phrase *saqfan maḥfūẓā* — a protected, guarded ceiling — describes a structure that keeps something out. The Qur'an describes the protection elsewhere:

> *"And We placed therein firmly set mountains and blessed it, and We made therein [grow] every balanced thing."* (Qur'an 50:7, of the earth)

> *"And We have certainly adorned the nearest heaven with lamps and made them [as] missiles for the devils."* (Qur'an 67:5)

The first of these functions — the keeping out — is one that the modern reader can verify independently. The earth is surrounded by structures that intercept and deflect radiation and debris which would otherwise make life impossible: the atmosphere, which burns up incoming bodies; the magnetic field, which deflects charged particles; the ozone layer, which absorbs a specific band of radiation. The Qur'an's description of a "protected ceiling" is not a scientific treatise, but the correspondence between the description and the observed arrangement is not the kind that embarrasses the reader.

**Why the Sūrah Describes the Sky Here**

The structural purpose of the verse is clear. Verse 27 asks the comparison; verse 28 begins to justify it by showing the reader what the sky actually is. It is not a vague expanse; it is a raised, fashioned, proportioned structure. And the verses that follow will continue the demonstration — the night, the day, the earth, the water, the pasture, the mountains — until the reader has been walked through the whole of his environment and shown that every part of it is made.

The argumentative strategy is worth noting, because it is the opposite of what most people expect from a religious text. The Qur'an does not ask the reader to accept its conclusion and then look at the evidence with a predisposition. It asks the reader to look — repeatedly, insistently, in almost every sūrah — and then draws the conclusion. The invitation is *return your gaze*. The conclusion comes second.

This is why the short Makkan sūrahs are so full of natural description. They are not providing atmosphere; they are providing evidence, and the evidence is chosen from things the listener can see before he finishes the sentence.

**The Humility the Verse Produces**

There is a psychological effect that the verse intends, and the Qur'an names it in the verse about the search for flaws: *your gaze will return to you humbled and exhausted* — *khāṣiʾan wa huwa ḥasīr*.

The two words describe the exact state that al-Nāziʿāt predicted for the eyes of the Last Day (79:9 — *abṣāruhā khāshiʿah*, "their eyes humbled"). The same *khushūʿ* is produced here by the sight of the fashioned sky and there by the sight of the record. The verse is thus an invitation to practise, in this life, the humility that will be compulsory in the next.

The person who looks at the sky and is humbled has begun the process the whole sūrah is describing. The person who looks and is not humbled has already indicated which of the two categories in verses 37–41 he belongs to.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

> **darkened its night, and brought forth its day.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Attributed to the Sky**

The verse contains a grammatical feature that is easily passed over and that carries real meaning. Both night and day are given possessive pronouns referring back to the sky: *laylahā* — "its night" — and *ḍuḥāhā* — "its forenoon." The night belongs to the sky. The day belongs to the sky.

The attribution is precise. Night and day are not properties of the earth alone; they are produced by the relation between the earth and the celestial bodies — by rotation, by the position of the sun, by the geometry of the system the previous verse described as built and fashioned. The sūrah is not describing a local weather pattern; it is describing the operation of the structure it has just named.

**Two Verbs of Opposite Motion**

*Wa-aghṭasha laylahā* — "and He darkened its night." The verb is from *gh-ṭ-sh*, to darken, to obscure. The same root appears in the oaths of Sūrah al-Shams:

> *"And [by] the night when it covers it."* (Qur'an 91:4)

— *wa'l-layli idhā yaghshāhā*, the night when it covers the sun. The darkening is described as an act of covering, and the Qur'an uses this image repeatedly:

> *"He wraps the night over the day and wraps the day over the night."* (Qur'an 39:5)

The verb *yūrij* — "to insert" — describes the gradual, overlapping transition rather than an abrupt switch, which is exactly what the phenomenon is: a long twilight in both directions, never a sudden change.

*Wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā* — "and He brought forth its forenoon." The verb *akhraja* is the verb of extraction and emergence, the same one used in verse 31 for the bringing forth of water and in verse 32 in a different sense. The day is described as something brought out — produced, revealed, made visible after concealment.

*Ḍuḥā* specifically means the forenoon, the period after sunrise when the light is at its clearest — the same word that gives its name to Sūrah 93:

> *"By the forenoon, and by the night when it settles."* (Qur'an 93:1–2)

The commentators note that *ḍuḥā* can also carry the sense of the brightness or light of the morning, and that the sūrah will use the same word in its final verse (79:46) for the "morning" of a lifetime.

**The Alternation as a Sign**

The alternation of night and day is one of the Qur'an's most frequently cited signs, and the frequency is itself significant. It is cited because it is universal: there is no human being on earth who has not observed it, no day of any life in which it did not occur.

> *"Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of the night and the day, are signs for those of understanding."* (Qur'an 3:190)

> *"And He it is Who made the night and the day successive, for whoever wishes to remember or wishes gratitude."* (Qur'an 25:62)

> *"And We made the night and the day two signs, and We effaced the sign of the night and made the sign of the day visible, that you may seek bounty from your Lord and know the number of years and the reckoning."* (Qur'an 17:12)

The last of these states three functions of the alternation: the seeking of livelihood, the counting of years, and the reckoning. The entire structure of human civilisation — agriculture, calendars, trade, navigation, the scheduling of labour — rests on the regularity that this verse describes in six words.

**The Verse's Function in the Argument for Resurrection**

It may seem that a description of night and day has little to do with the resurrection of the dead. The connection is direct and it is the Qur'an's standard argument.

The alternation is a daily death and resurrection. The day dies; the world goes dark; everything ceases. The day returns; light emerges; activity resumes. The cycle repeats without exception, and every human being has witnessed it thousands of times. The Qur'an uses it explicitly as a proof:

> *"And among His signs is that you see the earth humbled — then when We send down water upon it, it stirs and grows. Truly He Who gives it life is the Giver of life to the dead."* (Qur'an 41:39)

> *"…and We gave life thereby to a dead land. Thus is the resurrection."* (Qur'an 50:11)

The argument is not analogical in a loose sense; it is an argument about the identity of the agent. The One who performs the daily restoration of light is the One being asked whether He can restore a body. The difficulty alleged by the objector in verse 10 is a difficulty that has already been overcome, every morning, in front of everyone.

**What the Alternation Does to the Human Being**

There is a psychological dimension to the verse that the Islamic tradition developed extensively, because the alternation is not merely a sign to be observed; it is a structure within which the believer's life is organised.

The five daily prayers are anchored to the alternation — dawn, midday, afternoon, sunset, night — so that the believer's day is punctuated at five points by the very phenomenon the verse describes. The fast is bounded by the same markers: from the appearance of dawn to the setting of the sun. The month is determined by the moon. The pilgrimage by the month.

The effect is that the believer does not merely observe the alternation; he is scheduled by it. His obligations arrive at times fixed by a mechanism he cannot manipulate, postpone, or negotiate. This produces a specific psychological formation — a person whose life is measured by something outside himself, and who therefore cannot pretend that his time is entirely his own.

The Qur'an states the purpose of this arrangement:

> *"…and that you may know the number of years and the reckoning."* (Qur'an 17:12)

Reckoning — *ḥisāb* — is the same word used for the accounting of the Last Day. The person who has spent a lifetime being counted by the alternation of night and day has been practising, daily, for the Day on which he will be counted.

**The Person Who Does Not See It**

The verse's final irony is stated in the Qur'an with a directness that leaves no room for evasion:

> *"And how many a sign there is in the heavens and the earth that they pass by, while they turn away from it."* (Qur'an 12:105)

*They pass by it* — *yamurrūna ʿalayhā*. The sign is not hidden; it is passed. The person does not fail to see the alternation of night and day; he sees it twice every twenty-four hours and derives nothing from it.

This is the condition the short Makkan sūrahs are designed to interrupt. They are addressed to a person for whom the most powerful evidence available is entirely routine, and their method is not to introduce new evidence but to make the existing evidence strange again — to restore to the sky, the night, the day, the earth, the water, and the mountains the character of things that were *made*, and that therefore had a Maker.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

> **And after that He spread out the earth.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Daḥāhā": The Word That Means to Make Habitable**

The verb is *daḥāhā*, from the root *d-ḥ-w* / *d-ḥ-y*. The classical lexicographers define it as spreading out and levelling, and they cite a specific concrete image: the ostrich, which spreads and smooths the ground with its body and feet to make a place to lay its eggs. The *udḥiyy* in Arabic is the ostrich's prepared nest — ground that has been flattened and cleared for a purpose.

The word therefore carries more than "spread." It contains the notion of preparation for use. The earth was not merely extended; it was levelled and made ready, as a nest is prepared for what will be placed in it.

The Qur'an uses the same verb in a verse that makes the purpose explicit:

> *"And the earth, after that We spread it out — how excellent a spreader!"* (Qur'an 51:48, using the related form)

> *"And God has made the earth for you a wide expanse, that you may travel therein on ways."* (Qur'an 71:19–20)

> *"And the earth We spread out, and set therein firm mountains, and made grow therein everything in measure."* (Qur'an 15:19)

> *"It is He Who made the earth submissive to you — so walk among its tracts and eat of His provision."* (Qur'an 67:15)

The last of these states the theological point: the earth is *dhallūl* — made tractable, made manageable, made submissive to human use. It is not a hostile environment that humanity has conquered by ingenuity; it is a prepared environment whose preparedness is attributed to an act.

**The Order: "After That"**

The phrase *baʿda dhālika* — "after that" — has been much discussed, because the Qur'an states elsewhere that the earth was created before the heavens were completed:

> *"Say: 'Do you indeed disbelieve in He Who created the earth in two days, and you set up rivals to Him? That is the Lord of the worlds. And He placed therein firm mountains above it and blessed it and determined therein its sustenance in four days — equal for those who ask. Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke, and said to it and to the earth: Come, willingly or unwillingly…'"* (Qur'an 41:9–11)

The exegetes, including Ibn ʿAbbās as transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, resolved the apparent sequence by distinguishing between two acts: the earth's *creation* and the earth's *spreading*. The material of the earth was created first; the heavens were then built and fashioned; and after that the earth was spread out, levelled, and made habitable — its mountains set, its waters brought forth, its vegetation established.

Al-Qurṭubī notes this as the standard resolution and adds that the mention of the earth after the sky, night, and day indicates that the earth's habitation followed the establishment of the heavens. The reading is coherent with the description in verses 27–29: the structure above was completed first, and the surface below was then prepared within it.

This kind of exegetical care is characteristic of the tradition. Where two verses appear to conflict, the commentators do not choose one and discard the other; they seek the distinction that makes both true.

**The Preparedness of the Earth**

The claim embedded in the verse is stronger than it appears. The earth is described not merely as existing but as arranged — levelled, spread, and prepared for the inhabitants whose needs the following verses will enumerate: water, pasture, mountains, sustenance for people and livestock.

The Qur'an states the arrangement with an explicit statement of purpose:

> *"It is He Who made the earth a bed for you and the sky a structure, and sent down water from the heaven and brought forth with it fruits as provision for you. So do not set up rivals to God while you know."* (Qur'an 2:22)

> *"Is not He Who made the earth a resting place, and placed therein rivers, and made therein firm mountains, and placed between the two seas a barrier? Is there a god with God? No — but most of them know not."* (Qur'an 27:61)

The rhetorical question at the end of the second verse is the Qur'an's standard move: the description of the arrangement is followed by the question of who arranged it, and the question is left with the reader.

**Why the Verse Comes Here, in an Argument About the Resurrection**

The verse's function within the sūrah's argument is easy to miss, and it is important. The sequence of verses 27–33 is a catalogue of creative acts, and the catalogue has a purpose beyond demonstration.

The objector in verses 10–12 said: *when we are decayed bones, shall we be restored?* The answer being constructed here is not only "He made bigger things than you." It is also "He made a place for you, and the place is not finished."

The earth was spread, levelled, watered, planted, and anchored — an enormous investment of arrangement — for inhabitants who live on it for a few decades and then leave it. If the arrangement were the whole of the story, it would be an absurdity on a scale that the verse does not permit the reader to ignore. A house built with this much care, furnished with this much provision, for guests who are ejected after a short stay and never return, is not a description of a house. It is a description of a staging post.

The Qur'an makes this argument directly:

> *"Did you think that We created you in vain, and that to Us you would not be returned?"* (Qur'an 23:115)

> *"And We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in play. If We had intended to take a diversion, We could have taken it from [what is] with Us — if We were to do so."* (Qur'an 21:16–17)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt contributes to this argument the detail that the preparation was extensive and specific. It was not a general arrangement; it was provision for you and your flocks (v. 33). The specificity is the point. General environments do not require explanation; specifically furnished ones do.

**The Earth as a Loan**

There is a further implication that the tradition drew out and that has become increasingly urgent. The earth is described throughout the Qur'an as something prepared for human use — and the human being is described as a successor in it:

> *"And it is He Who made you successors upon the earth and raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may test you in what He has given you."* (Qur'an 6:165)

> *"The world is sweet and green, and God has made you successors in it, and He will see how you act."* (Muslim 2742, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Succession — *khilāfah* — is not ownership. A successor holds an estate on behalf of the owner and is answerable for its condition. The Qur'an pairs the grant with the accountability in the same verse, and it states the consequence of mismanagement:

> *"Corruption has appeared on land and sea by what people's hands have earned, that He may let them taste some of what they have done, that perhaps they may return."* (Qur'an 30:41)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt — He spread it out for you — is therefore not a licence. It is a description of a prepared environment held on terms, and the terms are stated two verses later: it is provision for you, and provision implies a Provider, and a Provider implies an accounting.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:31

> **From it He brought forth its water and its pastures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Water First, Then What Grows**

The verse names two things and puts them in an order that is not arbitrary. *Akhraja minhā māʾahā wa-marʿāhā* — "He brought forth from it its water and its pasture." Water precedes pasture, because pasture is impossible without it. The sequence is the sequence of dependence, and the verse states it in six words.

The verb *akhraja* — brought forth, extracted — is the same verb used in verse 29 for the bringing forth of the day. The same act of extraction produces the light of the morning and the water of the earth, and the parallel is deliberate: both are things that appear from where they were not visible.

**The Qur'an's Central Natural Sign**

Water is the most frequently cited natural sign in the Qur'an, and the reason is that it is the one the listener cannot do without. The Qur'an returns to it constantly, and always with the same rhetorical structure: observe it, then ask who produces it.

> *"And We made from water every living thing. Will they not then believe?"* (Qur'an 21:30)

> *"And God created every creature from water."* (Qur'an 24:45)

> *"Have you considered the water that you drink? Is it you who brought it down from the clouds, or are We the One Who brings it down? If We willed, We could make it bitter — so why are you not grateful?"* (Qur'an 56:68–70)

> *"Say: 'Have you considered — if your water were to sink away in the morning, who would bring you flowing water?'"* (Qur'an 67:30)

The last of these is the most searching. It does not ask whether water exists; it asks what would happen if the supply stopped, and who would restore it. The question is addressed to a person who has never once produced a single drop of water and who is entirely dependent on a supply he did not create and cannot maintain.

> *"Do you not see that God sends down water from the heaven and makes it flow as springs in the earth; then He brings forth with it crops of varying colours; then they wither and you see them yellowed; then He makes them chaff? Truly in that is a reminder for those of understanding."* (Qur'an 39:21)

That verse contains the entire cycle — descent, storage, emergence, growth, withering — and ends by noting that the same cycle is a reminder. The reminder is of the resurrection, since the cycle is itself a death and return performed annually in front of everyone.

**"Marʿā": The Pasture, and the Word's Other Meaning**

*Marʿā* is the place of grazing — pasture, fodder, the vegetation that animals eat. It is built on the root *r-ʿ-y*, to graze, to tend, to watch over. The same root gives Arabic the word for shepherd, *rāʿī*, and it is used in one of the most important reports about responsibility in the entire tradition:

> *"All of you are shepherds and all of you are responsible for your flock. The ruler is a shepherd and is responsible for his flock; a man is a shepherd over his household and is responsible for his flock; a woman is a shepherd over her husband's house and his children and is responsible for her flock; and a servant is a shepherd over his master's property and is responsible for his flock. All of you are shepherds and all of you are responsible for your flock."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829)

The word in the verse is therefore doubly loaded. It names the pasture that God provides for the animals, and it names, by its root, the relation of care that human beings are required to exercise over what is in their charge. The one who receives the pasture is a shepherd over those who eat it.

**Variety Within Unity**

The Qur'an draws a specific inference from the variety of what grows, and the inference is a remarkable one:

> *"And in the earth are neighbouring tracts, and gardens of vines, and crops, and palm trees — some sharing a root and some standing alone — watered with one water, and We make some of them excel others in taste. Truly in that are signs for a people who understand."* (Qur'an 13:4)

The observation is precise: adjacent plots, the same soil, the same water, the same climate — and different products with different flavours. The verse identifies the variable that accounts for the difference, and it is not the water.

This argument from variety within uniformity of input is one of the Qur'an's most sophisticated, and it anticipates a question that biology would take millennia to answer. The classical commentators understood it in general terms — as evidence of a will operating within nature rather than a mechanism alone — and the modern reader can supply the detail the commentators lacked without changing the argument.

> *"…and We made grow therein every blessed pair."* (Qur'an 50:7)

> *"[It is] as provision for the servants, and We gave life thereby to a dead land. Thus is the resurrection."* (Qur'an 50:11)

The final sentence of that verse is the sūrah's whole point in four words. The annual greening of dead ground is the resurrection, performed repeatedly, in public, on every continent.

**The Practical Reading of the Verse**

There is a practical dimension that the tradition developed, because the verse describes provision and provision implies obligation.

The water and the pasture are described as brought forth *from it* — from the earth. They are not manufactured; they are extracted from a system the reader did not build. The Qur'an's response to this fact is gratitude, and it names the alternative:

> *"And He gave you of all that you asked Him. And if you should count the favour of God, you could not enumerate it. Truly man is unjust and ungrateful."* (Qur'an 14:34)

The final words are a diagnosis of the normal human condition: *ẓallūm kafūr*, "greatly unjust, greatly ungrateful." The verse does not say that people are always ungrateful; it says that this is the default, and the default requires correction.

The correction the tradition prescribes is specific and physical. Gratitude in Islam is not a feeling; it is an action with defined forms — the mention of God's name before eating, the praise after, the obligatory alms that returns a portion of the provision to those without it, the prohibition of waste:

> *"…and eat and drink, but do not be excessive. Truly He does not love the excessive."* (Qur'an 7:31)

> *"And give the relative his right, and the poor and the traveller, and do not squander wastefully. Truly the squanderers are brothers of the devils."* (Qur'an 17:26–27)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt describes the provision; the rest of the revelation describes what the provision obliges. The two cannot be separated, and the person who receives the water and the pasture without acknowledging the terms has misread the verse.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

> **and the mountains He set firm,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Arsāhā": The Anchoring**

The verb is from *r-s-w*, a root denoting firmness, stability, and settledness — the state of a thing that does not move. Arabic uses the same root for the anchors of a ship and for the settling of a vessel in harbour. Applied to the mountains, *arsāhā* means He fixed them, He anchored them, He made them stable.

The Qur'an supplies the image that makes the word concrete in a nearby sūrah:

> *"Have We not made the earth a resting place, and the mountains as pegs?"* (Qur'an 78:6–7)

*Awtād* — pegs, stakes, the pins driven into the ground to hold a tent. The comparison is drawn from the most familiar technology of the Qur'an's first audience: a tent does not stand because of its fabric but because of its pegs, and the pegs are almost entirely hidden. The mountain, on this image, is a peg whose visible portion is a small fraction of what holds it.

**The Stated Purpose: Lest It Shake With You**

The Qur'an does not leave the function of the mountains to inference. It states it explicitly and repeats the statement:

> *"And He cast into the earth firm mountains lest it shake with you, and rivers and roads, that you might be guided."* (Qur'an 16:15)

> *"And We placed in the earth firm mountains lest it shake with them, and We made therein wide passes as ways, that they might be guided."* (Qur'an 21:31)

> *"He created the heavens without pillars that you see, and cast into the earth firm mountains lest it shake with you, and spread therein every kind of creature."* (Qur'an 31:10)

The phrase *an tamīda bikum* — "lest it shake with you" — is the key. The concern is not the stability of the earth in the abstract; it is the stability of the earth *under human beings*. The mountains are described in relation to the inhabitants' experience of the ground.

**The Recurring List**

The mountains appear in a fixed list of natural signs that the Qur'an repeats with only minor variation, and the repetition is itself significant. A list repeated many times is a list the author considers essential.

> *"It is He Who spread the earth and set therein firm mountains and rivers, and made therein every fruit in pairs."* (Qur'an 13:3)

> *"And the earth We spread out, and set therein firm mountains, and made grow therein everything in measure."* (Qur'an 15:19)

> *"And We placed therein firm mountains towering, and gave you to drink sweet water."* (Qur'an 77:27)

> *"And the mountains — how they are set up."* (Qur'an 88:19)

> *"And We placed therein firm, towering mountains and blessed it, and made therein every balanced thing."* (Qur'an 50:7)

The last of these, from Sūrah Qāf, connects the mountains to *mīzān* — balance, measure — and to *tabshīr*, the glad tidings, by ending with the statement about the resurrection. The connection is the one al-Nāziʿāt is making: the same hand that anchored the mountains will reassemble the body.

**The Argument the Mountains Carry**

The mountains perform two distinct functions in the Qur'an's argument, and both are active in this verse.

The first is the argument from stability. The ground beneath a person's feet is the precondition of every plan he makes, and the verse attributes its stability to an act. The earth is not self-stabilising; it was anchored. A person standing on solid ground is standing on something that was made stable for him.

The second is the argument from scale. The mountains are the largest permanent structures on the earth's surface, visible for hundreds of kilometres, unmoved by any human force. The Qur'an uses them repeatedly as a measure of the impossible:

> *"And they ask you about the mountains. Say: 'My Lord will blast them and scatter them as particles of dust.'"* (Qur'an 20:105)

> *"And the mountains will be as carded wool."* (Qur'an 101:5)

> *"And the mountains will be set in motion and become a mirage."* (Qur'an 78:20)

The contrast is deliberate. The most immobile things in human experience are described, in the eschatological passages, as wool being carded and rock becoming dust. The verse in al-Nāziʿāt establishes the firmness precisely so that the later verses can undo it — and the undoing of the firmest thing is the clearest possible demonstration that the firmness was never intrinsic.

**On Reading Natural Description Carefully**

A word of caution is appropriate here, because modern readers are frequently offered readings of these verses that the tradition would not recognise. It has become common to claim that the Qur'an anticipated specific geological theories — that the "pegs" of 78:7 refer to mountain roots in the crust, that "lest it shake" refers to isostatic compensation — and to present these as proofs of the text's divine origin.

The classical exegetes did not read the verses this way, and the reason for their restraint is sound. The Qur'an is describing the world as it appears to a human observer, in language that a seventh-century Arab could verify, for a theological purpose. Its claims are about God's power, provision, and wisdom, not about geophysical mechanisms. Ibn Taymiyyah warned specifically against binding Qur'anic statements to the scientific consensus of the moment, on the ground that the consensus changes while the text does not — and that a verse tied to a superseded theory would be discredited by its own defender.

The honest reading is both simpler and stronger. The verse says that the mountains were set firm, that their firmness serves the inhabitants, and that the One who set them is able to do what the objectors doubt. Nothing in that claim is in tension with any geological finding, and the claim does not depend on one.

**The Practical Lesson of the Anchored Mountain**

There is a moral application that the tradition drew from the mountains, and it is not far-fetched. The mountain is the image of a person whose stability does not depend on his circumstances. The Qur'an uses it this way:

> *"And recite to them the news of the one to whom We gave Our signs, and he detached himself from them; so Satan overtook him, and he became of the misguided. And had We willed, We could have raised him thereby — but he clung to the earth and followed his caprice. So his example is like the dog: if you chase him he pants, and if you leave him he pants."* (Qur'an 7:175–176)

The contrast is between the man who was given the signs and detached from them — unstable in every condition, panting whether chased or left — and the mountain, which is the same in storm and calm. The verse in al-Nāziʿāt describes a landscape in which the mountains were anchored by an act, and the sūrah will end by describing two kinds of people: one who preferred the world and one who forbade his soul its caprice. The mountain is the picture of the second.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the same comparison in a report about the believer's resilience:

> *"The example of the believer is that of a fresh plant which the wind bends and then straightens — it is bent and then straightened. And the example of the hypocrite is that of a cedar tree, which does not shake until it is uprooted in one go."* (al-Bukhārī 5644; Muslim 2810)

The comparison is subtle: the believer bends and recovers; the hypocrite is rigid until he breaks. Neither is the mountain exactly, and the three images together describe the range — flexibility that survives, rigidity that shatters, and anchoredness that endures. The mountains of verse 32 were given the third.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:33

> **as sustenance for you and for your flocks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose Clause That Ends the Argument**

The verses from 27 to 32 have described a sequence of creative acts: the sky built and raised and fashioned, its night darkened and its day brought forth, the earth spread, its water and pasture extracted, its mountains anchored. Verse 33 states what all of it was for: *matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum* — "[as] provision for you and for your grazing animals."

The word *matāʿ* is from *m-t-ʿ*, to be provided for, to enjoy, to take benefit. It is the same root that gives the Qur'an's description of worldly life as *matāʿ al-ghurūr* — "the provision of delusion" (3:185; 57:20) — and the word for a temporary grant. A *matāʿ* is a provision made available for use during a period.

That last connotation is significant and it belongs to the argument. A provision is made for a duration. It is not the permanent possession of the recipient; it is supplied for a term, and the term is not set by the recipient. The verse ends the catalogue of creation by naming the relation between the creation and the created: everything described was prepared for you, for a period.

**The Same Phrase in the Next Sūrah**

The verse has a near-twin, and its placement is worth noting:

> *"[As] provision for you and for your grazing animals."* (Qur'an 80:32)

The phrase is virtually identical, and Sūrah 80 — ʿAbasa — is the sūrah immediately following this one in the muṣḥaf and close to it in the order of revelation. Both are Makkan sūrahs concerned with the resurrection and the human response to it, and both end their catalogue of creation with the same statement of purpose. The repetition establishes that this is a fixed formula in the Makkan revelation, not an incidental phrase.

**"Anʿām": The Animals Named**

*Anʿām* is the plural that covers the grazing livestock — camels, cattle, sheep, and goats — and the Qur'an devotes considerable attention to them:

> *"And the grazing animals He created for you, in which is warmth and benefits, and of them you eat. And for you in them is beauty when you bring them home in the evening and when you take them out to pasture in the morning. And they carry your loads to a land you could not reach except with great hardship. Truly your Lord is Kind, Merciful."* (Qur'an 16:5–7)

> *"And there is a lesson for you in the grazing animals. We give you to drink from what is in their bellies, and you have in them many benefits, and of them you eat."* (Qur'an 23:21)

> *"It is God Who made for you the grazing animals, some for you to ride and some you eat."* (Qur'an 40:79)

The enumeration in Sūrah al-Naḥl is remarkably complete: warmth, food, transport, beauty, milk, and the carrying of loads to otherwise unreachable places. The verse ends with two divine names — Kind, Merciful — and the connection is explicit: the provision of animals is an act of kindness, not a neutral fact of nature.

**Provision as a Doctrine**

The verse belongs to one of the Qur'an's most developed doctrines: *rizq*, provision. The doctrine has specific claims and they are stated with unusual directness.

Provision is guaranteed:

> *"And there is no creature moving on the earth but that its provision is upon God."* (Qur'an 11:6)

Provision is not produced by the provider's effort alone:

> *"Say: 'Who provides for you from the heaven and the earth?' …Say: 'God.'"* (Qur'an 10:31)

Provision does not obligate God to the provider:

> *"And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me. I do not desire provision from them, nor do I desire that they feed Me. Truly God is the Provider, the Possessor of strength, the Firm."* (Qur'an 51:56–58)

The last of these reverses the ordinary assumption completely. In every human economy, the worker feeds the employer and the subject supports the ruler. The Qur'an states that the relation between God and His creatures is the opposite: He provides and requires no provision. This is why the doctrine of provision is, in Islamic theology, inseparable from the doctrine of God's independence — *ghinā*.

The psychological consequence is significant and it is one of the few doctrines that measurably reduces anxiety. A person who believes that his provision is entirely the product of his own effort must either work without rest or live without security. A person who believes that provision is guaranteed and that effort is the means rather than the cause can work seriously and rest genuinely — because the outcome was never in his hands to begin with.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the doctrine in an image that has been quoted for fourteen centuries:

> *"Had you relied upon God with true reliance, He would have provided for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning hungry and return in the evening full."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

The image is often misread as a counsel of passivity, and it is the opposite. The birds *go out*. They do not wait in the nest. The reliance is on the outcome, not a substitute for the effort.

**The Recipient and the Responsibility**

The verse names the recipients — you and your animals — and the naming carries an obligation that the rest of the revelation specifies.

The provision is for use, not for hoarding:

> *"And those in whose wealth is a recognised right, for the beggar and the deprived."* (Qur'an 70:24–25)

It is for use, not for waste:

> *"And do not squander wastefully. Truly the squanderers are brothers of the devils."* (Qur'an 17:26–27)

It is for use with gratitude, and the gratitude has a measurable consequence:

> *"And [remember] when your Lord proclaimed: 'If you are grateful, I will surely increase you; and if you are ungrateful, truly My punishment is severe.'"* (Qur'an 14:7)

And it is for use with accountability for the animals included in the provision. The tradition developed this with remarkable specificity. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Fear God in these animals that cannot speak. Ride them when they are fit to be ridden, and eat them when they are fit to be eaten."* (Abū Dāwūd 2548)

He passed by a bird whose young had been taken and said:

> *"Who has distressed her by taking her young? Return her young to her."* (Muslim 2241, in the longer report about the ants and the bird)

And he stated the general principle:

> *"In every living being with a moist liver there is a reward."* (al-Bukhārī 2363; Muslim 2244)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt places the animals inside the same provision as the human beings — *for you and for your flocks* — and the conjunction is not casual. The provision covers both, and the accountability covers the treatment of both.

**The Verse as the Hinge of the Sūrah**

Verse 33 closes the cosmological argument and opens the eschatological section, and the transition is immediate: the very next verse is *"So when the Great Calamity befalls—"*.

The juxtaposition is the sūrah's method. Here is the provision: water, pasture, mountains, sustenance for you and your animals. There is the Calamity. Between them lies the entire human life, and the sūrah's question to the reader is which of the two he has been living for.

The person who reads verses 27–33 and concludes that the world is a comfortable arrangement to be enjoyed indefinitely has read only half of the passage. The provision was made for a term. The term is not announced in advance. And the Calamity of verse 34 is the moment at which the term expires and the accounting begins.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:34

> **So when the Great Calamity befalls—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Al-Ṭāmmah": The Event That Overflows Every Bank**

The word is *al-ṭāmmah*, and the sūrah takes one of its alternate names from it. The root *ṭ-m-m* means to abound, to overflow, to rise above and submerge — the motion of a flood that comes over the banks and covers everything on the plain. Applied to an event, it denotes the calamity so comprehensive that it encompasses all things and leaves nothing outside it.

The classical commentators give the definition precisely: the *ṭāmmah* is the greatest of all tremendous events because its overabundance encompasses everything. Other calamities are partial — a flood covers a valley, a war covers a region, a plague covers a generation. The *ṭāmmah* covers all of them, including the ones already past.

**The Names of the Day**

The Qur'an gives the Last Day a remarkable number of names, and the multiplicity is itself instructive. Each name isolates one dimension of an event too large for a single word.

*Al-Sāʿah* — the Hour (used dozens of times). *Al-Qiyāmah* — the Rising (Sūrah 75). *Al-Ṭāmmah* — the Overwhelming Calamity (79:34). *Al-Qāriʿah* — the Striking Calamity (Sūrah 101). *Al-Ḥāqqah* — the Inevitable, the Realised (Sūrah 69). *Al-Wāqiʿah* — the Occurring (Sūrah 56). *Al-Ghāshiyah* — the Overwhelming (Sūrah 88). *Al-Āzifah* — the Imminent: *"So warn them of the Day of the Imminent"* (40:18). *Yawm al-Faṣl* — the Day of Separation. *Yawm al-Dīn* — the Day of Recompense. *Yawm al-Ḥasrah* — the Day of Regret (19:39). *Yawm al-Khulūd* — the Day of Eternity (50:34). *Yawm al-Jamʿ* — the Day of Gathering (42:7). *Yawm al-Ḥisāb* — the Day of Reckoning.

No other event in the Qur'an accumulates so many names. The reason is that each name corrects a specific misunderstanding. A person who thinks the Day is distant is addressed by *al-Āzifah*, the Imminent. A person who thinks it might not happen is addressed by *al-Ḥāqqah*, the Realised. A person who thinks it will be a general dissolution is addressed by *Yawm al-Faṣl*, the Day of Separation. A person who thinks it will be painless is addressed by *al-Qāriʿah*, the Striking.

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt contributes the dimension of comprehensiveness. Whatever else the Day is, it will not be survivable by opting out. There is no location outside it, no exemption from it, and no part of a person's life that falls outside its jurisdiction.

**"Idhā", Not "In": The Grammar of Certainty**

The verse begins with *fa-idhā jāʾat* — "so when it comes." The particle *idhā* is used in Arabic for events that are certain to occur; the particle *in* is used for events that are contingent. The Qur'an never says "*if* the Hour comes." It says *when*, every time, without exception.

This is a small grammatical fact with a large implication. The Qur'an does not present the Last Day as a possibility to be evaluated but as a certainty to be prepared for. The reader is not asked to assess the probability; he is asked to note the tense.

And the verb is *jāʾat* — "it comes," "it arrives." Not "it is created" or "it occurs" but "it comes," the verb of a visitor or an army arriving. The Calamity is described as something that travels and reaches, and the human being is in the position of one who is waiting for it whether he knows it or not.

**The Suspended Sentence**

The verse ends with a dash in every translation, and the dash represents a genuine grammatical feature. In Arabic, a conditional introduced by *idhā* requires a response — the *jawāb* — and the response here is not immediately given. The following verses describe the Day (v. 35), the visibility of Hell (v. 36), and the two destinies (vv. 37–41), and the commentators differ on which of these supplies the answer. Some take it to be v. 36; some take it to be the pair of vv. 39 and 41 — *truly Hellfire is the refuge* and *truly the Garden is the refuge*; and some hold that the response is deliberately withheld.

Al-Qurṭubī and others note the rhetorical effect of the suspension: the listener is held in the conditional, waiting for the completion, and the incompleteness itself conveys that what is coming cannot be fully stated. Some commentators read the omission as an affirmation in itself — that what must occur must occur, and the sentence does not need to be completed to make that plain.

The device is not unique to this verse. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly in eschatological passages (Sūrah 56 begins with the same suspended conditional), and its effect on the reciter is considerable: the voice rises at the conditional and does not resolve where expected.

**What the Commentators Take the Calamity to Be**

Three readings are transmitted. The first: the *ṭāmmah* is the second blast of the Trumpet, at which all human beings and jinn are gathered. The second: it is the Resurrection itself, because it surpasses and predominates over all things — the verb *yaṭimmu* being from the same root. The third: it is the moment at which the people of the Fire are led to the Fire and the people of the Garden to the Garden — the point of final separation.

The three are not competing accounts of different events; they are three moments within one sequence, and the word's meaning — the overwhelming, all-covering event — applies to each. The tradition's willingness to hold all three is characteristic of its method.

**Living Toward a Certainty**

The verse's practical force lies in the mismatch it exposes. Human beings organise their lives around a set of certainties: they will need money, they will age, their reputation matters, their health must be maintained, their children must be provided for. Each of these is treated as real, and each generates planning, anxiety, and effort in proportion to its perceived certainty.

One further certainty — the only one that is literally certain, since its probability is one — generates almost no planning at all. The verse names it and puts it in the grammatical form of the certain, and it does so in order to correct the mismatch.

The Prophet ﷺ described the correction in a report that has been the foundation of Islamic spiritual practice:

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures."* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī; graded ḥasan)

And Ibn ʿUmar narrated the practical form of the instruction:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ took me by the shoulder and said: 'Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveller.'"* And Ibn ʿUmar used to say: "When you reach the evening, do not wait for the morning; and when you reach the morning, do not wait for the evening. Take from your health for your illness, and from your life for your death." (al-Bukhārī 6416)

The instruction is not to abandon the world. It is to recalibrate the certainty assignments — to treat the one certain event with the seriousness that the others receive by default.

The verse is the sūrah's pivot for exactly this reason. Everything before it has been preparation: the oaths, the Day, the objection, the story of Pharaoh, the catalogue of creation. Verse 34 is the arrival of the thing all of it was pointing at, and it arrives in the grammatical form of a certainty, with the sentence deliberately left unfinished.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:35

> **a day when man shall remember that for which he endeavored,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return of Everything That Was Forgotten**

The verb is *yataẓakkaru* — "he remembers," in the reflexive-intensive form that denotes the full recovery of something to consciousness. The word *saʿā* — "he strove," "he worked," "he acted" — is the same root used in verse 22 of Pharaoh turning his back and striving, and in the Qur'an's description of the one who strives for the Hereafter (17:19).

So the verse says: on that Day the human being recovers to consciousness everything he strove for and everything he did. Not some of it. Not the significant portion. The whole.

The Qur'an states the mechanism elsewhere with an explicitness that removes any doubt:

> *"On the Day when God resurrects them all together, then informs them of what they did — God has kept account of it while they have forgotten it. And God is witness over all things."* (Qur'an 58:6)

The phrase *aḥṣāhu Allāhu wa nasūhu* — "God has counted it and they have forgotten it" — is the whole verse in four words. Two ledgers exist: one complete and one lossy. The human ledger loses entries constantly and inevitably; forgetting is not a moral failure but a structural feature of the human mind. The other ledger loses nothing.

**The Book That Speaks**

The Qur'an describes the presentation of this record in several places, and the descriptions are detailed enough to constitute a coherent account.

> *"And We have fastened every man's deed to his neck, and We bring forth for him on the Day of Resurrection a book which he will find spread open: 'Read your book. Your own soul suffices this Day as a reckoner against you.'"* (Qur'an 17:13–14)

The final clause is the most severe sentence in the passage. The reckoner is not an external prosecutor; it is the person himself. He will read his own record and confirm it, and the confirmation will not be coerced.

> *"And the book will be placed, and you will see the criminals fearful of what is in it, and they will say: 'Woe to us! What is this book that leaves nothing, small or great, except that it has enumerated it?' And they will find what they did present — and your Lord wrongs no one."* (Qur'an 18:49)

> *"This is Our book, speaking about you in truth. Truly We were recording what you used to do."* (Qur'an 45:29)

> *"And everything, small and great, is written."* (Qur'an 54:53)

The recording is not merely of actions. The Qur'an includes intention, and it includes what was never expressed:

> *"Or do they think that We do not hear their secret and their private counsel? Yes indeed — and Our messengers are with them, recording."* (Qur'an 43:80)

**Why Forgetting Is Necessary Here and Impossible There**

There is a mercy in human forgetting that the verse implies, and recognising it makes the verse's horror intelligible. A human being could not function if he retained the full detail of his life. The embarrassment of a remark made twenty years ago, the grief of a loss, the shame of a failure — the mind discards the intensity of these in order to permit continued action. Forgetting is not a defect; it is a condition of living.

The Day described in this verse is the removal of that condition. The record will not be presented as a list to be read; it will be recovered as an experience. The commentators describe the *tadhakkur* of this verse as the arrival of full awareness — not the learning of new facts but the cessation of the forgetting that made the facts bearable.

This is why the verse's word is *yataẓakkaru* rather than *yaʿlamu*. He does not come to know something new. He remembers.

**The Reckoning in the Reports**

The Sunnah supplies detail on the process that the Qur'an describes, and the details have shaped Muslim imagination for fourteen centuries.

> *"The first thing for which a servant will be called to account on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer. If it is sound, he has succeeded; and if it is deficient, it will be said: 'Look — does My servant have any voluntary prayers?' And his obligatory prayers will be completed from them. Then the rest of his deeds will be treated in the same way."* (al-Tirmidhī 413; Abū Dāwūd 864; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

The mechanism described is remarkable: the deficiency in the obligatory is made up from the voluntary. The record is not a mechanical tally but a reckoning with provisions built into it.

And the report that has given more hope to Muslims than almost any other:

> *"The people will be brought forward on the Day of Resurrection, and a man will be brought whose scrolls of sins will be spread out to ninety-nine records, each record extending as far as the eye can see. It will be said: 'Do you deny any of this? Do My recording angels oppress you?' He will say: 'No, my Lord.' It will be said: 'Do you have an excuse?' He will say: 'No, my Lord.' It will be said: 'Yes indeed — you have a card with Us, and you will not be wronged.' So a card will be brought out containing: 'I testify that there is no god but God, and that Muhammad is His servant and Messenger.' He will say: 'My Lord, what is this card against these scrolls?' It will be said: 'You will not be wronged.' So the scrolls will be placed on one scale and the card on the other, and the scrolls will rise and the card will outweigh them — for nothing is heavy against the Name of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2639; Ibn Mājah 4300; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

The report's function in the tradition is not to encourage presumption. The commentators note that the card outweighs the scrolls because of what it contains and because of the state of the one who held it — and that the same words said as a formula without their content would not weigh. But the report establishes, beyond any argument, that the reckoning is not a mechanical arithmetic.

**The Wise Man and the Foolish Man**

The practical application of the verse was given by the Prophet ﷺ in a single sentence that defines the entire discipline of self-accounting:

> *"The wise man is the one who calls himself to account and works for what comes after death. And the foolish man is the one who follows his caprice and wishes upon God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2459; graded ḥasan)

The two descriptions are precise. The wise man performs the reckoning in advance — *ḥāsaba nafsahu* — and he works. The foolish man follows his desire and then hopes that God will overlook it. The second description is a diagnosis of an extremely common condition: the person who does not examine his record but expects it to come out well.

ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's formulation of the practice became the standard reference:

> *"Hold yourselves to account before you are held to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed. Truly it is easier for you to hold yourselves to account today for the presentation tomorrow."* (attributed to ʿUmar; preserved in the ascetic literature and cited by al-Tirmidhī)

The practice the tradition developed from this — the daily review, performed after the night prayer — is not a morbid exercise. It is an accounting procedure, and its logic is that of any accounting: an error found early is cheap to correct; the same error found at the audit is ruinous.

**The Modern Archive and the Real One**

The contemporary world has developed, for the first time in history, a technology that resembles the record the verse describes. Everything a person writes is stored. Every transaction is logged. Every photograph is dated and located. Employers review it, courts subpoena it, and people lose careers over sentences written in adolescence.

The analogy is useful and it is also instructive in its limits. The digital archive records what was done. The record of verse 35 records what was done *and why* — and the why is what determines the weight. A person may have a spotless digital archive and a ruinous record, because the record includes the intentions behind the acts, the opportunities declined, the good he could have done and did not, and the harm he caused without appearing to.

The verse is therefore not satisfied by the modern solution of managing one's visible record. It describes a reckoning in which the visible record is the smallest component, and in which the reckoner is the person himself — reading, and unable to dispute what he reads, because he will recognise it as his own.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:36

> **and Hellfire is made visible for one who sees—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Burrizat": Brought Out Into the Open**

The verb is from *b-r-z*, to emerge into the open, to be exposed after concealment. It is the verb used for the coming forth of the dead before God:

> *"And they will come forth to God, the One, the Prevailing."* (Qur'an 14:48 in context)

Applied to Hell, it means that the Fire is not merely present but *revealed* — uncovered, displayed, made visible to those before it. The passive form is significant: someone uncovers it. It does not simply appear; it is shown.

The Qur'an uses the identical phrase in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ:

> *"And Hell will be made manifest to the deviators."* (Qur'an 26:91)

And it describes the sequence in Sūrah Maryam:

> *"Then We will surely bring them around Hell, kneeling."* (Qur'an 19:68, in context; cf. 19:68–72)

The image of being brought around the Fire — made to see it from every side before entering — is a stage of the punishment distinct from the punishment itself.

**The Punishment of Seeing**

There is a specific cruelty in the sequence that the Qur'an intends the reader to notice. The person is shown what he is about to enter. He is not simply deposited; he is given the sight first.

The Qur'an describes the Fire as itself seeing and reacting:

> *"When it sees them from a distant place, they will hear its raging and roaring."* (Qur'an 25:12)

> *"When they are thrown into a narrow place therein, chained together, they will cry out there for destruction."* (Qur'an 25:13)

> *"It all but bursts with rage. Every time a group is thrown into it, its keepers ask them: 'Did no warner come to you?'"* (Qur'an 67:8)

The last verse supplies the Fire's own question, and the question is the point. The keepers do not ask whether the person was informed. They ask whether a warner came — implying that the answer is yes, and that the sight of the Fire is the moment at which the warning becomes knowledge.

**"For One Who Sees": Who Is Included?**

The phrase *li-man yarā* — "for whoever sees" — has been discussed at length, because its indefiniteness admits several readings.

Some commentators hold that it refers to the disbelievers specifically, since they are the ones for whom the Fire is displayed as a prelude. Others hold that it refers to all human beings, since all will be brought around it:

> *"And there is none of you but will come to it. It is with your Lord a decree that must be fulfilled."* (Qur'an 19:71)

Al-Rāzī objects to the first reading on an interesting ground: the realities of the Hereafter can only be seen by those who have sight, and the eyes of the disbelievers are covered — as in:

> *"God has sealed their hearing and their hearts, and over their eyes is a covering."* (Qur'an 2:7)

The objection is a genuine exegetical difficulty and the tradition preserves it rather than resolving it away. One response is that the covering described in 2:7 is a covering in this world, in relation to guidance, and does not prevent physical sight in the next. Another is that the phrase means the Fire is shown to whoever has the capacity to see it — which, on that Day, is everyone.

**The Three Degrees of Certainty**

There is a reading of the verse that the Sufi commentators developed and that has real exegetical support. Sūrah al-Takāthur describes three grades of knowledge, and the vocabulary is technical:

> *"No — if you knew with the knowledge of certainty, you would surely see the Hellfire. Then you will surely see it with the eye of certainty."* (Qur'an 102:5–7)

The first grade is *ʿilm al-yaqīn* — the knowledge of certainty: knowing that the Fire exists because you have been told by one who does not lie. The second is *ʿayn al-yaqīn* — the eye of certainty: seeing it. The third, mentioned at the end of the sūrah, is *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* — the truth of certainty: experiencing it.

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt describes the transition from the first to the second. The Fire becomes visible to whoever sees — and the point is that the seeing is available, in a derived form, now. The person who believes the report has, in the tradition's language, a knowledge of the Fire; the person who arrives at the sight has the same knowledge with the uncertainty removed. Nothing new is learned. What was believed is now seen.

This is why the tradition treats the remembrance of the Hereafter as a form of seeing. The Prophet ﷺ defined excellence in religion as exactly this:

> *"Excellence (iḥsān) is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

The formulation is a graduated instruction: worship in the state of one who sees; and if that is beyond you, worship in the awareness of being seen. The second is available to everyone and it produces, over time, something approaching the first.

**Why the Fire Is Shown**

The question deserves an answer, because the showing appears to add suffering without adding information. The person already knows, by report, that the Fire exists.

The answer the tradition gives is that the sight is the completion of the argument. In this world, belief in the Hereafter is an act of trust in a report; it can be doubted, deferred, and reinterpreted, and most people spend their lives doing some combination of the three. The sight removes the possibility of all three. And the removal is not a punishment added to the punishment; it is the moment at which the person finally understands what he was told and did not take seriously.

The Qur'an describes this understanding arriving too late, repeatedly:

> *"And they will say: 'Had we listened or reasoned, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.'"* (Qur'an 67:10)

> *"And the Fire will be displayed to those who disbelieved. 'You exhausted your pleasures in your worldly life and enjoyed them. So today you are recompensed with the punishment of humiliation…'"* (Qur'an 46:20)

Note the two things named in the first verse: listening and reasoning. Both were available. The person had ears and he had a mind. The Fire's display is the moment at which he discovers that the faculties he declined to use were the faculties that would have saved him.

**The Verse Addressed to the Reader**

The verse's final phrase — *for one who sees* — is indefinite, and the indefiniteness is an invitation. It does not say "for the disbelievers." It says "for whoever sees."

The reader of the sūrah is, at the moment of reading, in the category of those who have not yet seen. The verse is telling him that the sight is coming and that its content will not surprise him. Everything he needs in order to know what he will see has been provided — the report, the description, the reasoning, the repeated reminders across 114 chapters.

The practical question the verse poses is therefore not "does the Fire exist?" but "do I live as though I have seen it?" The tradition answered that question with a discipline: the remembrance of death, the remembrance of the grave, the remembrance of the standing — practised not as morbidity but as the cultivation of the sight before the sight arrives. The person who has looked, deliberately and regularly, at the end of his own life has already seen, in the only way available to him, what verse 36 describes. And when the Fire is uncovered, he will not be learning. He will be confirming.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:37

> **as for one who rebels**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Word That Condemned Pharaoh**

The verse begins with the verb that the sūrah used, twenty verses earlier, of the tyrant of Egypt. *Ṭaghā* — the identical form, the identical word. In verse 17 God told Moses: *"Go to Pharaoh; truly he has transgressed (ṭaghā)."* In verse 37 the sūrah defines one of the two destinies by the same verb: *"as for one who rebels (ṭaghā)."*

The repetition is the sūrah's most important structural device. It closes the circle opened by the story of Moses and tells the reader, without stating it, that the story was not about Pharaoh. Pharaoh is the case study; the verb is the category; and the category includes the reader.

**What Transgression Is**

The root *ṭ-gh-y* was discussed in the commentary on verse 17, and its meaning needs to be recalled precisely because it is doing specific work here. The root denotes exceeding a set limit — the water that overtops its bank (69:11), the people who exceed what was permitted to them (91:11). The *ṭāghī* is not defined by the amount of harm he causes but by the fact that he has passed a boundary.

The definition implies the existence of the boundary. This is theologically important: a person cannot transgress in a universe without limits. The word carries within it the affirmation that there is a measure, that the measure was set by someone other than the transgressor, and that the transgression is a movement across it.

The Qur'an gives the mechanism of the movement in two verses from the earliest revelation:

> *"No — truly man transgresses, because he considers himself self-sufficient."* (Qur'an 96:6–7)

Self-sufficiency — the perception of needing nothing — is the precondition. And the perception is produced by conditions the Qur'an names: wealth, strength, unaccountability, the absence of anyone who will contradict.

**Al-Rāzī's Reading: The Corruption of the Intellect**

Al-Rāzī offers an interpretation that deserves attention because it locates the transgression in the intellect rather than in the appetite. He reads *"as for one who rebels"* as indicating a corruption of the faculty of knowing, and his argument is this: everyone who truly knows God knows his own insignificance before Him, and no one who knows his own insignificance will incline to rebellion and arrogance. Rebellion is therefore not compatible with genuine knowledge. It is a symptom of knowledge that has failed.

The reading has direct Qur'anic support:

> *"…and only those among His servants who have knowledge fear God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

> *"Truly it is not the eyes that are blinded, but the hearts within the breasts are blinded."* (Qur'an 22:46)

And it explains something that otherwise puzzles: why the Qur'an so consistently pairs transgression with claims about knowledge. The transgressor does not present himself as a sinner. He presents himself as one who sees through — who has understood that the rules are constructions, that the constraints are negotiable, that the accounts are not real. The transgression is preceded, in his own account, by an insight.

The Qur'an's response is that the insight is the transgression.

**The Pairing That Defines a Person**

The verse is the first half of a definition, and the second half follows immediately: *"and prefers the life of this world."* The two together constitute one of the two kinds of human being the sūrah recognises.

Notice what the definition does not contain. It does not mention belief or disbelief in the abstract. It does not mention nationality, lineage, wealth, education, or the number of rituals performed. It names two facts: a relation to limits, and a preference. Both are available to anyone at any moment, and both are chosen repeatedly rather than once.

The pairing is not accidental either. The person who exceeds a limit does so in order to obtain something, and what he obtains is, by definition, within this world — since the world beyond the limit is not his to take. Transgression and world-preference are the same act described from two angles: the act of taking, and the thing taken.

**The Gradations the Tradition Recognised**

The commentators were careful not to flatten the verse into a binary in which every imperfect person is a Pharaoh. Al-Rāzī explicitly holds that the description applies to the one who reaches the *greatest* degree of corruption, and that those below that degree may be punished without the Fire becoming their final abode.

The distinction matters practically. The vast majority of human beings are not in the category of verse 37; they are in the category of people who believe and disobey, who know and fail, who intend and lapse. The Qur'an addresses them constantly and never treats them as hopeless:

> *"Say: 'O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Truly God forgives all sins. Truly He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.'"* (Qur'an 39:53)

The verse in al-Nāziʿāt is not addressed primarily to that group. It is addressed to the person for whom transgression has become a settled posture — who has not lapsed but decided, and who has constructed a life around the decision. The distinction between the sin and the posture is one the tradition maintained carefully, and it is the difference between a person who needs forgiveness and a person who needs repentance.

**The Modern Register of the Same Verb**

The transgression described here has a contemporary form that is far more common than the tyrannical one, and it is worth naming because most readers will not recognise it in themselves.

The ordinary modern transgression is not the seizure of power. It is the quiet, systematic refusal to accept that anything is forbidden. The person who decides, without ever articulating the decision, that no rule binds him except the ones he finds useful — that he will observe the prohibitions that suit his interests and ignore the ones that do not, that he will adopt the beliefs that flatter him and dismiss the ones that cost him, that he is the final arbiter of what applies to him.

This is not announced. It does not require an assembly or a proclamation. It is simply the operating assumption of a life, and it is precisely *ṭughyān* in the Qur'an's sense: the movement past a limit on the ground that no limit is binding on the one who is strong enough or clever enough or honest enough with himself about what he wants.

The Qur'an gives this position its name in a verse already quoted in this commentary:

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?"* (Qur'an 45:23)

And verse 40 of this sūrah will give the opposite, in the same terms: the one who *forbids his soul from caprice*. The two verses are not describing a Pharaoh and a saint. They are describing two ways of relating to one's own desires, and every reader is, at every moment, in one of them.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:38

> **and prefers the life of this world,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Āthara": The Verb of Preference**

The word is from *a-th-r*, to prefer — to choose one thing over another when both are available. It is a neutral verb in itself, and the Qur'an uses it positively in one of its most admired descriptions:

> *"…and give preference over themselves, even though they are in privation. And whoever is protected from the greed of his own soul — those are the successful."* (Qur'an 59:9)

The Ansār of Madinah, in this verse, *preferred* others over themselves while in need. The same verb, the same structure, a wholly different object.

The moral question, in other words, is never whether one prefers. Every human being prefers constantly; preference is the basic operation of the will. The question is the order of the preference — what is chosen over what. Verse 38 names the disordered order: the life of this world preferred over the Hereafter. And verse 40 will name the ordered one.

**What "This World" Means Here**

*Al-ḥayāh al-dunyā* — "the lower life," "the nearest life." The word *dunyā* is the feminine of *adnā*, the nearer, the lower. The name is a diagnosis: this life is characterised by proximity. It is what is at hand, immediate, visible, available now.

The Qur'an describes its nature with an unflinching catalogue:

> *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion, adornment, boasting among you, and rivalry in wealth and children — like the example of a rain whose vegetation delights the sowers; then it dries and you see it yellowed; then it becomes chaff. And in the Hereafter is severe punishment, and forgiveness from God and pleasure. And the life of this world is not but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The five items — play, diversion, adornment, boasting, rivalry — are a precise inventory of what occupies most human attention, and the agricultural simile at the end is the Qur'an's characteristic move: not a denial of the beauty but a statement of its duration. The green growth is genuinely delightful. It is also genuinely temporary. The error is not in enjoying it; it is in preferring it, which means treating the temporary as though it were the lasting.

**The Comparison the Qur'an Insists On**

The preference is always a comparison, and the Qur'an states the terms of the comparison explicitly and repeatedly:

> *"No — you prefer the life of this world, while the Hereafter is better and more enduring."* (Qur'an 87:16–17)

> *"…and they rejoiced in the life of this world, and the life of this world compared with the Hereafter is but a [brief] enjoyment."* (Qur'an 13:26)

> *"What has satisfied you with the life of this world rather than the Hereafter? And the enjoyment of the life of this world compared with the Hereafter is but little."* (Qur'an 9:38)

The two criteria given in Sūrah al-Aʿlā are *khayr* (better) and *abqā* (more enduring) — quality and duration. The preference for this world fails on both counts, and the failure on the second is not a matter of degree. A finite quantity, however large, cannot compete with an infinite one, and the arithmetic is not close.

**The Report Al-Rāzī Cites**

Al-Rāzī grounds his interpretation of this verse in a saying transmitted in the ascetic literature:

> *"The love of the world is the source of every error."* (ḥubb al-dunyā raʾsu kulli khaṭīʾah — cited by al-Rāzī; the hadith critics have graded its chain as weak, though its meaning is supported by a wide body of evidence)

The grading should be stated honestly: this particular wording is not among the soundly transmitted reports. Its meaning, however, is not in doubt, and the Qur'an supplies the support directly in the verse al-Rāzī was reaching for:

> *"…and whoever is protected from the greed of his own soul — those are the successful."* (Qur'an 59:9)

The sound report that expresses the same content is the Prophet's ﷺ description of the world's position for the two kinds of people:

> *"The world is a prison for the believer and a garden for the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

The statement is not a claim that the believer's life is miserable. It is a claim about the believer's relation to the world: he is restrained in it, held back from what he could take, waiting for something else — which is the definition of a prisoner. The disbeliever, having no such restraint and no such expectation, experiences it as a garden.

**The Rider in the Shade**

The most economical description of the correct preference in the entire tradition is a single image from the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"What is my affair with the world? My example and the example of the world is that of a rider who travelled and rested in the shade of a tree on a summer day, then rose and left it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2377; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

Every element of the image is a correction of a specific error. The rider is travelling — the world is a segment of a journey, not a destination. He rests — the world's comforts are real and legitimate. In the shade — the comfort is borrowed, not owned, and it belongs to a tree he did not plant. On a summer day — the comfort is precisely what is needed at that moment. Then he rose and left it — the stay has an end that is not set by the rider.

The person who prefers the world is the rider who builds a house in the shade. The shade is not condemned; the house is.

**The Preference Is Not the Possession**

It is essential to note what the verse does *not* say. It does not say "as for one who possesses the world." The Companions included some of the wealthiest men in Arabia — ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, Ṭalḥah ibn ʿUbayd Allāh, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām — and none of them is described in the Qur'an or the Sunnah as having preferred the world.

The distinction is between holding and being held. The world in the hand does not harm; the world in the heart does. The tradition expressed this in a formula attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and repeated by the ascetics: the world is a place of passage, not a place of residence; take from it what serves the passage and refuse what would require you to stay.

The Qur'an states the same distinction as a command:

> *"And do not strain your eyes toward that with which We have given enjoyment to some groups of them — the splendour of the life of this world — that We may test them therein. And the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring."* (Qur'an 20:131)

The instruction is about the eyes — the direction of attention — not about the contents of the treasury.

**The Modern Manufacture of Preference**

The verse has acquired an unprecedented context. Every previous generation had to construct its preference for the world out of its own resources. The present generation has the preference manufactured for it, continuously, by industries whose revenue depends on it.

The mechanism is well documented and worth naming precisely, because the Qur'an's diagnosis is exact. The verse in Sūrah al-Takāthur describes it:

> *"Rivalry in increase distracts you, until you visit the graves."* (Qur'an 102:1–2)

*Takāthur* — the competition for more. The verse identifies both the activity and its endpoint, and the endpoint is the grave: the competition continues until it is terminated by death, at which point the competitor discovers what the competition was worth.

The modern apparatus of advertising, social comparison, and continuous novelty does not create a new human weakness; it industrialises an old one. What was previously a private inclination is now an engineered environment. The person who wishes not to prefer this world must now actively refuse a preference that is being produced in him every hour by parties who have studied his psychology more carefully than he has.

This is why verse 40's counterpart — *and forbids the soul from caprice* — is not a pious generality but a description of a real and difficult act. In an environment engineered to produce preference, the forbidding is work.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:39

> **truly Hellfire is the refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bitter Word for Home**

The word is *al-maʾwā*, from *a-w-y*, the root of resorting, taking shelter, coming home. The *maʾwā* is the place a person returns to at the end of the day — the shelter, the dwelling, the place of rest. It is one of the warmest words in Arabic, and the Qur'an uses it of the Garden as well as of the Fire.

Its use here is therefore deliberately bitter. The verse does not say "Hellfire is his punishment." It says "Hellfire is his *refuge*" — the place he comes home to, the shelter he resorts to, the only address he has. The word converts the destination into a dwelling, and the dwelling into an identity.

Al-Rāzī notes the implication: a *maʾwā* is a final abode, not a temporary stop. The verse is describing not a punishment with a term but a residence.

**The Restriction Al-Rāzī Places on the Verse**

Al-Rāzī reads this verse in close connection with verse 37, and his reading has been influential. The two verses together, on his account, describe the person who has reached the *greatest* degree of corruption — the combination of rebellion in the intellect and world-preference in the action. For such a person, Hellfire is the *maʾwā*, the final abode. For those who fall short of that combination, there may be punishment, but Hell will not be their ultimate refuge.

The reading is grounded in a large body of evidence, and it is the mainstream Sunni position. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever dies knowing that there is no god but God will enter the Garden."* (Muslim 26)

> *"God says: 'By My might and My majesty, and My glory and My exaltation, I will bring out of the Fire whoever said: there is no god but God.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 2593; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

And the reports describing the intercession and the emptying of the Fire of the believers are numerous:

> *"I know the last of the people of the Fire to leave it and the last of the people of the Garden to enter it: a man who will come crawling, and God will say to him: 'Go and enter the Garden.' …And he will be given the like of the world and ten times it."* (al-Bukhārī 6571; Muslim 186)

The doctrinal structure is therefore graded rather than binary. The two verses 37–41 describe the extremes, and between them lies the whole of ordinary human life — the believers who sin, the repentant, the deficient, the ones whose good outweighs and the ones whose evil does. The Qur'an addresses them elsewhere in detail, and it does not leave them in doubt:

> *"And others have acknowledged their sins, mixing a righteous deed with another evil. Perhaps God will relent upon them. Truly God is Forgiving, Merciful."* (Qur'an 9:102)

**What the Verse Actually Determines**

Given that restriction, the verse's force is not diminished; it is sharpened. It is addressed to the person for whom transgression has become a posture and world-preference a settled orientation — the person who has not merely sinned but organised himself around the sin, who has made the world his object and the limit his obstacle, and who has done so knowingly and persistently.

For such a person the verse states the outcome in the starkest available terms: it will be his home. Not a place he passes through. Not a correction he endures. A residence.

The Qur'an describes the psychology of that residence elsewhere, and the description is the point:

> *"And they will cry out therein: 'Our Lord, bring us out — we will do good, other than what we used to do.' [It will be said:] 'Did We not give you a life long enough for whoever would remember to remember therein? And the warner came to you. So taste — for the wrongdoers have no helper.'"* (Qur'an 35:37)

The reply contains the entire argument of this sūrah. The life was long enough. The warner came. Both facts were true, and both were available, and neither was used. The residence is the consequence of a preference that had every opportunity to be revised.

**Why the Word "Refuge" Is the Right Word**

There is a further reason for the Qur'an's choice, and it is the reason that makes the verse devastating rather than merely frightening.

Every human being seeks a refuge. The search for a place of final safety — where one cannot be harmed, where one belongs, where nothing further is required — is among the deepest of human drives. It expresses itself in the acquisition of a home, the building of a career, the securing of a reputation, the accumulation of resources, the pursuit of a relationship that will not fail.

The verse says that the search succeeds, and that the outcome depends on where the search was directed. The person who sought his refuge in this world, and took what he needed from it regardless of limit, arrives at the refuge he built. It is called Hellfire. The person who sought his refuge in the standing before his Lord, and restrained himself accordingly, arrives at the refuge he built. It is called the Garden.

In both cases the verse uses the same word, and the symmetry is the whole teaching: the *maʾwā* is not assigned arbitrarily. It is the destination of a direction maintained over a lifetime.

**The Verse Read From the Other Side**

The verse is severe, and its severity is precisely calibrated to its function. It is not a threat deployed to frighten people into compliance; it is a description given in advance so that the outcome is not a surprise. The Qur'an's consistent practice is to state the consequence before the choice, in terms clear enough that no one can later claim ignorance.

And the statement is made to people who still have the capacity to change their direction. This is the essential feature of every warning in the Qur'an: it is addressed to the living. The person described in verses 37–39 is not yet in the Fire; he is in the world, reading or hearing, with the preference still being made and the direction still alterable.

The verse that follows supplies the alternative in the same number of words, with the same structure, and with the same word for home.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:40

> **As for one who fears standing before his Lord and forbids the soul from caprice,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterpart, in Two Movements**

The verse is built as the exact inverse of verses 37–38, and the symmetry is precise:

*ṭaghā* — he transgressed → *khāfa maqāma rabbihi* — he feared the standing before his Lord.
*āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* — he preferred the life of this world → *nahā al-nafsa ʿani al-hawā* — he forbade the soul from caprice.

Each element of the first portrait has its mirror in the second, and the mirrors are not merely verbal. Al-Rāzī draws out the logic: rebellion implies ignorance, because no one who knows God and his own insignificance before Him will rebel; and fear implies knowledge, as the Qur'an states: *"only those among His servants who have knowledge fear God"* (35:28). So the first element of the pair is the intellectual correction. And just as rebellion is connected to love of the world, the fear of God leads the soul to obedience and good deeds — so the second element is the practical correction.

Two movements, then: the correction of what one knows, and the correction of what one does. Neither alone produces the person described here.

**"Maqām Rabbihī": The Standing, or the Station**

The phrase *maqāma rabbihi* admits two readings, and both are sound.

The first: the place of standing — the standing before God on the Day of Judgment, when every person will stand and answer. This is the reading most commentators prefer, and it is confirmed by the parallel verse in Sūrah al-Raḥmān:

> *"And for one who feared the standing before his Lord are two Gardens."* (Qur'an 55:46)

The second: the station of his Lord — that is, God's position of oversight, His standing over the servant, His knowledge of him. On this reading the fear is not of a future event but of a present fact: the awareness of being continuously observed by the One who sees everything.

The two readings are not in competition; they describe the same state at two moments. The person who lives in the awareness of being seen now will be able to stand then. The Qur'an describes the same awareness in the definition of excellence in religion:

> *"Excellence is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

The second half of that report — *and if you do not see Him, He sees you* — is the *maqām* of this verse in its second reading. It is the state available to everyone, and the tradition treats it as the achievable minimum of a spiritual life.

**The Analogy of the Thorny Path**

The tradition's most famous definition of this state was given by Ubayy ibn Kaʿb to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and it has been quoted ever since because it is exact. ʿUmar asked him what *taqwā* is, and Ubayy replied with a question:

> *"Have you ever walked on a path full of thorns?"* ʿUmar said: "Yes." He said: *"What did you do?"* ʿUmar said: "I gathered up my garment and looked at where I was placing my feet." Ubayy said: *"That is taqwā."* (preserved in the ascetic literature; attributed to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb)

The definition contains everything the verse says and nothing else. The path is dangerous, but the danger is not diffuse — it is specific, located, and knowable. The traveller does not panic; he prepares. He lifts his garment so it does not catch. He looks at each placement. He does not stop walking.

Three features of the analogy deserve attention. First, the fear is productive: it does not immobilise but organises. Second, it is continuous: it operates at each step, not only at the beginning of the journey. Third, it is precise: the person is not generally afraid of everything; he is specifically aware of where the thorns are, which requires knowledge.

The person who "fears the standing before his Lord" is walking the same path in the same way. He has not stopped living. He has gathered his garment and is looking at his feet.

**"Nahā al-Nafsa ʿAni al-Hawā": Forbidding the Soul**

The second half of the verse introduces three terms that require careful definition.

*Nahā* — he forbade, he restrained, he prohibited. The verb implies authority. One does not forbid an equal; one forbids someone under one's command. The verse therefore presupposes that the self is not the person — it is something the person has authority over, and the exercise of that authority is the act being described.

*Al-nafs* — the soul, the self. The Qur'an describes it in three states, and the three constitute its psychology of the inner life. The first:

> *"And I do not absolve the soul. Truly the soul commands evil, except what my Lord has mercy upon."* (Qur'an 12:53)

— the *nafs ammārah*, the commanding soul, whose default instruction is toward appetite. The second:

> *"And I swear by the self-reproaching soul."* (Qur'an 75:2)

— the *nafs lawwāmah*, which blames itself after the act. The third:

> *"O soul at peace, return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing."* (Qur'an 89:27–28)

— the *nafs muṭmaʾinnah*, at rest. The movement from the first to the third is the whole of the spiritual life, and verse 40 describes the mechanism of the movement: the forbidding.

*Al-hawā* — caprice, inclination, appetite, whim. The word is not neutral in the Qur'an. It is used consistently for desire that has not been ordered by revelation:

> *"…and do not follow caprice, for it will lead you astray from the way of God."* (Qur'an 38:26)

> *"And who is more astray than one who follows his caprice without guidance from God?"* (Qur'an 28:50)

> *"Have you seen the one who takes his caprice as his god?"* (Qur'an 45:23)

The Qur'an's anthropology is therefore clear: a person follows either God and His Messenger, or caprice. There is no third option, because every act is performed for a reason, and the reason is either ordered to a revealed standard or it is not.

**Why the Two Halves Are Both Necessary**

It would be possible to imagine either half alone, and the tradition addressed both possibilities.

Knowledge without restraint produces the person who knows exactly what is right and does it not. The Qur'an's diagnosis of the Communities of the Book includes this condition:

> *"The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not carry it is like a donkey carrying books."* (Qur'an 62:5)

Restraint without knowledge produces the ascetic who mortifies himself in the wrong directions — who forbids what is permitted, who exhausts himself in supererogation while neglecting the obligatory, whose discipline has no criterion. The Prophet ﷺ addressed this condition directly when he heard of the three men who vowed to pray all night, fast every day, and never marry:

> *"By God, I am the most fearful of God among you and the most mindful of Him — yet I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. So whoever turns away from my sunnah is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401)

The two halves of verse 40 are therefore a complete prescription: the fear of the standing supplies the criterion, and the forbidding of the soul supplies the execution. A person with one and not the other is, in the tradition's language, incomplete.

**The Struggle Named**

The tradition gave the practice in verse 40 a technical name: *mujāhadat al-nafs*, the striving against the self. The Prophet ﷺ described it in a report that redefined the word for struggle:

> *"The one who strives (al-mujāhid) is the one who strives against his own soul in obedience to God."* (al-Tirmidhī 1621; Aḥmad)

And he defined strength in the same terms:

> *"The strong man is not the one who overcomes people by force. The strong man is the one who controls himself at the time of anger."* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609)

The second report is precise about the locus of the struggle. It is not a general asceticism; it is the specific moment at which the appetite demands something and the person says no. The moment of anger is named because it is the clearest case — the appetite is unmistakable, the temptation to give in is enormous, and the outcome is visible within minutes.

The Prophet ﷺ asked for the qualities required in a single supplication:

> *"O God, I ask You for guidance, and God-consciousness, and chastity, and self-sufficiency."* (Muslim 2721)

The four terms map onto verse 40 exactly. Guidance (*hudā*) is the knowledge of the standing. God-consciousness (*taqwā*) is the fear itself. Chastity (*ʿafāf*) is the forbidding of the soul from what is forbidden. Self-sufficiency (*ghinā*) is the independence from the world that makes the forbidding possible.

**The Verse Applied**

The verse describes a person in two acts, and both acts are available today.

The first is the maintenance of the awareness: that there will be a standing, that it will be before the One who already knows, that the file is complete and the reckoning will not be delegated to anyone else. This is not a mood; it is a practice, and the tradition legislated its forms — the five prayers as fixed appointments with the standing, the daily review, the remembrance of death, the recitation that keeps the fact present.

The second is the forbidding: the actual, repeated, unglamorous refusals. The thing not said in the argument. The money not spent. The look not taken. The revenge not pursued. The obligation performed when no one would have noticed its omission. Each of these is a *nahy* — a prohibition issued by a person to his own soul, from a position of authority he has to keep asserting, because the soul does not accept the authority as permanent.

The person who performs both is the one described in the verse, and the verse's promise is given in the next line. It is worth noticing what the promise does not say. It does not say that such a person was sinless, or that he never failed, or that his desires were extinguished. It says he feared, and he forbade. Both are verbs of continuing effort, not states of achieved perfection. The verse is addressed to the person who is trying, and it promises the Garden to him — not to the one who arrived.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:41

> **truly the Garden is the refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Word, the Other Home**

The verse is identical in structure to verse 39, and the identity is the point. *Fa-inna al-jannata hiya al-maʾwā* — "truly the Garden is the refuge." The same emphatic particle, the same pronoun of specification, the same word for home.

The Qur'an has constructed a perfect antithesis across five verses:

| verse 37–39 | verse 40–41 |
|---|---|
| he transgressed | he feared the standing before his Lord |
| he preferred the life of this world | he forbade the soul from caprice |
| Hellfire is the refuge | the Garden is the refuge |

Two acts on one side, two acts on the other, one destination each. No third category is described here, and no other factor is mentioned — not lineage, not wealth, not ethnicity, not the quantity of ritual, not the opinion of others. The verse reduces the human situation to two directions and two homes.

**"Al-Jannah": The Word Means a Garden, and It Means Covering**

The Arabic word for Paradise is *jannah*, from the root *j-n-n*, to cover, to conceal, to shield. The same root gives *junnah* (a shield), *jinn* (beings concealed from sight), and *janīn* (the fetus, concealed in the womb). A *jannah* is a garden because its ground is covered by vegetation — a place of shade, enclosure, and concealment.

The etymology is theologically significant. The Garden is named not for its pleasures but for its covering: it is a place where the person is sheltered, hidden from harm, enclosed in safety. It is the *maʾwā* in the fullest sense — not merely a destination but a protection.

The Qur'an's descriptions are extensive and sensory: rivers of water, milk, honey, and wine that does not intoxicate; shade extended; fruits within reach; couches raised; garments of silk and brocade; companions purified; and beneath all of it, the removal of every form of harm:

> *"They will not hear therein idle speech or sin, only the saying: 'Peace, peace.'"* (Qur'an 56:25–26)

> *"And We will remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment — [they will be] as brothers, upon thrones facing one another. No fatigue will touch them therein, and they will not be removed from it."* (Qur'an 15:47–48)

The last clause is the definition of the *maʾwā*: they will not be removed from it. This is the one feature of the Garden that no worldly refuge possesses. Every shelter in this world is temporary, and the knowledge of its temporariness is what prevents it from being a rest.

**The Report That Sets the Limit of the Description**

The tradition is careful to state that all such descriptions are approximations, and the Prophet ﷺ said so in terms that have become the standard reference:

> *"God says: 'I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824)

The report is a quotation of the Qur'an's own statement (32:17), and its function is to prevent the descriptions from being taken as complete. The rivers, the shade, the fruits are true; they are also, by definition, described in the vocabulary of a world in which rivers, shade, and fruits are the best things available. The reality exceeds the language.

The same principle governs the greatest of the Garden's contents:

> *"[Some] faces that Day will be radiant, looking at their Lord."* (Qur'an 75:22–23)

The vision of God is, in the tradition's consensus, the summit of the Garden's reward, and it is not describable in sensory terms at all.

**The Human Search for a Home**

The verse's use of *maʾwā* addresses something deeper than the promise of pleasure, and it is worth naming because it explains why the verse is not merely a reward but an answer.

Every human being seeks a place of final rest — a condition in which nothing further is needed, nothing further is at risk, and nothing further is required of him. The search is universal and it is unsuccessful in this world, for a structural reason: everything in this world is subject to loss, and a refuge that can be lost is not a refuge.

The Qur'an states the point directly:

> *"And the life of this world is not but play and diversion. And the abode of the Hereafter is the true life, if only they knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

The phrase *la-hiya al-ḥayawān* — "it is the true life," or "the life that is life" — uses an intensive form that means life in its fullest sense. The implication is that what passes for life here is a preliminary version, and the search for rest here is a search conducted in the wrong location.

This is why no achievement satisfies. The person who obtains the house discovers that the house is not the rest. The person who obtains the position discovers that the position brings new anxieties. The person who obtains the relationship discovers that the relationship is subject to time. Each was pursued as a *maʾwā* and each turned out to be a stage. The Qur'an's account of this is not pessimism; it is a diagnosis with a remedy, and the remedy is the relocation of the search to the only place where the object exists.

**Why the Verse Follows the Two Acts**

The placement of the promise after the description is deliberate, and it excludes a reading that the tradition consistently rejected.

The Garden is not promised to the person who believes in a proposition. It is promised to the person who *feared the standing* and *forbade the soul*. Both are acts, both are continuous, and both are performed in this world under conditions of difficulty. The verse is not offering a reward for assent; it is offering a destination for a direction.

The Prophet ﷺ described the relation between the two with a precision that has been quoted for fourteen centuries:

> *"The Garden is veiled by hardships, and the Fire is veiled by desires."* (al-Bukhārī 6487; Muslim 2822)

The two destinations are equally real and equally reachable, and each is hidden behind the opposite of what attracts. The Garden is behind the things that are difficult — the early prayer, the fast, the restraint, the honesty that costs, the forgiveness that humiliates. The Fire is behind the things that are attractive. The human being's ordinary tendency is therefore to move toward the veiled Fire and away from the veiled Garden, and the entire function of revelation is to move the veils.

**The Two Verses Together**

Read as a pair, verses 37–41 constitute the moral centre of the sūrah, and they are among the most compact ethical statements in the Qur'an.

They define a person by his relation to limits and his relation to desire. They assign destinations on that basis alone. They make the destinations permanent — *maʾwā*, home, not a temporary lodging. And they do all of this in five short verses, addressed to a person who has not yet arrived at either.

The last four verses of the sūrah turn, after this, to a question the Makkans actually asked. Having established that there are two destinations and that the direction is chosen by acts, the sūrah addresses the question of when the destinations open — and refuses to answer it, for a reason that the following verses will make clear.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:42

> **They ask thee about the Hour, "When shall it set in?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Nautical Question**

The phrase translated "when shall it set in" is *ayyāna mursāhā*, and it contains a metaphor that the translation loses. The word *mursā* is built on the root *r-s-w* — the same root used in verse 32 of this sūrah for the anchoring of the mountains (*arsāhā*) — and it denotes the mooring or berthing of a ship, the point at which a vessel arrives at its destination and is made fast.

The question is therefore: *when does it dock?* The image presents the Hour as a ship already at sea, travelling toward a harbour, and the questioners are asking for an arrival time.

Al-Qurṭubī notes the implication of the metaphor: a ship that is berthing is a ship that is near. The metaphor itself, whether the questioners intended it or not, contains the answer to the question they think they are asking — the Hour is not a distant possibility but an approaching arrival.

The same root appears in the Qur'an in the description of the Fire as fixed and immovable, and in the description of mountains as anchors. The Hour is, on the metaphor of this verse, a vessel that will berth and be made fast, and no one will be able to move it.

**Who Was Asking, and Why**

The commentators record two categories of questioner.

The first, and the one al-Ālūsī and al-Qurṭubī emphasise, is the mocker. The Makkans who asked were not seeking information; they were using the question as an argument. If Muhammad cannot say when it will come, they reasoned, then either he does not know it exists or his claim is hollow. The question was a trap, and the sūrah's response declines to enter it.

The second is the sincere inquirer. Not everyone who asked was mocking, and the Qur'an addresses the same question in a verse that supplies a full answer to the sincere form of it:

> *"They question thee about the Hour, when it will set in. Say: 'Knowledge thereof lies only with my Lord. None save He shall manifest it at its proper time. Heavy shall it weigh upon the heavens and the earth. It shall not come upon you but suddenly.' They question thee as if thou knew it well. Say: 'Knowledge thereof lies only with God, but most of mankind know not.'"* (Qur'an 7:187)

That verse is the fullest statement in the Qur'an on the subject, and it contains four claims: the knowledge is God's alone; He alone will manifest it at its time; it is heavy upon the heavens and the earth; and it will come suddenly.

**"Heavy Upon the Heavens and the Earth"**

The phrase *thaqulat fī al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "heavy in the heavens and the earth" — has been explained in several ways. Some say it means that the knowledge of it is heavy, i.e. concealed and inaccessible to the inhabitants of both. Others say it means the event itself will be tremendous for both realms. Others say it means that the heavens and the earth are weighed down by its approach.

All three are consistent with the rest of the Qur'anic description. The Hour is not a local event; it is the termination of the entire created order, and the phrase indicates that the termination affects the heavens as well as the earth.

**The Suddenness**

The fourth claim of 7:187 — *it shall not come upon you but suddenly*, *lā taʾtīkum illā baghtah* — is confirmed elsewhere and illustrated in the Sunnah with an image that has become standard in the tradition:

> *"The Hour will be established while two men are spreading out a garment between them, and they will neither fold it nor trade in it; and the Hour will be established while a man is lifting a morsel to his mouth, and he will not eat it."* (al-Bukhārī 7121; Muslim 2954)

The two images are chosen for their absolute ordinariness. A transaction in progress. A meal in progress. The Hour does not arrive after a warning period; it arrives inside an unremarkable moment, and the unremarkable moment is never completed.

This is why the question "when?" is not merely unanswerable but practically irrelevant. A person who knew the date would prepare before the date; a person who does not know it must prepare always. The concealment does not deprive him of anything useful — it removes the only piece of information that would permit him to postpone.

**"I and the Hour Were Sent Like These Two"**

The Prophet ﷺ answered the question of the Hour's proximity in a gesture that has become one of the most quoted images in the tradition. Sahl ibn Saʿd narrated:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ said, holding up his index and middle fingers: 'I and the Hour were sent like these two.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6504; Muslim 2950)

The two fingers held side by side, one slightly ahead of the other — the distance between the Prophet's ﷺ mission and the Hour is the distance between two adjacent fingers. The report does not give a date; it gives a relation. The mission and the Hour belong to the same dispensation, and there is no further prophet and no further scripture between them.

This is why the Islamic tradition has always understood itself as living in the last age. Not because anyone knows the duration, but because the sequence is closed: the final messenger has come, and the only thing that remains is the final event.

**Why the Question Persists**

The question "when?" has been asked of every religious tradition by every generation, and the persistence is instructive. It is not usually a request for information. It is a way of managing a fact that is uncomfortable.

A person who believes an event is distant can hold the belief without adjusting his life. A person who believes it is near must either adjust or disbelieve. The question "when?" is therefore often a negotiation: give me a date, and I will decide how seriously to take it. The Qur'an's refusal to provide a date is not a withholding of information; it is a refusal of the negotiation.

The response given in Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt is addressed to exactly this motive:

> *"They ask you: 'When is the Day of Judgment?' [It is] the Day they are tried upon the Fire. 'Taste your trial — this is what you were seeking to hasten.'"* (Qur'an 51:12–14)

The reply does not give a date. It gives the content of the Day, and it notes that the questioners had been asking for it to be hastened. The question and the answer are then brought into contact: you asked when; here is what; do you still want it hastened?

The sūrah you are reading gives the same answer in different words. The Hour is not a date to be awaited but an arrival to be prepared for, and verses 43–46 explain why the date is withheld and what should be done instead.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:43

> **What hast thou to do with mentioning it?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Addressed to the Prophet**

The verse is one of the few places in the Qur'an where God addresses the Prophet ﷺ with what appears to be a rebuke, and its exact sense has been discussed carefully by the commentators.

*Fīma anta min dhikrāhā* — literally, "in what [concern] are you regarding its mention?" The two principal readings are complementary.

The first: God is telling the Prophet ﷺ that he has no business speaking of a matter about which he has no knowledge. The knowledge of the Hour is not his, and he is not to engage with the question on the questioner's terms.

The second, recorded by al-Ṭabarī and others: the verse is a response to the objection of the Makkans, who contended that the Prophet ﷺ had no right to speak about the Hour, the Resurrection, and the Unseen if he did not know when they would occur. On this reading the verse is not a rebuke at all but a reply to the critics: *what business is it of yours to demand that he mention it on your terms?*

Al-Qurṭubī notes that the former reading is supported by a report from ʿĀʾishah that the Prophet ﷺ wished to ask God about the moment of the Hour, and was told that he was asking for knowledge he had no right to. The report's chain is not among the strongest, and it should be cited with that caveat — but the doctrine it expresses is established beyond doubt by many other texts.

**The Prophet's Knowledge Is Bounded by Revelation**

The Qur'an states the boundary explicitly and repeatedly, and the statements are remarkable for their directness.

> *"Say: 'I do not say to you that I have the treasures of God, nor that I know the Unseen, nor do I say to you that I am an angel. I only follow what is revealed to me.'"* (Qur'an 6:50)

> *"Say: 'I do not possess for myself any benefit or harm, except what God wills. And if I knew the Unseen, I would have multiplied good, and evil would not have touched me. I am only a warner and a bringer of glad tidings to a people who believe.'"* (Qur'an 7:188)

> *"Say: 'I am no innovation among the messengers, and I know not what will be done with me or with you. I only follow that which has been revealed to me, and I am naught but a clear warner.'"* (Qur'an 46:9)

The third of these is the most striking. The Prophet ﷺ is instructed to state publicly that he does not know what will be done with him or with his questioners — that is, that he has no privileged information about his own outcome beyond what has been revealed.

> *"[He is] the Knower of the Unseen, and He does not disclose His Unseen to anyone — except to a messenger with whom He is pleased, and He places before him watchers and behind him."* (Qur'an 72:26–27)

The exception is real: the Prophet ﷺ knew a great deal of the Unseen, because a great deal was disclosed to him. But the disclosure was selective and it did not include the Hour.

**The Epistemic Honesty as Evidence**

There is an argument embedded in this verse that the tradition developed, and it is a strong one.

Every claimant to prophetic authority in history has been pressed for predictions, and the ordinary response is to provide them. The claimant who can say when the world will end, who will win the battle, what the price will be, has an enormous advantage over the one who cannot. The incentive to predict is overwhelming, and the history of religion is full of people who yielded to it.

Muhammad ﷺ did not. He was asked repeatedly, by friends and enemies, and he consistently declined — not with evasion but with an explicit statement that the knowledge was not his. The Qur'an supplied the doctrinal ground for the refusal and made it permanent.

Ibn Taymiyyah made this one of the evidences of the Prophet's ﷺ truthfulness. A liar claiming prophetic authority would exploit every available claim, and the claim to knowledge of the Unseen is the most valuable one available. The consistent refusal — maintained under pressure, recorded in the scripture, and never revised — is difficult to explain on the assumption of imposture.

**The Modern Demand for Predictions**

The verse has a contemporary application that is easy to miss. Modern readers frequently approach religion with a specific expectation: that it should provide information about the future, that it should predict, that its value should be measured by its forecasts.

This expectation is not new. It is the expectation of the questioners in verse 42, and the Qur'an's answer to them is the same as its answer to every subsequent generation. The revelation is not an information service. It is a warning and a guidance, and its authority does not rest on its predictions.

The verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ — *what have you to do with mentioning it?* — and in doing so it establishes a rule for everyone who transmits the message. The preacher, the teacher, the writer, and the believer are not authorised to supply what the Prophet ﷺ declined to supply. Every attempt to date the Hour, to identify its signs with current events, or to present speculation as knowledge is a departure from the position this verse establishes.

The tradition's practice was accordingly cautious. The great exegetes recorded the reports about the signs of the Hour with their chains and their gradings, and they distinguished clearly between what was authenticated and what was not. Where the text was silent, they said so. This is the discipline the verse requires, and it is the discipline that the popular religious literature most often abandons.

**What the Verse Leaves the Prophet**

The refusal is not a deprivation. The verse that follows immediately supplies what remains, and what remains is enough:

> *"Unto thy Lord is [knowledge of] the end thereof."* (79:44)

> *"Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it."* (79:45)

The knowledge belongs to God. The warning belongs to the Prophet ﷺ. Between the two lies the whole of the prophetic office, and the verse defines it by subtraction: the Prophet ﷺ is not an oracle, and he was never meant to be one.

There is a liberation in this for everyone who transmits the message. The teacher who does not know the answer is not thereby disqualified; he is correctly situated. The obligation is to deliver what has been delivered, and to say plainly, when the question exceeds it, that the knowledge is with God.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:44

> **Unto thy Lord is [knowledge of] the end thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Where the Knowledge Stops**

The word *muntahāhā* is from *n-h-y*, the root of ending, terminating, reaching the furthest point. The *muntahā* is the terminus, the limit beyond which a thing does not go. The verse states that the terminus of the knowledge of the Hour is God — that the knowledge reaches its limit at Him, and does not extend past Him to anyone.

The formulation is precise and it excludes every intermediate. It does not say that the knowledge is difficult, or hidden, or available to the initiated, or disclosed to particular saints. It says that its terminus is God.

**The Five Keys of the Unseen**

The Prophet ﷺ specified the scope of this exclusive knowledge in a report that the tradition has treated as definitive. Ibn ʿUmar narrated:

> *"The keys of the Unseen are five, and none knows them but God: none knows what will happen tomorrow but God; none knows what is in the wombs but God; none knows when the rain will come but God; none knows in what land he will die but God; and none knows when the Hour will come but God."* (al-Bukhārī 4697)

The report enumerates the five things the Qur'an reserves in Sūrah Luqmān:

> *"Truly with God is the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and He knows what is in the wombs. And no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die. Truly God is All-Knowing, All-Aware."* (Qur'an 31:34)

The five are not arbitrary. They are the five facts about which human beings most desperately want knowledge and about which they are most completely powerless: the future, the unborn, the weather, the place of death, and the end. In every age, entire industries have been built on the pretence of supplying them. The verse names all five and assigns them to God alone.

**The Report of Jibrīl**

The most famous exchange on the subject occurred in the presence of the Companions, and it is the report by which the tradition defines the religion's basic categories. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb narrated the arrival of a man in white clothing with black hair, who sat before the Prophet ﷺ and asked him about Islam, then about faith, then about excellence — and then:

> *"He said: 'Inform me about the Hour.' The Prophet ﷺ said: 'The one asked about it knows no more than the one asking.'"* (Muslim 8; al-Bukhārī 50)

*Bi-lladhī yusʾalu ʿanhā bi-aʿlama mina al-sāʾil* — "the one questioned is not more knowing than the questioner." The answer is a statement of complete equality in ignorance: neither of us knows.

The man then asked about its signs, and the Prophet ﷺ answered — the slave girl giving birth to her mistress, the barefoot naked shepherds competing in tall buildings. So the signs were disclosed; the time was not. The distinction is exact and it governs the entire tradition's treatment of the subject: the signs are within the revealed knowledge, the date is not.

ʿUmar concludes the report by noting that the questioner was Jibrīl, who came to teach the people their religion — which means that the exchange was a lesson, and the lesson's content was the limit.

**Why the Knowledge Is Withheld**

The question deserves an answer, because the withholding is not arbitrary.

Consider the alternative. Suppose the date were known. What would follow? Every person who knew it would postpone preparation until shortly before the date. The knowledge would not produce vigilance; it would produce procrastination, calibrated precisely to the amount of warning available. The person who knows the examination is in a year does not study today.

The concealment is therefore not a deprivation but the condition of the warning's effectiveness. Because no one knows, every moment is a possible moment, and the only rational posture is continuous readiness. The Qur'an states this logic in a verse that is often quoted and rarely followed to its conclusion:

> *"And the Hour is not but a blink of the eye, or nearer still."* (Qur'an 16:77)

> *"Mankind's reckoning has drawn near, while they turn away in heedlessness."* (Qur'an 21:1)

The second verse contains the whole situation: the reckoning is near, and the response is turning away. The nearness is not the problem; the turning is.

**The Practical Consequence**

The verse has an immediate application, and it is one of the most useful in the sūrah.

A person who does not know when he will die — and no person does — is in exactly the position of a person who does not know when the Hour will come. The two ignorances are the same ignorance at different scales, and the correct response is the same.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the instruction in the form of a list that has been memorised by Muslim children for fourteen centuries:

> *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

The five pairs are a complete inventory of the resources that are available now and will not be available later. The instruction is not to wait for a warning, because no warning is coming. It is to act on the assumption that the present condition is temporary, which is the only assumption the evidence supports.

The verse is therefore not a statement about God's knowledge for its own sake. It is a statement about the reader's situation: the information that would permit you to postpone does not exist, and it does not exist for you at the level of your own life either.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:45

> **Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Office Defined by Its Limits**

The particle *innamā* — "only," "nothing but" — restricts, and the restriction here is a definition of the prophetic office. *Innamā anta mundhiru man yakhshāhā* — "you are only a warner for whoever fears it."

The word *mundhir* is from *n-dh-r*, to warn of an approaching danger that the warned person cannot see. The *nadhīr* is not a punisher, not an enforcer, and not a guarantor of outcomes. He is the one who has seen what is coming and tells you.

The Qur'an states the same limitation repeatedly, and the repetition is deliberate:

> *"And you are not over them a controller."* (Qur'an 88:22)

> *"So remind — you are only a reminder."* (Qur'an 88:21)

> *"Truly you do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 28:56)

> *"Not upon you is their guidance, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 2:272)

> *"If they turn away, your duty is only the delivery, and Ours is the reckoning."* (Qur'an 3:20)

The last of these is the cleanest statement of the division of labour: delivery is the messenger's; the outcome is God's. The Prophet ﷺ is not responsible for the response, and he is not authorised to produce it by any means other than the warning.

**"For Whoever Fears It": The Condition of Reception**

The final phrase restricts the warning's audience, and the restriction is the sūrah's closing echo. Verse 26 said: *"Truly in that is a lesson for whosoever fears."* Verse 45 says: *"a warner for whosoever fears it."* The same condition, in the same word — *yakhshā* — appears at the end of the Moses section and at the end of the sūrah, framing the whole chapter.

The condition is not a gatekeeping device. It is a statement about how warnings work. A warning is only received by a person who is capable of being warned — that is, by a person who believes that danger is real, that he is susceptible to it, and that his actions can affect the outcome. Remove any of the three and the warning is noise.

The Qur'an states this in a verse that pairs the two conditions explicitly:

> *"You only warn one who follows the reminder and fears the Compassionate in the Unseen. So give him glad tidings of forgiveness and a noble reward."* (Qur'an 36:11)

Following the reminder and fearing in the Unseen — the two dispositions that make a warning effective. Both are absent in the person who mocks, and the mockers of verse 42 are therefore outside the audience, not because they have been excluded but because they have made themselves incapable of hearing.

**The Image of the Fire and the Moths**

The Prophet ﷺ described his own office in an image that is among the most vivid in the entire tradition, and it is the perfect commentary on this verse. Abū Hurayrah narrated:

> *"My example and your example is that of a man who lit a fire, and when it lit up what was around him, the moths and the creatures that fall into fire began falling into it, and he began pushing them away — but they overcame him and threw themselves in. That is my example and yours: I take hold of you by your waistbands, [saying] 'Away from the Fire, away from the Fire' — and you overcome me and throw yourselves into it."* (al-Bukhārī 6483; Muslim 2284)

Every element of the image corresponds to a feature of the verse. The man lit the fire — the danger is visible, not hidden. The moths fly into it — not because they are pushed but because they are drawn. He pushes them away — the effort is physical, continuous, and desperate. They overcome him — the warning does not succeed. They throw themselves in — the agency is theirs.

The phrase *"I take hold of you by your waistbands"* — *ākhudhu bi-ḥujuzikum* — is the image of a person physically pulling people back from a fire by their belts. It is not a detached announcement. It is an effort, and the effort fails, and the failure is not the warner's.

The verse's two parts are both present in the image: the office (*you are a warner*) and the condition (*for whoever fears it*). The moths do not fear the fire; they are attracted to it. That is the whole explanation.

**Why the Warning Is Not Compulsion**

The limitation established by this verse is not a weakness in the message; it is a condition of its validity.

If belief could be compelled, it would not be belief. A coerced assent is a report about the coercion, not a conviction about the truth. The Qur'an states the principle with total clarity:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion. The right course has become distinct from error."* (Qur'an 2:256)

> *"And had your Lord willed, all who are on the earth would have believed together. Will you then compel people until they are believers?"* (Qur'an 10:99)

The second verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself and answers a question he evidently felt: whether he should try harder, push harder, compel. The answer is that compulsion is neither possible nor legitimate, because belief is not the kind of thing that can be produced by force.

This doctrine has consequences that the tradition drew out in law. The person who is warned and refuses is not thereby subject to punishment in this world for his refusal. The verse assigns the reckoning to God, and the tradition consistently held that the punishment for rejection belongs to the Hereafter.

**The Verse Applied to Everyone Who Warns**

The verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and in addressing him it establishes the position of everyone who transmits a warning — the parent, the teacher, the friend, the preacher, the physician, the person who tells another that what he is doing is destroying him.

The position is defined by two facts. The delivery is yours, and the outcome is not.

The first fact imposes an obligation: you must speak. The person who sees the danger and says nothing has not discharged his duty, and the Qur'an condemns the concealment of known truth in the strongest terms:

> *"Truly those who conceal what We have sent down of clear signs and guidance, after We have made it clear for the people in the Book — those are cursed by God and cursed by those who curse."* (Qur'an 2:159)

The second fact imposes a limit: you are not responsible for the result. The parent whose child refuses the warning, the friend whose advice is rejected, the teacher whose student does not listen — none of them has failed, provided the delivery was made. This is one of the most practically relieving doctrines in the tradition, because it releases the warner from an impossible burden while preserving his obligation entirely.

The Prophet ﷺ lived this position for twenty-three years. He warned his own uncle, who died without accepting. He warned his own tribe on the mountain of al-Ṣafā. He warned the delegation from Ṭāʾif, who sent their children to throw stones at him. He warned, in every case, and he did not control the outcome in any case — and the tradition does not record any of these as failures.

**The Verse's Place in the Chapter**

Verse 45 completes the sūrah's argument by removing the last objection. The questioners asked when the Hour would come. The answer is that the knowledge belongs to God (v. 44), that the Prophet's ﷺ office is the warning and not the timetable (v. 45), and that the warning is addressed to the person who fears (v. 45) — which is to say, to the reader.

The sūrah has now exhausted its case. The oaths established the administration of the unseen. The Day was described. The objection of the bones was answered. The story of Pharaoh supplied the historical precedent. The catalogue of creation supplied the cosmological argument. The two destinies were defined. And the question of the date has been refused, with the reason given.

One verse remains, and it does not add an argument. It supplies the measure.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:46

> **The Day they see it, it will be as if they had tarried but an evening or the morning thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest of the Estimates**

The verse's two time-words are *ʿashiyyah* and *ḍuḥā*. The *ʿashiyyah* is the evening — from midday until sunset. The *ḍuḥā* is the forenoon, the bright part of the morning, the same word used in verse 29 for the day the sky brings forth and the word that names Sūrah 93.

The two together describe less than a full day: an afternoon, or the morning that precedes it. Of all the Qur'an's estimates of the felt length of a human life, this is the shortest.

Compare the other passages in which the resurrected estimate their stay:

> *"And [mention] the Day when He gathers them — [it will be] as if they had not tarried except an hour of the day, recognising one another."* (Qur'an 10:45)

> *"They will whisper among themselves: 'You tarried only ten.' …The most upright among them in speech will say: 'You tarried only a day.'"* (Qur'an 20:103–104)

> *"He will say: 'How many years did you tarry on earth?' They will say: 'We tarried a day or part of a day — ask those who count.'"* (Qur'an 23:112–113)

> *"And on the Day when the Hour is established, the criminals will swear that they tarried not but an hour. Thus were they deluded."* (Qur'an 30:55)

> *"And on the Day they see what they were promised, it will be as if they had tarried only an hour of a day. [This is] a clear communication — but will any be destroyed except the defiantly disobedient people?"* (Qur'an 46:35)

The range across these verses is from an hour to a day, and verse 46 gives the smallest unit in the Qur'an's inventory: an evening, or its morning. The commentators explain the variation as reflecting different states and different moments of the reckoning, and al-Rāzī notes that the estimates differ because the experience differs — the person in torment will experience the duration differently from the person at rest.

**The Mechanism of Retrospective Compression**

The verse describes a psychological phenomenon that every adult has experienced in miniature, and its operation is worth stating precisely.

Time is not experienced prospectively and retrospectively in the same way. In prospect, a decade is unimaginably long. In retrospect, it is a single object that can be held in the mind at once — and the holding takes a few seconds. The person who has lived seventy years can review the whole of it in a few minutes, and the review will feel shorter than a single afternoon of the years themselves.

This is why older people say that time accelerated. It has not. The mechanism of memory compresses the past into a form whose length is proportional to the act of recall rather than to the duration recalled. The verse describes the completion of that compression: the entire life, seen from the other side, has the felt length of an evening.

**The Report That Completes the Verse**

The tradition supplies a report that is the most powerful commentary on this verse, and it works in both directions — for the one who suffered and the one who enjoyed. Anas ibn Mālik narrated:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'A person will be brought from the people of the Garden who was the most wretched of the people of this world, and he will be dipped once in the Garden, and it will be said to him: O son of Adam, did you ever see any misery? Did any hardship ever pass over you? He will say: No, by God, my Lord — I never saw any misery, and no hardship ever passed over me. And a person will be brought from the people of the Fire who was the most blessed of the people of this world, and he will be dipped once in the Fire, and it will be said to him: O son of Adam, did you ever see any good? Did any comfort ever pass over you? He will say: No, by God, my Lord — I never saw any good, and no comfort ever passed over me.'"* (Muslim 2807)

The report's symmetry is exact. A single moment of the destination erases the entirety of the preceding experience — not merely from the record but from the felt memory. The man who was the most miserable person on earth, having been in the Garden for one moment, cannot recall a single instance of misery. The man who had everything, having been in the Fire for one moment, cannot recall a single instance of good.

The report does not claim that the suffering and the pleasure were not real. They were entirely real. It claims that their felt significance is determined by where they end. A life of seventy years of hardship, ending in the Garden, is retrospectively nothing. A life of seventy years of pleasure, ending in the Fire, is retrospectively nothing.

This is the verse's claim, and the report is its demonstration.

**The Two Estimates, and Which One Matters**

Verse 46 therefore contains two propositions, and they are of very different kinds.

The first is a fact about perception: the duration of a life will feel, at the end, like an evening. This is not a promise or a threat; it is a description of how the retrospect will work.

The second is the implication: the value of a life is therefore not a function of its length or its comfort. If seventy years compress into an evening, then the seventy years were not what mattered. What matters is what the evening was spent on — the direction, the acts, the preference established across verses 37–41.

The Qur'an states the same conclusion in a verse that has been quoted by ascetics for fourteen centuries:

> *"And the life of this world is not but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 3:185)

> *"The life of this world is but play and diversion, and the abode of the Hereafter is the true life, if only they knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

**The Practical Use of the Verse**

The verse is not intended to produce despair about the brevity of life. It is intended to reorganise priorities, and the reorganisation is available now rather than at the end.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the instruction in a list that is the practical form of this verse:

> *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim; declared ṣaḥīḥ)

Each of the five is a resource that is present now and will be absent later, and none of them announces its departure. The person who waits for the announcement will never receive it.

There is also a consoling use of the verse that is easy to miss. If the hardships of a life compress into an evening, then the hardships were shorter than they felt while they were happening. The person in the middle of a difficulty — an illness, a loss, a period of failure, a season of grief — experiences it as the whole of his existence. The verse tells him that it is not. It is part of an evening, and the evening is not what is being evaluated.

This is why the tradition pairs the remembrance of death with the remembrance of the Garden. Neither alone is sufficient. The remembrance of death without the Garden produces despair; the remembrance of the Garden without death produces presumption. Together they produce the state the sūrah has been describing since verse 40: a person who fears the standing and forbids his soul its caprice, working in an evening he knows is short, toward a home he knows is real.

**The Sūrah's Arc**

The chapter began with a wrenching — souls pulled violently from bodies — and it ends with a measurement: an evening, or its morning. Between the two lies everything the sūrah has established.

There is an administration, staffed by agents who do not fail (vv. 1–5). There is a Day on which the settled order loses its composure (vv. 6–9). The objection that the dead cannot return was answered with the argument from the greater creation and the argument from the One who did it first (vv. 10–14, 27–33). The historical precedent was given: a tyrant who was offered purification, refused, claimed divinity, and was seized (vv. 15–26). The two destinies were defined by two acts each — transgression and world-preference, fear and restraint (vv. 37–41). The date was refused, and the reason for the refusal was given (vv. 42–44). The office was defined: a warner, for whoever fears (v. 45).

And the final verse supplies the scale on which all of it should be measured. Whatever you are doing with your life, it is an evening's work. The question the sūrah leaves with the reader is the one it has been building toward since its first word: what are you sending ahead of you, in an evening that is already half over?
