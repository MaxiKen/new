# Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt (Chapter 79) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Late Makkan Sūrah at the Dawn of the Hour-Preaching**

Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt is a Makkan revelation of the middle-to-late Makkan period, and the classical orderers of the chronology — the tradition represented by ʿĀṣim al-Aḥwal, al-Farrāʾ, and the narrations gathered in the introductions to the great tafsīrs — place it in the neighborhood of Sūrah 78, al-Nabaʾ, most likely shortly before or after it. That placement is not a trivial fact of catalogues. The end of Sūrah 78 thunders: *"Truly, We have warned you of a chastisement near at hand"* (78:40), and al-Nāziʿāt opens as though resuming the sentence mid-breath: five oaths hurled like hammer-blows, then *"A Day when the Piercing Blast shall quake"* — the same nearness, the same urgency, now sworn to by God Himself. When one makes an oath by a thing, the commentators observed, the intent is either to display the greatness of the thing sworn by, or to magnify in the listener's heart what is sworn to — or both; and when it is God who swears by His own creatures, both purposes are present at once, because nothing is great except by Him, and He stands in no need of confirming anything to anyone except as a mercy to the one who listens.

The sūrah's own historical hour must be recovered if its pressure is to be felt. Makkah in the middle Makkan years was a city under economic siege and social taunt; its merchants had a single intellectual objection to the whole message, repeated like a refrain: "*Shall we indeed be restored to our former state, even when we are decayed bones?*" — a question the sūrah itself quotes verbatim (79:10–11). The revelation that meets this objection does not argue from the ground up, from the difficulty of reassembling bones; it argues from the sky down, from the Oath-Giver's own power: the beings that wrench souls at death, the heavens raised without a seam, the earth spread with water and pasture — the same Power that built the larger thing can without labor re-make the smaller. This is the sūrah's governing logic, and every one of its forty-six verses serves it.

**Three Names, Three Faces of One Day**

The sūrah is called al-Nāziʿāt, "Those That Wrest Forth," from its first word — the name of the oath itself, and of the angels who tear the soul from the body, or, on other readings, of the stars and of death itself in its five modes. But the older sources record two other names, and each names a different face of the same Day. It is called al-Sāḥirah, "the Wide Expanse," after verse 14 — *"then, behold, they will be upon a wide expanse"* — the bare, flat, uncovered plain on which the whole of humanity will stand at the end of the single cry. And it is called al-Ṭāmmah, "the Overwhelming Calamity," after verse 34 — *"So when the Great Calamity befalls"* — from the root ṭ-m-m, "to be complete, to rise over," as the flood-water rises over everything in its path. A reader who holds the three names together holds the sūrah's outline: the manner of dying (al-Nāziʿāt), the manner of being gathered (al-Sāḥirah), and the manner of the final reckoning that overwhelms every prior trouble (al-Ṭāmmah). The first death is the rehearsal of the last; the last is the first made permanent.

**The Architecture of the Argument**

The sūrah moves in five distinct movements, and the transitions between them are the arguments themselves. Verses 1–5 are the solemn oaths by the wrestlers, the drawners, the gliders, the racers, the governors — five classes of God's obedient agents, on the reality of the Resurrection. Verses 6–14 describe the Day that the oaths guarantee: the Quaker that quakes, its follower, hearts that tremble, eyes that fall, the sneer of the deniers — *"Are we to be restored as we were before?"* — and the answer of divine ease: *"Yet it shall be but a single cry, then, behold, they will be upon a wide expanse."* Verses 15–26 shift from cosmology to history: the first account of Moses and Pharaoh in the chronological order of revelation, a case study in rebellion (ṭughyān) — the word that binds Pharaoh to verse 37's "*as for one who rebels*" — ending with the sentence that labels the whole section: *"Truly in that is a lesson for whosoever fears."* Verses 27–33 turn to the sky, the night and day, the earth, water, pastures, and mountains: the argument from greater to lesser in its purest form — *"Are you more difficult to create or the sky that He has built?"* Verses 34–41 paint the overwhelming Day in its final verdicts: remembrance, the Hellfire brought into plain view, the two abodes for the two kinds of life. And verses 42–46 close the sūrah on the one question the deniers were truly forbidden to ask — *"When shall the Hour set in?"* — and the one thing the Prophet ﷺ was sent to be: *"a warner for whosoever fears it"* — with the verse that dissolves the objection of decay: when they see that Day, all of worldly life will be to them *"but an evening or the morning thereof."*

Read structurally, the sūrah is a single syllogism in five acts: death is attended by angels whose office you cannot see (1–5); therefore the Day they herald is certain, and easy for God (6–14); every tyranny is invited beforehand to repentance and destroyed after confirmation (15–26); the Builder of the heavens can re-build you without difficulty (27–33); the only question worth asking about the Hour is not its date but your readiness (34–46).

**Why the Oaths Fit What Comes After**

Arabic rhetoric calls the oaths at the sūrah's head an *iqsām* whose point is *tanbīh* — the striking of attention — and *taʿẓīm* — the magnifying of what is sworn to. But here the fit between oath and oathed-to is extraordinary. The first word, *al-nāziʿāt*, means those that pull with wrenching strain — the root n-z-ʿ carries the sound of effort, as a rope dragged hand over hand, as an arrow snatched from the quiver, as the bucket hauled from a deep well. The last of the five, *al-mudabbirāt amrā*, means those that manage the affair — order arriving where the wrenching began. The sūrah swears, in sequence, by violence, by extraction, by smooth gliding, by racing, and by governance: the entire career of every soul from the agony of departure, through the transit, to the settled management of the Divine affair. The oaths are not decoration over the subject; they are the subject in motion. And the rhyme-scheme drives this home physically: the short, hammered rhymes of the Makkan sūrahs here fall on *‑an* (gharsan, nashṭan, subhan, sabqan) like the grunting of rope-pullers, then break into the long, open, level vowel of *sāḥirah* — the wide expanse — the very flatness the rhyme itself spreads out before the hearer's ear.

**The Sūrah in the Prophet's Own Prayer**

The place of this sūrah in the earliest Muslim practice is established by a report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (Book of the Calls to Prayer, and again in the Book of Exegesis): an Ansari man led his people in prayer at Juḥāt, and in his prayer he would recite *"By those that wrest violently"* (al-Nāziʿāt) and go no further with it; when his people objected that he was contenting himself with a short sūrah, he answered:

> *"I watched the Messenger of God ﷺ reciting something like it, and I shall not give it up for anything after it."*

The point the reporters drew from the story is that the Prophet ﷺ himself recited al-Nāziʿāt in the dawn prayer — the hour when verses about death and resurrection fall on waking hearts with a particular weight. A community that heard these forty-six verses at dawn, on flat ground before sunrise, heard the sūrah's own imagery enacted: souls drawn out of sleep as from bodies, the light of day brought forth from the darkened night, the gathered congregation a small rehearsal of the wide expanse.

**The Tyrant's Blunder, and Why It Comes First**

That the first full narrative in the chronological order of revelation should be the story of Pharaoh is itself a datum. Pharaoh is the Qur'an's type-case of the man who was given everything — power, a kingdom, a witnessing sign — and answered the sign with the claim to divinity: *"I am your lord most high."* The Makkan notables were not being asked to believe a new myth; they were being told that the old story was a mirror. And the sūrah, to underscore the mirror, refuses Pharaoh even a name: he is "Pharaoh" — a title — seventy-four times in the Qur'an while Moses is named one hundred and thirty-six times, as though the Book were executing in its very usage the verdict of verse 25: God seized him with a punishment exemplary in the Hereafter and in this world, and this-worldly among other things is the erasure of the tyrant's name from the lips of the centuries. The drowning Pharaoh's preserved body became, in the Qur'an's own words, "*a sign for those who come after*" (10:92) — a claim the commentators could read as fulfilled long before Egyptology, because the story outlived the empire, and the empire outlived its pharaohs only as an argument against them.

**How This Edition Proceeds**

The following is an expanded, verse-by-verse commentary on the complete sūrah, generated under the standing project instructions: every one of the forty-six verses is treated as an independent, fully developed discourse — never compressed, never merged, even where the received commentary treats verses in pairs or blocks (the oaths in 1–5, the Day's opening in 6–7, Pharaoh's summons in 18–19, the creation sequence in 28–30, the two fates in 37–39 and 40–41 are here split verse from verse and each given its own weight). The Arabic is unfolded from its roots; the Qur'an's own cross-references are quoted at length in their actual wording; authentic ḥadīth are cited with their collections identified; the views of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAbbās, and other authorities — including the reports gathered in the source's own apparatus — are weighed where they bear; and the reasoning is carried, without sensationalism, into the psychology, the society, and the everyday life of the modern reader. Where the classical commentators themselves suspended judgment — as al-Ṭabarī does on the identity of the five oathed-to beings, and al-Rāzī with his counsel that the interpretations are possible rather than demonstrative — that suspension is reported as part of the scholarship, not as its failure.

*In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.*

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:1

> **By those that wrest violently,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Blow of Five Oaths**

The sūrah opens with a single Arabic word of terrible texture: *al-nāziʿāt* — "those that wrest forth." The article with the plural ending *-āt* marks a class of beings defined by one activity: *nazaʿa* means to pull out, to extract by strain, and the morphology of *fāʿilāt* here is habitual — not "those who once pulled" but "those whose very identity is pulling." Classical lexicographers paint the root's field of vision: the archer snatches an arrow from the quiver (*nazaʿa al-sahm min al-kidān*); the well-drawer hauls the bucket hand over hand until the rope groans; the thorn is wrenched from the cloth. Into every one of these images the Qur'an threads the same nerve: effort applied against resistance. The oath, then, begins with the sound of resistance being overcome — before a single word about the Resurrection is spoken, the ear has already heard something being torn loose. The word *gharsan* that completes the verse is a cognate accusative — a verbal noun from the same field of meaning, intensifying the manner: "a wrestling forth" that is also a *striking*: gh-r-s carries in older Arabic the sense of beating and of uprooting, and the commentators report that the phrase means they wrench the soul with a violence like that of a plant torn out root and branch, earth still clinging, roots snapping one by one.

**What Is Being Sworn By — and Why It Is Left Open**

Most of the earliest authorities — Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī transmit it as the dominant reading from Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, and others (the source records IK, Q, Ṭ, Z) — take *al-nāziʿāt* as the angels of death who wrest the souls of the disbelievers from their bodies. The violence is not cruelty; it is the physics of the situation: the soul *does not wish to face the Judgment*, and it clings, so it must be pulled. A second school saw here the stars or the celestial bodies — the fixed stars and wanderers whose courses, on older cosmological language, "wrench" the vapors and tides of the lower world (Q, Ṭ, Z). A third, attributed in the sources to various Salaf, made the verse a description of death itself, whose pangs are violent for some and gentle for others. Al-Ṭabarī, surveying the whole garden, judged that no single reading need exclude the others: God swears by everything that displays these characteristics, so the oath embraces angels, stars, and the passage of death in all its modes. Al-Rāzī adds the methodological caution that haunts all five verses: we cannot say with certainty which meaning God *intended*, but we may say which meanings are *possible* — and the text, on his analysis, was made capacious on purpose.

**The Wrenching Described in the Prophet's Own Teaching**

The tradition supplies the sūrah's closest commentary. In the long, authenticated ḥadīth of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, transmitted by Aḥmad and Abū Dāwūd (no. 4753) — al-Ḥākim graded its chains sound, and the commentators from Ibn Kathīr onward cited it as a pillar of belief in the states after death — the passing of the unrepentant soul is painted in terms that echo *gharsan* almost syllable for syllable: the angels of punishment come with coarse cloth, and the soul is snatched out with such force that it is torn along with veins and nerves, "as a thorn-laden spike is pulled through wet wool," and the earth it leaves curses it. This is the world of *al-nāziʿāt* not as myth but as witnessed reality; the oath-sworn beings of verse 1 are the actors of the scene the Prophet ﷺ described. The violence, the ḥadīth tradition insists, is proportionate and pedagogical, not arbitrary: the soul that spent its life clutching at everything except God is met at the last with hands that do not let go.

**Why God Swears at All — and Why We May Not**

The five-fold oath also legislates, by contrast, a rule of the Muslim tongue. A report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim preserves the Prophet's ﷺ prohibition of the pagan habit of swearing by fathers and idols, and a sound tradition in Sunan Abī Dāwūd (no. 3251) and al-Tirmidhī (no. 1535) from Ibn ʿUmar states the principle with frightening clarity:

> *"Whoever swears by other than God has committed disbelief (or: has associated a partner with God)."*

The difference between God swearing by His creatures and a person swearing by them is not a double standard but a description of reality: God swears by what He made because He owns it, and His oath certifies; man swears by what he does not own, elevating a creature into a guarantee — which is the seed-act of idolatry. Makkah's merchants swore by al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā; this sūrah opens by swearing by the Most High's most obedient functionaries — angels who do nothing of their own motion — so that the very form of the oath re-trains the city's mouth: reverence goes to the Commander, astonishment to the commanded.

**From Cosmology to Conscience**

What does the swearing by soul-wrestlers achieve in the listener? It collapses the distance between cosmology and conscience. The denier of resurrection asked his question about bones in a laboratory of pure abstraction — reassembly, decay, impossibility. The oath answers that resurrection is not an engineering problem at all; it is a phase of the one operation everyone has already undergone in miniature: being pulled out of this life. Every death witnessed, every body washed, every funeral attended is a performed proof of the verse. A person who has sat beside a dying one knows something the philosophers of denial never learned: that the exit is not peaceful because it is natural, and that what departs is not identical with what remains. The logic of taqwā applies here in its starkest form: as the careful driver treats the dangerous road as already curved rather than hypothetically so, the believer treats the wresting-forth as a certainty with an appointment unknown — and arranges the whole drive accordingly. If the soul is real enough to be pulled, it is real enough to be returned; the second act costs the Actor nothing, and the sūrah will say so in its own words three times over.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:2

> **by those that draw out quickly,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Different Kind of Pull**

The second oath changes the verb, and with it changes the entire physics of departure. *Al-nāshiṭāt* comes from n-sh-ṭ, which retains the pulling of n-z-ʿ but purges its strain: the root is used of the bucket that comes up the well smoothly and quickly, of a thing stretched out taut and then released into its full shape, of the camel or horse that bounds — *nashṭa* — with a springing gait that costs no effort to onlookers and gives delight to the rider. So the verse paints extraction *as ascent*: not a wrenching but a drawing out that flows. The cognate accusative *nashṭan* — "a drawing-out" — names the manner itself, and the Arabic ear, struck a single verse earlier by *gharsan*, now hears the opposite texture from the same class of beings, or, on most readings, a second class of them: gentle in place of violent, speed without pain. This single-word contrast is the sūrah's first answer to its first audience. The deniers had framed resurrection as an absurd hardship upon God — bones rotting, atoms scattered. The oaths reply by describing the two deaths the crowd already knew about: the hard one and the easy one. Pulling souls is not impossible; it is routine. What differs is not the ability of the Puller but the condition of what is pulled.

**Whose Souls Come Up Smoothly**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī transmit the majority reading: *al-nāshiṭāt* are the angels who draw out the souls of the believers, who go willingly toward the Hereafter — souls that have spent a lifetime loosening their grip on the world, and so meet no resistance at the final extraction. The mechanics are honest: a hand long trained to open releases easily; a hand long clenched must be pried. Yet the sources preserve a sobering alternative cited by al-Qurṭubī: some held that the verse continues describing the disbelievers and hypocrites, because *nashiṭa* in older usage can also describe what must be hauled out with great force — the rope strained to its creaking point. On this reading, verses 1 and 2 are two intensities of one agony: the strike, then the slow pull. A third reading, also recorded in Q and Ṭ, unifies verses 1–2 under one subject — death itself — whose passage is violent for some and gentle for others, so that the oaths describe the same phenomenon's two faces rather than two separate crews of angels. Al-Ṭabarī's inclusive principle applies here as it did in verse 1: the oath embraces every being and every mode that draws forth in these fashions, and the wise listener lets all the possibilities work on him at once.

**The Believer's Passage, as the Prophet ﷺ Described It**

The ḥadīth literature paints *nashṭ* with almost the same vocabulary the sūrah uses. In the authenticated account of the believer's departure in the long ḥadīth of al-Barāʾ (Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd 4753 — graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Ḥākim, and received by the community of muḥaddithūn as the sun of narrations on the tomb), the soul of the servant of faith, addressed with tidings of honor, leaves him with such ease that, in the Prophet's ﷺ words, it flows — *tasilat* — the way a drop flows from the mouth of a waterskin; the angels of mercy receive it in shrouds and perfume from Paradise, and it ascends past every company of angels who ask only, "Whose is the good spirit?", and pray for the soul they have caught. The image of the waterskin matters: a full skin held upright gives up its water without tipping, without shaking, without force — gravity and an open nozzle do the work. So the believer's soul is not dragged upward against its grain; it is drawn along the line its own nature has been trained toward for a lifetime. Al-Rāzī in his *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb* adds the interior reading the source summarizes: *"those that draw out quickly"* are the spirits, having returned from bodily things, that take up the striving toward God with zeal (*nishāṭ*) — in this world already, before any angel arrives; the angels of verse 2 are the outer mirror of an inner habit.

**A Lesson Drawn from the Prophet's Own Death-Wish**

The tradition supplies a counter-case worth placing beside the verse. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 1374) and Muslim (no. 2372), Abū Hurayrah relates that the angel of death came to Mūsā in human form, and Mūsā, not recognizing him, struck out his eye. The angel returned to God with the message: *"You sent me to a servant who does not want to die."* And God offered Mūsā — through the tradition's remarkable imagery of a hand laid upon an ox's back, one year of life for every hair his palm covered — the choice to prolong his life; Mūsā asked how long remained, learned the answer was nothing, and said: *"Now,"* and asked only to be brought a stone's throw from the Holy Land. The Prophet ﷺ commented: *"Were I there, I would show you his grave beside the road, by the red sandhill."* Why does this belong with "those that draw out quickly"? Because it is the portrait of a soul so fully detached, so completely turned toward its Lord, that death is not an enemy to be wrestled but a courier to be welcomed — the exact texture of *nashṭ*. The contrast between Pharaoh (who will die clutching his kingdom and be pulled out with blows, verses 15–25) and Mūsā (who lets go of the years offered him and goes in a moment) is the sūrah itself in two of its actors.

**What the Believer Is Invited to Do Today**

Read as practical instruction, verses 1–2 form a pair of mirrors held up before the same face. Whatever grips are tightening now — wealth hoarded past need, grudges rehearsed for sport, status defended at the cost of truth, time buried in what will not be taken with us — each grip is a root wrapped more deeply before the day of pulling, and the tradition's plain arithmetic is that every tightened grip costs strain at the exit while every loosened one buys flow. This is not morbidity but engineering: the believer, like the swimmer who has spent years learning to stop fighting the water, wants the one movement he cannot control to be the one movement that costs him nothing. And the social consequence is real too: the community that hears these two oaths every dawn — the sūrah's own Bukhārī report has it in the Prophet's ﷺ dawn recitation — is a community that practices letting go together, giving before being asked, forgiving before the sunset, so that when the pulling starts, there will be nothing in the hand but air.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:3

> **by those that glide serenely,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Pulling to Swimming**

The third oath releases the rope entirely. *Al-sābiḥāt* is built on the root s-b-ḥ, whose home image is swimming — the one motion in which the body that was dragged, pulled, or wrenched now moves by its own element, with no hand above it and no resistance beneath it except what it knows how to divide. "By those that glide serenely" translates the participle exactly in its mood: the cognate accusative *subhan* — "a gliding" — strips the word of labor and leaves only the glide. After verse 1's violence and verse 2's strain-with-speed, the sequence of the oaths is itself a lesson in progression: the same class of act — the drawing out and carrying of what God commits to it — is now shown at its serene limit. The three verbs map the whole range of how what leaves this world travels to the next: torn, drawn, and simply swimming.

**Who Are the Gliders**

The classical authorities give three principal referents, and the source records all of them with their schools. Al-Ṭabarī reads the verse of angels who descend with God's nourishment — rain, sustenance, revelation — moving through the lower air with the unhurried certainty of creatures that never tire and never improvise. The same *sābiḥāt* language fits the stars as the Arabs used it: luminaries that "swim" in their courses; the Qur'an's own phrasing is echoed in this reading, *"Each swimming in an orbit"* (21:33), which the sūrah's audience knew as a proverbial image for motion so smooth the mover seems at rest. Al-Ṭabarī also records the third option: ships, which glide upon the sea exactly as the soul glides upon the sea of the unseen — ships being, in the Qur'an's own theology, standing signs of the One who "brings forth" a vessel from timber and the One who holds it afloat with wind and command. A fourth detail recorded in the apparatus: read against the word *ṣubḥ*, dawn, the verb *sabaha* can describe the angels' descent "in the early morning," the hour of the very prayer in which this sūrah was known to be recited.

**Mujāhid's Three Deaths**

A reading preserved from Mujāhid (d. 104/722–23), the Meccan exegete whose tafsīr underlies much of Ṭabarī's, treats verses 1–3 as three registers of one event — death — graded by the rank of the dying: verse 1 for the disbeliever, wrenched; verse 2 for the believer, drawn; verse 3 for the *khawāṣṣ*, the elect among the believers, who depart serenely, needing no pulling at all, gliding the way water levels itself. Mujāhid's scheme does three things worth noting. It reads the oaths as a ladder of intimacy rather than a taxonomy of function. It makes the difference between deaths a matter of the soul's training, not the angels' temperaments. And it quietly refuses to localize terror or peace in groups of people and instead locates both in grades of hearts — which is why the reading so impressed later commentators, al-Rāzī prominent among them, who loved its moral severity and its spiritual hope at once.

**The Serenity of the Trained**

Linguists of the Qur'an observe that *sabāḥa* in Arabic idiom was used of horses run at full gallop on the racetrack — speed with grace — so "glide serenely" carries no suggestion of slowness; the serene are not the idle but the perfectly deployed. Al-Rāzī's interior reading (in the source's summary) takes the gliding as the spirit, after its striving, "gliding in the realm of sovereignty" — crossing the oceans of the unseen as an expert swimmer crosses open water: no thrashing, because the element and the swimmer have become compatible. This is a psychology the modern reader can test without mysticism. Watch a master in any discipline — a surgeon, a calligrapher, a pianist, the old sailor handling a small boat in chop — and the outside of excellence is ease: what cost the apprentice effort costs the adept only presence. The Qur'an's promise, stated in the Prophet's ﷺ own words in a ḥadīth agreed upon, is that this law of mastery extends to the very matter of the soul:

> *"The one skilled in the Qur'an will be with the honoured, recording angels; and the one who recites it with difficulty, stammering, has two rewards."* (al-Bukhārī 4937; Muslim 798)

Gliding, then, is not a natural gift but an acquired element: practice makes the swimmer, and the swimmer makes the glide. The third oath swears by those who have reached that state — and invites the hearer into the practice.

**Why Serenity Is Sworn To Alongside Violence**

A rhetorical question sharpens the verse: why does the Resurrection-sūrah need a serene oath at all, between the striker and the racer? Because if the oaths described only force and speed, the Day would be pure coercion, and the denier could console himself that he is merely being overpowered — which is how Pharaoh will think in verse 21. The gliding verse inserts a third witness: that some souls arrive at the end as at home. The Day, in other words, is not only an extraction but an *arrival*, and the angels include crews whose whole duty is gentleness. The practical synthesis the three verses force on the reader is exact and not at all frightening: what pulls you out at the end will be matched, stroke for stroke, to what you have spent a life becoming. The hand that never let go of the world will be opened with pain. The hand trained to give will come up without fuss. The heart tuned here to the rhythm of obedience will find, there, that the water knows its own — and the glide, which is resurrection as seen from the inside, will feel like nothing so much as relief.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:4

> **by those that race to the fore, outstripping,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Oath: Competition Itself Made Angelic**

The participle *al-sābiqāt* belongs to s-b-q, the verb of the racetrack: to outstrip, to reach the end ahead of another. *Sabqan*, the cognate accusative, names the very event of outstripping — "a racing ahead." After the wrenching (v. 1), the drawing (v. 2), and the gliding (v. 3), the oaths now introduce, for the first time, plurality of competitors: racing is inherently against someone. Yet there is no anxiety in this track; the angels do not race each other for a prize that only one can win. What the fourth verse swears by is *the joy of precedence itself* — motion toward God at full speed, each competitor ahead only of its own yesterday. The image compresses the sūrah's entire ethics of time: the life of the believer is not a waiting room but a course, and the resurrection is the finish line where, as the verse says, some are first.

**The Racers Identified**

Al-Ṭabarī reports the dominant reading: the angels who race with the souls to their destinations — to Paradise bearing the believer's spirit upward, and, on the other side, the speed of the terrible errand for the denier. The same verb, applied to the same errand elsewhere in the Qur'an, is what makes the image legible to a Meccan ear: *"How would you guard yourselves on the Day that makes children grey-haired?"* (73:17) is the *tone*, but the *movement* is the angels' — they "come forth" at command without the fatigue of travel. The alternate referents recorded for verse 4 (Ṭ) continue the sequence's logic: stars that overtake one another's horizons — *"and the sun running its course... each swimming in an orbit"* (21:33) in the older, seamless astronomy of the surah-world — and death again, which no living rider can outrun, the single racer the Arabs already confessed in their proverbial imagery. What all three candidates share is the defining feature of the root: *precedence obtained at speed*, and the sūrah, for its part, swears by the property itself, whichever carriers own it.

**The Qur'an's Own Racetrack: Sabq as Virtue**

Elsewhere the Qur'an takes this same root out of cosmology and places it in the believer's hands, and those verses are the fourth oath's practical commentary. *"Race to forgiveness from your Lord, and a Garden whose breadth is the heavens and the earth"* (57:21) — the image is of the starting-gate of a great contest where the prizes are all shared, because forgiveness and Paradise are not scarce. *"They vie with one another in good deeds"* (23:61, *ulāʾika yusbiqu*) describes the very class of people the elect of verse 3 were: those whose souls glide because their lives sprinted. And when the Qur'an describes the angels bearing the Throne, they arrive where they are sent with the swiftness the oaths assume: *"descending with it upon your heart, by God's leave"* (26:194) of the trust-bearer Gabriel. The fourth oath thus welds the angelic and the human tracks into one: the same *sabq* that is the angels' nature is commanded as the believers' habit.

**A Permitted Fever: Competition in the Prophet's Practice**

Islam's ethics of racing is documented with unusual specificity. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the ḥadīth transmitted by Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī (no. 1700) and judged ḥasan-ṣaḥīḥ: *"There is no racing for a prize save with hoof, feather, or pad"* — camels, horses, falcons, in other words, in the martial arts that served the community's defense; the track was licensed for a purpose. And the Sunnah preserves sport for its own joy: ʿĀʾishah relates that she raced the Prophet ﷺ on foot and won, and later, when she had gained weight, he won, and he laughed: *"This is for that"* (Aḥmad; Abū Dāwūd 2578) — a race run in a household, with a joke at the finish line, is a race sanctified as play. Yet the same Sunnah disciplined the fever: the Prophet ﷺ forbade the bettor's obsession and the rider's vanity — *"he who ran to be seen"* — teaching that speed in Islam is neither idleness nor exhibition. Verse 4's angels, racing without rivals and without spoils, become the measure by which human competition is weighed: are we racing toward something, or merely ahead of somebody?

**Why the Racers Belong in a Resurrection-Oath**

The placement of this oath before the Day's description (vv. 6–14) answers an objection the deniers never voiced in words but lived in fact: "If resurrection is real, why does it delay?" The racers answer: delay is an attribute of the competitor on a long road, not of the errand itself; there are runners already at the finish, angels for whom the distance between here and there is one stride, and the Day is not approaching through years so much as descending through them, at racing speed, at every hour. The psychology is exact: the human heart, faced with promised consequences, chronically mistakes the courier's patience for the letter's non-existence. To swear by *al-sābiqāt* is to tell the hearer that what he is putting off has been in motion since before he was born, and passes him daily. The practical demand falls out of the verse with the force of an arrow from a bow drawn to the ear: the one thing a believer is forbidden to do in this sūrah, at this oath, is to stand still — and the one thing he need never fear is arriving late, for the track's Owner calls every sincere sprint a win. The Day will find the obedient exactly as the angels found their target: outstripping, first, unharassed, and home.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:5

> **and by those that govern affairs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath That Crowns the Five**

The fifth and final oath closes the sequence not with another movement but with a function: *wa-al-mudabbirāti amrā* — "by those that govern the affair." The root d-b-r means to follow after, to bring something to its conclusion, to manage from behind as a herder manages a flock; *tadbīr* is therefore, in its literal anatomy, "after-sight" — planning that sees the end in the beginning. The classical lexicographers note that the word was used for the governor who sets affairs right by foresight, and the Qur'an itself employs the noun of the same root for counsel and remand: *"the affair is theirs"* in the language of decision. So the five oaths complete an arc: what is wrenched (1), what is drawn (2), what glides (3), what races (4) — and what, at the summit, *governs* (5). Every exertion of the previous four is here revealed as execution of a plan. The oaths do not end with power but with administration: the most magnificent thing about God's agents is not their strength but their order.

**The Governors Identified: Angels, Archangels, and Planets**

The majority of exegetes, as al-Qurṭubī records (Q), take the verse of the angels charged with the affairs of this world — rain and vegetation, death and birth, revelation and provision — each delegated a domain, none acting outside the envelope of command. A more specific report in al-Qurṭubī narrows the verse to the four archangels: Jibrīl in charge of revelation, Mīkāʾīl of sustenance and rain, ʿAzrāʾīl of death, and Isrāfīl of the trumpet — and where the source's apparatus names "Seraphiel" (Ṭs, Q), the Arabic tradition reads ʿIsrāfīn or Mālak, with the exact roster deliberately left open, because the functional point survives any list: creation is run by appointed offices, not by chance or by caprice. Al-Rāzī reports the alternative that some attached verse 5 to the *planets* — an astrological register the mufassirūn record in order to refute: the verse, he insists, governs *by* appointment, which is the very thing stars, in Islamic theology, never do; whoever reads the planets as rulers reads them as *governed*, which is precisely what the oath guarantees for them. Ibn Kathīr's gloss stands as the sober middle: the angels execute what is written, and "the affair" in the singular — *amrā*, not *umūr* — hints that their plurality resolves into one Command, a unity of direction in a multiplicity of workers.

**The Relay of Command in the Qur'an's Own Words**

This same image — a government of heaven with a cabinet of agents — is spelled out elsewhere in the Qur'an in the exact idiom of verse 5, and the cross-references should be quoted whole, because they are the tradition's own tafsīr:

> *"He governs the affair from the heaven to the earth; then it will ascend to Him in a Day whose measure is a thousand years of what you count."* (Qur'an 32:5)

> *"Who provides for you from the heaven and the earth? Who owns hearing and sight? Who brings forth the living from the dead and brings forth the dead from the living? Who governs the affair? They will say, 'God.' So will they not remember?"* (Qur'an 10:31)

Read beneath the oaths, 10:31 becomes the sūrah's argument in the polytheists' own dialectic: the Meccans conceded that their idols ran nothing — the rain fell, the crops grew, the dying died, all of it through forces no idol could arrest — and the Qur'an presses the concession: whatever chain of governance you observe in nature, its terminal cause is One, so *"so will they not remember?"* Verse 5's oath swears by the middle links, while the remembrance the sūrah aims at is of the First Link. The tadbīr of the angels is, rhetorically, a bridge between a visible world that runs on pattern and an invisible Commander who runs on will; a theology that loses the bridge ends up either deifying the patterns or evacuating the Commander.

**The Heart's Own Governance**

Al-Rāzī — in the interior reading the source preserves — makes the fifth oath the crown of the soul's journey: *"those that govern affairs are an allusion to the connection between the last levels of humanness and the first degrees of angelhood,"* so that when the human spirit reaches the limit of outstripping (verse 4), it arrives at the angelic register — the self in which the lower faculties, once wrenched, drawn, glided, and raced, now execute the Divine plan in the member's own chest without rebellion. The language of governance (*tadbīr*) is, in this register, the definition of moral maturity: a person is *governed* when the affair of his own house — appetite, anger, attention, time — answers to foresight rather than to *hawā*, caprice, which the sūrah will meet head-on in verse 40 as the thing the believer forbids to his soul. And the plain, communal lesson stands beside the subtle one: the same verb that describes angels running the weather describes a household run well, an office run honestly, a soul with an ordered interior; Islam does not split the sacred from the administrative, because verse 5 swears by administrators.

**Five Oaths, One Oathed-To**

Why did the sūrah need five oaths where one would swear the point? Because the audience was not doubtful about one thing but scattered about many: some denied souls entirely, some denied angels, some conceded stars but not sovereignty, some worshipped the governing forces as partners. The five-fold oath assembles all the contested parties as *witnesses* — each class summoned as God's servant, none as His rival — and then, without transition, the sūrah swings to its verdict: the Day of the Quaker (v. 6). The grammar of Arabic oaths leaves the answer-clause of so many oaths implicit; here, however, the answer arrives visible, a whole sentence in plain sight: *on that Day* the resurrection occurs — as if the sūrah's five swearings had been the drawing of a bow whose released arrow is the Day itself. The listener who has heard the bowstring — wrestling, drawing, gliding, racing, governing — cannot miss the shot: every movement in the cosmos is aimed at the reckoning, and the Oath-Giver has already pledged His name to it, which is the only warrant a human being can stand on when at last the pull begins.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:6

> **on the Day when the quaker quakes**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sworn-To Arrives, and Its Name Is a Tremor**

The answer-clause of five tremendous oaths begins, and it begins with a single word of earthquake: *rajfah*. *Yawma tarjufu al-rājifa* — "a Day when the Quaker quakes." The root r-j-f in Arabic describes a shaking so thorough that the thing shaken loses its footing: the sand that shifts underfoot, the vessel tossed past recovery, the heart that will not settle. Al-Zamakhsharī, ever attentive to such selections, notes that the Qur'an did not say "the Day when the earth quakes," though the verb is the earth's own (cf. *"when the earth is shaken with its [final] earthquake"*, 99:1), but clothed the event itself with a noun — *al-rājifa*, "the Quaking One" — as if to make the quake an entity, a living shock that quakes by its own nature and does to everything else what its name implies. The repetition within the clause — the quaker quaking, the verb from the same root as its own subject — is the Arabic device of *mafʿūl muṭlaq* pressed into service here as totality: the shaking will not be something the Day *has*; it will be everything the Day *is*.

**What Quakes: the trumpet, the earth, or the Hour itself?**

The commentators distribute the referent, and the distribution itself is instructive. Qatāda, al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī record the majority reading — the Quaker is the first blast of the Trumpet, whose sound is a shaking of the whole order of the living, and the successor of verse 7 is the second blast on which the dead rise; the Qur'an's own pairing of the two blasts is quoted below at verse 7. A second reading attaches *rājifa* to the earth and the mountains, whose convulsion on that Day the Qur'an states in terms almost identical to this one — *"the Day when the earth will quake with its quaking"* (cf. 99:1; and 73:14, *"the Day the earth and mountains will quake"*), the Day when "*the earth throws out its burdens and speaks its tidings*" (99:2–4, paraphrased). A third reading, preserved by al-Qurṭubī and Ṭabarī, takes the Quaker as the Hour itself, which the Qur'an likewise calls by a verb of shaking: *"The convulsion of the Hour is a tremendous thing"* (22:1) — and a minority (Q, Ṭ) applied verses 6–7 to every particular death, the private earthquake of each soul's extraction, which is why the oaths before them fit so naturally: the Day that quakes globally is the sum of the days that quake person by person. Al-Ṭabarī's rule — that the oath covers every real bearer of the attribute — gives the verse its maximum seismic range: nothing from the heart to the heavens is exempt.

**"Hearts that Day shall tremble" — The Chain Reaction of the Quake**

Verse 6 has no verb of seeing and no verb of hearing; it does not need them, because the quake transmits itself down the entire scale of existence at once: the Trumpet into the earth, the earth into the mountains, the cosmological shock into the anatomy of every witness — verse 8's *hearts*, verse 9's *eyes*, the shrouds and the graves. The sūrah's sequence is physiology as eschatology: the outer tremor and the inner tremor are one event observed at two distances. This is why the Qur'anic *rājiʿāt* vocabulary is also the vocabulary of the human chest: *wa-jāʾat sakratu al-mawti bi-al-ḥaqq* — death's own intoxication arrives "with the truth" (50:19), and *"its hearts will reach the throats"* describes at 40:18 what 79:6 sets in motion. A reader who has lived through a real earthquake knows the sūrah's physics better in one night than the philosophers know in a library: the ground that was simply *there* reveals that it was never still, and the trust of seconds collapses into the knowledge that stability was on loan all along. The Day's quake universalizes that one night to every living being, permanently.

**Why a Quake and Not a Decree — the Logic of the Warning**

It would have been sufficient, and precise, to say "on the Day God commands the resurrection." The choice of shaking instead of the bare command performs two arguments at once. First, the argument of power-by-ease: the shaking of all things occurs in this sūrah as *an event without labor* — no reassembly is described, only the tremor and its successor (v. 7) and the standing (v. 14) — which answers, in advance, the deniers' "how," not by mechanism but by dissolving the premise that reunion of atoms is hard: what was raised from nothing with a word is shaken back into being with a tremor. Second, the argument of psychology: a decree informs; a quake *unmakes* the hearer's composure — and resurrection's whole purpose in this sūrah is not to add a fact to the polytheists' encyclopedia but to dislodge them from *ghafla*, the sleep of the taken-for-granted. A sign you can remain seated under is a sign that has already failed. This is the same logic the Prophet ﷺ practiced with his whole ministry: revelation did not arrive to Mecca as an announcement about the future, but, in the words of one of its own sūrahs, as a convulsion in the present tense — *"near has come to people their reckoning, while they are heedless, turning away"* (21:1).

**What to Do While the Ground Is Borrowed**

The verse's practical residue for the modern reader is not terror but calibration. Everything we build, we build on a substrate whose stillness is temporary by contract, and the only honest planning is the one that accounts for both: the sower sows as if the ground will hold a century, and the believer sows also as if the ground will shift tomorrow. The quake-oath forbids neither engineering nor medicine nor architecture — the tradition's own engineers were the most pious builders — it forbids the *metaphysical* overbuilding of a life whose load-bearing wall is a lie about permanence. *"The Day when the quaker quakes"* is, until it arrives, the one certainty that disciplines every other certainty; and the person who has let verse 6 shake him a little, nightly, will find when the real thing comes that he has been rehearsing his footing, and the shock that is total for the unprepared will be for him only the last door of a long and merciful corridor.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:7

> **and the successor follows upon it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word Built for Following**

*Wa-tatbaʿuhā al-rādifa* — "and the successor follows upon it." The root r-d-f in Arabic is the root of the *radīf*: the rider seated behind the rider on the same mount, close enough to share a stride. From it comes *tartīb*, arrangement in sequence, and the *murādafa* of camels walking single-file in the desert night, each following the one ahead so that the caravan becomes, in the darkness, one animal with many humps. To name the second blast — or the second event of the Day — *al-rādifah*, the Follower, is to load a small word with an enormous claim about time: nothing in creation will then be independent; everything will be in file, and the file itself will be the meaning. The verb *tatbaʿu* — "follows" — is present tense in a verse about the future, the Qur'an's signature device of *taḥqīq al-wāqid bi-ṣīghat al-ḥāḍir*, the making-real of what is coming by speaking it as though it is already happening: the Follower is already putting one foot in front of the other.

**The First and Second Blasts, in the Qur'an's Own Account**

The majority of the sources the apparatus cites (Q, R, Ṭ) identify verses 6–7 as the Trumpet's two blasts: the Quaker is *al-rajfa*, the blast of stunning and collapse, and the Successor is *al-radifa*, the blast of the resurrection itself. The Qur'an elsewhere gives that pair in a single narrative sentence, which stands as the verse's clearest self-commentary:

> *"And the Trumpet will be blown, and all who are in the heavens and all who are on the earth will fall unconscious, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown again, and at once they will be standing, looking about."* (Qur'an 39:68)

The parallel is exact down to the sūrah's own verbs: falling unconscious answering *rajfa*, "standing" answering *radifa*, and the Qur'an's *fa-idhā hum* — "at once they are" — matching the surprise of *fa-idhā hum ʿalā al-sāḥirah* (79:14). That the two blasts are two, while a later verse says the whole affair is *"a single cry"* (79:13), is resolved by the commentators with characteristic calm: the two are reckoned as one because of the nothing between them, the interval so short that the dazed never fully recover between the first sound and the second; or because the first is the call to the court and the second the opening of its doors — a summons and an arrival, one case, two knocks.

**The Interval No One Was Told**

The interval itself became the subject of one of the tradition's most candid reports. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (Book of Exegesis, chapter on Sūrat Qāf; likewise cited by Ibn Kathīr here), Abū Hurayrah relates from the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Between the two blasts of the Trumpet is forty." They said: "Forty days? Forty months? Forty years?" He said: "I refused (to specify)."*

Two lessons are stored in that refusal. The first is epistemological: the transmission of the unseen keeps its own silence where revelation kept silence, and a scholar who will not complete a gap the Prophet ﷺ left open is teaching, by his restraint, that the ledger of certainty matters more than the appetite for curiosity. The second is pedagogical, and it belongs to this sūrah's argument with the deniers: *whoever has the interval has time*, and the unbeliever's deepest wish — to be left in the bones — is answered neither by postponement nor by duration. The second blow arrives; the question has never been whether there will be a gap between the blasts, but whether the space of one life — unnumbered days between the first trumpet of every warning and the last of every breath — has been used.

**Other Successions the Verse Leaves Open**

The apparatus records richer alternatives, each amplifying the same theme of *irreversible sequence*. Some read the Quaker as the earth's first convulsion and the Successor as the second shattering when it is split and "fades to a level plain" (R; cf. 20:106); some as earth and mountains quaking first, then the heavens and the planets following (IK, R) — the dissolution marching from the bottom floor upward, as the creation marched downward; and al-Rāzī records the reading that the Quaker is the shaking of the Hour's onset and the Successor the Day itself "which follows it inescapably." What the alternatives share — and why the pious agnosticism of Ṭabarī's inclusive rule suits them all — is the shape of the image: the End is structured as a *file of events*, each one bound to the one before, so that no creature can be overtaken by the Successor without having first been overtaken by the Quaker. Death, in this eschatology, is never a single event; it is always a sequence — and every sequence, in verse 13's arithmetic, will be counted as a cry.

**How the Believer Reads a Follow-Order World**

Practically, verse 7 inverts the denier's model of time. The men of verses 10–12 live as if days were separate coins to be spent; this verse says the days are a caravan, and the one riding behind is always entering the tent the one ahead just left — the day after follows the day as the resurrection follows the death, the reckoning follows the resurrection, the Fire or the Garden follows the reckoning. A person who has internalized the grammar of *rādiʿ* walks differently: he ties his camel and trusts, he plants his seedling and trusts, he repents in the morning before the evening can tie him up, because he has learned that "after" is not the future's name; it is the present's other foot. The tradition crystallizes exactly this posture in the Prophet's ﷺ saying recorded by al-Tirmidhī, on the authority of Abū Hurayrah, with a chain soundness the commentators accepted: *"Hasten to deeds before trials of which a man will enter them and leave them as he emerged from them — or before a death that will settle him."* Two blasts, one interval, no gaps for the wise: the Successor is the most punctual of guests, and the believer, by verse 7, is the one who has already set the second place at the table.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:8

> **Hearts that Day shall tremble,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Quake Moves from Ground to Chest**

After two verses of cosmic tremor, the sūrah zooms — without transition, without "and then" — into the human chest: *qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifa*, "Hearts that Day shall tremble." The root w-j-f describes the violent beat of a heart whose rhythm has slipped its tether; *wājifa* is used of the panicked heart, and the lexicographers record the same root for the arrow's last flutter and fall from the bow. The commentators draw the obvious contrast with the Qur'an's other great Day-portraits, in which the true believers' hearts are anything but fluttering: *"They have no fear among them, and they will not grieve"* (15:47), and the hadith of the seven under God's shade names *"a man whose heart is attached to the mosques"* — not a heart frightened out of it. The tremor here is, accordingly, qualified by context: it is the quake as the guilty body experiences it, and the sūrah's own grammar will supply the qualifier in a moment (verse 10: "*They will say*" — the speakers of this dread are its deniers).

**Why the Plural "Hearts," and Why Indefinite**

Al-Rāzī's grammatical observation on this verse is one of the quiet gems of the tradition: the word *qulūb* stands indefinite in a threatening context — not "the hearts" but "hearts" — and Arabic's ruling in such cases is that the threat attaches to a *subset*: not every heart will tremble that Day, but the hearts of those who knew and denied. On the believers' side, that Day is the answering of the Fātiḥah's deepest clause — the *falāḥ* promised to the reverent — and the Qur'an's parallel scenes describe the other hearts by their opposite verbs: faces bright, books in the right hand, *"no fear concerning them, nor will they grieve"* (2:62 and passim). This is the sūrah's theology of justice in miniature: the same shock, read by different hearts, is torture to one and delivery to the other. The quake does not create the trembling; it discloses what the heart had already been carrying — which is precisely how the Qur'an frames the whole anatomy of dread: *"So warn them of the Day of the Approaching Event, when hearts will be in the throats, choking with grief"* (40:18), the source's own cross-reference at this verse. The heart rises to the throat because it has, all along, been rising there: the guilty live in a held breath, and the Day only removes the hand.

**The Trembling Heart as the Qur'an's Chief Instrument of Knowledge**

To read verse 8 in the Qur'an's own idiom, one must note what "heart" — *qalb* — is *for* in the Book's psychology: not sentiment but sight. *"It is not the eyes that are blinded, but the hearts within the breasts"* (22:46); *"in that are reminders for whoever has a heart, or gives ear while he is a witness"* (50:37). The trembling of verse 8 is therefore epistemic before it is emotional: it is what knowledge feels like when it finally cannot be argued with. The deniers of the Resurrection are not ignorant *and then* afraid; their fear is the return of everything they refused to know — the fitra's testimony that the self is accountable, suppressed in the daylight of career and mockery, restored with interest in the one light that cannot be dodged. The Prophet ﷺ gave the same diagnosis in the plainest possible terms in the agreed-upon ḥadīth of Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr: *"There is a morsel of flesh in the body which, if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt — beware, it is the heart."* And in the ḥadīth qudsī of the forty friends, of sound chains in the Ṣaḥīḥayn's margins, God describes the heart of the ally who drew near by obligatory deeds as that "which does not hesitate" — obedience made second nature, so that its Day-counterpart is peace, not palpitations. Fear and calm, the sūrah teaches, are both acquired before they are felt.

**Anxiety, Conscience, and the Panic Room of the Modern**

The modern clinical vocabulary has learned to describe what verse 8 named first: the racing heart, the somatic alarm with no object in sight, the anxiety that is "about" nothing and therefore about everything. The Qur'an's reading of that phenomenon is neither dismissal nor medicalization but *moral orientation*: the heartbeat that alarms in the middle of safety is a testimony that will not be silenced — a conscience with information the ego has refused to file, beating against the door of the chest. The tradition's counsel is as practical as it is ancient: *"the wise is the one who takes his soul to account"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Anas, sound as graded) — audit the beating deliberately, by repentance, restitution, and reform, so that the tremor discharges now as repair rather than accumulating, unfiled, for its single great discharge that Day. Conversely, the sūrah offers the other physiology, the one it will seal in verse 40: the heart that *feared the Standing of its Lord* acquires — through exactly that practice of deliberate trembling — a permanent steadiness; and on the Day that shakes the ground, what was wājifa by choice becomes sākinah by gift: *"O soul at peace, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing"* (89:27–28). Verse 8 is the hinge between the two hearts, read in a single breath.

**The Mercy Hiding in a Threat**

Finally, the placement of verse 8 carries a subtlety: the quake reaches the heart *before* it reaches anything external that will harm the person — the Fire is not yet mentioned, the verdict not yet spoken. In Qur'anic pedagogy, the interior warning is the second mercy, not the first: the first was the whole sūrah of oaths, recited at dawn for years; the second is the conscience's alarm that says, *here, now, while there is still a hand on the tiller*. Every real tremor of regret — the flush at a remembered wrong, the weight at an hour wasted — the sūrah addresses as a rehearsal, and the rehearsal is the mercy. The hearts that accept the rehearsal tonight meet tomorrow's quake as a curtain-call, not a catastrophe; the hearts that silence it by distraction, by mockery, by the world's anesthetics, are, the sequence implies, building the tremor a larger chest to be in — until the Day when it will have the whole body at once, forever, and the heart will not be able to tremble anywhere else.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:9

> **with eyes humbled.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eye Completes What the Heart Began**

*Absārun khāḍiʿa* — "eyes cast down, humbled." The root kh-f-ʿ carries two interlocking senses in Arabic: to be low in place, and to be low in spirit; the *khāḍiʿ* is the camel that has been made to kneel, and by extension the man whose arrogance has been folded. Placed immediately after the trembling heart, the humbled eye is anatomy as argument: the sūrah proceeds from the inside out — quake (v. 6), succession (v. 7), heart (v. 8), eye (v. 9) — the way a lie proceeds from conviction to face. There is no shame in the Qur'an's eschatology that does not finally arrive at the countenance; the tradition's own description of the Resurrection's first scene, preserved in a sound report cited at 80:38–42 and by al-Tirmidhī, fixes the gaze of every soul on its own book, because no one will then be able to look at anyone else. The eye is last of the five senses to speak in this sequence because the eye is the seat of the will's final appeal: you can hide a trembling heart behind a still face, but you cannot hide the face behind the eyes.

**Humbling as the Opposite of the Lifting**

The Qur'an's usage lets us measure exactly what *khāḍiʿ* reverses. The proud in this Book are described by the idiom of *lifting the gaze*: the people of Lot are the paradigm — *"do you approach males and cut off the road and lift your assemblies to the outrageous?"* (29:29) — and prayer itself legislates the discipline of the lowered gaze, both before the Book and in the Prophet's ﷺ practice. The hadith literature makes the etiquette a matter of divine sight and human awe: in the *ḥadīth of Iḥsān*, worship is defined as *"to worship God as though you see Him, for though you see Him not, He sees you"* (al-Bukhārī 8; Muslim 8) — an exchange in which the servant's gaze is lowered and God's is not, freely, in love; while on the Day, the same geometry will be enforced, unwillingly, in justice. The two humbled eyes of this verse, then, are the mirror of a lifetime's either training or defiance: the eye that in this world lowered itself from what God forbade — a modesty the Prophet ﷺ praised as a branch of faith (al-Bukhārī 9; Muslim 35) — will find humility, that Day, a native climate rather than a sentence; the eye that was lifted in contempt over servants and over signs will find it can look at nothing. Al-Tirmidhī (no. 1639, judged sound) records from Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"Two eyes the Fire will not touch: an eye that guarded in God's path, and an eye that wept from the fear of God."*

**"Their Eyes Humbled, Abasement Overcoming Them"**

The source apparatus sets beside this verse the sūrah's twin passages, and the actual texts deserve to stand in full, because their shared idiom is the tafsīr: *"Their eyes will be cast down, abasement will cover them — that is the Day they were promised"* (70:43–44), and *"humbled will be their eyes, as if they were locusts spread abroad"* (68:42–43, in the sense of 68:43's imagery; the source cites 68:43). The locust simile teaches the sūrah's physics of that crowd: locusts swarm with no fear of falling because the fall is already total; the humbled-eyed, similarly, will move in every direction at once — *"rushing toward the Caller"* (54:8) — with no pride in any of the directions left. That is the final meaning of *khāḍiʿ*: not merely downcast eyes but the loss of the very option of haughtiness. Even the Pharaoh of this sūrah — verses 23–24 — will arrive at the Day's first scene stripped of the one posture his claim required: to be "most high" and *khāḍiʿ* is, in the Book's arithmetic, a contradiction that resolves only in the humbling, which is why the narrative of vv. 15–26 sits between the oaths and this anatomy.

**What the Eye Looks At in the Hour of Dying**

The humbling begins, the tradition insists, at the last moment of this world, in the deathbed's frozen gaze. Muslim (no. 920) preserves from Umm Salama that the Prophet ﷺ said: *"When the soul is taken, the sight follows it"* — the eyes of the dying tracking the departure they cannot follow, a closing lesson in the anatomy this sūrah opened. There is no cruelty in the image; there is diagnosis. The gaze that spent its decades on what does not outlive it is rehearsing, at its last look, the one look it will never again control: the eye, emptied of its horizon, having to watch its own ledger pass into hands other than its excuses. The social law the verse draws from this is plain and practical: what we look at, we become, and the Qur'an's commands about the gaze (24:30–31) are hygiene for a self that will one day stand in a court where its eyes are exhibits.

**The Eye That Is Humbled in Time Saves the Eye Humbled Forever**

Al-Rāzī's note at this verse is worth quoting in sense, if not in letter: the pairing of trembling heart and humbled eye — where other verses mention hearts in the throats, and yet others describe faces blackened — shows that the Day's abasement is not an addition to the self but the self's own states made visible; God "does not wrong the atoms' weight," and He also does not invent a humiliation the person has not built. The sūrah, for its part, places the cure inside the very sequence: the next verses will quote the deniers sneering — *"Are we to be restored?"* — and the sneer is itself the *lifting* this verse answers: they raise their eyes at the idea of return precisely because they will not lower them before the reality of the Requirer. To read verse 9 as intended is to perform it: to lower, deliberately, now, what will be lowered eventually then — in worship, in mercy toward the small, in refusing to feed on what the eye should not feast upon — until the heart trembles less, because it has learned to bow, and the Day's quake finds a creature already in the posture of prayer, eyes humbled, in the best sense, into a vision wider than itself. The eye that has knelt here will stand there; and the verse, four words long, is the sūrah's last anatomy before the argument moves from what they will feel to what they will say.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:10

> **They will say, "Are we to be restored as we were before?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Anatomy Gives Way to the Argument**

The sūrah has described the Day from the outside in — quake, succession, heart, eye — and now it opens the mouths of the mocked-by-Day: *yaqūlūna a-innā la-mardūdūn fī al-nāziʿa*, "They say: Are we indeed to be restored in the Wrestle?" (the source: "as we were before"). The particle sequence is doing heavy rhetorical work: the interrogation's *hamza*, the emphatic *inna*, and the intensive *la-mardūdūn* — an interrogative of incredulity, three markers of emphasis stacked on a question the askers know is not really about knowledge. Nobody asks "am I going to die?" with that many emphatic particles unless the question is a shield: the grammatical form is a query; the pragmatic content is a refusal. The Qur'an quotes the deniers directly, in their own tense, at the moment of the Day itself — a narrative device the sūrah uses repeatedly (*fa-idhā hum*, "then behold, they are," v. 14): the hearer of the sūrah, in Makkan times, is overheard as though already standing in the scene where mockery goes silent.

**"Restored" — *Mardūdūn* and Its Two Directions**

The root r-d-d, to return something to its place, governs the verse, and the commentators track two directions of the return. Most read *mardūdūn fī al-nāziʿa* as "returned to the state of being wrested" — that is, back through death, or back to the question the oaths opened: will *we* be pulled out and put back like the camel dragged from the pit? Al-Ṭabarī's alternative, also grammatically firm, reads the phrase as "returned to the life of this world" — the wish for reinvestment, the very regret of the Qur'an's other Day: *"And if you could see when they will be stationed before the Fire. They will say: If only we could be returned, we would not deny the signs of our Lord, and we would be among the believers"* (Qur'an 6:27). The second reading is devastating on its own: the men who mocked "am I to be sent back?" will, on the other side of the question, beg to be sent back. The Qur'an's irony is engineered with the precision of a legal cross-examination, and its verdict, in another sūrah, is stated in the same verb's tense: *"and they will return to that which they were forbidden"* (34:53, in sense). The same word, mocked then begged — the verse is a whole eschatology of wishful reversal compressed in a single noun.

**The Same Question in the Same Words Throughout the Qur'an**

The source apparatus sets the parallel passages side by side, and their actual texts show how the objection is archetypal: *"They say: What! When we are bones and dust, shall we indeed be resurrected as a new creation? Say: Become stones or iron, or a created thing greater in your breasts"* (17:98–99); and at the very period the sūrah addresses, *"They say: When we die and become dust, is that a return far away?"* (cf. 50:3), while the same denial's fate is drawn in the sūrah's own later verdict for its people: *"in scorching wind and boiling water, and a shadow of black smoke, neither cool nor welcome"* (56:42–44). What is remarkable, and why the objection appears everywhere from Makkah to Madinah, is its permanence: verse for verse, generation for generation, the denier's case reduces to one sentence — *the dead do not get back up* — which the sūrah treats, in verse 11, at the height of its grossness ("decayed bones"), and answers, in verses 27–33, at the height of its cosmology. The Qur'an never dignifies the objection with a new premise; it keeps answering the same premise with the same force: the Same who began (v. 13, *kun*) repeats.

**Why the Denier Asks a Question He Cannot Mean**

The sūrah's placement of this speech-act carries psychology of the first order. The question is uttered *on the Day* ("they will say"), i.e., at a moment when nothing is hidden and no rhetorical point can be gained; the speakers are not debating — they are hoping the facts to be otherwise. That is the tradition's reading of *al-tamannī*: the wish for a false undoing — and the Qur'an's own verdict on the genre: *"they wish that they could be rid of their torments"* and *"the deniers wish they were dust"* (cf. 78:40) — the wish for dust, quoted above, is the verse 10 question with the sarcasm burned away. The modern reader should not be smug. The posture of "am I *really* to be asked?" — at an audit, at a hospital bedside, in the small of the night when a long-deferred wrong stands up in the memory — is the universal human reflex of the unrepentant conscience: it always dresses as a question about fact what is a protest against accountability. The sūrah lets the protest speak aloud, in its own words, because hearing it aloud is the first way back to honesty: the only honest form of the question is "how do I prepare," and the sūrah will make that pivot in the same breath (vv. 35–41).

**The One Thing the Question Cannot Touch**

The verse ends with a rising *hamza* and the promise of the next verse's grotesquerie; but its answer has already been sworn — five oaths deep — before the objection was ever uttered. This is the sūrah's architecture of certainty: the denier's question is quoted inside a text whose every antecedent clause is a divine oath that he is answered, restored, and shown the Fire in plain view (v. 36). Rhetorically, the Qur'an is teaching a logic of testimony: when the One who swears the oaths is the One who made the swearers (angels, stars, death itself) and made the objector's bones from clay, the objection cannot be weighed against the oath — it is weighed *by* it. Which is why the sūrah, after two verses of sneering, does not descend into argument at all; in one line it collapses the whole distance between the dead man's bones and the Day's verdict: *"Yet it shall be but a single cry"* (v. 13). The question of verse 10 is answered by verse 13 not with a proof but with a tempo: the Unquestioned speed of the affair, against which the slow, decayed bones of the objection simply fall silent — not because they are refuted by the next sneer, but because the next word is already on the lips of the angel doing the drawing.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:11

> **What! When we have become decayed bones?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection at Its Most Corporal**

*ḥāmiyatun naḫirah* — the two nouns the mockers choose are the sūrah's own anatomy lesson in a single line. *Ḥāmiya* (from ḥ-m-m) is that which has been parched with fire and left to blacken and crumble — rendered in classical lexica as "rotten, decayed," used of old bones dried until they crack under a fingernail; *nakhirah* (from n-kh-r) is what has become brittle with age, hollow, powder-dry, the very word for an antler that has dried and been gnawed smooth. The pairing is deliberately nauseating: not "dust" as in the poets' elegies, but bones at the exact stage the Quraysh could fetch from any of the burial pits of the Ḥijāz — bones that snap when handled, whose marrow is long gone, whose sockets admit air. The deniers are not being poetic; they are holding up, in words, the specimen they held up in fact. Which brings us to the reason this verse's context is one of the sūrah's most vividly remembered scenes.

**The Rotten Bone in the Prophet's Face**

The commentators — al-Ḥākim in his *Mustadrak*, Ibn Kathīr in his *Tafsīr*, and the exegetical chains running through al-Ṭabarī — transmit the occasion-of-revelation scene that fits verse 11 like a key: a man of the Quraysh — in the reports, al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, or Ubayy ibn Khalaf, the two arch-scofflaws of the sīrah literature — came to the Messenger of God ﷺ with a crumbled, putrid bone in his hand, pulverizing it between his fingers as he asked, with a sneer, whether this would really be brought back to life. The Prophet ﷺ answered, in the transmitted wording: *"Yes — God will give you life, then cause you to die, then gather you into the Fire."* And revelation answered the same incident, in the sūrah of Yā Sīn, with the Book's permanent record of the gesture and the answer:

> *"And he presents for Us an example and forgets his [own] creation. He says: 'Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?' Say: 'He will give them life who produced them the first time; and He is, of all creation, Knowing.'"* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

"*And he forgets his own creation*" — the phrase is the Qur'an's diagnosis of verse 11's psychology: the man so busy examining the bone that he never examines the coming into being of the hand that examines it. The sūrah's sequence here (v. 10's question, v. 11's specimen, v. 12's admission of ruin) is thus not abstract debate but courtroom recall: the mockery is reproduced from a memory both the Prophet and the mockers shared.

**Why Decay Is the Problem No Materialist Escapes**

Verse 11 states, in one line, the strongest possible form of the objection, which makes the verse a gift to the reader: the sūrah lets the denial pick its own weapon. The weapon is entropy — the universal drift of structures into crumbs — and the denier's syllogism is airtight if the premises are all: reassembly of scattered, pulverized bodies is impossible; God is said to do it; therefore God is said to do the impossible. The Qur'an's refutation never disputes the first premise about *scattering*; it disputes the hidden premise that scattering equals non-existence-for-God. The answer arrives in a chain: "*Who produced them the first time*" (36:79); "*Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them?*" (2:216, in sense; cf. 36:81); "*And He is He who originates creation, then repeats it, and it is most easy for Him*" (30:27 — the verse's own superlative: *ahwana ʿalayhi*). But verse 11 does more than point at the answer: by preserving the objection at its most material, it forces the answer itself to be material, and the sūrah obliges in the next verses — with the single cry and the instant plain. Islamic eschatology is famously, stubbornly bodily; the denier's rotten bone is answered not with a soul's poetry but with a re-formation the Qur'an describes as a quake, a blast, a standing, a plain — because the objection was always about the bones, and God does not dodge the objection.

**One Bone That Does Not Rot — and Why the Detail Matters**

The Sunnah supplies a detail the tradition loves for its symmetry, in a report of al-Bukhārī (Book of Exegesis, chapter on Sūrat Qāf, no. 4935) from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"Every part of the son of Adam decays except one bone: the coccyx (al-ʿajbu al-dhanab); from it he was created and from it he will be reconstructed."*

The commentators link the report to this verse precisely because the denier's claim is about *total* decay: "when we have become decayed bones." The tradition's answer is that decay was never total to begin with — the body that seems to dissolve carries, in this account, one kernel of continuity, the tail-bone's seed, and re-creation proceeds from a residue the corpse itself never owned. Whatever weight a reader gives the anatomical detail — and the muḥaddithūn cite the report without embarrassment — the logic it serves is the sūrah's: the bones do not get to write the terms of their own story; they will "*come forth from the tombs*" as the Qur'an says of them in the very next breath (cf. 36:51), scattering "*as if they were locusts spread abroad*" (54:7) — decay's last image not as an argument against resurrection but as resurrection's opening curtain.

**A Lesson for Whoever Fears — and the Curious Honesty of the Denier**

Finally, note what the sūrah does *not* do at verse 11: it does not censor the disgust. The Qur'an lets its audience keep the image of powder and dry socket because the verse is one of the "lessons" the sūrah will name (v. 26): the body of every mocker and every believer is at this moment, while both still speak, assembling the materials of the resurrection hour by hour, the same materials — which is why the hadith's most surgical single sentence about this world fits here: the Prophet ﷺ said, "*The world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the denier*" (Muslim 2956). To the denier, bones are the sentence that ends the trial: the grave is paradise because he is sure nothing answers. To the believer, the same decay is the prison wall he is trained to lean against: the body dissolves, so it cannot be what he was made for. Verse 11's rotten bone, read by the two, becomes verse 12's two verdicts: ruin claimed as a threat by those who mean it as a dismissal — and a door, for those who learn early enough to fear it, opened from the other side by hands that never once lost grip of what they had made first.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:12

> **They say, "This, then, would be a ruinous return!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Slip Out Loud**

*Tilka ʾidan katrujatun khāsirah* — "That, then, is a loser's return!" The grammarians mark the verse's structure: the demonstrative *tilka*, "that," with the consequence-particle *ʾidan*, "then," — a little syllogism in three words: *if* what you preach is true, *then* this thing called being-returned *is* ruin. The verse is the Qur'an letting the denial say, out loud, what it privately fears. It is a slip of the tongue preserved forever: they are not arguing that the resurrection is false; they are conceding that if it is real, they are the ones on the losing side of it. Nobody in the history of argument has ever called a mere fantasy "a ruinous" anything. Ruin is a category that requires a stakeholder, and the stakeholder's voice is audible in the very word: *khāsirah*, from kh-s-r, the loss of a merchant whose cargo arrived spoiled, whose trade went under. The sneer of verses 10–11 has, in verse 12, become a lament wearing the sneer's clothes.

**Khāsira: A Merchant's Word for an Afterlife's Bankruptcy**

The root kh-s-r saturates the Qur'an's eschatology, and its commercial idiom is no accident of translation. *"Those who have lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection — unarguably, that is the clear loss"* (39:15) defines the day's ledger in the vocabulary of a transaction gone fatal; *"indeed, the ones who recite God's book, established prayer, and spent from what We provided them, secretly and openly, hope for a trade that will not fail"* (35:29) defines the believer by the opposite trade. Against that background, verse 12's *khāsira* is a confession in market language: these men have run their arithmetic and the arithmetic has come back wrong — and their answer, in the Qur'an's portrait, is not to change the calculation but to deny the currency. The source's note on the verse (Q, R) records the classical reading precisely: *they know that if this were the case, they would be ruined.* Al-Qurṭubī cites it as proof-text that the deniers' objection is not, at bottom, intellectual — their intellect has already lost the case; what rebels is their will, and the sūrah keeps the two apart on purpose, because its next verses address the will directly (36–41: the Fire displayed, the two abodes for the two loves).

**Why the Qur'an Reports the Verdict Rather Than Refuting It**

The rhetoric here is quietly masterful. Verse 12 is not an argument the sūrah must rebut; it is a witness the sūrah can *use*. The logic runs: the objector admits that a return to accountability would be his ruin; ruin is known only to a party who expected profit elsewhere; therefore the objector's real position in this life is that of a man who has invested everything in a market he suspects will default. This is exactly the condition the Qur'an elsewhere names as the psychology of denial: *"They know the truth of it the way they know their own sons, yet a group of them suppress the truth while they know it"* (2:146, in sense; and the Quraysh's own admission in 6:33: *"unarguably, they do not call you a liar, but the evildoers deny God's signs"*). The sūrah's method — quote them, let the quote convict them — is the same method it will use with Pharaoh at verses 23–24: the tyrant's own sentence, preserved, is his verdict. And this is why the verse belongs in the Resurrection-oath sequence: verses 1–5 swore the Day in; verses 6–11 described it; verse 12 reveals that the denial of it was never about evidence — which lets verses 13–14 dispense with evidence entirely and simply state the event: *a single cry, and they are on the plain.*

**The Pragmatic Insight the Verse Contains**

There is a reasoning in verse 12 worth transcribing for the modern reader, because it is the only purely rational argument any sūrah will concede to the denier's side — and the Qur'an lets it stand in the denier's own mouth as a warning, not a boast: *if* the return is possible, and these men treat it as certain-ruin-if-true, then dismissing it is not caution but the most reckless speculation in the book — staking everything on a zero that admits no proof. Al-Rāzī, who loved such reasoning, pressed exactly this shape in his *Mafātīḥ*: the denial demands certainty that the bones stay bones, which no eye has ever been given, while the warning comes from a One whose oaths the sūrah itself has just sworn — leaving the prudent between two unequal gambles, one of which costs nothing if the believer is wrong and saves everything if he is right. The tradition's phrasing is blunter and stays the Qur'an's register: Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, in the sound report of his death-hour counsel to his sons, spoke the same logic in miniature: had he a mass of gold as large as Uhud he would spend it before meeting his Lord with a single unreckoned day — ruin or no ruin, he had already chosen which word he would not be able to say. The denier of verse 12 has, by his own tongue, named the term; the believer has been named the stake; and the sūrah will, three verses later, show the merchant's account closed with a single cry.

**A Return Named, a Return Refused**

One last lexical note completes the verse: *karrah* — the word they use for "return" — is the noun of the root k-r-r, used of the charge of cavalry, of water returning to a basin, of the repeated return of the same event: *karrat*, "another time around." In the sūrah of verses 10–12, it is the only word for the afterlife these men have not yet surrendered: they will concede (v. 10) and lament (v. 12), but the *karrah* itself, the second charge, remains the thing they are absolutely sure will not come — which is precisely what verse 13 answers: *it will come as a single cry.* The mockers named the return; God named its tempo; and the whole exchange — three verses of sneer and concession — exists in the sūrah to show that even the refusal of resurrection confesses resurrection's logic, because the man who fears a return most is the man who will not stop, somewhere inside, believing that there is one. The sūrah's next word is therefore not a rebuttal at all, but a countdown.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:13

> **Yet it shall be but a single cry,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sound Against All That Refutation**

*Fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidaḫ* — "So it is but a single rebuke-cry." The instrument *fa-innamā*, "only, merely," and the noun *zajra*, a shout of driving or rebuke — the word a herdsman uses to move the herd, a driver uses to turn the beasts — reduce the entire apparatus of resurrection that the denier built up in his mind (bones re-knit, ages of sorting, an assembly of nations) to one vocal act. The commentators of the source (IK) press the contrast deliberately: the objectors of verses 10–12 were imagining a labor; the verse announces a word. Al-Rāzī notes that the sūrah could have sworn the event by its own name — the Trumpet's Blast — but chose *zajra*, the human herding-cry, so that the comparison would bite: as the flock rises at the shout without being consulted, so the dead rise, without a committee, a repair, or a second thought, at one sound. The *ha* of *hiya* refers back to the affair, the matter of resurrection — the whole affair, the Day, the reckoning: all of it, in this verse's economy, one cry.

**"A Single Cry" and the Two Blasts: the Reconciliation**

The objection the commentators answer here is textual and obvious: elsewhere the Qur'an counts two blasts — *"And the Trumpet will be blown, then all who are in the heavens and on the earth will fall unconscious... then it will be blown again"* (39:68) — while this verse, and its twins in Sād and al-Qamar, says one. The source (IK) preserves the standard resolutions: the sūrah speaks of the *second* blast, after which there is no further blast, the whole of the gathering being sealed by that one cry; or it counts the two as one because the interval between them is as nothing, so that the resurrection occurs, so to speak, between two heartbeats of the same shout; or the "single cry" is generic for the event-class: in God's economy, the matter *is* one utterance, whatever its humanly discernible stages. The parallel verses clinch the rhetoric rather than the arithmetic — Yā Sīn, in three separate scenes, repeats the identical idiom: *"We sent down upon their people only one cry (zajrat), and they were annihilated"* (36:29); *"They await nothing but a single cry (zajra), which will seize them while they dispute"* (36:49); and, of Pharaoh's people's mock-assembly, *"This is but a single cry (zajra) in the dust"* (38:15). The cry that ended nations in the past is, in the sūrah's argument, the same instrument — with one pull — that ends history.

**The Tempo of the Divine Affair: Blinking, or Nearer**

The verse's answer-clause in the next line — *then behold, they are on the wide expanse* (v. 14) — is prefaced here by the sūrah's own physics of instantaneity, quoted in full because the Qur'an's cross-references are this tradition's exegesis:

> *"And to God belongs the unseen of the heavens and the earth. And the matter of the Hour is but as the blink of an eye, or nearer still."* (Qur'an 16:77)

and its twin oath in the sūrah of the Moon:

> *"And Our command is not but one, like the blink of an eye."* (Qur'an 54:50)

And the creational formula the sūrah-tradition always hears behind "one cry": *"His affair, when He wills a thing, is but to say to it, 'Be' — and it is"* (Qur'an 36:82). Read in the sūrah's sequence, these are not poetry but argument by scale: the denier's impossibility-calculation — scattered atoms, reassembled selves — assumes a God who works by handling parts. The Book's God works by willing, and the sūrah's oaths already named the workers: the angels who draw souls do not reassemble them first; they *take* — and taking is one motion. The single cry, on this reading, is the Day's answer to the bone-crusher's premise: decay is a process observed by those inside time; outside it, the affair is one. This is why the hadith-tradition ties verse 13 to the trumpet's terror in devotional practice: the Prophet ﷺ said, in the sound report of Abū Saʿīd in Sunan Abī Dāwūd (no. 3175) and al-Tirmidhī (no. 2431): *"How could I rest at ease, while the one who blows has swallowed the horn, listened for the permission, and inclined his forehead?"* — the whole Day compressed into one figure already leaning into one breath, awaiting one word.

**Zajra Also for Death: the Private Single Cry**

The source records the reading of Q and Ṭ that verses 13–14 also touch every private resurrection — death — and the sūrah's oaths (vv. 1–2) guarantee that each soul's affair *is*, literally, a single pull: however long the dying, the moment of extraction has no parts, no intervals in which to reconsider. The classical authors loved this double scope, and it deserves restating for a modern reader whose experience of catastrophe is precisely that discovery: in every crisis finally reached — the diagnosis, the accident, the news that splits a life — people report the same odd compression, events arriving in a flash that leaves the slowest lives behind, as though the mind's time and the affair's time are two clocks, and at the great moments the second clock simply overrules the first. The verse trains the hearer to live by the second clock: the Day you deny is not distant because it is difficult; it is, in the divine arithmetic, already loaded — one word from your own last one.

**Why No Refutation Follows**

The last feature of verse 13 is the absence of one. After verses 10–12, the reader expects a rebuttal — evidence of God's power, fossils of mercy in the anatomy of return. The sūrah instead utters a fact: *but a single cry.* The argument by refutation gives way to argument by announcement, because a claim as absurd as "the Almighty cannot re-make what He first made" is not answered with more data but with the correction of the picture of power the question smuggled. This is the method Ibn Taymiyyah later generalized in his *Darʾ taʿāruḍ*: the sound intellect and the sound transmission do not clash, because the clash was manufactured by a false premise about the terms; here the false premise is that time-bound reassembly measures God, and the verse's single cry dissolves it by describing, in one stroke, what the affair *is* rather than what it *costs*. And with the cost-question dissolved, the sūrah can, and does, simply open the next line onto the scene itself — the plain, the standing, the gathered — verse 14's terrible, open sky: *fa-idhā hum bi-al-sāḥirah*: and there they all are.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:14

> **then, behold, they will be upon a wide expanse.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fāʾ of Surprise, and the Plain That Replaces the World**

*Fa-idhā hum bi-al-sāḥirah* — in the Arabic's single short line: "and then — there they are, on the wide expanse." The *fāʾ* followed by *idhā* is the grammarians' particle of *surprise-juncture* (fāʾ al-mufājʾa): used when an expected interval collapses into no interval at all — "he set out, and behold, he was at the gate." The sūrah has just sworn that the affair is one cry (v. 13); this verse performs the swearing in syntax: there is no verb of travel, no clause of arrival; the subjects of the cry are already *there*, the way the traveler wakes in the destination city. And the name of the destination is the verse's own treasure: *al-sāḥiraḫ*, the wide expanse — the noun that gave the sūrah, in the earlier reciters' lists, its second title, Sūrat al-Sāḥira.

**What the Word Sāḥira Means**

The root s-ḥ-r in Arabic names the bare, open face of the earth — the salt-flat, the desert pan, the surface on which nothing grows and nothing stands between the ground and the sky. Al-Zamakhsharī and the lexicographers transmit the proverb *ard sāḥira*, a land stripped bare; the same root's other family — *sāḥir*, the "top" or "surface" of a thing, and the earth's *ṣaḥra*, its crust — converge on one image: a level surface, unbroken, uncovered, exposed. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī (IK, Q) read the verse thus: the earth's own face, but transfigured — *"The Day when the earth will be changed into a different earth, and the heavens as well, and they will stand forth before God, the One, the Subduer"* (14:48), the sūrah's own best commentary, quoted in full because its verb, *baraẓū*, is the very exposure this plain is for. Two other determinations appear in the sources: the barren, level ground promised at *"your Lord will leave it a level plain, barren, seeing in it neither hollow nor ridge"* (20:106–107); and, in some reports, a specific plain near Jerusalem, the vicinity of Arīḥā — a localization the sober exegetes record and do not press. Al-Rāzī and Abū Muslim preserve the brilliant secondary reading, present in the source apparatus: *sāḥira* from s-h-r in the sense of being wide *awake* — the earth "awake," or the people so terrified they cannot sleep; and both senses, surface and wakefulness, meet in a place from which no awareness has ever been removed: on that plain, nothing is closed and nothing is asleep.

**Barefoot, Naked, Uncircumcised — the Scene's Own Ḥadīth**

The Sunnah supplies the expanse's human detail in a ḥadīth agreed upon, Abū Hurayrah reporting the Prophet's ﷺ words:

> *"People will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised."* Then he recited: "As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it" (21:104). (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2860 — with the addition that the first to be clothed will be Ibrāhīm, God's intimate friend)

The three negations are a sociology of the plain: barefoot — no land to stand on as property; naked — no clothes to stand for rank; uncircumcised — even the marks of identity and lineage stripped. Every boundary the self used to draw on the old earth — mine and yours, honored and humiliated, insider and outsider — is absent from the wide expanse, and the hadith's next line, in Muslim's version, tells what replaces them: the sun drawn near, and the sweat sinking to each person's heels in proportion to their deeds (Muslim 2864) — the one dignity left being the one that is earned. When al-Nāziʿāt's deniers sneered about "restored" bones (v. 10), they imagined restoration as reinstatement in their former world; the sūrah answers with a gathering where their shoes, their robes, and their names are already gone, and where the only thing that walks with a man across the flat is the weight of what he did.

**Why a Plain and Not a Palace: the Logic of the Setting**

Exegetes have asked why the Qur'an fixes this scene on openness rather than, as the later sūrahs describe, gardens and thrones for the saved and their opposites for the lost. The answer the sequence itself gives is judicial: before verdicts there must be an arena, and an arena's first property is sight-lines. The plain is the court's floor: no ridges for the guilty to hide behind (20:106's "neither hollow nor ridge"), no walls for the powerful to shelter inside, no distance between the Witnessed and the Witnesses. This is why the Makkan sūrah uses it precisely against Makkan pride — the deniers of verses 10–12 were the owners of the Ḥaram, the people of the tents at Minā, the men of the trade-routes' sheltered caravan-courts; the sūrah's verdict on their worldview is an expanse with no awning, the caravan's end. And it is why the same word *sāḥir* must have stung in the Prophet's ﷺ recital at dawn: to Arab ears, *sāḥira* half-rhymed with *siḥr*, magic — and Makkah had already called this Warner a *sāḥir*, a sorcerer, "*as if they were a startled herd*" facing his *ʿajāʾib* (cf. 37:14–15; 51:52's refrain, quoted at the sūrah's first oaths, applies nowhere better than here). The miracle they denied is answered by a miracle with no incantation: the whole race of men relocated, in a breath, onto the open palm of a renewed world.

**Between the Cry and the Court: the Mercy of the Interval**

Read as part of the sūrah's argument, verse 14 does one final, quiet thing: it shows the distance between the last mockery of verse 12 and the first moment of the standing on the plain — a distance of a single cry. The denier's imagined eternity of non-being shrinks to nothing; but so does, the reciters have always felt, the believer's imagined eternity of waiting. There is no third stage, no waiting-room of ages between death and the plain; the sūrah's sequence is oaths — cry — expanse. This compression is the entire sūrah's moral instrument, in this life and beyond: the hadith tradition's most quoted word on the Hour, agreed upon from Ibn ʿUmar, has the Prophet ﷺ say: *"I have been sent thus and the Hour thus," joining his forefinger and middle finger* (al-Bukhārī 6503) — the long Makkan night's sermons boiling down to one gesture of two touching fingers. The wide expanse is that close; its openness, accordingly, is not exposure for exposure's sake. What the plain will make visible — verses 35–36's remembered deeds and the displayed Fire — is already visible to the One whose breath moved the angels of verse 5; the Day's only novelty is that the rest of us will finally see what the open sky has never had to hide.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:15

> **Hast thou heard tell of Moses,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Opens a Courtroom**

*Hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā* — "Has the account of Moses reached you?" The interrogative here is not a request for information; the Book that opens with "the account (ḥadīth) of" questions is not asking whether the Messenger has heard. The formula *hal atāka* belongs to the Qur'an's high rhetoric of summons — used at the head of Sūrat al-Ghāshiya ("Has the Overwhelming reached you?"), at the companions of the Elephant, at Pharaoh's hosts (79:15's own verse-family elsewhere: 88:1; 89:11; 85:17) — and its function, as the balāgha scholars explain, is *tashwīq*: to prick the ear awake, to make the listener lean in before a single fact is delivered. The sūrah has just drawn, in five oaths and a single cry, the whole cosmos tilting toward the plain of gathering; now, without transition, it asks a question about a man and a tyrant, and every Arab who heard it knew that a question about Moses means an account of Pharaoh. The courtroom of verse 6–14's Day opens onto a precedent from history — and the precedent's case number is "reached you," as if to say: you have all this before heard, and it was not enough, so hear it again in the shape that convicts.

**Why Moses, Why Now: the Chronology the Source Preserves**

The source's introduction makes the sharpest single observation available on this verse: this is most likely the first account of Moses in the chronological order of revelation — revealed when the Prophet ﷺ was still a small, besieged voice in Makkah, decades before the Medinan verses would legislate, and centuries before the sūrah of al-Qaṣaṣ would narrate the story in full. Two consequences follow. First, the Makkan Arabs were not hearing the story for the first time: the names Firawn, Hamān, Mūsā circulated through the markets and through Jewish and Christian communities along the trade routes, and the Qur'an presumes what its own verse states — *that it would "assume some basic knowledge of the story of Moses among the Arabs"* — and the early sūrahs' tactic is to use the known narrative as evidence, not to teach it as news. Second, the placement is an act of consolation with legal teeth: to a Messenger told he is a sorcerer, a madman, a poet, the God who swore five oaths by the soul-takers says: the greatest prophet before you also stood before a man who claimed to be god, and this is what happened. As the Qur'an generalizes the same therapeutic function of its stories: *"And each [account] We relate to you to strengthen thereby your heart"* (11:120) — the narration is *taṭyīb al-qalb* — heart-steadying: history as ballast.

**The Anatomy of a Story Told in Eleven Verses**

What follows in verses 15–25 is the entire career of a confrontation compressed to its judicial essentials: the call at Ṭuwā (16), the commission against the tyrant (17), the gentle opening gambit (18–19), the greatest sign (20), the denial and the hardening (21), the hasty turn to counter-conspiracy (22), the muster and the proclamation of divinity (23–24), and the seizure — *a punishment exemplary in the Hereafter and in this world* (25). The compression itself is the commentary: the sūrah selects nothing that does not serve the resurrection-argument. Moses' childhood, the burning bush's details, the plagues' catalogue — the later Makkan and Medinan sūrahs will take these up at length — are all held back, because al-Nāziʿāt needs precisely one thing from the story: that Pharaoh, who thought power was the final word, was answered by a Word, and that the drowning of his army and the drying of his name are one event with the Day. This is the Qur'an's editorial genius on display, and the classical authors (al-Jurjānī among them) counted such *maqāṣid*-shaped selection among the proofs of its inimitability: a narrative in the Qur'an is never a story complete in itself; it is an argument whose every joint bears weight.

**"Reached You": the Prophet ﷺ and the Prophet Mūsā**

The diction deserves one further look, because the sūrah has already reached, by the earliest reports, its reciters to the practice of the Prophet ﷺ: the Bukhārī transmission that an Ansari imam at Juḥāt recited this very sūrah at dawn prayer, saying he had watched the Messenger of God ﷺ recite its like, means that "has the account reached you" was uttered, year after year, in the half-dark of Fajr, to people who knew it had reached them — and were asked by the re-utterance whether it had reached them *deeply enough*. The tradition of the *sunan* preserves a related texture: the Prophet ﷺ, on the day of 'Āshūrā, fasted and commanded the fast *because* "God saved Mūsā and drowned Firawn" — a claim to kinship with Mūsā stated in the middle of Arabia (al-Bukhārī 2004; Muslim 1130). The account that "reached" this community was therefore never neutral history: to be told of Mūsā in this sūrah is to be enrolled in his side of the case — the side that stands, at dawn, before God, and the side that drowns, at the end, every claimant to His throne.

**For the Reader Who Has Heard It All Before**

Finally, the verse carries a mercy for every congregation that has heard the story a hundred times, including our own. "Has the account of Moses reached you?" is the question the Book asks at every rehearsal: not whether you know the content, but whether the content has reached you — whether eleven verses about a drowned tyrant are functioning, in your heart, as they functioned in the hearts of Makkah's first hearers, as an earthquake small and private, a single cry before the wide expanse. The Arabic allows *atāka* in this idiom to mean "come to you" in the sense of arriving, becoming present — which is the difference between information and address. The sūrah's five oaths said the Day is certain; this verse says: and the Day's verdict has already been rehearsed once in history, with witnesses, with documents, and with a body dragged out of the sea as the sūrah will say in two minutes. The account has reached you. The question of the verse is now the question of the sūrah: where, in the story, are you standing?

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:16

> **when his Lord called out to him in the holy valley of Ṭuwā?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Passive Voice, Private Call**

*Nūdī bi-ṭuwā al-wādi al-muqaddasi* — "he was called out to, in Ṭuwā, the holy valley." Note what the grammar removes: no subject is spoken here, because the caller needs no introduction after *rabbihī* — "his Lord" — has already attached the most intimate of titles to the most private of moments. The verb is passive, and Arabic rhetoric reads the passive of address for its humility: the one who is called, is called. Mūsā initiates nothing in this scene; not even the hearing. The verse also preserves, in the definite article of *al-wādi*, a known geography — a valley already named among the peoples of the Sinai — but the honor lies not in the coordinates. As God says in the account of this same call in Ṭā Hā: *"Indeed, I am your Lord, so remove your sandals; you are in the sacred valley, Ṭuwā"* (20:12) — the sanctity is in the nearness, not in the soil: the ground is holy because One who is nearer to Mūsā than the ground addresses him from it.

**What Ṭuwā Means**

The toponym carries two exegetical layers recorded in the source. The majority treat *Ṭuwā* as the name of the valley itself — and some of the early grammarians (Sībawayhi's school cited the point) held that the bare verbal form *ṭuwā* without "valley" in this verse is itself a miracle of concision: the place-name and the sentence "in the valley" collapse into one word, as later Arabic poets imitated the style. The second layer is the source's own: a derivation from *ṭawā / ṭayya*, "to roll up" — so that *Ṭuwā* names a valley "wrapped in sanctity," or, in the reading al-Qurṭubī preserves, a land blessed *twice over* (ṭawwatan: once at its making, once at the call). Al-Rāzī adds an interior gloss that the tradition loves: what is rolled up is rolled toward what remains — the valley is where the world was, for one night, rolled away from Mūsā, leaving only the Addresser. The word *al-muqaddas* — holy, pure, set apart — shares its root with *quds*, the sanctuary's clearing, and with the angels' *quddūs*: holiness here is not decoration; it is the removal of the ordinary from a place so the ordinary can be re-crowned with command.

**The God Who Calls by Name: the Cross-Accounts in Their Own Words**

The Qur'an narrates this call five times, and the other accounts are this verse's running commentary — each adds one facet, and the classical mufassirūn read them together precisely as the sūrah invites:

> *"Indeed, I am your Lord, so remove your sandals; you are in the sacred valley, Ṭuwā. And I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed."* (Qur'an 20:12–13)

> *"So when he came to it, he was called: blessed is whoever is in the fire and whoever is around it — and exalted is God, Lord of the worlds."* (Qur'an 27:8)

> *"Then when he came to it, he was called from the right bank of the valley, in the blessed tree: O Mūsā, indeed I am your Lord, so remove your sandals."* (Qur'an 28:30)

Three features recur that bear on verse 16: the *call* ("he was called") rather than a theophany — God speaks, and the mountains later crumble when Mūsā asks to see (7:143), so that the tradition's creed holds both truths at once: no sight, real speech; the *barefoot* etiquette, removing the sandals whose leather carries the dust of other owners and other concerns; and the *election* preceding the commission — "I have chosen you" (20:13) before "go to Pharaoh" (79:17): the mission stands on a prior love, which is why the mission's terrors do not destroy the missionary. Ibn Kathīr underscores against the philosophers of the age: the God of Mūsā calls from outside, in a real voice that is heard — the *kalām* doctrine the school of ahl al-sunna would spend centuries defending, that God truly speaks, wills, and chooses whom He wills, as in the verse: *"And God spoke to Mūsā directly, saying"* — *kallama Allāhu Mūsā taklīman* (4:164), the emphatic double-accusative of speech that the creed-books cite as their pillar.

**The Right Bank, the Fire, the Tree — an Etiquette of Approaches**

The details clustered around *bi-ṭuwā* in the parallel accounts were mined by the Sufi-exegetical strand — preserved, among others, in al-Qushayrī's *Latāʾif* — for the psychology of every human audience with God: the fire (divine presence) that Mūsā first took for a campfire, and approached with family and baggage (20:10), shows how revelation often arrives disguised as ordinary need; the tree, described in some reports as green in winter and in summer at once, shows a nature that obeys only the One who calls from within it; and the voice addressing Mūsā by name — "yā Mūsā" — the grammarians note is the *nidāʾ* of tenderness: a name, not a title. The Muʿtazilite exegetes and the traditionalists differed fiercely about what exactly was heard (a created sound? an attribute eternal?); al-Nāziʿāt, with the quiet of its passive voice, stands above the quarrel and teaches the verse's true point: the same God who called a shepherd on a mountainside in the middle of Egyptian exile is the God who swore five oaths that He will call every corpse out of its plain (v. 14). The valley is the proof of the plain: if He spoke then, He will speak at the trumpet's cry — and what one voice did for one man in one valley, one cry will do for all men on all earth.

**Barefoot Before Power, and Before Fire**

Last, the practical seam verses 15–16 open onto the rest of the narrative: Mūsā stands naked-footed, un-armed, un-sent, addressed by a holy valley — and is sent to the most armed, sent man of his age, the man whose courts ran on prostration and gold. The commission that follows (v. 17) is believable only because of the manner of verse 16: the one sent into Firawn's court has already stood, barefoot, in a court where no one is clothed in office but in call. Every later reader who carries a duty into a hostile room learns the verse's technique by the reverse: first know where you were summoned from, and you will know how to stand where you are sent.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:17

> **"Go unto Pharaoh! Truly he has rebelled!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Imperative of Descent**

*Iḍhab ilā Firʿawna innaḥ ṭaghā* — "Go to Pharaoh; he has transgressed." After the intimacy of the holy valley, a bare command of exposure, and the sequence is deliberate: election in 16, commission in 17 — the pattern the Qur'an repeats for every prophet, and which the exegetes of the *ʿuṣūl* extracted as a rule of prophethood: the call is never for the called; "go," not "stay and glow." And the direction of the motion matters: from the high valley down into the capital, from the *muqaddas* to the palace where a mortal calls himself god — the geography of the sūrah is already theology. Al-Rāzī notes that the sūrah does not say "call Pharaoh to your Lord" but "go," as if the mere approach, the walking into the room, is the first half of the miracle: truth that goes to meet power is itself a sign, the sign the Meccan audience — told to preach in a city that mocked them at dawn — needed before the story's content.

**Ṭaghā: The Disease the Command Diagnoses**

The single cause the verse cites is not "he disbelieved" but *innaḥ ṭaghā* — "he tyrannically transgressed." The root ṭ-gh-y describes water that overflows its banks, a person who exceeds every measure set for him; *ṭughyān* is therefore, in the tradition's moral physics, sin as *excess* rather than sin as defect — which is why the sūrah will later pair it precisely with *al-ākhira*'s loss (37:37: *ammā man ṭaghā... fa-inna al-jaḥīma hiya al-maʾwā*). Ibn Kathīr, glossing this verse, insists: Pharaoh was not led astray by doubt about the evidence — he saw the greatest sign (v. 20) — but by the overflow of self that the evidence could not drain. This diagnosis answers the whole sūrah's argument: the resurrection-deniers of verses 10–12 are not victims of insufficient data; they are patients of the same ṭughyān, "the same disease under a milder sky," and the mission to Pharaoh is the mission to Makkah restated (the source says exactly this: the narrative is offered "as an example for the Prophet and his followers of what will eventually become of unjust rulers, like those of Makkah"). The word also etymologizes the sūrah's own first oath: God swears by *al-nāziʿāt* — the wrestlers — and sends his messenger to the overflowing one, as if to say: water that floods is met, in the end, by a hand that wrenches (v. 1), gently for the penitent, otherwise.

**The Etiquette of Confrontation: "Speak to Him a Gentle Word"**

The parallel commission in Ṭā Hā preserves the manners that al-Nāziʿāt's curt imperative contains by implication: *"Go, both of you, to Pharaoh, for he has transgressed, and speak to him a gentle word, perhaps he will remember or fear"* (20:43–44). The tradition read the pair as the constitution of daʿwa to power, and its detail — *qawlan layyinan*, "a soft word," the same root used of ripe dates, of water cooled and easy down a parched throat — sets two limits that the sūrah's own story then obeys exactly: the word to a tyrant is soft, but the address is still the address; Mūsā goes to Pharaoh (v. 17), offers purification (v. 18), shows the sign (v. 20), and never once calls the false god "god," nor the lie "taste." Al-Qurṭubī draws out a further rule the source names: no people are punished until the message has reached them in gentleness — *"We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15), and the verses the source strings together (28:59; 26:208; 6:131; 10:13) make the whole narrative a proof of divine fairness, which matters in a sūrah whose end-claim is a judgment-day court: Pharaoh is destroyed *after* being offered purification (vv. 18–19), so that when the Great Calamity comes (v. 34), no one can say: the mercy was short.

**Why Send a Man with a Stammer to an Embodied Nation**

The verse, with its two-word diagnosis, answers the unasked objection: the Prophet ﷺ was, like Mūsā in the fuller accounts, un-armed and out-spoken — the sūrah sends both across the same distance with the same credentials. The tradition knows Mūsā's own complaint ("my chest tightens, my tongue looses its knot," 20:25–27), and it remembers that the same mouth was also promised: *"I will be with you, hearing and seeing"* (20:46). The lesson the exegetes attach here is structural rather than sentimental: the commission is sized to the sender, not to the sender's equipment. Pharaoh's court ran on speakers, sorcerers, and treasury; Mūsā was sent with a staff, a hand, and a verse — and the sūrah's five oaths had already sworn that the staff-and-hand economy is the one the universe actually runs on. Ibn Taymiyyah, preaching the ethics of confronting rulers in his *al-Siyāsa al-sharʿiyya* and in his letters, returned to exactly this passage for the two poles of the prophetic method: firmness in the truth delivered, gentleness in the way it is delivered — the gentleness not a dilution but the very sunna of this verse: God could have crushed; He sent a speech-act, and He said: *qulā lahu qawlan layyinan* — so every caller after Mūsā enters his court with soft speech and a straight spine, which is the only posture that both disobeys the tyrant and refuses to become him.

**A Mission Makkah Could Still Refuse**

Finally, the verse's tense is present and live. "He has transgressed" is stated as fact; "Go" is issued as command; and, as the story will show (v. 21), even the *result* is not guaranteed: the sign is shown, and Pharaoh denies. This is the sūrah's answer to every age's temptation to measure missions by conversions: Mūsā's mission to Pharaoh "failed" in the sense the court records would file — no believers, one drowning — and succeeded in the sense the Book of the ages records: the empire's god unmasked as a drowned man, and the slave-nation's shepherd named *kalīm Allāh* forever. The Meccans, hearing "go to Pharaoh," were being offered the same ledger: the dawn they stoned in mockery was, verse for verse, the court they would one day see drowned in the plain.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:18

> **And say, 'Dost thou wish to be purified**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Softest Door Ever Offered to the Worst Man**

*Qul hal laka ilā an tazakkā* — "Say: is there for you toward that you purify?" The Arabic idiom *hal laka ilā...* is the language of offering a destination: not "obey," not "submit," but "is the road open for you — do you want it?" A tyrant who had made himself a god is addressed, at the first word of the miracle age, with an option. Ibn Kathīr marks this as the sūrah's proof of God's mercy being prior to His wrath: the plagues of the flood, the locusts, the lice and the frogs (7:133) and the sea are still ahead, and the first and only proposal delivered to the Pharaoh is purification. The source draws the parallel that makes the verse a mirror held up to the sūrah's own audience: just as the Prophet ﷺ invited Quraysh — his own kin, with stones in hand — Moses was ordered to begin with the greatest criminal of the age by an invitation, not an indictment; *"and say: my purification (tazkiya) and yours is not upon you"* is the logic, as in the Book's own line for the Prophet: *"You are not a guardian over them"* (88:22) — every offer ends with the offerer's hands open.

**Zakā: the Root of Cleansing, Growing, and Purity-in-Exchange**

The verb *tazakkā* belongs to z-k-w, the root whose field runs through the whole moral economy of the Qur'an: *zakā* is pasture that grew because it was watered; *zakāt* is alms that purify wealth precisely by shrinking it (9:103: *"take from their wealth a charity by which you purify them"*); *al-zakī* is the blameless, and God's own name in the Sūrat al-Aʿlā is *qad aflaha man tazakkā, wa-dhakara isma rabbihi fa-ṣallā* — "successful is whoever purifies himself, remembering his Lord and praying" (87:14–15) — which the classical mufassirūn note is this verse's twin: the same verb *tazakkā*, the same offer, addressed in al-Aʿlā to everyone, here to Pharaoh. The word's anatomy forbids any reading that makes "purification" a ritual bath of the conscience: z-k-w is cleansing *joined to increase*, a pruning that grows the tree — so Mūsā's question to Pharaoh means: do you want the cutting that will make you live? And it is why the same root's *munāfiqūn* are those who *claim* purity while withholding growth: the offer to Pharaoh is not about stain-removal but about whether the man will let himself be pruned at all.

**Why a Question: the Logic of Addressing Will**

The verse's form teaches the sūrah's entire theory of human responsibility. An imperative ("purify yourself") could be deflected as force — and Pharaoh's whole state existed as reaction to force (v. 23: he "gathered and proclaimed," armies, magicians, marshals); a question ("do you wish?") lands inside the one citadel power never conquers: the will's own consent. The exegetes point to the Book's own verdict on the same man's method: "so he belittled his people, and they obeyed him; indeed they were a defiant people" (43:54) — a kingdom built on belittling answers commands, not questions; the question reaches where the command never could. Al-Rāzī presses it further: the verse implies no purification is *imposed* even on the tyrant whose imposition of falsehood enslaved nations — divine justice refuses to answer ṭughyān with a compelled purity, because a purity not wanted is not purity (which is the whole reason faith that is not chosen is not faith: *lā ikrāha fī al-dīn*, 2:256). And the psychology is exact: offers preserve the dignity of the addressee, and dignity is the only soil in which even a Pharaoh could have repented — the offer is not weakness; it is the strongest force in the room: an escape route the proud man can take without shame, because God calls taking it "purification," not surrender.

**Hal Laka: the Mercy Against the Man Who Feared Being Human**

The scene's grandeur sharpens against the fuller narratives: the Mūsā-Pharaoh exchanges elsewhere show a court already drowning in self-deification — "I know no god for you other than me" (28:38) — and this sūrah's verse 18 is the one hour in which the drowning man was offered air, and offered it in the words the Book reserves for its friends: "successful is whoever purifies" (87:14). Ibn ʿAbbās's transmitted nuance — that the offer was genuine, Pharaoh could have believed and been saved, and was then sealed by his own election of arrogance (cf. 47:16; 4:155 in sense) — belongs here to the theology of verse 25's "punishment exemplary": the exemplary punishment is exemplary precisely because the alternative was real. The sūrah, addressing its own Pharaohs — the Makkan lords who called the dawn-sermons sorcery — is performing, through this scene, the very offer it reports: *hal laka ilā an tazakkā*: the door stands; the price of refusal is only ever your own stain.

**For the Believer: the Question That Never Retires**

The reciters of this sūrah at Fajr — the dawn-imam of Juḥāt in al-Bukhārī — heard the verse as a second, personal summons: the Pharaoh in the narrative is the pharaoh in the breast. The Sufi-exegetical stream (al-Sulamī, and the source's own quotation of al-Rāzī's inner reading of vv. 1–5) read the sūrah's five oaths as the soul's journey; here the journey's first stage is named by its one verb: *tazakkā* — not perfection demanded but direction offered. So the tradition's simple practice: when this verse is recited, ask aloud, privately, what the verse asked of the greatest tyrant — "do you wish to be purified?" — and answer, honestly, with the day's one small pruning: a debt returned, a grudge released, a look lowered, a prayer delayed no further. A heart that can be addressed this way has less Pharaoh in it than it fears; and the verse's mercy is that the question is not "are you pure?" but "do you want the purification?" — the will's movement, however small, being the movement for which the whole story, from valley to sea, was told.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:19

> **and that I guide thee to thy Lord that thou mightest have fear?'"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guide Who Points Beyond Himself**

*Wa-aʿluka ilā rabbika fa-takhshā* — "and I guide you to your Lord, so that you fear." The syntax performs the ethics of every true messenger: the verb of guidance takes, as its destination, not the guide but *your Lord*. Mūsā does not say "follow me"; he says "I will show the road to Him who is already your Lord." The possessive — *rabbika*, your Lord — is equally deliberate: the God Mūsā offers is already Pharaoh's Creator, Sustainer, and Owner; the guidance adds no new master, only the end of pretense about the old one. The whole daʿwa is therefore restorative, not invasive: you are not being recruited to a foreign lord; you are being walked back to your own. This is the verse's answer to every age's suspicion that faith is the exchange of one authority for another: the caller in the Qur'an's school is a waymarker who retires himself at the boundary stone.

**Fear as the Goal, Not Terror as the Means**

*Fa-takhshā* — "so that you fear" — and the sūrah has chosen *khawf*-family language twice now (vv. 16–19 in the source's note; v. 26: *liman yakhshā*; v. 40: *man khāsha maqāma rabbihī*), because *khashya* in the Qur'an is not the flinching of a hunted animal but the reverence of a knower. *"Only the scholars fear God among His servants"* (35:28) — knowledge and *khashya* welded together. Offered to Pharaoh, the aim of the call is stated as conversion of the heart's posture, not the court's protocol: not "that you might cease opposing me" but "that you might stand before God with awe." Al-Qurṭubī notes here that the verse proves the daʿwa's objective is taqwā — the fear that reformulates a life — and that any invitation to God which stops at allegiance, ritual, or identity has misread the destination Mūsā names. The sound criterion the scholars prefer here is the Book's own picture — *"He brings them out of the darknesses into the light"* (2:257) — and *khashya* is the first light: the moment a man feels he is answerable, the wall of impunity on which ṭughyān leaned starts to crack. The tradition's renewing word for this is the Prophet's ﷺ counsel: *"Renew your faith,"* he said, *"for faith wears out in the heart just as a new garment wears out"* (al-Ḥākim; graded ṣaḥīḥ by modern revisers) — and what renews it is exactly the pair verses 18–19 weld together: the wish to be purified, and the fear that keeps the wish awake.

**"Perhaps He Will Remember or Fear": The Humility of Method**

The commission's parallel wording in Ṭā Hā — *wa-qūlā lahu al-qawla al-layyina laʿallahu yadhakkaru aw yakhshā* (20:44) — adds the particle *laʿalla*, "perhaps," and here al-Rāzī sees the entire epistemology of warning: God addresses the tyrant with "perhaps," and the Messenger of Makkah must say "perhaps" too — not because the result is doubtful to the All-Knowing, but because human persuasion, by nature, offers motives to a free will, and cannot convert the offer into certainty without annihilating the addressee. This is why there is no coercion in this verse's program (2:256 in letter), and why the sūrah's later verdicts read, in the classical courts of theology, as the vindication of divine fairness: He warned — gently, by name, with an exit — *then* seized (v. 25). The Makkan hearers, addressed by the same "perhaps," are enrolled as Pharaoh's colleagues in the offer; and the meekness of the instrument — one word "perhaps" — is what makes the sūrah's whole narrative unanswerable to the charge of divine bullying.

**How Fear and Hope Share One Root Here**

Notice the verse's emotional sequence: *huda* (guidance), *khashya* (fear) — but the call began with *hal laka* ("would you wish?") in v. 18, which is hope in the shape of a question. The sūrah thus presents the two wings of the believer's heart — *rajāʾ* and *khawf* — inside a single prophetic sentence, which is precisely the balanced formula the Sunnah teaches: *"My Lord, I ask of You Paradise, and I seek refuge in You from the Fire"* (Ibn Mājah 3831, from ʿĀʾishah, who said she never heard him ask, at the head of the dawn prayer, for anything else; graded ṣaḥīḥ). Scholars of *akhlāq* derived from verses 18–19, in the source's own frame, the rule of address to the stubborn: hope opens the door ("do you wish to be purified?"), fear sets the house in order ("that you may fear"), and the guide walks out the way he came, pointing back. A single Makkan dawn-hearer could carry the formula home and use it that same day on his brother, his apprentice, his child — and this is the point of the narrative: the Qur'an is not filing Pharaoh in the museum of antiquity; it is handing the world the conversation that would have saved him.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:20

> **Then he showed him the greatest sign.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sign, All the Weight of Heaven**

*Fa-arāhu al-āyata al-kubrā* — "so he showed him the great sign, the greatest." The feminine superlative *kubrā* marks the noun *āya* (sign, verse, miracle) in its absolute size: of all the wonders Mūsā stood before Pharaoh with, this sūrah selects the supreme one and names no more — which is itself a lesson in rhetoric: the audience knows the file; the advocate cites the single strongest exhibit. The commentators differ over which exhibit this is (the source records all the candidates: the staff that became a serpent, the white hand, the cleaving sea, or the whole package of signs), because the Qur'an elsewhere calls the same confrontation the nine clear signs — *and We certainly gave Moses nine evident signs* (17:101) — without attaching *kubrā* to a single item. Al-Ṭabarī's inclusive preference: whatever the greatest sign was, its defining trait is what the sūrah needs: a demonstration on Pharaoh's own terrain, so total that no counter-sign could rival it, shown to one man who would spend his remaining hours pretending it had not happened (v. 21).

**Why the Staff and the Serpent Fit "the Greatest"**

Among the candidates, the serpent carries the strongest claim to *kubrā*, and not merely for the terror of it. Egypt's throne bore the *uraeus* — the rearing cobra of royal and divine protection on the pharaonic crown; the goddess Wadjet was depicted as a serpent, and serpent-magic was the state's own profession: *Mūsā's sign was not a generic miracle but a precision strike at the regime's symbols, turning the empire's own emblem of power into a living demonstration that the God of the slaves eats the emblems.* The fuller account gives the verse its dramatic contour: *He threw it, and behold, it was a serpent swiftly moving* (20:20); and when the magicians' ropes and staffs seemed to crawl by their illusion, the command came to Mūsā: *"Do not fear; you are uppermost. And throw what is in your right hand; it will swallow what they have fabricated"* (20:68–69) — and it did: *"so the serpent swallowed up what they had feigned"* (7:117) — a miracle that begins by swallowing Pharaoh's entire intelligence service of the marvelous, after which no conjuror in the hall could pretend the phenomenon was competition rather than conquest. The white hand — *He drew out his hand, and behold, it was white to the onlookers* (7:108) — is the second contender for "greatest," and Ibn Kathīr notes its medical cruelty: to a court that worshipped a king, a hand of flawless light and a staff of hungering serpent were signs not in nature's grammar, so the sign could not be negotiated with as an omen; it could only be believed or called *siḥr*, magic — which is exactly what verse 21's two verbs record: *he denied and disobeyed*.

**The Sign and the Heart That Will Not Bow: Al-Rāzī's Question**

Al-Rāzī's interrogation at this verse is worth preserving because it frames theodicy for every age: if God's purpose was to convince Pharaoh, why give a sign that could be (and was) re-labeled sorcery? His answer, and the source's frame for the whole narrative (vv. 15–26 as *example*, not compulsion), runs: signs are given to *establish proof* (ḥujja) on the universe's terms, not to *coerce belief* on the individual's terms. A coerced faith would be a contradiction — the staff swallowed the sorcery, but no staff could swallow the will; and to have made belief unfalsifiable would have ended the entire human experiment the sūrah will spend verses 35–41 adjudicating: a deed *remembered* (v. 35) only counts where a denial was *possible*. This is why the Qur'an states the same rule for the Prophet of the dawn-recitation — *Had your Lord willed, whoever is on earth would all have believed; will you then coerce people to be believers?* (10:99). The greatest sign was thus already a mercy, not a trap: it was the last and best offer to a man whom nothing short of annihilation could have stopped from choosing, and the drowning that follows is the choosing, not the serpent's.

**The Sea as Greatest Sign — and the Body as Afterword**

A final reading in the source (and in Qatāda's circle): *al-āya al-kubrā* is the parting of the sea — the staff is a miracle before Pharaoh, the sea a miracle after him, and for the sūrah's present audience — told in verses 13–14 that the resurrection is one cry — the sea is the sign whose shape is resurrection: a road of dry ground for the slaves, a tomb for the self-deified, and the sea itself, which swallowed Pharaoh's claim, was *swallowed back*, as if the element that drowned the god-king had been ashamed of the service. This reading welds verse 20 to verses 21–25 — the denial, the muster, the proclamation, the seizure — and explains the sūrah's compression: it tells of the *greatest sign* precisely because that sign's second half is the drowning the whole Makkan audience used as proverb: *God did with Pharaoh* — the idiom of the Ḥijāz for the abrupt toppling of the insolent. The miracle, on this verse's reading, is double: the serpent before the court, and the pattern after — and the pattern, al-Nāziʿāt asserts, will repeat at the trumpet: the sea for those who deny resurrection is the same sea for those who deny the Caller, and the sūrah has been, since its first oath, about the one event the drowned man of verse 11 and the drowned tyrant of verse 25 share: restoration, whether they wish it or not.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:21

> **But he denied and disobeyed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs, One Freezes**

*Fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā* — "so he denied and disobeyed." The past tense, after the sūrah's present-tense oaths and Day-sequences, is the historian's hammer: it is done. The pairing of the two verbs, which the Qur'an repeats in this very form (its only occurrence of the exact pair elsewhere is 88:23 about the one who turns away, *fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā*, a signature of this verse-family), marks the two doors through which every defiance enters: *takdhīb* is the verdict of the mind and tongue — this is false — and *ʿiṣyān* is the motion of the limbs — I will act against it. One without the other is a different, lesser sin: the doubter who obeys anyway is still in the mosque; the sinner who affirms the truth anyway is still within the community; the combination — denying the true *and* disobeying knowingly — is Pharaoh's compound, and the compound is what hardens. Al-Rāzī adds the sequence's psychology: *kadhdhaba* came first here not because the sign failed but because denial was the precondition of the disobedience he had already chosen: the staff was judged before it moved. Which is why, in the sūrah's moral physics, signs do not create dispositions; they expose them.

**The Courtroom Record: What Pharaoh Actually Said About the Sign**

The source frames the verse against the sorcerers' surrender: *even after Pharaoh's magicians acknowledged the superiority of Moses, Pharaoh continued to deny, considering the staff-turned-serpent and the white hand mere sorcery* (cf. 7:111–126). The denial, therefore, was not made in the dark: he had the court's best experts, the judges of the marvelous, unanimously against him, and he reached for the word *siḥr* anyway — *This is surely skilled magic* (7:109); and the Qur'an preserves the court's own register: Pharaoh's counter-order to his minister — *"O Hāmān, build me a tower that I may reach the roads, the roads of the heavens, that I may look upon the god of Moses, though I think he is a liar"* (40:36–37) — and the machinery of the state set in motion: sorcerers assembled, the treasury consulted, the crowds marshalled (7:112; 26:36–39). This is the verse's warning to every later reader in a managerial position: *kadhdhaba* is the sin of the one who convenes the experts, hears the experts, and issues the opposite ruling — denial not as an epistemic state but as an administrative act, and *ʿaṣā* is what happens when a man's office and his soul vote the same way. The Book anatomizes the same chest elsewhere, in the verse commentators set beside this one: *"And if We make him taste mercy from Us after distress has touched him, he says: 'This is my due, and I do not think the Hour will ever come to pass; and if I am returned to my Lord, I will indeed have the best [portion] with Him'"* (41:50) — entitlement to the gift, presumption against the Hour, and presumption on the afterworld, all at once: denial of resurrection is the sequel of the man who calls mercy his property.

**Why the Drowning Tyrant Serves the Resurrection-Argument**

The sūrah's structure keeps its eye on the prize: Pharaoh's *kadhdhaba* is the pattern of Makkan *kadhdhabū* (v. 21's verb is the same root the sūrah used in vv. 10–12 for "are we to be restored?"), and the exegetes (IK on this passage) press the equation: the men who will not believe that the dead rise are performing, daily, the same two-step that ended in a sea: reject the sign, then rush to enforce the rejection (*thumma adbara yasʿā*, v. 22). Pharaoh is Makkah read large. But the equation cuts both ways — and here the verse teaches its hardest logic lesson: Pharaoh denied a *seen* miracle and was drowned in the seen world; the Quraysh deny an *unseen* Day — and the drowning of the seen (v. 25's "this world" half) is the sūrah's promissory note for the unseen one: the payment of the first was witnessed; the second will be. This is why the story sits exactly where it does — after the single-cry resurrection (13–14) and before the creation-argument (27–33): it is the historical proof that resurrection-judgment is real, evidenced by an act of judgment that Makkah could verify from its own trade-routes' ruins and proverbs.

**"He Denied and Disobeyed" — and the Verse That Never Says "He Could Not Help It"**

One last theological note, which the Muʿtazila and the traditionalists fought over and which this verse settles quietly on the side of justice: the sūrah attributes both verbs to Pharaoh as his own acts, and nowhere in vv. 20–25 — not once — does it plead compulsion, prior decree, or the divine seal as the ground of his punishment (the seizure arriving, as the sūrah itself narrates it, always as *response*: *"Pharaoh pursued them with troops, and the sea overwhelmed them"* — *fa-ghashiyahu mina al-yammi ma-ghashiyā* (20:78) — never as pre-empting cause). The whole narrative is thus an argument against the fatalism Makkah's poets loved — that a man drowns because water exists — and for the moral universe verses 35–41 will adjudicate on: *each soul is rewarded for what it endeavored* (v. 35's law, *mā saʿā* — the very word the sūrah used of Pharaoh's frantic "striving," *yasʿā*, v. 22: his endeavor was real, and it was remembered). The drowned denier of verse 21 is, in the sūrah's ledger, not a puppet who was made to dance but a man who heard the greatest sign and chose the smallest lie — and the Day, the sūrah tells its scofflaws, is the day when the choosing is finally what is real about them.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:22

> **Then turned his back, moving swiftly.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Motions of a Cornered Ego**

*Thumma adbara yasʿā* — "then he turned his back, hurrying to and fro." The verse stacks two verbs of motion and empties both of direction. *Adbara* (to turn one's back; also, in the lexicon, to become desperate — *adbarta al-ʿaqā*: "the situation became back-against-wall") supplies the posture: the exit from the room of the sign. *Yasʿā* (saʿy: brisk effort, a runner's labor, a worker's stride) supplies the pace — but pace without road: he is *moving fast and going nowhere*, the classic physics of panic. The commentators of the source record the double sense of the hurry: for al-Rāzī and others, *yasʿā* means he fled — a full-grown tyrant running from a shepherd's staff; for the others, it means he *went hard at planning* — mustering, marshalling, summoning the sorcerers, drafting the proclamation that follows in v. 23. Both readings agree in the sūrah's verdict: the same root *saʿy*, which will appear in v. 35 as the object of the Day's memory — *the Day when man remembers what he endeavored (mā saʿā)* — is here caught in mid-sprint, doing what all *saʿy* will be revealed to have been: running. The verse makes its point by vocabulary alone: Pharaoh's energy is genuine; his direction is borrowed from panic; and the Day's job is to read the difference.

**Adbara: The Back Turned, and the Door It Closes**

In the Qur'an's idiom, turning the back (*iʿrāḍ, ʿajb, idbār*) is the formal opposite of *taqwā*'s posture of attentiveness, and it is always self-incriminating: *when they turn away, We leave them to their breasts* (cf. 6:43 in sense; 7:146: *I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant*). The Sufi exegetes read verse 22 against the verse of the Fire itself: Hell in Sūrat al-Muddathir is described as *saqar — lā tubqi wa-lā adhara — layāḥu ʿalā al-bashar* — "she neither leaves nor releases" (74:28–29) — and here the man whom the sign came to *leaves and releases himself*, and the sūrah's irony is exact: whoever turns his back on the sign turns his front to the Fire, and the Fire is behind the front. Al-Qurtubi adds the historical frame that makes *adbara* concrete: after the sign, Pharaoh's next recorded moves are the escalation — the interrogation of the magicians, the threat of crucifixion on palm-trunks (20:71), the pursuit across the sea (26:52–61). The back turned is not a moment but a policy; and this is what the Makkan hearers were being shown about their own laughter at dawn: their *idbār* was already legislating its own ending.

**Yasʿā: The Labor of Building What Will Be Demolished**

The participle *yasʿā* — he *is* sprinting, the imperfect of continuing haste — was picked by the classical authors for its moral comedy: empires that run to preserve themselves only accelerate what they fear. The Qur'an states the law elsewhere for Pharaoh in a single sentence: *So he belittled his people and they obeyed him* (43:54) — haste manufactures obedience, and obedience manufactures the army, and the army marches to the sea that is already closing (cf. 10:90 — *until, when drowning overtook him, he said: I believe*). The Makkan reader is meant to finish the arithmetic on his own account: what is your *saʿy* building — the storehouse you will leave, or the soul you will take? The hadith of 79:35's own vocabulary answers from the Prophet's ﷺ lips in the sound tradition of al-Tirmidhī (no. 2417): *the feet of the son of Adam will not stir on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about five things: his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he wore it out, his wealth — how he earned it and how he spent it, and what he knew and what he did with it.* Every one of those questions is a question about *saʿy*: about the direction of one's hurry. Verse 22 is the verse for people in a hurry: it shows the greatest sprinter of the age, all momentum, zero mileage, and it shows him, verse for verse, being *caught by his own motion* — the sea arriving exactly where haste was taking him.

**Why the Sūrah Tells You He Hurried**

The narrative placement carries the verse's last and subtlest function. Al-Nāziʿāt has built a sūrah of tempo: the cry of one (v. 13), the blink nearer than that, the race to the fore (v. 4), the Day "near, nearer" — and here, at the center of the sūrah, the man who *outspeeds* every mercy: he turns and hurries, and the sūrah lets the hurry speak for him; no verdict needed at v. 22, because v. 25 already holds the end of the road. This is the technique the tradition of the *maqāṣid* most admired in Makkan narrative: the sūrah does not moralize its characters, it *tempo-marks* them — the speed of their flight is the evidence of their fear, and the fear is the proof they already believed. Pharaoh is the sūrah's answer to verse 12's "ruinous return": the ruinous return does not begin at the trumpet; it begins the moment a man with the truth in front of him turns his back and starts running, because he has just begun the race whose finish line the trumpet guards. The reader's task, the verse whispers, is not to stop running — everyone runs; it is to notice, now, at what, and toward which turning, and to remember that the one who *adbara* here will one day, in another verse of this sūrah, find the wide expanse ahead of him whether he hurries or not.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:23

> **Then he gathered and proclaimed,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of the Propaganda Machine**

*Thumma ḥashara fa-nādā* — "then he gathered, and called out." The verbs are administrative, not military: *ḥashara* is the mustering of a crowd or an army for a purpose (the same root gives *al-ḥashr*, the Resurrection-gathering the sūrah itself will use in v. 14 and in the Day's title *yawma al-ḥashr* elsewhere); *nādā* is the public cry, the proclamation made where the farthest man can hear. Ibn Kathīr records the classical identification of the verse: Pharaoh assembled his people and cried out among them his claim (the source: "gathered all the people to declare his sovereignty"); others link it to the muster for the pursuit — *then Pharaoh sent marshals to the cities* (26:53) — in which case the gathering is the army, and the proclamation its mobilization order. Either way, the sūrah gives us the machinery in two strokes: assembly, announcement. No argument is described; none was available. What follows (v. 24) is the content the machinery exists to deliver — and that, the sequence implies, is the whole of what tyranny has to say when the miracle has been swallowed: not a counter-proof but a crowd, not a rebuttal but a shout.

**The Gathering That Mimics the Gathering**

Set inside this sūrah, verse 23 does something quietly devastating: it pits two *ḥashrs* against each other. Pharaoh gathers Makkah's model of an assembly — the muster of the king's court, the sorcerers at their appointed hour (7:112: "we will certainly win," they say), the cities roused for pursuit — and the sūrah has already sworn a greater assembly: "and then, behold, they will be upon a wide expanse" (v. 14), the Day "for which the people will stand before the Lord of the worlds" (83:6), when every *ḥāshir* and every proclaimed claimant of lordship will be in the same open place, with no marshals, no heralds, and no crowd to hide behind. The structure is the sūrah's answer to every state spectacle of the age, ours included: power manufactures crowds to make its claims feel majority-sized, and the Book keeps one crowd in reserve in front of which all manufactured ones evaporate. Pharaoh's *nida'* — his cry — will last an afternoon in court-records and forever in this sūrah; the Caller of the Day, the Qur'an says elsewhere, calls with a voice no summons has rivaled: "the Day when the Caller calls from a near place" (50:42).

**Nādā: The Proclamation Without a Predicate of Evidence**

Arabic rhetoric marks the verse's odd ellipsis: the sūrah says "he gathered and proclaimed" — and only in the next verse supplies what was proclaimed. That delay is pedagogy: the proclamation is left standing naked, pure public address, so that the hearer first *sees the form* of the thing — a man filling a plaza with his voice — and only then hears its content, and the deferral makes the content monstrous at the moment it lands: "I am your lord most high" (v. 24). The commentators notice the same technique in the Qur'an's other Pharaoh-scenes, where the claim is always given in quotation, never endorsed in narration — God reports the sermon without signing it. And there is a legal note the jurists extract from *ḥashara fa-nādā*: the proclamation of falsehood that is *gathered for* — organized, funded, staged — is a graver act, in the Book's moral ledger, than the falsehood uttered in private, because it recruits the crowd as accomplice, which is exactly the sin of the *ḥāshir* as well as the *munādī*: 26:54 — "and We caused the others to inherit [all]," and the crowds of Egypt marched because the herald's cry made the lie sound like policy.

**The Day Every Assembly Is Un-gathered**

The verse's place in the story, three verses before the seizure, supplies its final lesson: the muster was for the drowning. Pharaoh gathers (v. 23) to pursue the slaves of God (26:52–53), and the sea is the one gathering that no dispersal undoes — but for the pursued it is deliverance and for the pursuer a tomb, so that the sūrah can say to Makkah: your assemblies against the message are already enrolled in this ledger, and their end is already written. The tradition's own summation of such musters is the Qur'an's verdict on the Red Sea day: "then he gathered and cried out, and said: I am your lord most high" — the sūrah keeps the order exact (23 then 24) because the order is the diagnosis: first the crowd, then the claim; first the platform, then the godship. Every age's Pharaoh follows the sequence, and every age's sea follows the sequence. The Makkan dawn-hearer, walking to the sanctuary past the merchants' councils and the poets' assemblies, could not mistake which side of the verse his city stood on — and that was the point of his Prophet's reciting it there.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:24

> **and said, "I am your lord most high."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence the Sūrah Exists to Unmake**

*Fa-qāla anā rabbukum al-aʿlā* — "so he said: I am your lord, the most high." The sūrah's climax of blasphemy is built from the plainest words in Arabic: *rabb* — lord, owner, sustainer, rearer — and its superlative *aʿlā*. Pharaoh does not claim to be a god among gods, which would at least stay inside his nation's polytheistic grammar; he claims the whole of lordship, *your* lord, and he claims it in the superlative — the highest rung of the only ladder he has spent the narrative climbing (his own minister, in 28:38, had ordered a tower "that I may look upon the god of Moses"; the tower's construction and the throne's theology are the same project). The Qur'an elsewhere sets the two halves of his claim side by side: *O eminent ones, I know no god for you other than myself* (28:38) — the negative; and here, *I am your lord most high* — the positive; and the source records the transmitted order (R, Ṭ, Ṭs): first 28:38, then this verse — denial of every other god consummated in the deification of the self. Pharaoh's sermon is the whole of what the sūrah, since verse 1, is sworn against; every angel who wrests a soul is a standing refutation of the sentence, because none of them serves him — they serve the *Rabb* he stole the title of.

**The Political Theology of "Most High"**

The superlative *al-aʿlā* is not an embellishment; it is the claimant's answer to heaven. The exegetes (IK cites the reports; al-Suddī and Qatāda expand) note the historical texture: the kings of Egypt were, in the idiom of their monuments, the living horizon of divinity, and Pharaoh's *al-aʿlā* stakes the divine title on the one office that cannot share it — he who is "most high" permits no rival, no intercession, no appeal, which is precisely why the sūrah places this cry at the exact center of a chapter whose entire subject is the Day when all appeal runs to God alone. Ibn Taymiyyah's analysis of *rubūbiyya* — lordship as the exclusive ground of worship — is aimed at this structure of claim: to make a man the *rabb* is to make obedience to him unconditional, and the Book's war against that totalization of any human is the very line the sūrah's Pharaoh narrative serves: it is the Makkan notables' claim ("our assemblies, our gods, our poets") read at its logical end. That is why the sūrah does not bother to argue the sentence: *kubrā* against *kubrā* — the Greatest Sign (v. 20) has already argued it; the verse is kept in the text as a defendant's own deposition.

**The Ummah's Counter-Sentence, Said Five Times a Day**

Against Pharaoh's words, the tradition sets the Prophet's ﷺ cure, and the echo is audible to every worshipper: in bowing and prostration the Muslim says *subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā* — "Glory to my Lord, the Most High" — the tasbīḥ the Messenger of God ﷺ made the prayer's own law for his companions' hands: "As for the bowing, magnify the Lord in it; as for the prostration, strive mightily in supplication, for it is appointed to be answered" (Muslim 479). Every rakʿah of every day, the ummah puts the same superlative where Pharaoh put his — *al-aʿlā* — but under *subḥāna*, stripped of ownership, confessed as God's alone; and in the *takbīr* that opens each prayer — *Allāhu akbar* — it kills the claim again by making the greatest-name a verb of God, not a noun of the king. The commentators of this very sūrah note the counter-history the sentence had already entered: the Prophet ﷺ at the hour of his own death, in the sound reports, kept returning to *al-rafīq al-aʿlā* — "the companionship of the Most High [in heaven]" — choosing, against every Pharaoh's logic that the high one owns the low ones, to *leave* the throne for the Friend. Pharaoh said *anā* — "I"; the believer's tongue answers, five times a day: *huwa*.

**How the Claim Dies: the Grammar of the Sea**

The sūrah never describes the sentence being refuted by argument; it describes it being refuted by drowning (v. 25) — and that choice is the verse's deepest teaching about such claims. *Al-aʿlā* needs elevation to survive; it lives on distance, on the crowd below and the tower above, on the plaza that makes a man look large. The sea inverts the geometry absolutely: the "most high" dies face-down in the lowest element, his body — as the Book has it in Yūnus — a corpse carried up on a wave "to be a sign for those after him" (10:92), and the tradition's added detail is the sūrah's epilogue to verse 24: when Pharaoh, at the last moment, cried "I believe" (10:90), the answer came: now? — you were defiant before, one of the corrupters. What verse 24 proves, on the sūrah's showing, is not that such claims are false — the serpent and the sea proved that — but that they are *late*: every Pharaoh says *anā rabbukum al-aʿlā* at the precise hour when his office is already expiring, and the crowd that gathers to hear it is already the crowd of a funeral. The dawn-hearer of the Juḥāt prayer, reciting this in the light before sunrise, was standing inside the verse's counter-proof: the sun, which Egypt's pharaohs themselves once rode, rose every morning over Makkah without consulting anyone's title, and the Most High, the sūrah had sworn by five classes of His angels, needed no one — least of all a drowning man — to be who He is.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:25

> **So God seized him with a punishment exemplary in the Hereafter and in this world.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of the Final Take**

*Fa-ʿaqabahu Allāhu al-nakāla al-ākhirata wa-al-ūlā* — "so God seized him with the exemplary punishment, the later and the former." The verb *ʿaqaba*, in its transitive use here, carries the double sense the lexicographers preserve: to follow after (the *ʿuqba* is what comes after a thing), and to exact due recompense; and the sūrah, by pairing it with *al-nakāl* — punishment as deterrent example, from n-k-l, whose field is the stake left visible so travelers remember — makes the seizure do two jobs at once: it pays Pharaoh his due, and it files his case for every later jury. The source's etymological note belongs here: *nakāl* also shades into weakness and inability — the seizure rendered Pharaoh impotent against the shepherd he had called liar — and the impotence is half the exemplary force: the state's terror is unmade not by a bigger terror but by the exposure of how powerless its god was at the hour of its proclamation.

**The Two Halves of the Punishment — the Disputed Order**

The classical commentators dispute the pairing *al-ākhirata wa-al-ūlā*, and the dispute is instructive. Most read it straightforwardly: God punished him in the Hereafter and in this world — the drowning and the Fire (IK, Q, Ṭ) — the present-tense sea being the Makkan audience's only visible half, and the unseen half the sūrah's sworn guarantee. A second reading (Q, Ṭ, Ṭs, from Ibn ʿAbbās's circle) takes the two nouns as *the later saying and the former saying*: the first blasphemy, "I know no god for you other than myself" (28:38), and the second, "I am your lord most high" (v. 24) — punishment heaped for both speeches; and a third variant in the same school: the first for denying the message's earliest summons, the last for deifying himself at its height. Al-Rāzī weighs the order of the words — why *ākhirata* is mentioned before *ūlā* — and answers with the sūrah's own logic of proof: the Hereafter is the *true* half of the sentence (it is where the punishment finally becomes *nakāl*, deterrent forever), and the world's drowning is its rehearsal and its receipt. The point all the readings share: no Pharaoh escapes into "this was only the sea"; the sea is the down-payment, and the down-payment is the proof.

**The Drowned Man as Archive**

The verse closes the narrative by turning the event into evidence. The seizure is *nakāl* in the technical sense the jurists use: a punishment whose office is deterrence of the living, not merely the ending of the guilty — which is why the Qur'an, in Yūnus, adds the detail no other tyrant is granted: *today We will preserve your body, so that you may be a sign for those who come after you* (10:92). The body, in that verse's frame, becomes an archive: the sea gave up what it drowned, and the Book kept what the sea gave up, so that every generation since has had to explain the pharaohs rather than pray to them. Al-Ṭabarī's historical register supports the verse's world-half in plain terms — the drowning of the pursuer (26:61: "so We overtook him from where he did not perceive"), the inheritance of his houses by the slaves he had pressed into mortar (26:57–59; 44:25–28: "how many gardens and springs, and treasures and a noble station, We left him to inherit — and so the heaven and the earth did not weep for him," a line this verse's tradition loves, for the Pharaoh who claimed to be the sky's owner was not mourned by either element when he left it). The exemplary, in the Qur'an's arithmetic, includes the silence of the cosmos at his going.

**The Day ʿĀshūrā: the Seizure Remembered in the Prophet's Own Practice**

The verse's world-half is dated, in the Sunnah, to the tenth of Muḥarram. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 2004) and Muslim (no. 1130), Ibn ʿAbbās reports that when the Prophet ﷺ came to Madinah and found the Jews fasting ʿĀshūrā and answering his question: "This is the day on which God saved Moses and his people and drowned Pharaoh and his people, so Moses fasted it in gratitude — and we fast it," he said: *fa-anā aḥaqqu bi-Mūsā minkum* — "I have more right to Moses than you" — and fasted it, and ordered the fasting. Three lessons the commentators draw for this verse: the drowning was historically remembered by others before the Prophet confirmed it — the *nakāl* is public record, not sectarian legend; gratitude for God's seizure of the oppressor is a *legitimate fast*, meaning the fall of tyrants belongs, in Islam, to worship and not to triumph; and the Ummah's kinship is with the pursued Moses, not the proclaimed Pharaoh — the verse's verdict on every reader's team-assignment. A nation that re-enacts its liberation with an annual hunger is a nation that has read 79:25 as the sūrah intends: the sea is still running, and so is the Fire, and both are one act of the same *ʿuqba*.

**The Sufi's Question and the Seizer's Answer**

The source preserves, at this verse, the celebrated report of ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla al-Simnānī (d. 787/1336): seeing the soul of al-Ḥallāj — the martyr-saint who cried *anā al-ḥaqq*, "I am the Real" — in the highest garden, he asked how this could stand beside Pharaoh's hell, since Pharaoh too had uttered a first-person claim to divinity: *anā rabbukum al-aʿlā*. The reply in the story: Pharaoh saw only himself and forgot God; al-Ḥallāj saw only God and forgot himself. The tale is offered by its tellers as discernment, not exoneration — orthodoxy never admitted *anā al-ḥaqq* as speech a Muslim could stand — but its logic serves this verse perfectly: the punishing gaze of God reads not the words of a claim but the self that speaks it, and the same sentence from two hearts is two different sentences. That, too, is *nakāl*: the exemplary seizure establishes that before the Real, the only thing separating a throne from a pyre is not the rhetoric of the claimant but the disappearance or the inflation of his own *anā* — his "I." The sūrah has now finished history; from the next verse it turns, with the phrase "in that is a lesson," from Pharaoh's sea to the reader's chest.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:26

> **Truly in that is a lesson for whosoever fears.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Names the Genre**

*Inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan liman yakhshā* — "in that, indeed, is a lesson for whoever fears." Twelve words, and they perform the hinge on which the whole Makkan Qur'an turns: the *qīṣaṣ* (narratives) of the Book are not here as story but as *ʿibra*, a word whose root ʿ-b-r means to cross over — the lesson is the crossing from what happened to what it means, from the drowning of one man to the drowning of a claim that drowns in every century. The sūrah has told Pharaoh in five movements (call, sign, denial, muster, seizure); this verse does not moralize the tale but licenses the reader's own inference, and licenses it with the most engineered form Arabic argument possesses: *inna* for certainty, the *lām* of emphasis inside *la-ʿibratan*, the demonstrative *dhālika* pointing back at the whole episode as a single object of inspection. Al-Rāzī, who loved this construction, calls it the verse that makes the narrative a *burhān* — a proof — rather than a *khurāfa* — a campfire tale: proofs, unlike tales, can be re-run by anyone on the raw material the text preserves.

**Who the Lesson Is For: The Fear as Faculty of Insight**

The qualifier *liman yakhshā* — "for whoever fears" — is the sūrah's recurring signature (v. 19's goal, v. 40's name for the saved), and the tradition reads it here as epistemology, not etiquette: *khashya* is the sense organ by which history becomes legible. Al-Rāzī (in the source's own summary at 40–41) supplies the equation that unlocks the verse: rebellion *ṭughyān* is ignorance hardened; fear is knowledge humbled — and the proof-text he cites is the Book's definition of the fear itself: *"Only those fear God, among His servants, who have knowledge"* (35:28). The same logic makes ʿibrā a privilege of the soft-hearted in the Qur'an's wider idiom: *"in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or gives ear while he is a witness"* (50:37) — heart, ear, presence: the three instruments verse 26 presupposes. Pharaoh himself had a heart, an ear, and a witness-chair in the front row — he *saw* the sign (v. 20) and got nothing from it, which is why the verse's condition is not optional: the same sea that is a *ʿibra* to the fearing is only, to the fearless, an embarrassing weather event in a dead empire's file.

**The Lesson Generalizes — and That Is Its Function**

Because the tale is archived as *ʿibra* and not as miracle, its field of application extends by the sūrah's own design: whoever "fears standing before his Lord" (v. 40) recognizes himself in it at either end — as the saved slave whose flight is pursued, or as the pursued man who was offered a question ("do you wish to be purified?", v. 18) and answered it with a proclamation. The Makkan audience heard the extension in the verse's very ambiguity: *dhālika*, "in that," points at the drowning and, equally, at the offer refused — Makkah was, dawn by dawn, the valley between Ṭuwā and the sea, still inside verse 18's question and not yet at verse 25. This is why the verse sits where it does — immediately before the creation-argument (vv. 27–33): having made history prove the judgment, the sūrah will make cosmology prove the power, and "whoever fears" is the reader competent to follow either argument. The hadith-literature supplies the verse's practical maxim in the Prophet's ﷺ line preserved by Muslim (no. 2586): *"None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself"* — for the *ʿibra* to work, the reader must first accept that the lesson's addressee is every man, his brother included, tyrant included; the fear that reads 26 is the same fear that, in the tale's first half, was asked to open for Pharaoh a road out of it.

**A Single Sentence Against Fatalism**

The final touch is the verse's quiet polemic against the fatalism the sūrah's audience, like every audience, practices: "if we had been destined for it, we would have done it" (cf. 43:20 — *"if the Most Merciful had willed, we would not have worshipped them"* — the exact sentence the Qur'an quotes in order to dismantle, 43:21: *"do they have knowledge of that? or do they give proof manifest?"*). Verse 26 asserts the opposite grammar of responsibility: there *is* a lesson — a knowable, transferable, usable lesson — and whoever fears *takes* it; the verb *yakhshā* is present-tense, active, addressed to a faculty the denier still owns. Ibn Kathīr's gloss on the verse-family closes the circle with the sūrah's first oath: just as souls are wrested out of bodies (v. 1) by agents obeying a governance (v. 5), the lessons are laid, in plain sentences, inside events, for agents capable of obedience; the whole cosmos is a *nāziʿāt* pulling the heedless toward their good by whatever tools — a miracle, a drowning, a recited dawn-sūrah — and the fearing man is the one who lets himself be pulled into the meaning. The verse's last word is thus the sūrah's whole aim, in miniature: not information about Egypt, but *khashya* in Makkah — and, four centuries and fourteen centuries later, in whoever is reading: the lesson is still crossing over; the question is which side of the water he is standing on.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:27

> **Are you more difficult to create or the sky that He has built?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Reverses the Objection**

*A-antum aṣʿabu khalqan am al-samāʾu banāhā* — "are you, then, harder to create, or the sky that He built?" The sūrah has just finished with history (vv. 15–26, sealed "in that is a lesson for whoever fears"), and verse 27 opens the cosmological argument with the Book's most surgical *a fortiori*: the deniers of verse 10 had reasoned that re-creating them is too hard for God — the sūrah concedes their entire scale of difficulty and then reverses its direction: on the scale of difficulty, the heavens stand above you; you have already granted that the heavens were built; the builder of the harder thing does not stumble on the softer. This is not borrowed Greek logic but the Qur'an's own native form, the *istishhād bi-l-aʿẓam ʿalā al-aṣghar*, the appeal to the greater as proof by the lesser, and the source cites its twin: *"Surely the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind, but most of mankind know not"* (40:57) — the verse that states, as doctrine, what this verse performs as rhetoric.

**Aṣʿab: The Word That Measures Effort Against the Almighty**

The comparative *aṣʿab* (from ṣ-ʿ-b, hardship, unattainable difficulty) is the pivot, and the commentators mark its audacity: the sūrah invites the objector into a calculation of *effort* — which is the very frame he brought ("who will give life to bones while they are decayed?", 36:78) — and lets the calculation run to its own collapse. For if God has an "easier" and a "harder," the difference is in the things, not in the power; and the things in question — a universe of sky and a heap of bones — make the objector's arithmetic absurd on its face, since he concedes the first as no trouble at all. Al-Zamakhsharī catches the irony in his *Kashshāf*: the verse does not argue "nothing is hard for God" in the abstract; it makes a man's own admitted certainties — the sky exists, and it was made — convict his own doubt. The indefinite *khalqan* ("in creation") universalizes: not just your origin but your re-making, not just flesh but resurrection — every *khalq* of yours is bracketed by the one *banāʾ* (building) you never questioned. The Muʿtazilī exegetes of the source (Z, R) press the verse as proof of the *qudra before the act* — the power to make what is harder standing, undeniable, behind the power to re-make what is easier — while al-Rāzī, for the Ashʿarī side, notes the deeper point neither school can skip: the comparison itself is condescension, for to the Will that says *kun*, "be," sizes do not exist; the verse is measured to *us*, not to *Him*.

**Banāhā: the Sky as Built Thing, Not Eternal Thing**

The verb *banā* — to build, to construct — is chosen against the Arabs' own cosmological habits: the sky was, in their poetry, the "raised roof" and the "canopy," and in Hellenic philosophy that reached the Islamic schools later, the heavens were the ungenerated, the necessary, the divine. The Qur'an's single transitive verb — *He built it* — does the theology in one stroke: what is built is, by the meaning of the word, not eternal, not divine, not self-explanatory; it has a builder, a beginning, a plan (v. 28 will add: raised canopy and proportioned; v. 29: lit and darkened; v. 30: the earth spread after). Ibn Taymiyyah, arguing through his *Darʾ taʿāruḍ al-ʿaql wa-l-naql* against the necessitation of the heavens, leaned exactly on this idiom: a *bunyān* — a structure — implies, in the very language of the Qur'an, a Will that chose it, and the fitra, he held, reads the sentence the same way it reads the sky: something that had to be *done*, standing there. To a modern reader who has watched construction sites — a sky-scraper going up story by story against a city that said it couldn't — the verb carries a second, lived resonance: buildings do not assemble themselves, and no one standing in the finished tower asks whether the architect "had it in Him" to finish the lobby.

**The Sky Every Dawn, the Argument Once**

The Makkan who heard this verse had no domes of stone over their city; their ceiling was the sky itself — by day the canopied blaze (cf. 2:22: *"He made for you the earth a bed and the sky a canopy"*, and 21:32: *"We made the sky a protected ceiling"*), by night the lamped vault the poets swore by. The sūrah's argument, then, is scheduled to the actual visual field of its audience, re-recruited every sunrise and every dusk, which is why it reads as an invitation rather than a lecture: the harder thing was built, every evening, under your eyes; and the easier thing — you, again — will be re-built at the cry. The source's own cross-reference completes the pair at the end of the argument-cluster: *"Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? Yes — He is the Creator, the All-Knowing. His command, when He wills a thing, is only to say to it, 'Be' — and it is"* (36:81–82). Between verse 27's question and 36:82's answer lies the whole sūrah's psychology of resurrection: the objector was handed his own measuring-stick, measured himself against the sky, found himself lighter than he feared — and learned that the God of the heavy heaven was never going to strain at the light dust of him.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:28

> **He raised high its canopy and fashioned it,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of Architecture: Height and Fitting**

*Rafaʿa samkahā fa-sawwāhā* — "He raised its height, and made it even, proportioned it." The first noun, *samk*, is the vertical: the height of a ceiling, the elevation of a wall — and *rafaʿa* completes it: the lifting of the sky's vault above the earth, the act the Book registers everywhere as a sign set over against the spreading of the ground beneath it (71:15–16: *"Have you not seen how He created seven heavens in layers, and made the moon therein a light, and made the sun a lamp"*; and 2:22's canopy). The second verb, *sawwā* — to make level, even, whole — is the perfecting act, and the source preserves its exact force: *fashioned it* means "whole, with no fissures, cracks, or imperfections." The sequence — raised, then proportioned — mirrors every honest construction: structure before finish; and in the divine case, the finish is flawless *as* structure, a building whose raising and whose evenness are one act. The *fāʾ* of *fa-sawwāhā* lets the two stand as cause-and-effect and as time's own sequence at once: height is *for* proportion; the sky is lifted *in order to be* made even, a ceiling whose purpose is the room it encloses.

**"No Inconsistency in the Creation": the Verse's Own Cross-Proof**

This verse's perfective *sawwāhā* detonates, in the sūrah's audience, a passage of al-Mulk the commentators pair with it, quoted in the source at the section's head (67:3–4): *"It is He who created seven heavens in layers; you do not see, in the creation of the Most Merciful, any inconsistency (tafāwut). Return your vision: do you see any fissure (fuṭūr)? Then return your vision again and again — your vision will come back to you humbled, exhausted."* The fit is exact: *sawwāhā* (even) is the semantic twin of *lā tafāwut* (no mismatch), and *fuṭūr* (cracks) the very defect the source says the verse negates. What is striking, and what the classical exegetes press, is that the verse makes the perfection of the sky an *observational claim*: the reader is told to inspect — look, look again, look until your eye gives out — and the sky's defect-free seamlessness is offered as evidence, not dogma, that the Builder's building is finished work; resurrection, by the same architecture of argument (v. 27), is the second finished act of the same builder. Al-Rāzī, in his enormous treatment of this locus, adds the epistemic humility the verse commands for the whole sky-cluster: whoever claims to have catalogued the defects of the heavens has simply stopped counting stars; the text invites the second return of the gaze precisely because the first return — the astronomer's, the farmer's, the poet's — already finds nothing to hand the skeptic.

**Raised *for* You: the Canopy as Mercy, Not Decoration**

The sūrah's cosmology never presents the sky as scenery; *rafaʿa* without a *bi-* of purpose would be a mere physical fact, and the Book always supplies the purpose: *"and We made the night a covering, and the day a livelihood"* (78:10–11); *"and the heaven We built with might, and We are indeed expanding it"* (51:47 — a verse the classical commentators read, in some registers, exactly as this one's *bi-idn*: raised, and *holding* — a roof with a keeper). The canopy motif carries, in Makkan ears, the safety of the walled town and the vulnerability of the tent: the sky is the tent pitched over the whole race of men, raised to the height a tent must be to hold a people, *fastened* — and the fastening is the *sawwā*: every peg even, every seam closed. This is why the next verses' night and day, water and pasture, and mountains will all read as *furnishings of the tent*: the sūrah is describing a dwelling, and the argument to the denier is domestic — you do not accuse the one who built your house, and roofed it, and weatherproofed it, of being unable to mend the door you broke. The modern reader, under his own tin roof of satellite orbits, forecast models, and the thin ozone the chemists found they could dent, hears the verse's *sawwāhā* as a warning as much as a proof: canopies are for living under, and the living under them, the sūrah will say by v. 33, is for the One who raised them.

**Ibn Kathīr's Chain: from the Ceiling Down to the Chest**

Ibn Kathīr glosses this verse inside the sūrah's larger movement in a way worth preserving: the raising and the proportioning of the sky are the outer wall of the argument whose inner chamber is v. 27's question — are you harder? — and he draws the line of descent from ceiling to conscience: the sky was *made* even so that the eyes that lift to it might *be made* humbled, the way 67:4 says the gaze returns "humbled, exhausted (ḥasīr)." *Ḥasīr* — worn out, defeated, unable to find the seam — is the adjective the commentators note with pleasure here: the verse's promised effect of astronomy is not pride in discovery but the discovery of *no exit from the Builder*: every deeper look, every longer telescope, returns the eye still *sawwā*. And the theological footnote the mufassirūn attach: *sawwā* excludes the ancient claim that the heavens are themselves divine or ensouled — an even, built, liftable thing is by definition not a god, and the sūrah, fresh from Pharaoh's *al-aʿlā* claim (v. 24), lets its reader finish the sentence: the only "Most High" is the one who raised *that* canopy and proportioned *that* seam, and the sky itself, for all its height, is the floor of the argument that stands beneath its Maker.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:29

> **darkened its night, and brought forth its day.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Acts of the Second Sentence: Covering and Uncovering**

*Wa-aghṭasha laylahā wa-akhraja ḍuḥāhā* — "and He drew its night over it, and brought forth its brightness." The verbs are chosen for their textures. *Aghṭasha* — from ghaṭasha, to cover, cloak, overwhelm: the same root that will name the Day of Judgment itself in the Qur'an's other Makkan title, *al-Ghāshiya*, "the Overwhelming" (88:1), and that describes the sea's final favor to Pharaoh: *"the covering of the sea covered him"* (20:97). Darkness in this verse is not an absence but an act — a cloth thrown, a thing *done to* the sky by a Doer; and the verb's own object is *its* night, the sky's covering drawn down over the world like a lid. Against it, *akhraja* — brought forth, led out, as an army is marched out of a gate, as the source notes for verse 31's parallel "He brought forth *its* water": the morning light is not generated by the sky but *produced out of it*, extracted, brought up — the same verb the oaths used for the angels drawing souls from bodies (vv. 1–2). The verse thus welds the sky and the soul into one grammar of extraction and covering: night covers, day is drawn out; the body is covered in the grave, the soul is drawn out at the cry — and between the two pairs the sūrah lets its audience feel the difference that matters: the sky's lights return by appointment, and the soul's does too, but only the second appointment has a judgment behind it.

**Ḍuḥā: the Name of the Hour That Opens the Day**

*Ḍuḥāhā* — "its ḍuḥā" — and the source apparatus insists on the richness: the word is not generic "day" but the specific light of the mid-morning, the brightness when the sun has fairly cleared the horizon and the world is at work; the sūrah of Ḍuḥā swears by it as a divine mercy-title — *"By the forenoon brightness, and by the night when it is still"* (93:1–2) — and the commentators there note the pairing this verse repeats: light-out-of-dark as *given*, stillness-as-*given*, the daybreak and the quiet as two favors laid side by side. The choice of *ḍuḥā* here, at the peak of a cosmological argument about resurrection, is the sūrah's own sunrise-proof: every morning is the *ikhraj* (bringing-forth) that the deniers said was impossible for bones — the day is extracted from the night's closed fist, on schedule, for six billion people, daily, without consultation. When 79:46 shrinks the whole worldly life, at the end, to "an evening or its *ḍuḥā*," this word will be doing the final arithmetic: the same hour that proves power proves brevity, and the sūrah plants both in the middle of its argument so the reader cannot weigh one without the other.

**Night for Rest, Day for Livelihood — the Verse's Human Half**

The Makkan exegetes — the source cites the note that "its *ḍuḥā*" translates "its light" or "its morning brightness" as in 93:1, or "the morning" as in v. 46 — keep the verse's second half in view: night and day are, in the Book's constant idiom, *benefactions*, not phenomena: *"and of His mercy He made for you the night and the day therein you rest and therein you seek of His bounty"* (28:73); *"It is He who made the night for you to withdraw into and the day to see by"* (10:67). The rhythm — covering, uncovering — is the rhythm of the sūrah's own audience: the caravan halts at dark, moves at first light; the herds settle, the wells are drawn; and the sky's double act is addressed to *you* the way verse 33 will say plainly: the whole cosmology exists "as sustenance for you and for your herds." This is why the passage cannot be read, in the sūrah's frame, as deism — a builder who built and left: the *aghṭasha / akhraja* verbs are imperfective in their effect, the two mercies *still* performed, hour by hour, by the One whose present-tense care for the sky is the standing proof of His present-tense claim on the soul. The modern echo is unavoidable and the tradition would sanction it: a civilization that has legislated against the *ghaṭā'sa* — lights that banish the covering night, schedules that strangle the morning — has not merely hurt its sleep; it has muted the sūrah's two daily preachers, and the verse's argument from the sky runs, for such a people, on a volume they must learn to turn back up.

**One Sentence Against Two Ancient Errors**

Finally, the verse's placement inside the argument kills, in one line, both of the theological errors the Qur'an's cosmology exists to correct. Against the deified sky, the verb of covering says: the sky *has* night and day done to it, and what has things done to it is not a god but a building with lights switched on and off; against the deified nature — the sky as self-running necessity — the verbs *aghṭasha* and *akhraja* supply a will and a schedule: the night is *imposed* and the day *brought out*, and the whole sequence, the sūrah's next verses will say, runs toward the one *ikhraj* that will not set again: *"He brings the living out of the dead and brings the dead out of the living"* (6:95) — which is the resurrection argument of verse 27, restated as astronomy, with the reader's own buried bones assigned their turn in the *akhraja* that already lights the east every morning of the world.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:30

> **And after that He spread out the earth.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Daḥāhā: the Ostrich's Bed and the Table of the World**

*Wa-al-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā* — "and the earth, after that, He spread it out." The verb *daḥa* supplies one of the Arabic lexicon's loveliest roots: the lexicographers note that the *ḍaḥiyah* is the ostrich's nest in the sand — the hollow the great bird scrapes, levels, and spreads into a bed that will hold its eggs against the desert's extremes; and from the same root the Arabs called the egg itself, and the earth's surface, *maḥṣūrah* of the *dāḥiy*. To *daḥa* is thus not merely to flatten but to prepare: spreading out a thing *for occupancy* — a floor laid, a table spread (the *ḍiyāfa*, hospitality, shares the root in the idiom of many dialects), a bed made. The Makkan hearer, sleeping on precisely such spread ground, with precisely such nests in the dunes around the city, received the verse as domestic news: the cosmology of vv. 27–29 (sky raised, night drawn, day brought out) has now reached the furnishing of the room, and the room is the world. This is why the sūrah, having said "are you harder to create, or the sky?" (v. 27), can now say: *and the earth* — the two halves of the created dwelling, the canopy and the carpet, the ceiling and the bed.

**"After That": the Order of the Verses and the Order of Creation**

The adverbial phrase *baʿda dhālika* — "after that" — is the verse's crux, and the source (Q) records the classical conclusion: the mention of the earth *after* the sky, night, and day indicates the earth was spread after the heavens were established. Yet elsewhere the Qur'an narrates the other direction — *"He it is who created for you all that is in the earth; then He directed Himself to the heaven and proportioned them seven heavens"* (2:29) — and the commentators take up the apparent contradiction with a care this verse rewards retelling. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the report al-Ṭabarī preserves, resolves it by separating the acts: God created the earth *first* as a mass, then made the heavens; then — *baʿda dhālika* — He *spread it out* (daḥāhā): leveled it, clove it with rivers, made it habitable; and al-Rāzī offers the same reconciliation in his own register, that the "after" is of the *spreading*, not of the earth's existence — the ground was laid before the roof, and the interior finished after, exactly as a house is built. A second school adds that *baʿda* can carry, in classical idiom, the sense of "along with, besides" — the sūrah lists favors, not a geology — and either way the verse is kept, by the tradition's caution, out of the business of dating strata: its point is the *daḥā*, the spread, not the calendar of the spreading. This is a methodological lesson the mufassirūn press upon readers who try to make the Qur'an answer modern cosmogony: the Book orders its signs rhetorically — canopy, then darkness, then light, then the spread bed — and the sequence is an *argument*, not a *schedule*; the schedule, when the text does give one (41:9–12's "two days for the earth, then the heaven in proportion..."), is itself given in the service of the same proof: whoever raised this and spread that is not stalled at re-making you. (The sūrah, accordingly, never asks its hearers to believe a chronology; it asks them to look at a dwelling and remember whose it is.)

**The Spread Earth as the Resurrection's Anteroom**

Why is *daḥāhā* here, in a sūrah sworn on resurrection? Because verse 14 already stood on the same verb's cousin: the *sāḥira*, the wide expanse, the bare leveled surface on which the dead will stand; and the sūrah's middle now completes a theological circle drawn by its opening oaths: the earth spread out *for you* in verses 30–33 is the earth re-spread *upon you* — the plain — in verse 14, and the makings of a dwelling are the makings of a courtroom that one day needs no walls because its floor is the whole planet. The exegetes note the tenderness hidden in the placement: the very ground the denier walks on while he says "bones and decay" (v. 11) is, in the sūrah's sentence, an *ostrich's bed* prepared for him — the argument does not taunt; it indicts *gently*, by the courtesy of the furnishings. And the hadith-tradition's most vivid dawn-picture fits: the Prophet ﷺ, in agreed-upon reports on the Day's first hour, taught that the sun will come near and the earth will be *changed* (14:48) — the spread bed folded up, the canopy taken down — so that the same *daḥā* that made the world habitable now measures the Day's severity by contrast: He who spread it can roll it (39:67: *"the heavens rolled up in His right hand"*) — and rolling is not difficulty for the One for whom spreading was, likewise, only a verb.

**Baʿda Dhālika, for Whoever Fears**

One last register: the phrase *baʿda dhālika* reads, in the sūrah's ear, as "after all that," the after of a long kindness. The structure of the passage — sky, night, day, earth, water, pasture, mountains (27–33) — is the *niʿam* (blessings) catalogue the Qur'an elsewhere uses as its standing proof of divine *rahma*: the favors are counted so the denier's presumption ("this is my due," 41:50) can be shown exactly how many things he did not due. "And after that He spread out the earth" is, on that reading, not an astronomy lemma at all; it is the next line in a bill of grace, and the bill is addressed — as verse 26 said — to *whoever fears*: the one still capable of receiving a favor as a favor, which is the beginning of resurrection-faith, because a resurrection is, on the sūrah's showing, the last and largest of the *daḥā*-acts: the ground of the world spread out, one final morning, beneath the feet of every man who ever slept in it.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:31

> **From it He brought forth its water and its pastures,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Nouns That Feed the Whole Argument**

*Makhraja minhā mā'ahā wa-mar'āhā* — "He brought forth from it its water and its pasture." The verb is *ikhraj* again (v. 29's *akhraja ḍuḥāhā*): the light drawn out of the sky, now the water drawn out of the ground — the sūrah's cosmic verb of extraction, the same root as the angels who draw forth souls (*nāshiṭāt nashṭan*, v. 2), runs through the whole creation-cluster like a refrain, and the echo is not ornament: bringing water from a sealed earth is the resurrection argument in the key of daily life, because water from the ground *is* life from dead matter, seen, tasted, and farmed by every hearer. The two nouns are a complete food-chain in miniature: *mā'* — water, plain, for drinking — and *mar'ā* — pasture, grass grown for grazing, the word from r-'-w whose field is vegetation rising, hope springing (the same root gives *arwā*, to be watered, to be satisfied). Pasture is what water becomes when it is *sent* rather than merely *present*: the earth drinks, the earth gives grass, the herds eat the grass, people drink milk, and the sūrah's next verse (v. 33) will name the whole chain's purpose — *for you and for your herds.* The argument to the denier is therefore a two-step syllogism of the obvious: the one who brings water and grass out of a stone-hard ground every spring has, visibly, a habitual power to bring living things out of dead matter; and the bones of verse 11 are only a harder spring.

**"From It": the Earth as a Sealed Vault That Opens**

The preposition *minhā* — "from it, out of the earth" — holds a theological weight the commentators mark with care: the water is *out of* the ground, and the pasture out of the ground, not dropped onto it as from a stockpile. This is the verse's answer, in the sūrah's own vocabulary, to the storage-objection of the deniers: what you imagine as locked in the earth's fist — decayed bones, scattered dust — is, for the One who *brings forth*, exactly like the water and the seed: matter the earth was always holding, under a seal only one hand can lift. The Qur'an elsewhere states the mechanism of the sign as doctrine: *"and We send down from the sky pure water, that We may give life to a dead land and give to drink from it many of those We have created"* (25:48–49); *"what you sow — is it you who make it grow, or are We the planters?"* (56:63–64); and, of the resurrection itself, in the sūrah's own neighborhood: *"and the earth He spread (daḥāhā) — from it He brought forth its water and its pasture"* — the *minhā* of this verse is the *fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidat* of verse 13 in agricultural dress: one bringing-forth is the other, at a smaller scale and on a longer lease. The exegetes (al-Biqāʿī's *Nazm* tradition presses this) note the further neatness: the same earth that will vomit out its dead in Sūrat al-Zalzala (99:2: *wa-akhrajati al-arḍu athqālahā* — "the earth brings forth its burdens") has already, in this verse, been shown at work on the living: the resurrection is not an exception to the earth's nature but the earth's nature completed — a ground whose whole career is *ikhraj*, and whose last crop is us.

**Mar'ā: the Pasture, Its Two Readings, and the Flocks**

The source preserves the interpretive spread of *mar'ā*: some take it as every kind of plant (Q), others as specifically what humans and animals eat — the fodder and the fields (and, in the sūrah's next verses, the "provisions" the mountains and pastures jointly serve). The second reading makes the verse an ecological proposition: the pasture is *its* pasture — the earth's own grass, evolved for the mouths of its animals, in the classical language "from it for its creatures" — and the argument of provision binds human and herd into one economy: the Makkan hearer's goat, his camel, his child all depend, within a day, on this *ikhraj* — nobody in the desert could read verse 31 as abstraction. Ibn Qayyim, the great reader of the sūrah's practical psychology, treats this cluster (30–33) as the Qur'an's standing *duʿā* against anxiety: sustenance is drawn out daily, from a vault you do not control, by a Hand whose track-record the verse cites; and worry about tomorrow, on that reading, is a minor resurrection-denial — a claim that the *makhraj* has retired. The verse's own grammar, with its double *-hā* possessive — *its* water, *its* pasture — keeps the ownership where the sūrah put it in verse 27: the earth is *He* who built, darkened, brought forth, spread: the pronouns on the ground are signs of the owner's title-deed, and the herds grazing the *mar'ā* are, in the Book's idiom, the de facto tenants recognizing the landlord better than the denier in verse 10 who lives rent-free inside the proof he refuses.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Machine**

Finally, the structural reader will notice where this *ikhraj* falls: between the sky-cluster and the mountains (v. 32), and the sequence is the makings of a habitable valley — roof, day-night, spread floor, water, fodder, walls to hold the weather in rhythm: a dwelling furnished floor by floor before the Qur'an asks anyone to believe one further thing (the resurrection). The verse is thus the *climax of concessions*: everything in 27–33 is a fact the Makkan economy already conceded — nobody in that trade-city argued with "it rains and grass grows" — and the sūrah spends these conceded facts, one per line, to buy, by the time verse 34 opens, the one contested fact: the Great Calamity that overwhelms all the rest. It is the Book's answer, long before the philosophers made it a syllogism, to the question "why believe what you cannot see?" — because you have seen, daily, a thousand small resurrections whose Author you have not disputed, and the last one will be on the same signature as the first.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

> **and the mountains He set firm,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Awsāhā: the Verb of Driving Things Home**

*Wa-al-jibāla awsāhā* — "and the mountains — He drove them in, set them firm." The verb *awsā* is from w-s-' (as in *awsa'a al-watar*: he stretched the bowstring to full draw; and *al-wasi'*: the ample, the enriched) and in this construction it means to establish with force and with breadth: the mountains as stakes (the word of 78:7, *awtān*) hammered into the ground so that the ground stays. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr gloss *awsāhā* as *awsada* — He fixed them so the earth does not sway — and the sūrah says as much in its own elsewhere: *"and He has set on the earth firm mountains, lest it should sway with you"* (31:10; and 16:15, 21:31 in identical grammar). The mountains in this verse are thus not scenery, and not even primarily geology; they are *ballast*, the answer to a ground that would otherwise be, in the Makkan idiom, *mayt* — restless, unquiet — and they belong, in the sūrah's dwelling-metaphor, with the pegs that hold the tent walls: canopy (v. 28), floor spread (v. 30), water and pasture drawn up (v. 31), and now the tent-pegs driven down, the whole earth made, in four lines, a livable encampment.

**The Pegs and the Quaking: a Verse Read Against Its Sūrah**

The argumentative placement does the heavy work. This is the sūrah whose verses 6–7 swore that "the Day the Quaker quakes and the Successor follows," and the mountains here are the creation's *visible* answer to that earthquake-in-waiting: the pegs that hold the ground *today* are the same pegs that the Day will pull — *"and they ask you about the mountains; say: my Lord will uproot them and scatter them, and leave them a level plain, in which you see no hollow and no ridge"* (20:105–106); *"the Day when the sky will be a boiling cauldron, and the mountains will be a woolen fluff"* (70:8–9, in the idiom of carded wool flying apart); *"and the mountains will be blown away and be a mirage"* (78:20). To recite verse 32 inside verse 79 is to hold, at once, the mountain as firm and the mountain as fluff — and the tension is the sūrah's chosen pedagogy of the heart: firmness is a *favor*, not a property; stability is *given* by the One who also *lends* it, and the Day is the day of collection. The Makkan miner and herder knew the favor physically — the mountain was the watershed that fed the *mā'* of v. 31 and the refuge of the valley's weather — and the same mountain belonged to the very list of wonders the Quraysh had demanded as the price of belief: *"and they say: We will never believe you until you burst forth for us a spring from the earth, or you have a garden of palm-trees and grapes and you burst rivers within it forth abundantly, or you cause the sky to fall upon us in fragments as you claimed, or you bring God and the angels before us face to face"* (17:90–92). The alternatives they demanded — springs, rivers, falling sky-fragments — are, verse for verse, the powers this cluster says He already exercises daily without being asked: water brought forth (v. 31), pastures grown (v. 31), mountains pegged and, if He wills, uprooted (v. 32 with 20:105). The Quraysh wanted a miracle billed to order; the sūrah answers with the miracle already invoiced, paid, and delivered — the ground they were standing on.

**The Mountains as the Earth's Mercy — and the Flock's Fence**

The classical commentaries note a second, quieter function of *awsāhā* inside the sūrah's provision-catalogue (vv. 31–33): the mountains are *part of the sustenance*, not a separate miracle. Al-Rāzī, in the long *naẓm* treatment of this cluster, lists their favors exactly as the verse implies them: they are the watersheds that condense and store the water v. 31 brings forth, the barriers that temper the winds and fix the climate of the valleys where the *mar'ā* grows, and — read with v. 33's *anʿām*, your herds — the pastures themselves: the high slopes whose summer grass fed precisely the flocks the Makkan economy rode and traded upon; the mountain was, to that audience, at one and the same time the water-tower, the wind-break, and the grazing. This is why the three verses 31–33 must be read as one gesture of the one verb of *giving*: from it its water, its pasture, the mountains firm, provision *for you and for your herds* — and the whole cosmology of 27–33, which began as an argument from greater-to-lesser for resurrection, is here revealed also as an argument from care: the One whose power is large enough to build a sky (v. 27) is attentive enough to peg down the ground a child sleeps on, and a God of that double scale does not, the sūrah implies, *lose* a bone in the earth; He is keeping account of every stone beneath it for the day He weighs it.

**One Firmness at a Time**

The verse's last echo is the one the reciters carry: *awsāhā* is a completed act, past tense in the Arabic — the mountains *were* driven in, and remain; firmness, in the sūrah's grammar, is a finished favor with an appointed expiry, and the Day is that expiry (v. 34 opens on the *Ṭāmmah* two verses later). The reader who has watched a storm hold off because the hills held the weather, who has drunk from the spring that the mountain's stone filtered, hears v. 32 as a receipt; the reader who has seen a landslide — the pegs slipping, the ground *māda*, swaying, the mercy withdrawn for an hour — hears it as a warning that the receipt is revocable. The sūrah's architecture wants both readings at once, and they meet in its own verb: the God who *awsā* the mountains is the God who will *burriq* — display — the Fire (v. 36), and between those two acts the human being stands, on a firm ground he did not lay, in a night he did not darken, under a day he did not bring forth, eating a pasture he did not grow — every verse of this cluster repeating the single, unanswerable, merciful sentence of the Book: *all of it was brought, and none of it by you.*

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:33

> **as sustenance for you and for your flocks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Turns Cosmology into Household**

*Matā'an lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum* — "a provision for you and for your herds." The cluster of creation-verses (27–32) lands here, and its landing is domestic: not "signs for the philosophically inclined" but *matāʿ* — provision, enjoyment, the goods a traveler takes with him, from m-t-ʿ, the root the Qur'an uses for the whole short capital of this life (*"the provision of delusion"*, 3:185). The grammar is a gift-clause: the sky-built, night-darkened, day-brightened, earth-spread, water-and-pasture-brought-forth, mountain-pegged cosmos is, in one noun, declared *for you* — and the sentence's two objects, *you and your herds*, complete the circle the sūrah began with the angels who draw out souls (v. 1): everything between the canopy and the bedrock is arranged around living creatures, and the living creatures are not owners but beneficiaries. Al-Rāzī marks the order — *for you* first, *for your herds* after — as the sūrah's own modest hierarchy: the herds exist within your provision, and you exist within God's; the list of favors has a direction, and its direction runs from the Giver down, never back up.

**Anʿām: the Animals That Carry the Covenant of the Pasture**

*Anʿām* — grazing livestock: camels, cattle, sheep, goats, "the pair of each" as 6:143 will spell them out — and the word's presence here is the sūrah's ecological footnote: the pastures of verse 31 are grown for mouths that do not pray, yet the Book counts those mouths inside the blessing addressed to the ones who do. The Makkan economy knew the debt exactly: milk, wool, leather, transport, ploughing, the *zakāt* that purified the numbers of the flocks, the *ʿaqīqa* and the ḥajj-sacrifice that returned the animal to God at the believer's most sacred hours. The tradition generalizes the verse into a rule of tenancy: humans are vicegerents on this spread earth (2:30), and vicegerency over a provision *shared with the herds* is the sūrah's own definition of stewardship — the pasture is *their* pasture too, and the mountains hold the water that fills *their* troughs. The Prophet's ﷺ ḥadīth, agreed upon, makes the same economy of mercy a road to forgiveness: *"a man was walking and was overcome by thirst, and descended into a well and drank, and then saw a dog panting and eating the moist earth from thirst. He said: this one is overcome by what overcame me — so he filled his shoe with water and held it in his mouth until he gave the dog a drink. So God thanked him and forgave him."* (al-Bukhārī 2363; Muslim 2244 — the Companions asked: "is there for us, in the beasts, a reward?" He said: *"in every moist, thirsting liver, a reward."*) Verse 33, beside that ḥadīth, reads as the same sentence from the Creator's side: the provision is for you *and for your herds* — and the reward is for you *in* them.

**"For You": the Ethics of Receipt**

The dative *lakum* does more than flatter; it legislates. To name the earth's produce *matāʿ* "for you" is to teach the believer the correct first move in the presence of a resource: receive it — it *was made for you* — and the receiving, in the Book's idiom, is itself worship when it stops at gratitude and circulates to the co-heirs of the verse ("your herds," and, in the wider Book, your neighbor and the traveler: the pasture is *ʿām*, common, until men fence it). The verse therefore stands athwart two old distortions: the ascetic who will not enjoy what was made for enjoyment — a slight on the Giver's gift, as the sound ḥadīth warns against the one who forbids what God made lawful — and the consumer who enjoys without receipt-card, treating the *matāʿ* as property rather than provision, which is the Qur'an's own definition of the Pharaoh-logic (his "I am your lord most high" is the claim of the beneficiary to be the owner). The Makkan dawn-hearer, milking before the sunrise-sūrah was finished, was, by verse 33, holding in his hands the argument of the entire passage: sky, night, day, earth, water, pasture, mountains — seven favors — and the milk in the bucket is the sūrah's verdict on the bones in the denier's fist: the same system that gives you milk from an animal of grass and water and stone *is* the system that will give you back your bones, because the one is no lighter on the Giver than the other.

**The Small Word That Ends the Cosmological Proof**

And *matāʿ* closes with a second edge the commentators keep: the provision is called, in the Book's most repeated phrase, *matāʿ al-ghurūr* for those who mistake it for capital (3:185; 4:77; 57:20) — provision is, in the root's own grammar, *portable and perishable*: a traveler's stock, consumed on the road, not the road's destination. So verse 33 finishes the creation-argument with the sūrah's own scale of values re-installed: the heavens were built (v. 28) and you were made easier than them (v. 27) — and everything between, spread, pegged, darkened, brightened, watered, and sown, is provision, addressed to the two classes of living, and *temporal by name*. The resurrection is not the threat that hangs over the *matāʿ*; it is the day the *matāʿ* is audited — what was received with thanks has become weight on the scale, and what was seized as property has become the witness against its seizer, and the sūrah, past its midpoint, now turns from the built universe to the coming Day, its next five verses beginning with the conjunction "so," as if everything 27–33 were only the preparation of that single, overwhelming consequence.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:34

> **So when the Great Calamity befalls—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conditional Without an Answer — the Rhetoric of a Broken Sentence**

*Fa-idhā jāʾati al-ṭāmmatu al-kubrā* — "so when the Great Calamity comes…" — and the sūrah, for the first time in its long chain of answered clauses, leaves the *idhā* hanging: the verse sets the clock and says nothing about what happens when it strikes. The source records the rhetorical judgment (Q, Ṭs): the apodosis is suppressed because no apodosis would be large enough — the coming of the *Ṭāmmah* is, in its own arrival, the whole answer; everything after it in the sūrah (verses 35–41) is, in effect, a list of consequences too many to pack into one clause. The *fāʾ* opening the verse — "so" — welds the scene to the creation-cluster just completed: the sky built, the earth spread, the provision given, *and so*, when the Calamity comes, the account of all of it opens: the Day of judgment is the Day the *matāʿ* is audited (v. 33's hinge). Al-Zamakhsharī's style-note belongs here: the Arabs used the suspended *idhā* when the speaker is too overwhelmed to continue — and the Qur'an, saying the unsayable by leaving it unsaid, enacts on the page exactly the trembling it described at verses 8–9: the sentence itself cannot go on, and the heart cannot.

**Ṭāmmah: the Word That Means "Having Covered Everything"**

The name is the argument. *Ṭāmmah* is from ṭ-m-m — to be complete, to overflow, to rise above: the source's gloss is precise: "to be abundant, to rise over, as would a flood," so that *al-ṭāmmat al-kubrā* names an event *by its overtopping*: a calamity whose water-line is above every other calamity's roof, an affliction so complete that nothing it does not afflict counts as real. The diminutive-then-great construction — *ṭāmmah* as a noun of occurrence, enlarged by the epithet *kubrā* — is, in Arabic rhetoric, the pair that means "the overwhelming thing, and its being overwhelming is its only describable face." The Day of Judgment is elsewhere named by the same device of totality: *al-Qāriʿah* — the Striking that strikes the heart — "what is the Striking? And what will explain to you what the Striking is? The Day when people will be like moths scattered, and the mountains like carded wool" (101:1–5); *al-Ḥāqqah* — the Reality — "what is the Reality? And what will explain to you what the Reality is?" (69:1–3); *al-Ghāshiya* — the Overwhelming — "has the account of the Overwhelming reached you?" (88:1). Al-Nāziʿāt's *ṭāmmah* stands in this family with one further shade the lexicographers keep: *al-ṭāmmah* was used of a calamity that no patience can repair and no help reaches from outside — the disaster complete *in kind*, not merely in size; and the source (Q) records the twin identifications: the second blast of the Trumpet, at which all peoples and jinn are gathered, or the Resurrection itself, "because it surpasses and predominates over (*yaṭimmu*) all things," and, for some, the precise moment when the people of Hell are led to Hell and the people of Paradise to Paradise — the moment, that is, when the *ṭ-m-m* is finally in the past tense and the doors have closed over the last decision.

**"Kubrā": the Second Superlative, and the First One's Fate**

The epithet *kubrā* puts this verse inside the sūrah's own memory, for its only other occurrence is the greatest sign of Moses: *al-āyata al-kubrā* (v. 20). The pairing is a verdict in miniature: Pharaoh saw the Greatest Sign and called it sorcery; every Pharaoh since — including the Makkan lords whose "ruinous return" sneer the sūrah quoted (vv. 10–12) — stands before the Greater Calamity, and *this time* the sign and the thing signified arrive in the same gesture. The superlative also organizes the sūrah's scale of fear for the two final clusters: the *kubrā* that overwhelms without (v. 34) answers, exactly, the *khashya* that a person cultivates within (v. 40: *man khāsha maqāma rabbihī*) — the Day is *ṭāmmah* to the fearless and *maṭāʿ*-audited, light as a morning, to the fearing (v. 46's evening-and-morning arithmetic). Ibn ʿAbbās's transmitted phrase in the tafsīr-corpus — *al-ṭāmmah: al-qāriʿah ṭammat jamīʿa al-umūr* — "it completed, encompassed, all affairs" — is the single line that explains why the sūrah's answer-clause is suppressed: when an event has swallowed every other event, all that remains to be said about it is what happens inside it, and verses 35–41 are precisely that: remembrance, the Fire displayed, and the two homes.

**"So When…" — the Sūrah's Practical Suspense**

The grammar's suspension has an ethical function the reciters knew. A conditional without its answer is a held breath, and the Makkan dawn-hearer — the sūrah of the Juḥāt imam's prayer — stood each Fajr inside that held breath: the calamity's *when* is the one datum the sūrah has refused to name all chapter long, and its refusal is its instruction: suspense, in the Book's moral physics, is not ignorance; it is the condition of preparedness, because a dated terror is scheduled around and a dateless one is carried. The hadith of the Hour's unknowability (vv. 42–44 will seal it) is the same device at the level of doctrine: *"they ask you about the Hour… say: its knowledge is only with my Lord"* (7:187), and this verse is its rhetorical twin at the level of syntax — the *idhā* waits. The reader who finishes the verse unfinished has, for one instant, the posture the whole sūrah intends: a person standing in the middle of a spread, watered, mountain-pegged world, in the middle of a sentence whose ending God has not yet recited, with everything he ever did — verse 35's *mā saʿā* — already inside him, and the flood-line rising, verse by verse, to his own chest.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:35

> **a day when man shall remember that for which he endeavored,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Consequence of the Suspended Sentence: Memory Itself**

*Yawma yadhkuru al-insānu mā saʿā* — "the day when man remembers what he strove for." The suppressed answer of verse 34 now unfolds, and the first thing that happens on the Great Day is not a trumpet or a throne but a *remembering*: the Calamity's first effect is total recall. The sūrah's theology of memory is at work in the word *yadhkur* — the same root as *dhikr*, the remembrance that was, in this life, the voluntary act of the heart (v. 9's humbled hearts, v. 40's fearing one), and the Day's gift-sentence is that nobody will any longer be able to forget. *Mā saʿā* — "what he endeavored" — completes the circle: the sūrah has used the very noun *saʿy* twice before, once for Pharaoh's scurrying conspiracy (*adbara yasʿā*, v. 22: he went about, striving, mustering, proclaiming) and, in the background, for the herder's and farmer's whole labor of the provision-cluster (31–33). Every *saʿy* was watched; the Day does not add to the record; it transfers it from the archive into the man: what you did, you will *have* done, in the first person, without the anesthesia of distance. The phrase is the Qur'an's own technical term for this scene, and its twin occurs in al-Nabaʾ in identical wording — *yawma yadhkur al-insānu mā saʿā* (78:40) — a Makkan pairing the commentators use as mutual commentary: the two earliest sūrahs of the Hour, quoting each other, letter for letter.

**The Books the Sūrah Itself Cites — Record as Second Memory**

The cross-references the source gathers are, in effect, the sūrah's exegesis from its own body, and their actual words make the mechanism plain: *"Every person's deed We have fastened to his neck like a bird, and on the Day of Resurrection We will bring out for him a scroll he will find spread open: 'Read your scroll — your own self is sufficient, this day, as a reckoner against you' "* (17:13–14); *"We give life to the dead and We write what they have sent before and their footprints"* (36:12); *"or do they think that We do not hear their secrets and their private counsel? Yes — the messengers with them record what is being said"* (43:80); *"and the deeds are made to pass away, and you will see them set down"* (paraphrase of 18:49's *"Woe to us — what is this book that leaves neither small nor great without recording it"*); and the source's chief citation: *"the Day when God resurrects them all and informs them of what they did — God has kept count of it while they have forgotten it"* (58:6). Against this apparatus, *yadhkuru* is precise: the Day's memory is not a new information but a *relived record* — God counting what they forgot is the sūrah's most frightening and most just sentence: nothing was lost in the first place, and the one who "forgot" himself will now be handed back, complete, the person he kept mislaying.

**The Psychology of a Memory Without Defenses**

Modern psychology has named the machinery verse 35 assumes: memory is reconstructive, and the self survives by editing — forgetting, reframing, the vast quiet industry of not-recalling that makes an unexamined life endurable. The sūrah's Day is the abolition of the editing function: *yadhkuru al-insānu mā saʿā* — not "he will be told," but "he will remember," from the inside, with the whole context restored: the excuse will find, that day, the moment of choice it never mentions; the offense will recover, from the memory of the one who committed it, the face of the one who felt it — which is why the prophetic ethics of this life is, so constantly, an ethics of rehearsed remembrance: *"the servant's feet will not stir on the Day of Resurrection until he is questioned about five things: about his life — how he spent it; his youth — how he wore it out; his wealth — how he earned it and how he spent it; and what he knew — what did he do thereby"* (al-Tirmidhī 2417, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal, graded ṣaḥīḥ): the self-audit now is the memory the Day will not have to reconstruct; the believer arrives at verse 35 already having done its work, and the Day's recall, for him, is confirmation rather than ambush. Ibn Kathīr's gloss on the twin verse in al-Nabaʾ captures the double edge: "the obedient will rejoice at his remembrance of what he sent before, and the sinner will wish that his memory were as his forgetfulness was."

**"Man" — Indefinite, Therefore Universal**

Al-Nāziʿāt keeps the noun *al-insān* — generic man — in the accusative of what is remembered, *mā saʿā*, open: no virtue named, no vice named, no class exempt; the verse is the sūrah's own general summons, and the commentators note its quiet completeness: *saʿy* includes the deliberate and the merely permitted — every effort, every neglect dressed as effort — which is exactly the scope the Prophet's ﷺ maxim draws around this verse: *"the keen-sighted (al-kāyis) is the one who takes his soul to account and works for what is after death; and the one rendered helpless is the one who follows his soul its desire (hawā) and lengthens his hopes upon God"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws, graded ḥasan) — and that very word *hawā* is the one verse 40 will name as the thing the saved forbade to their souls. Verse 35, standing between the display of the Fire (v. 36) and the two homes (37–41), is thus the sūrah's fulcrum of responsibility: resurrection is believed in the heart that expects its own memory; and the Calamity, on the Book's scales, is not that everything returns to the earth — it is that nothing ever left.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:36

> **and Hellfire is made visible for one who sees—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Consequence: What Becomes Seen**

*Wa-burrizati al-jaḥīm liman yabsur* — "and the Blaze is displayed for whoever sees." The verb is the sūrah's own vocabulary of exposure (v. 9's humbled eyes will be the eyes that *do* see, here): *baraza* is to come out into the open, to stand on the *barāz*, the clear ground where nothing intervenes between the viewer and the viewed — and the passive form makes the display an act of God: He brings the Fire out, as one brings the accused into court, *for* the seeing of each. The pairing with verse 35 is the sūrah's complete psychology of the Hour: first the *inside* becomes transparent — man remembers what he did — then the *outside* becomes transparent — the Fire he forgot is shown, and the two transparencies meet: the memory and the sight were always one record at two distances. The source records the classical spread of *liman yabsur* — "for one who sees": some take it as the disbelievers specifically (Q, R), some as every human being, because on that Day all will see and only some will be seized by the seeing (Q); and some (R) press the subtlety that the verse cannot mean the disbelievers alone, since to *see* the realities of the Hereafter requires eyes of the kind faith opens in this life — an argument al-Rāzī handles with his characteristic dialectical patience, and whose real force is pastoral: the Fire, once displayed, is evidence enough to convict every witness; the only escape, the sūrah implies, is the one verse 40 names — the fear that sees *without* being shown.

**Jaḥīm: the Fire by Its Temperature**

*Al-jaḥīm* — the Blazing — from j-ḥ-m: intense heat; the name keeps the punishment in physics rather than fantasy, the Qur'an's consistent habit (the *saʿīr* that kindles, the *saqar* that scorches, *ḍarīq* that burns the face — 88:10's "roaring fire," *jaḥīma*, is named there too). The Makkan audience lived with fire as the desert's second sun — the camp-fire, the branding-iron, the mirage-heat that kills thirst without drinking — and the sūrah's *jaḥīm* borrows that familiarity precisely to invert it: the fire they gathered around for news and warmth becomes the thing gathered *around them*, displayed, in full daylight, on a flat plain where there is no ridge to hide behind (v. 14's *sāḥirah*, the same ground-level horror). The Qur'an's own companion-verses fill the display with its full scene: the Day of *burūz* is the Day of the Lord's coming in judgment — *"and your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank"* (89:22) — and the display of the Fire is that same advancing unveiling seen from below: *"a Day when they will be uncovered from the shin, and they will be called to prostrate, but they will not be able"* (68:42). *Burrizat* and *yunkashafūn* are the same event in two sūrahs: the Day's essence is disclosure; the Fire is what disclosure does to the unready.

**The Verse's Other Half: Who Is "One Who Sees"**

Al-Ṭabarī's inclusive rule returns here with teeth: read the verse at its widest — the Blaze is displayed *to every seer* — and its sharpest at its narrowest: for the one whose whole life was a decision about this exact visibility, *seeing* becomes his permanent state: no more argument, no more metaphor, no more "if." The source's note on this verse records the theological refinement (R): the disbeliever's eyes, in the Qur'an's idiom, are veiled *now* — *"God has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and over their eyes is a covering"* (2:7) — so verse 36's "one who sees" is the veil's removal: seeing is, that day, the final mercy revoked into final proof. This is the sūrah's answer to every reader who treats the Fire as a scare-crow: the Fire, in verse 36, is not a threat to frighten the imagination but the *already-built consequence* placed in the field of vision — *"and your Lord never wrongs the people"* (10:44; 41:46), and what is displayed was, in the sūrah's architecture (v. 35), *remembered from inside*: the person sees, that Day, precisely what he chose not to see now. The ethical pressure is singular: every refusal to see the Fire's reality at the moment it is described in revelation is a rehearsal of the Day's seeing without preparation, and the sūrah's dawn-reciters (the Juḥāt imam, Bukhārī) were trained, at the hour the sun's light first becomes visible, to have seen *this* light first — in the Book, voluntarily — so that the forced seeing, v. 36's, would find the eyes already used to the glare and turned, by practice, toward the Other whose fear (v. 40) is the only sight that saves.

**Between the Remembering and the Abode**

Structurally, verse 36 is the hinge between v. 35 (the inner record) and vv. 37–39 (the outer verdict): the displayed Fire is the *court's exhibit*, and the verdicts that follow — the transgressor's home, the fearing man's home — are the *sentences*. The dash of the source's translation ("for one who sees—") is the sūrah's own syntax: the display halts the sentence so the hearer stands, an instant, *inside* the seeing, before the next three verses walk him to whichever abode his stance in that instant has chosen for him. The commentators note that the sūrah never describes the Fire's torments here at all, only its *visibility*, and they read the refusal as deliberate severity: the Qur'an's Makkan Fire-verses, in the estimation of Ibn al-Qayyim (in *al-Wāfil*), terrify more by the display than by the description, because the description can be argued with, and the seeing cannot: on the Day, nobody will dispute the Fire; the whole sūrah exists to make the dispute, while there is still a dispute to have, the thing that matters — and *liman yabsur* is the verse's open, terrible courtesy: whoever has eyes, look.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:37

> **as for one who rebels**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict Begins With the Man's Own Verb**

*Fa-ammā man ṭaghā* — "so as for the one who transgressed." The sūrah has spent its middle section with this exact verb: Pharaoh "rebelled" (v. 17, *innaḥ ṭaghā*), and now, at the edge of the two verdicts, the same root reopens the case: the man standing in the dock of verse 37 is Pharaoh's kinsman by deed, and the Makkan scofflaw by name. The construction — *ammā* with its rhetorical pause (the "as for..." that, in Arabic grammar, sets the topic before the ruling) — makes the sentence read like a judge turning a page to a labeled file: the file of *ṭughyān*. And the labeling is deliberate: the sūrah does not say "as for the one who *disbelieved*" but "the one who *transgressed*," because the resurrection-denial of verses 10–12 has, in this sūrah's diagnosis, a moral before it is an intellectual condition: the men who sneered at the return were men who had exceeded their measure, and *ṭughyān* — the water over the bank, the camel beyond the tether — names the exceedance in a single word, the way the older Arabic proverb did: *"al-ṭughyān ilā al-ṭughyān dhulm"* — transgression piled on transgression is, itself, the injustice. Al-Rāzī's interior gloss at this verse, recorded in the source (at 37–39), is the classical deep-reading: *ṭughyān* here indicates *the corruption of the intellect* — for, he argues, whoever truly knows God knows his own smallness, and so never inclines to rebel; the rebel's first sin is not pride's act but the mind's consent to a false measure of itself, and the verse, opening with the bare verb, indicts the mind that approved the reach.

**What Ṭaghā Adds That Kufr Does Not**

The distinction carries the sūrah's whole theory of responsibility, and the commentators press it in two directions. Against the defaulter's excuse — "I was merely not convinced" — *ṭaghā* says: unbelief, in this Book, is never a passive state; it is an active exceeding, a trespass onto what is not yours, and every denial of the Hour is a *claim* (the claim to be unaccountable) before it is a conclusion. Against the believer's self-flattery — "I am not a tyrant, only a sinner" — the same verb, applied equally to Pharaoh (v. 17) and to the anonymous *man* of v. 37 (*man ṭaghā*, indefinite as to name, definite as to species), says: the distance between a king's "I am your lord most high" and a common man's small daily overreach is a distance of *scale*, not of *kind* — and the verdict here is written against the kind. This is why the tradition's moralists (al-Muḥāsibī's *Riʿāya*, and the Sufi reading of the oaths the source preserves at vv. 1–5) read *ṭaghā* as the soul's single governing mis-shape: the heart that takes more than its measure — more rest than its duty, more wealth than its need, more honor than its truth — is the heart that will, at the end, be given the only abode its exceedance fitted it for (v. 39), and the Makkan dawn-hearer, leaving the Juḥāt prayer into the bargaining of the market, understood that his scales were being spoken of in two senses at once.

**The Parallel Case: Thamūd, the Strong Man, and the She-Camel**

The Qur'an tells of another people's *ṭughyān* in twin wording this verse cannot help recalling — the Thamūd of Sūrat al-Shams: *"Thamūd denied, through their transgression (kadhdhabat Thamūdu bi-ṭaghwāhā)"* (91:11) — denial and exceedance fused in one clause — and then, for *"when the most wretched of them rose up"* (91:12), the Prophet's ﷺ own explanation, preserved in Muslim's Book of Righteousness: he recited the verse and said the man who hamstrung God's she-camel rose up *"a mighty one, strong and honored"* among his people — the strength, the capacity, the social weight, all present, and all of them turned into the single act of exceedance the sūrah names. So God, in the sūrah's own next line, *"levels them for their sin and evens it"* (91:14), and the twin-passage of the cattle asks the question that makes the ancient case this verse's mirror: *"do they not see that We have created for them, of what Our Hands have made, cattle — so they are their owners?"* (36:71) — owned, and in turn owning nothing of the hour their exceedance will meet; and the she-camel's story concludes in the sūrah of al-Qamar with the sentence that stands over verse 37 like a verdict already passed: *"We sent upon them a single shout, and behold, they were like the dry stems of the fold"* (54:14) — one cry, and the exceeding ones become stubble. And 91:15 adds, of God's leveling of them, the line this sūrah has been swearing since verse 6: *"and He does not fear its consequence (wa-lā yakhāfu ʿuqbāhā)."* The parallel is verse 37's commentary: *ṭughyān* recurs as the sin of the *capable* man who trusts in capability — which is precisely Pharaoh's sin (his magicians, his armies, his "I showed you not what I see," 40:29) and precisely the Makkan lords' sin, and this is why the sūrah makes the verdict of v. 37 general: the strength that does not bow becomes the strength that overflows, and the overflow, *fa-inna al-jaḥīma hiya al-maʾwā* (v. 39), is the level the overflowing things come to rest at — the lowest place being, the sūrah says, the only fit for the one who insisted that no place stood above him.

**The Verse's Single Word and the Reader's Mirror**

One final note on the verse's brevity: two words, *fa-ammā man ṭaghā*, and the sūrah stops — the verdict itself is withheld until v. 38's second charge and v. 39's sentence. The arrested verdict is rhetoric of self-inspection: every hearer completes the clause privately, in his own handwriting, and the sūrah's structure ensures the completion is honest, because it has just shown him Pharaoh (15–26) and will immediately show him the preference that produced the rebellion (v. 38: *and preferred the life of this world*) — so that no one can say "the rebel, the *other* one": the file of *ṭughyān* is opened at the place where the sūrah knows every reader keeps his own record, and the one word *ṭaghā* is, on the Book's showing, the only entry that matters. The classical counsel attached to this verse by the Sufi exegetes is the practical close: watch the small overreachings — the extra glance, the extra dirham kept, the extra word in your own defense that truth did not require — because they are the same verb, on a shorter leash, as the sentence that drowned an empire; and the Day that files them together, as v. 35 said, will *remind* the man of what he himself forgot, line by line, until the whole of *ṭughyā* stands revealed as one long decision made small, daily, and never once reversed.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:38

> **and prefers the life of this world,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Charge: a Preference, Not a Doubt**

*Wa-āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* — "and he preferred, he chose to place higher, the life of this world." The verb *āthara* (from th-r, the root of selecting, of setting one thing above another in the scale of love) is the sūrah's diagnosis of the heart's economy, and it is worth pausing on how gentle the word looks beside the verdict it earns: the man in verses 37–39 is not accused of a dramatic hatred of God but of a *placement error* — the nearer life loved past the further one — and the sūrah lets that quiet verb carry the sentence, because, in the Qur'an's psychology, world-preference is not one sin among others; it is the *allocator* of all the others, the single preference that spends every later loyalty. The source notes al-Rāzī's pairing here: rebellion corrupted the intellect (v. 37), preference corrupts the deeds (v. 38) — and for the second half he cites as the verse's own commentary the Prophet's maxim: *"the love of the world is the head of every error"* — the saying transmitted in the prophetic tradition and adopted by the exegetes as the key to this verse (al-Bayhaqī in *al-Zuhd*, and earlier in the chains Ibn Ḥibbān grades; the meaning stands whether or not every chain is insisted upon). Al-Nāziʿāt's middle section will spend its provision-cluster on the same word in reverse — the *matāʿ* given *for you* (v. 33) is not the *dunyā* preferred *over* Him — and the verse thus functions as the sūrah's definition of the disease whose only symptom is that the gift has been made the god.

**Dunyā: the Word That Means "Lower"**

The name of the thing preferred is itself the argument: *dunyā* is the feminine of *adnā* — nearer, lower — so the verse literally says "and he preferred the nearer life," and the Qur'an's usage keeps the comparative in place wherever the word appears: *"do they not see that We visit the land, diminishing it at its edges?"* (13:41); *"and the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion"* (3:185) — the verse the source cites here, quoted in full because it is the tradition's standing tafsīr of *āthara*: *"the life of this world is but play and diversion; and the abode of the Hereafter — that is the life, if they only knew"* (29:64); and, for the Makkan ear, the sharpest twin of all, in the sūrah the commentators pair with ours: *"Nay, but you prefer the life of this world, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting"* (87:16–17) — *khayrun wa-abqā*: better and longer, the two axes on which any preference ought to be measured, and the two axes verse 38 says the *mutakhāyyir* — the chooser — has ignored by choice, not by miscalculation. The logic the sūrah forces is a child's arithmetic made adult: when one of two goods is *greater* and the other *present*, the animal scale tilts one way and the rational the other; world-preference is, on the Book's definition, the victory of presence over measure — the reason the Day will be, in the next verse-pair, all *presence* and no more postponement.

**The Economics of Preference: What the Believer Is Told to Do Instead**

Because *īthār* is an economic verb — the placing of a higher price on a thing — the verse legislates, by negation, the believer's whole conduct of the world: he is not commanded to despise the *dunyā* (the provision-cluster of vv. 30–33 is a blessing with a receipt), but to refuse to *prefer* it — that is, to price the two lives on the same scale, and let the permanent side of the ledger decide the temporary one's allocations. The tradition's practical form of this is the Prophet's ﷺ two-sentence rule, agreed upon from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar: *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler along a road."* — and from Ibn ʿUmar again, in al-Tirmidhī's sound report: the Prophet ﷺ said: *"What have I to do with the world? My example and the world's is like a man who traveled, sheltered under a tree for a while, then left it and went on."* Both sayings are *āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* inverted: use, do not reside; price the rest as shade, not as home. The social consequence the exegetes draw is exactly as important as the ascetic one: the world-preferrer is not only the ascetic's opposite but the tyrant's supplier — every ṭāghiy of v. 37 stood on the backs of men and women who preferred present safety, present profit, present comfort; Pharaoh's tower and Makkah's mockery alike were financed by preferences, which is why the sūrah binds v. 38 to v. 37 with the one conjunction *wa-* and hands both to v. 39's single sentence: the Fire does not punish the doubter; it receives the person who already chose, daily, the thing that cannot receive him.

**"The Life" — and the Other Life the Verse Leaves Unnamed**

One last precision: the verse says "the life of this world" — *al-ḥayāta* with the definite article, the whole of it, as a single object of love — and does not speak here of what was preferred *against*, leaving the Hereafter unnamed in the very clause of its losing, a rhetorical device the balāgha scholars call *ḥadf al-musammā* (the omitting of the named for the sake of its enormity): the second life is so large it cannot fit in the sentence, and its absence from the verse is its presence to the reader. The next verses (39–41) will name both abodes at once — the Fire for the preferer, the Garden for the fearing one — and the symmetry will then complete the diagnosis: each person's abode, on this sūrah's showing, is simply the *ḥayāh* he preferred, extended forever; the world, loved as final, becomes the Fire; the world, used as passage, becomes the Garden; and the verse's one verb — *āthara* — is the only door in the whole chapter that both classes of humanity enter through, each of them, on the Makkan dawn-hearer's side of it, in the small, daily, entirely free hour of choosing.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:39

> **truly Hellfire is the refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Maʾwā: the Word That Means "the Home You Returned To"**

*Fa-inna al-jaḥīma hiya al-maʾwā* — "then the Blaze — it is the home." The noun *maʾwā* is built from ʾ-w-ʾ, the root of lodging, of the bird's return to its roost at dusk, of the night's dwelling; and the sūrah, in the entire verse, offers no verb of punishing, no image of chains, no sentence at all — only possession: the Fire *is* his home. The commentators read this deliberate plainness as the verse's severity: a threat describes; a verdict re-houses. And the grammar of *hiya al-maʾwā* — the pronoun closing the class, "it, and nothing else, is the home" — is, in Arabic's copular idiom, exclusivity itself: not "one of his homes," but *the* home; every other lodging he built in the preferred life (v. 38) is revealed, in this article's definiteness, to have never been a lodging at all. The source records al-Rāzī's careful gradation on the pair 37–39: the two charges combined — intellect corrupted by rebellion, deeds corrupted by world-love — mark, he says, the *complete* corruption, for whose people the Fire is the permanent refuge; while he who bears one of the two and not the other, or whose corruption is not sealed, *may still be punished* for a season and not abide — "the refuge" in the verse's exclusive sense belongs to those who reached, and died upon, the full equation. This is the sūrah's way of saying what the whole Qur'an says elsewhere — the threat is absolute for the absolutists of the world, and the door of tawḥīd remains for everyone else: *"whoever dies not associating anything with God will enter Paradise, and whoever dies associating will enter the Fire"* (a meaning carried by sound reports in Muslim, no. 91 from ʿUthmān: *"whoever dies knowing that there is no god but God enters Paradise"*).

**The Home the Inhabitant Chose: Maʾwā Against Maʾwā**

The verse's quiet genius is its symmetry with verse 41 — *fa-inna al-janna hiya al-maʾwā* — the identical noun for the identical copula, two homes with one name. The sūrah will not let the Fire be a dungeon (a place imposed) nor the Garden a bonus (a place conferred on strangers): both are *maʾwāʾ* — homes — which is to say, both are answered longings, dwellings fitted to the one who built his wanting into them. The theology is the Book's standard, and this verse's word makes it precise: punishment in the Qur'an is, more often than not, the self's choice grown permanent — *"and We have fastened every person's divining-bird to his neck"* (17:13), and on this Day: *"the Day when God resurrects them all and informs them of what they did"* (58:6) — the Fire being, in the sūrah's own sequence, precisely *displayed to the one who sees* (v. 36), the thing the man preferred made visible and inhabited. Ibn al-Qayyim, the great reader of this idiom, builds his *al-Rūḥ* around the axiom the verse implies: the heart's love is the truest law of its motion, and God's verdicts, on the Day, are mostly *ratifications*: the refugee to the Fire and the refugee to the Garden are, in their homes, at last with what they loved — which is why *maʾwā* is the most just word in the Arabic of the Qur'an for the last estate, and why the Makkan dawn-hearer heard verse 39 not as menace but as the closing of a door he had, by preference after preference, already walked through.

**"The Blaze": Why Jaḥīm and Not Nār**

The name *jaḥīm* recurs at the sūrah's center (v. 36) and its verdict, and the root j-ḥ-m, as the lexicographers note, is the blazing itself — the fire at its hottest, most consuming state — and its deployment here, instead of the more common *nār*, ties the abode to the act just named: the world-lover is housed in *the Blaze*, because blaze is what love for a vanishing thing becomes when the vanishing arrives: the heat of the fire the man fed all his life, now the only warmth left, now consuming permanently what it was always going to consume. The exegetes record, beside this, the hadith's most surgical image of the *āthir*'s disorientation — in the two Ṣaḥīḥaḥ, in the sound reports of the two collections — the man from the people of Paradise of whom a single dip in the Fire is made, and he is asked afterward: *"Did you ever see any good? Did any blessing ever reach you?"* and he answers: *"No — by God, my Lord; I am with the people of the Fire"* (Muslim 2806) — and the least-tormented man in the Fire, told: *"If you possessed everything on earth, would you ransom yourself with it?"* — *"Yes"* — and the reply that ends the matter: *"I asked you for something less than that and easier, and you did not do it"* (Muslim 2805) — the single touch of the other estate unmakes every claim of the beloved life. Verse 39 is that hadith in grammar-form: *hiya al-maʾwā* — it is the home: and the word *mā* of *māʾ*, water, the very provision of verse 31 "from it He brought forth its water," has been inverted: the home whose element is the fire that never quenches, against the Garden whose rivers (Muḥammad 47:15 — *"its rivers of water unpolluted, rivers of milk whose taste does not change, rivers of wine delightful to the drinkers, rivers of honey clarified"*) are the *mā'* of this world's verse, perfected and never again rationed: the two homes, on the sūrah's own map, are the water's two futures, and the man of verses 37–39, the refuser of the Lesser, has been given, in exchange, the greater of the two eternities he never saw. The Makkan dawn-hearer, stepping out of the Fajr prayer into the cold, holding the warm cup before the sun's rise, stood, in that ordinary minute, inside verse 39's argument — every small comfort a referendum on the great one.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:40

> **As for one who fears standing before his Lord and forbids the soul from caprice,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse of the Two Labors**

*Wa-ammā man khāsha maqāma rabbihi wa-nahā al-nafsa ʿan al-hawā* — "and as for the one who feared the standing of his Lord, and forbade the soul its desire." Two acts, and the sūrah has built its whole middle argument to place them exactly here: the verse answers, term for term, the pair it answers in verses 37–38 — *man ṭaghā* (rebellion of intellect) against *man khāsha* (fear that is knowledge), and *āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* (the world's preference) against *nahā al-nafsa ʿan al-hawā* (the desire's restraint). The source's summary of al-Rāzī states the correspondence with precision: just as rebellion implies ignorance — for whoever knows God knows his own smallness — fear implies knowledge, proved from the Book: *"Only those fear God, among His servants, who have knowledge"* (35:28); and just as world-preference is the deed-love of the lower appetite, the forbidding of *hawā* is the deed-hatred of the saved — the two halves of the verse thus map the whole human religion onto its two fronts: the heart's view upward (Standing) and the soul's rein inward (desire), and the sūrah gives no third category, because in its arithmetic every person alive is composing one or the other sentence right now.

**Maqām: the Fear of a Place**

The first labor is named *khawf maqām rabbihi* — literally, "the fear of the *station* of his Lord," and the source preserves the double reading that classical grammar allows: (1) the Standing — the soul's being made to stand, in the wide expanse of verse 14, before the Lord: the fear of the audit, of account, of the face-to-face; this is how 55:46, the sūrah's own twin, is read by the majority — *"and for whoever feared the Standing of his Lord, two gardens"* — a verse al-Nāziʿāt 40–41 now quotes in structure and reward, the gardens and the refuge answering each other across the two chapters; or (2) the Station — God's own station of dominion and seeing, the fear that He is there whenever you are, the *iḥsān* of the famous hadith: *"to worship God as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you"* (al-Bukhārī 8; Muslim 8) — the classical authors loved this second reading at 55:46 because it locates the fear before the Day: the one who fears the *Standing* now will not have to fear it then. Both readings meet in the sūrah's psychology of accountability: a person who truly expects to be *there, seen, answered for*, is the person whose heart this verse is about, and the exegetes note the phrase's strange gentleness — God's name for the saved quality here is not *taqwā* generically but *khashya*: the reverence of a knower, the trembling of one who has read verse 34's *ṭāmmah* and now walks through the day with the Calamity kept, by deliberate memory, at the back of the mind — and, at the front, the provision-cluster (water, pasture, mountains) as mercy: fear and gratitude are, in this sūrah's construction, the same two-handed act.

**Nahā: the Forbidden Hunt**

The second labor, *wa-nahā al-nafsa ʿan al-hawā*, is where the sūrah names its psychology of struggle. *Nahā* (n-h-y: to forbid) is the verb of the shepherd who turns the animal from the forbidden field — the image the jurists of *al-ḥisba* loved, the market and the pasture needing a forbidding voice, and here the voice is a man's own, speaking to his *nafs*: the self that wants is addressed as an animal that must be stopped, which is the Qur'an's constant realism about appetite: desire is not to be abolished (the soul is *nafs*, breath-life, not a demon to kill) but *restricted* — forbidden *from* its *hawā*. And *hawā* is the word of the sūrah's hour: from h-w-y, "to fall down, to descend with the thing falling," so *hawāʾ* is the appetite's downward gravity, the desire whose name is already its vector — and the same root gives *hāwiya*, the Pit, one of the Fire's own names elsewhere in the Book (101:9: *"his mother is the Hāwiya"*). The Makkan ear, hearing *hawā* at verse 40 and *al-jaḥīm* at verse 39, caught the geometry the sūrah intends: follow the falling desire, and the fall is not punished with a hole; the desire *is* the hole — and the saved man's one achievement, in this verse, is that he forbade his own falling to be the whole of him. The hadith-tradition's own definition of the mujāhid belongs precisely here, sound in al-Tirmidhī's and Aḥmad's collections: *"the emigrant is the one who leaves what God forbade; and the mujāhid is the one who strives against his own self in God's obedience"* (al-Tirmidhī 1621, from Fudāla ibn ʿUbayd, graded ḥasan) — the war the sūrah installs, at the summit of its forty-one verses of argument, as the only war every believer can win.

**The Two Labors in the Life of the Believer**

Finally, what the verse *demands* is worth stating plainly for the reader who is not Pharaoh and not yet angel. The first labor — fearing the Standing — the tradition disciplines into the practice of *muḥāsaba*, the daily accounting: *"the keen-sighted is the one who takes his soul to account"* (al-Tirmidhī 2459), the evening audit that rehearses the Day's memory of verse 35 until the audit and the memory are the same act; and the second labor — forbidding the soul its *hawā* — the Sunnah narrows to its doable form: the Prophet's ﷺ definition in al-Bukhārī and Muslim of the *strong*: *"the strong one is not the wrestler; the strong one is the one who owns himself at the hour of anger"* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609) — that is, *naha* in the exact instant the *nafs* is about to take, the single act of restraint in which the whole sūrah's fear, and the whole sūrah's knowledge, become, if only once today, real. The classical ethics (al-Ghazālī in the *Iḥyāʾ* reads this very verse) draw the practical ladder: fear first disciplines by warning, the restraint becomes habit, and the habit, in the end, becomes the soul's taste — the *nafs* that was forbidden *ʿan al-hawā* arrives, by the sūrah's last promise, at its own *riḍā*: "O soul at peace, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing" (89:27–28) — verse 40's two labors, completed, are verse 41's home, and the man who feared the Standing, the Sūrah has sworn, will find, when he finally stands, that the Standing had been made, for him, not a court but a door.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:41

> **truly the Garden is the refuge.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Noun, Flipped: Reward as Homecoming**

*Fa-inna al-janna hiya al-maʾwā* — "then the Garden — it is the home." The verse is built on the pattern of its opposite (v. 39) to the letter — noun, copula-pronoun, article, one sentence — and the mirror is the sūrah's final teaching on the two abodes: judgment, on the Day of the *Ṭāmmah*, is not two verdicts but one verdict read twice; both clauses say *maʾwā*, home, because both men go, at the end, to the place they were already building by preference (38) and forbidding (40), and the Garden is therefore named not as gift alone but as recognition. The word *janna* itself keeps, in its root, the covering of a garden by its own density — *j-n-n*: the night in Arabic is *junnā* (the covered), a well-spring *jinān* (screened by trees), and the *majnūn* (the "covered," the madman of the Quraysh's slur, v. 51's echo) — and here, at the sūrah's peak, the root's gentleness surfaces: the saved man's home is a place *screened* — sheltered from the heat, the exposure, the *burūz* (36) in which the other is displayed forever: the Garden is what the covering night (v. 29: *aghṭasha laylahā*) promised, executed; the sky's one mercy of shadow, finalized into a dwelling whose shade (cf. 13:35: *"its shade perpetual"*, and 76:14: *"and its near-bowers bend down low"*) is the sūrah's own verse-29 darkness, purified of fear.

**Two Gardens, and the Sūrah's Twin in al-Raḥmān**

The source opens the pair 40–41 with its cross-reference to 55:46, and the twin-verse deserves to stand in full here: *"and for whoever fears the Standing of his Lord, two gardens"* — and the two sūrahs meet at the same joint: al-Raḥmān, having counted God's bounty, ends its inventory where al-Nāziʿāt ends its argument: the fearing heart's estate, in that sūrah, with its rivers of water unpolluted (55:50 — the same *mā'* of v. 31, now made permanent) and its two gardens — the green and the other — and the mufassirūn, al-Qurṭubī prominent, read our sūrah's singular *al-janna* against 55's dual *jannatān* as the usual compression: one Garden, many folds, the reward doubled for the doubled labor of verse 40 (the Standing feared *and* the desire forbidden), exactly as the two abodes in v. 25 were one seizure with two faces (this world's and the next). The pairing teaches, in the tradition's words, the balance of the whole sūrah's moral economy: the verse-37–39 path (rebel, prefer, Blaze) required no extra labor from its traveler — desire is gravity, falling is free — while the verse-40–41 path costs two deliberate acts and pays two gardens; and the Makkan dawn-hearer, stepping out of the Fajr prayer into the market's *matāʿ*, stood between the two equations every hour of his day, which is why his Prophet ﷺ kept reciting this sūrah in the half-light: the arithmetic of verse 41, the tradition says, is learned best at the hour when every appetite is still asleep and every fear is still honest.

**The Refuge the Fearful Had Already Reached**

The verse's quiet theology is its article: *al-maʾwā* — *the* home — the same exclusivity the Fire clause carried, and the commentators press what it costs to say it: the Garden is *the* refuge, not because the Fire is unreal, but because for the *khāshiʾ* — the man of v. 40 — the Garden was already his refuge in this life: the heart that fled to the Standing has been dwelling, for years, in what verse 39's men will only *find*, the way a man exiled in a foreign court is already at home in the letter from his own land, and the Day is the transport, not the tenancy. This is the tradition's deepest reading of *maʾwā*, and the ḥadīth-qudsī that stands over this verse in every tafsīr-majlis of the gardens states the same from the divine side: *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no heart of a human has conceived"* (al-Bukhārī 4780; Muslim 2844 — and the Muslim addition: *"if you wish, recite: 'no soul knows what is kept hidden for them of joy as a reward for what they used to do'"* (32:17)). The three negatives — no eye, no ear, no heart — are verse 41's own footnote: the *home* is named in the sūrah's ordinary nouns, Garden, refuge, and the article *al-*, because language for a place no language has visited must stop at the doorway; the makkan of the *muqarrabūn* is the letter *j-n-n* fulfilled: screened from the very possibility of comparison. And in that screening, the sūrah's argument ends where the *ʿibra* (v. 26) began: in that is a lesson — for whoever *fears*: the same verb, the same man, verse 26's condition standing, at the very summit of the chapter, in his own two-labor shape.

**Between Verse 41 and Verse 42: the Turn Back to the Questioners**

Finally, the placement: with 41, the sūrah has finished the architecture — oaths, Day, history, creation, the two verdicts — and one movement remains (42–46): the mockers' last question, "when is the Hour?" The transition is the chapter's most pedagogically complete: having described the *homes*, the sūrah now dissolves the obsession with *dates*, and the reader who has arrived here knows why: the man busy with verse 40's two labors does not ask verse 42's question — the question is always the deniers' substitute for the preparation, the calendar for the conscience — and the Garden, named *the* home in a verse of six words, has already out-answered, in the sūrah's balance, the whole snooping calendar of the scofflaws. What follows will teach the Prophet ﷺ — and every preacher since — the limits of his office in four verses of mercy: you are not their keeper; you are the warner; and the seeing of verse 46, when it arrives, will shrink the whole mockery, and the whole world, to one morning. But verse 41 pauses, as the sūrah's heart-pause: the Garden — *the* home; the saved man's end has no adjective left, and the sūrah, wisely, adds none.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:42

> **They ask thee about the Hour, "When shall it set in?"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Movement: From Verdicts to the Question That Was Never the Point**

*Yas'alūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna murāsāhā* — "they ask you about the Hour: when is its anchoring?" The sūrah's five movements (oaths, Day, Pharaoh, creation, verdicts) now turn, in five closing verses, to the one thing its audience actually said out loud: the Hour's *date*. The source records the frame (Āl, Q): this refers to the Makkan idolaters, and their asking was not inquiry but mockery — the question asked to wound, not to learn — and the verses that answer it (43–46) are the Qur'an's calmest and most complete theory of prophetic limits. The verb *yas'alūnaka*, present tense, makes it a habit: they *kept* asking, at every dawn, at every market encounter — a taunt the Prophet ﷺ carried, and the sūrah names it, here, once, so that it never needs naming again. And the answer's architecture, in the four verses that follow, is a triple closure: you have no business with it (43), its end belongs to God alone (44), your office is warner — not chronologer (45), and when they *see* it, the whole question of when will be a joke the past tells about the present (46).

**Murāsāhā: the Ship, the Anchor, and the Harbor of the Hour**

The noun the source's note unpacks — *murāsā* — is the anchoring-time, the hour a ship comes to its moorings in the harbor, from r-s-ʾ / *rasā*, to cast anchor; the Qur'an uses it in only one other place, in the question the polytheists actually posed to the Prophet ﷺ: *"They ask you about the Hour: when is its anchoring (mattā murāsāhā)? Say: the knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none but He will reveal it at its time; heavy has it become in the heavens and the earth; it will not come to you except suddenly"* (7:187). Two textures of the metaphor make the verse's point. First, the anchor is what ends motion: to ask "when does the Hour anchor?" is to ask when the ship of the world stops moving — and the sūrah's whole middle (night-darkened, day-brought-forth, earth-spread, provision-given: vv. 27–33) has described the world as a sea deliberately made for travel; the anchor-time is the end of the voyage whose sea they are using to trade. Second, as the source notes (Q), some of the classical readers found, in the nautical image, a warning of nearness: anchors are cast *within sight of the port*, and the idiom "when its anchoring?" spoken by a sūrah that has just sworn the Day's display of the Fire was itself the Makkan sea's final approach; the questioners, the implication runs, stand in the harbor and ask the hour of docking, the sound of their own mockery the only anchor-cries still in the air. And the sūrah's grammar lets the metaphor answer itself: a ship that will not miss the port needs no schedule — arrival is its nature, and the only real questions left are the cargo (v. 35's *mā saʿā*) and the harbor's owner (v. 44's *ilā rabbika muntahāhā*).

**"They Ask You" — and Whom the Question Really Addressed**

The closing section's rhetoric rests on a transfer the commentators mark: the mockers aimed their question at the Prophet ﷺ — "if you are truthful, when is this promise?" (cf. 10:68; 67:25: *"they say: when is this promise, if you are truthful?"*) — and the sūrah converts the shot into doctrine for everyone who asks. For the date of the Hour, the Book has already ruled, is not withheld to frustrate; it is withheld by design, because its certainty is sufficient and its schedule would be corrosive: a dated Day invites postponement ("there are still forty years"), while a dateless Day claims today. The exegetes set beside 7:187's *"it will not come to you except suddenly"* the Prophet's ﷺ own line on the point of all Makkan mockery — the hour is so near that the distance between his sent-being and the Hour is the distance of two fingers (al-Bukhārī 6503: *"I and the Hour have been sent like these two"*) — and the joke the sūrah quietly makes at verse 42 is that the men who demand a date are demanding, in the idiom of 7:187, the one datum whose disclosure would end the whole experiment: a Day on the calendar is a Day that can be managed, argued past, and finally arrived at unprepared, while a Day that only *anchors* when the Master of the harbor decides turns every man's ordinary noon into the last page of his book, which is precisely what the sūrah has been writing since verse 1, in the vocabulary of angels already standing at the bedside, the extraction already in progress, the Day already breaking, verse by verse, over the heads of the questioners, who have never once been late — only never early enough.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:43

> **What hast thou to do with mentioning it?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Retracts a Question**

*Fīmā anta min dhikrāhā* — "about what is your business with mentioning it?" The idiom *fīmā anta min* is the Arabic of total disengagement — "where do you stand in this affair? — nowhere" — and the source records its two classical senses, one address to the Prophet ﷺ and one to the mockers, both of them standing. First sense: God relieves His messenger of the burden of the question — you are not responsible for the Hour's date, so stop carrying their taunts as debts; stop, as it were, *wanting to know*: the report preserved from ʿĀʾisha, in the tafsīr-sources, gives this reading its pathos — the Prophet ﷺ, we are told, wished to know the Hour's moment, and the verse came: what have you to do with it? — the inquiry itself retracted with tenderness, not rebuke. Second sense: the questioners' own sneer is answered by the sūrah's dismissal of their terms — the mockery was that the Prophet *should not speak* of what he cannot date ("if you are truthful, when is this promise?"), and verse 43 concedes the premise *entirely* while collapsing the conclusion: you are right — his business is not the date; his business is verses 1–41, the anchor not the schedule, and the sneer, read correctly, condemns a man not sent to do what he now is blamed for not doing. The two senses meet in one rule for every later bearer of the message: the preacher is discharged, in the Book's own grammar, from the questions that are not his office, and the discharge is the sūrah's last mercy to *him* before its last warning to *them*.

**The Aptitude of the Unanswered**

What the verse legislates is an epistemology of mission. The Hour's date is one of the five "keys of the unseen" the Prophet ﷺ enumerated in the ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī (no. 4692, from Ibn ʿUmar): *"God has placed the knowledge of the Hour with Him alone; He sends down the rain and knows what is in the wombs; and no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow; and no soul knows in what land it will die"* — the verse cited (31:34) and its hadith's frame make verse 43's *dhikrāhā* precise: mentioning the Hour *as warning* is the office (v. 45 will confirm it); mentioning its *time* is not, because the time belongs to a list of realities God has reserved as *ghayb* by design — and a datum reserved is not a hole in the message but one of its load-bearing walls. The commentators press the social lesson at this verse: a community that knows the Hour's date would organize its virtue around the calendar (repentance scheduled, justice deferred, the hereafter budgeted by fiscal years), while a community that does not must organize it around the *hour*, which is where the sūrah, since verse 1 with its angels already pulling, has kept insisting the real life is: extraction is not scheduled, it is *ongoing*, and the mention the Prophet is *not* asked to make is therefore exactly the mention he is *always* making — the Day, now, in every dawn's recitation, without a date, without rest.

**The Verse's Cure for the Preacher's Three Diseases**

Applied to its office-holders, verse 43 names, in its four Arabic words, three sicknesses of the caller, and its cure. (1) The *curiosity* that consumes daʿwa in speculative calendars — the verse's *fīmā anta* is the divine scissors on the thread; the tradition's line for the cured preacher is the Prophet's ﷺ maxim of disentanglement: *"among the excellence of a man's Islam is his leaving what does not concern him"* (al-Tirmidhī 2317, graded ḥasan): a ḥadīth whose whole verse-application the mufassirūn draw at this verse. (2) The *over-responsibility* that reads every taunt as a failure — "they ask me for the date and I have none, therefore I am failing" — the verse lifts the burden: their question was never his assignment, and the sūrah's own ledger for the preacher is the one he keeps repeating: *"your duty is only to convey; upon Us is the reckoning"* (13:40; and 88:21–22: *"remind — you are only a reminder; you are not a taskmaster over them"*). (3) The *temptation of authority* — to answer what only God answers, and so to set oneself, in effect, as the Hour's keeper, which is the small, polite Pharaoh-sin of the pulpit; *fīmā anta* says: abdicate the office no one offered you. And the Makkan dawn-hearer — the imam of Juḥāt's congregation — learned all three cures in his own body at the hour when the world is softest: he rose from the Fajr of this sūrah disencumbered of the date, the taunt, and the throne, and went out to trade and to father and to pray the next prayer as one who mentions the Day and keeps, without flinching, entirely ignorant of its calendar, which is, on this sūrah's showing, the only way the Day is ever really mentioned.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:44

> **Unto thy Lord is [knowledge of] the end thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Relocates the Question**

*Ilā rabbika muntahāhā* — "to your Lord is its end." Two syntactic facts carry the verse. First, *ilā* — "to, toward": the Hour is not a corridor with doors at both ends; it is a trajectory whose terminus is not a date but a Person's authority: the *muntaḥā* (n-h-y, to reach an end beyond which there is no arrival) of the Hour is not its *when* but its *whose* — and the sūrah, by answering a question about time with a sentence about lordship, performs its whole chapter's argument in four words: the men who asked "when?" are told the affair ends where it began, with *rabbika* — your Lord — the same *rabb* Pharaoh stole (v. 24, "I am your lord") and the same *rabb* whose standing the saved man feared (v. 40): the Hour, the sūrah says, is not on a calendar; it is in a hand, and the hand, as the oaths swore (v. 5, "those who govern the affair"), is the one that governs. Second, *rabbika* — *your* Lord: the possessive is the makkan dawn-hearer's own comfort and his own indictment at once: the Hour that ends at the One you already worship is, for you, a homecoming; the One you mock with "when?" is, for the mockers, the very end they ask about — and in either reading the datum sought is *relocated*, never disclosed.

**Why the Knowledge Is Reserved: the Jurisprudence of a Closed File**

The verse's brevity hides a complete theology of the unseen, and the sūrah's twin, 7:187, states its clause-list: *"Say: its knowledge is only with my Lord; none but He will disclose it at its time; heavy has it become in the heavens and the earth; it will not come to you except suddenly"* — and the commentators unpack the four pieces exactly as verse 44 demands: knowledge with God *alone* (no angel proximate, no prophet informed — as in the ṣaḥīḥ report of the man who asked, "When is the Hour?" and received from the Prophet ﷺ not a date but the question that matters: *"What have you prepared for it?"* — the man answered, "The love of God and His Messenger," and he was told: *"You are with what you love"* (al-Bukhārī 6168; Muslim 2641): the reserved datum is turned, in that exchange, into the practical one: the closed file is a mercy because the open question is the one that saves); *none but He discloses it at its time* — revelation of the Hour is itself reserved to the Hour, the cover only lifted when lifting it costs nothing; *heavy in the heavens and earth* — the angels, who do not sin, and the cosmos, which never errs, both weigh with the burden of the unknown day: a thing heavy to the bearers of light is not a thing to be small-talked by the bearers of clay; and *it will not come except suddenly (baghtatan)* — the adverb is the sūrah's own answer to every timetable ever written on the Hour's wall: a dated event can be prepared for slowly, and a sudden one can only be prepared for *always*, which is the exact grammar of the whole chapter: the extraction of the soul (vv. 1–4), the quake and the follower (6–7), the single cry and the plain (13–14) are each, in their own verse, one *bagṭa* rehearsed, so that verse 44's *muntaḥā* reads, to the reciter of the Fajr of this sūrah, as the only sane eschatology: to live with the end unnumbered is to live every day at the harbor of 79:14, anchored or about to be, and the question "when?" dissolves into the only question the reserved knowledge was reserved to protect: *where am I standing when the end arrives — and its end is my Lord, so am I, too, standing before Him as one He knows?*

**The Limits of Gabriel: the Ḥadīth the Sūrah Itself Recalls**

That even the highest messenger is shut out of this datum is the sūrah's sharpest proof of its seriousness, and the tradition states it at the source's own citation: in the ḥadīth of Gabriel's visit — the sūrah of faith, preserved in al-Bukhārī (no. 50) and Muslim (no. 8), in which the angel comes in human form and asks the Prophet ﷺ about Islam, faith, iḥsān, and then: *"He said: when is the Hour? He said: the one asked does not know more about it than the asker."* — Gabriel, who came to teach, was himself the second party to this ignorance, and the formula *al-masʾūl laysa bi-aʿlam min al-sāʾil* is the ummah's charter of epistemic humility: the answer that closes a door teaches more than any answer that opens one, and the sūrah that quotes it (via the source's IK note) places it under verse 44 as the tradition's own tafsīr: if the trusted messenger of revelation says "we are even," the mockers' taunt — *if he does not know the date, his message is a guess* — has been answered from the side of the questioner, not the question: knowledge of *what the Hour means* is complete in every prophet's mouth, and knowledge of *when it arrives* was never part of the message's contract, which is why verse 45 will confirm the office that remains — *you are only a warner* — the office the whole sūrah has been staffing since verse 1.

**Ilā Rabbika: the End That Makes the Middle Bearable**

The verse's final mercy, and the one the commentators of the *maqāṣid* press, is the dative's direction: *ilā rabbika* — the end arrives *to your Lord*, and the sūrah's audience had watched, in the previous thirty verses, every other end vanish: Pharaoh's body into archive, the canopy and the bed into judgment, the provision into the audit; and now the sūrah says: all of it terminates, not into nothing, but into *Him* — the end of the affair is His, the reckoning is His, the homes (39–41) are dispensed by the One to whom the affair returns — the same idiom as the Book's great calm sentence: *"Indeed, to your Lord is the end (inna ilā rabbika al-muntahā)"* (53:42), the verse the reciters pair with ours, where the *muntahā* governs not only the Hour but every sight, every deed, every "nor does a soul know what is concealed before it": all roads end at the One who made them, and the sūrah, which began with angels wrenching the dying out of the world, ends its argument about time with the sentence that makes the wrenching meaningful: every exit was a *muntaḥā*, an arrival at Him, and the Day's date — the one date He kept — is simply the date of the last arrival, after which no one asks "when?" anymore, because the questioners will all have answered it with their standing, and the Standing (v. 40) will have become, for each of them, a home.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:45

> **Thou art but a warner for whosoever fears it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Office, in Two Words, Against the Whole Idolatry of Charisma**

*Innamā anta mundhiru man yakhshāhā* — "you are only a warner — of whoever fears it." The *innamā* is the grammar of limitation, and the sūrah uses it here as the Book uses it at its most decisive moments (the same construction that defines the Prophet's message in 12:108, *"I call you to God upon clear sight, I and whoever follows me"*), and the two words it joins — *mundhir* and *yakhshā* — weld the whole sūrah's psychology shut at both ends: the warner gives the alarm; the fearful one receives it; between the two, nothing else is required or permitted — no power to compel, no knowledge of the unseen (cf. the source's citations 38:69–70: *"Say: I am no innovation among the messengers, and I do not know what will be done with me or with you; I only follow what is revealed to me, and I am nothing but a clear warner"* (46:9), and *"Say: I do not tell you I hold the treasures of God, nor that I know the unseen"* (6:50)). The source records the Makkan frame (Āl): the people who asked "when?" were the same who demanded the Prophet be a wonder-worker, a treasurer, a date-teller — the office this verse defines refuses all of it at once: he is sent *to warn*, and the warning's *success* is defined — not by mass conversion, not by spectacular proof, not by the answer to verse 42's taunt — but by the single heart that *fears*: whoever is reached into khashya, the warner has "succeeded" by the only standard the sūrah recognizes, and the classical preachers of the *sunan* of daʿwa (the school of Ibn al-Qayyim's *Ṭuruq al-ḥikma*) read this verse as the charter of method: the warner warns *the condition he was given*, and leaves the result to the One whose the end is (v. 44) — which is the verse's second, personal mercy: a preacher crushed by his convert-count is, by this verse, not being judged by the court he is trying himself in.

**Mundhir: the Warner Whose Fire Alarm Is a Love**

The noun *mundhir* derives from n-dhr, the root of the warning that saves by alarming, and the Qur'an's usage supplies the sūrah's own gloss: the Hour is named in 79:14's twin as *al-ṭāmmah al-kubrā* whose arrival the warner cries, and the Book's other word for his errand — *bashīr* (bearer of glad tidings) — is here deliberately withheld to define his *limits*, not to deny his *hope*: the same men in the same Makkan season heard, from the same mouth, both halves: *"Indeed, We have sent you with the truth, a bearer of glad tidings and a warner, and there is no community but that there passes within it a warner"* (35:24) — the *wa-* of that twin-verse the exegetes note is not ornamental: the glad tidings and the warning are one delivery, and the *fearing one* of our verse is the *mubashshar* of the other, for khashya, in the sūrah's own economy (v. 40), is the door through which the Garden opens: the warner who sounds the alarm is, to whoever hears it right, the man bringing good news of the fire escape, and the source's whole sūrah — sworn on angels who draw the soul gently *and* violently (vv. 1–2) — is that single delivery, performed at dawn, in the half-light of a town that called its warner a sorcerer because a warner is always, to the asleep, an intruder.

**Li-man yakhshāhā: the Sūrah's Last Word About Its Audience**

The restriction *man yakhshāhā* — "whoever fears it" — closes the circle the sūrah opened at verse 8 (hearts trembling) and verse 9 (eyes humbled), and its exclusivity teaches the doctrine the commentators call *taʿlīl al-ḥukm bi-l-waṣf*: the warning is, in its effect, for the fearing, because the ear of the fearless is, by definition, closed — *"you cannot make the dead hear, nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and flee"* (27:80; and 30:52: *"you cannot guide the blind from their error"*). Yet the mufassirūn insist the phrase is *descriptive, not restrictive* in the order of preaching: the Prophet ﷺ warned *everyone* — Pharaoh first among the dead and Quraysh last among the living, the sūrah itself a record of both — and the *man yakhshāhā* tells the warner where his labor will *land*, not whom he may *speak to*; the two halves of this distinction, the tradition's preachers say, guard the pulpit from its two chronic diseases: the despair that stops speaking because few fear, and the rage that keeps speaking *at* the unafraid as though alarm were conversion. What *yakhshā* is — the reverence of knowledge (35:28, cited by the source at v. 40) — is therefore, at this verse, the very seed the warner plants by the act of warning: fear of the Hour is not a prerequisite the hearer must bring but the effect the sūrah itself, recited and *heard as the Day's approach* (v. 46, the next verse), grows in the soil it is poured on — and the Makkan dawn-hearer, finishing the last verse of the surah in the first light, stood, by the verse's own promise, in the state the verse names: a person with khashya in his chest, warned, fearing, and therefore — verses 40–41 already sealed — a person home.

**"But a Warner" — and the Ummah's Long Obedience to the Limits**

One historical footnote completes the verse: the ummah took it at its word, and the *sīra*-and-tafsīr tradition preserves the practice it licensed — when the Prophet ﷺ, in the sound ḥadīth of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, was asked "when is the Hour," he replied with the closed door and the open one (6168, cited above), and when the taunt came back at him — "you claim a message, but you do not know what will be done with you or with us" — the revelation's charter had already answered it (46:9: "I do not know what will be done with me or with you; I only follow what is revealed to me"), and that charter was the verse the preachers have preached since, even from the final pulpit of the one who delivered it: *"O God, have I conveyed?" — and the people answered: "Yes." He said: "O God, bear witness"* (al-Bukhārī, Book of Knowledge; Muslim 1673) — and the sūrah, whose warner ended his career in exactly that sentence, leaves verse 45, for all later readers, as the charter of the finite messenger in an infinite affair: convey, alarm, hope for the fearing, leave the rest — the rest was always *ilā rabbika*, and the rest is, the next and final verse will say, already on its way at the speed of a blink, arriving in the form of the shortest possible answer to the longest possible mockery: when they see it, they will laugh at the only joke the world ever took seriously, and the joke will be its own duration.

## Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt 79:46

> **The Day they see it, it will be as if they had tarried but an evening or the morning thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Verse: the World Measured by Its Own Disappearance**

*Ka-annahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbashū illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā* — "the Day they see it, they will be as though they had tarried only an evening or its morning." The sūrah's forty-six verses end, as they began, in movement: the first verses wrenched the soul out at the hour of death; this final verse pulls the whole of world-history out at the hour of sight, and the instrument of the pulling is the verse's own small verb: *yalbashū* — to *tarried*, from l-b-th, the root of dwelling and delay, used of a guest's stay: the world, the sūrah's last word says, was a stay, and the Day is the departure in which the stay is *measured*. The measure chosen is the shortest pair the Arabic of the sūrah has used before in this chapter: *ʿashiyya* — one evening — and *ḍuḥāhā* — its morning, the same *ḍuḥā* of verse 29's "He brought forth its day-brightness" and, in the source's note (IK), the very word some read at v. 46 as the sign of worldly life itself: *the whole of the world, in the eyes of its people when they see the Hereafter, is the morning that ends the day of their life* — and the pairing does the arithmetic: not a year, not a generation — an evening and a morning, or a morning, the two intervals the Bedouin knows as the *whole* of a halt between two pastures, a camp struck before the dew dries. The source records the two classical applications: most say the verse speaks of the grave's wait — between death and resurrection the souls will have "tarried" no time at all (IK, Q, Ṭ; cf. 2:259's sleeper of a hundred years who was told *"but as a day or part of a day"*); and the others say it shrinks the *world* itself, that the deniers' long mockery about "decayed bones" (v. 11) will, in the hour of seeing, sound as the joke of a man who slept through one afternoon. The two readings are one: all time before the Day is a tarrying, and the Day is the waking, and the waking does not remember its sleep as long.

**"The Day They See It": Why Sight, Not Date, Dissolves the Question**

The verse's condition is the sūrah's final answer to the men of verse 42: they asked *when*, and the sūrah has said the knowledge is God's (44), the office is warning (45), and here, at the close, it explains *why the question will not survive being answered*: *yawma yarawnahā* — the day *they see it*. Seeing is the end of questions about the unseen, and every datum the mockers demanded — the date, the provenance, the staff-and-hand evidence (v. 20's rejected *kubrā*) — will be, that Day, delivered all at once in the single mode that converts: sight. The Qur'an's own twin-scenes sharpen it: *"and they will see the punishment as a certainty, and on that Day it will be said: where are those you used to associate?"* (cf. 30:12–14's division of the companies), and the sūrah's earlier *burūz* of the Fire (v. 36: *displayed for one who sees*) meets its end here in the general *ruʾya*: what was displayed to the seer becomes, on that Day, the only sight everyone has, and the measuring of the world to an evening is, precisely, the *effect* of the seeing — the thing that makes the world short is not the Day's terror but the Day's *clarity*: under full light, the near things recede, and the world, the nearest thing, vanishes first, like a lamp at sunrise whose small life is swallowed by the larger. The psychology is exact, and the tradition knows it from the other side of the same hadith literature: the sleeper wakes and the dream that felt an age long is gone in the act of opening the eyes — the Prophet's ﷺ own image for exactly this arithmetic, in Muslim's ṣaḥīḥ from al-Miqdād ibn Maʿdikarib: *"what remains of this world in comparison with the next is nothing like what a finger dipped in the sea brings back when it is withdrawn"* — and beside it stands his ﷺ word to ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar: *"be in the world as though you were a stranger or a man passing along a road"* (al-Bukhārī 6416) — verse 46 is that ḥadīth written in the future perfect: on the Day they see it, every stranger will discover he had, all along, been a man walking.

**An Evening or Its Morning: the Mercy of a Short Story**

Read as the sūrah's last word to the *fearing* hearer — the one the whole chapter has been building toward, the *man khāsha maqāma rabbihi* (v. 40) — the verse changes register and becomes, of all the Makkan verses, one of the gentlest: for the believer, the evening-and-morning measure is not only the world's brevity but the *end of the trial*: a bad night of this life, met with patience and khashya, will, in the seeing, be *just that* — one evening — and the tradition's twin experiments: the man from the people of Paradise dipped a single time into the Fire and asked whether he had *ever* seen any blessing — "No, by God, my Lord" — and the least-tormented man in the Fire offered the whole world to ransom himself and reminded that he was once asked for less than that (Muslim 2806; al-Bukhārī 6561) — the single-dip reports are verse 46 in experiment-form: the other estate compresses *this* estate to nothing, which is why the sūrah ends not by frightening its hearer past endurance but by handing him the correct proportion of everything: the world is one evening; stand in it like a man who will see his Lord at dawn, and the night, whichever night it is, will not outlast the morning. And the verse's ring with the sūrah's first scene is now complete: the *nāziʿāt* that wrench the soul at its last evening (v. 1) and the *nāshiṭāt* that draw it gently at its first morning there (v. 2) were, all along, verses about *this* one — the tarrying measured in one pull from the evening of death to the morning of the plain (v. 14), and the sūrah's forty-six verses, at their end, have said nothing else: the extraction is short, the tarrying is shorter, and the abode — the refuge, the *maʾwā* (39, 41) — is the only datum that does not shrink, which is why the one who feared the Standing walks through the evening of this world the way the careful driver runs a dangerous road at night — the sūrah's own taqwā-idiom, the source's governing image: awareness, not panic; hands steady, eyes on the wide path — because the road is one evening long, and the house at its end is the Garden, and the Garden is *the* home, and the warner has conveyed, and the Lord of the end is He who began the making — and the reciter of Juḥāt, finishing the sūrah as the true morning broke, was left, like every hearer since, with the whole of verse 46's message in one lived second: the night was never the story; the morning is coming, and then the morning *is* — forever, for whoever feared the Standing — and the chapter, whose first word was the wrestlers and whose last was an evening and its morning, closes the circle of the sūrah's aim: the tarrying is short; the home is real; the affair ends at your Lord; so arrive.
