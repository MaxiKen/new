# Sūrah Quraysh (Chapter 106) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter That Names a People**

Sūrah Quraysh is among the shortest chapters of the Qur'an — four verses, a handful of clauses, a single argument that can be recited in a breath — and yet it addresses one of the most consequential human communities in sacred history by name. It is a Makkan sūrah, revealed in the period when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was still calling his own tribe from within their city, before the migration to Madinah, and it takes its title from the opening word after the connecting particle: *Quraysh*, the tribe of the Prophet ﷺ, the custodians of the Kaʿbah, the merchants whose caravans moved between Yemen and Syria, and the people to whom this revelation was first spoken as both reminder and summons.

The classical scholars counted it among the early Makkan revelations. It follows Sūrah al-Fīl (105) in the written muṣḥaf, and that placement is not an accident of later arrangement. The two chapters form, in the judgment of a wide stream of commentators, a single arc of meaning: al-Fīl recounts how God destroyed the army of the elephant that marched on Makkah in the year of the Prophet's ﷺ birth; Quraysh then turns to the survivors of that protection and asks them, in effect, what they have done with the safety they were given. Al-Fīl is the act; Quraysh is the invoice. Protection is named; then gratitude is demanded.

**Why Two Sūrahs Were Recited as One**

The relationship between 105 and 106 is so close that some of the earliest written witnesses treated them as a unit. According to several commentators, the manuscript of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb (d. 22/642 or 30/650) — the Companion whom the Prophet ﷺ named among the foremost reciters of the Qur'an — had al-Fīl and Quraysh together as one sūrah. Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī (d. 852/1448), in *Fatḥ al-Bārī*, regarded Ubayy's version as evidence that this pairing reflects a genuine early reading of the two as a continuous recitation. Al-Zamakhsharī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī transmit that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb (d. 23/644) recited the two sūrahs as one when leading the prayer. The grammatical hinge that makes such recitation feel natural is the opening *lām* of 106:1 — *li-īlāfi Quraysh* — which many linguists read as a *lām* of causation attached to the preceding narrative: God did what He did to the masters of the elephant *so that* the Quraysh might have their secure passage.

Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373) and others argue, with equal firmness, that although the two sūrahs are connected in meaning they are to be considered separate chapters. The ʿUthmānic muṣḥaf separates them with a basmalah; the community has prayed them as two; and the Prophet ﷺ is not reported to have treated them as a single sūrah in the way he treated, for example, the two refuge-sūrahs as a pair of distinct units. The responsible conclusion is therefore the one the tradition itself reached: they are two sūrahs, and they are one argument. Distinction of recitation does not cancel unity of sense. A reader who finishes 105 and does not begin 106 has heard the miracle and missed the moral.

**Four Verses, One Proof**

The architecture of the sūrah is as tight as a legal syllogism. Verses 1 and 2 name the blessing: the Quraysh were given *īlāf* — a word that will require a full verse's attention — meaning the accustomed, protected, habitual security of their winter and summer trade. Verse 3 draws the only honest conclusion from that blessing: *so let them worship the Lord of this House*. Verse 4 then identifies that Lord by two acts that no idol, no ancestor, and no market force can claim: He is the One *who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear*. The first two verses are the evidence. The third is the verdict. The fourth is the identification of the Judge.

The source commentary states the theme with accuracy: the first two verses remind the Quraysh of the blessing they have received in God's having preserved them and provided them with means for prosperity; verses 3 and 4 provide the central teaching, that one should worship God, since all prosperity and protection come from Him. What follows in this expansion is not a restatement of that summary but a working-out of its inner parts: the language, the history, the theology, the psychology of a people who had been given everything a desert city could want — food, safety, prestige, a sacred House — and who were being asked, in four verses, to stop thanking the wrong party.

**A Tribe Chosen for a House, Not a House for a Tribe**

The Quraysh were not addressed because they were the Prophet's relatives, though they were. They were addressed because they sat at the intersection of two gifts that, taken together, constitute the material basis of any civilization: a sanctuary and a market. The sanctuary was the Kaʿbah, rebuilt by Abraham and Ishmael, standing in a valley without agriculture, as Abraham himself had confessed in his prayer:

> *\"Our Lord, I have settled some of my descendants in an uncultivated valley near Your sacred House, our Lord, that they may establish prayer. So make hearts among the people incline toward them, and provide for them from the fruits, that they may be grateful.\"* (Qur'an 14:37)

The market was the network of winter and summer caravans that answered that prayer in history: hearts did incline toward Makkah, and fruits did arrive, not from Makkan soil but from Yemen and Syria, carried by Qurayshī merchants under treaties their grandfathers had secured. Sūrah Quraysh is the divine commentary on Abraham's prayer come true — and the warning that a prayer answered can still be a prayer betrayed, if the children of the blessed man thank the caravan instead of the One who sent the caravan.

The sūrah therefore belongs to a family of Qur'anic texts that convert lived privilege into theological evidence. It stands beside 29:67, which asks whether the Makkans have not noticed that they dwell in a secure sanctuary while people are snatched away all around them; beside 16:112–113, which sets forth the parable of a town that had security and abundant provision and then tasted the garment of hunger and fear for its ingratitude; beside 95:1–3, which swears by the fig, the olive, Mount Sinai, and *this secure city*; and beside 2:126, Abraham's request that Makkah be made a city of security and provision. Sūrah Quraysh is the shortest member of that family and, in some ways, the most pointed: it does not tell a story, legislate a rite, or unfold a cosmology. It simply looks a prosperous people in the face and says: you know who fed you. Worship Him.

**The Prophet's Own Tribe as the First Audience**

There is a particular sting, and a particular mercy, in the fact that this address is aimed at Quraysh. The Prophet ﷺ was one of them. Muslim records from Wāthilah ibn al-Asqaʿ:

> *\"God chose Ishmael from the children of Abraham, and He chose Banū Kinānah from the children of Ishmael, and He chose Quraysh from Banū Kinānah, and He chose Banū Hāshim from Quraysh, and He chose me from Banū Hāshim.\"* (Muslim 2276)

Election here is not racial theology. It is historical placement. God placed His final Messenger inside the tribe that already held the House, already ran the trade, already spoke the dialect in which the Qur'an would be inimitable, and already enjoyed the two blessings this sūrah names. The call to worship in verse 3 is therefore not an invitation issued to strangers. It is a family accounting. The people who should have been the first to recognize the Lord of the House were the people who lived in its shadow, ate from its pilgrimage economy, and slept inside the security that the Year of the Elephant had publicly demonstrated. That they delayed, resisted, and in many cases persecuted the Messenger ﷺ who recited this sūrah to them is the human plot against which the four verses stand as both evidence and warning.

What follows treats each of the four verses as an independent discourse, in the order of the sūrah, without compressing 106:1 with 106:2 or 106:3 with 106:4, even though the source commentary groups them. Each verse is given its own language, its own scholarly conversation, its own reasoning, and its own bearing on a life that still, in every city, depends on food and safety and still, in every heart, is tempted to thank the supply chain instead of the Supplier.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:1

> **For the secure passage of the Quraysh,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lām That Ties Two Histories Together**

The sūrah does not begin with a name of God, a command, or a narrative. It begins with a preposition. *Li-īlāfi Quraysh* — "For the *īlāf* of Quraysh." The Arabic *lām* that opens the chapter has been, from the first generations, one of the small hinges on which large meanings turn. Al-Ṭabarī gathers the early readings. One group of grammarians and commentators treated this *lām* as a *lām* of wonder (*taʿajjub*): "Marvel at the *īlāf* of Quraysh!" — as though the verse were pointing at a blessing so ordinary to its recipients that only an outsider's astonishment could restore its scale. Another group connected it to an implied verb of gratitude or observance: "Give thanks for," or "Remain in," the *īlāf* of Quraysh. A third, and in some ways the most architecturally satisfying, read it as a *lām* of causation attached to the preceding sūrah: God destroyed the army of the elephant *for the sake of* the Quraysh's accustomed security — so that their *īlāf* might continue, so that the House might remain, so that the Messenger ﷺ who would be born in that year might grow up in a city still standing.

Al-Zamakhsharī, who was a master of the language before he was a commentator of the Book, leaned into this last reading: the two sūrahs are so joined that the first supplies the act and the second supplies the purpose. Al-Rāzī, always alert to the theology inside grammar, noted that if the *lām* is causative, then the destruction of Abrahah is not a local miracle for a local shrine; it is a means ordered toward a people's ongoing life. Ibn Kathīr, while insisting that the two chapters remain two, does not deny the semantic hinge. The upshot for a careful reader is this: the first word of Sūrah Quraysh is already an argument about how history works. Empires do not fall at the edge of a desert sanctuary by accident. Birds do not pelt an army with baked clay so that a tribe may congratulate itself. If 105 happened, 106:1 says, it happened *for* something — and that something is now being named.

**Īlāf: Habit, Covenant, and the Gift of Continuity**

*Īlāf* is the keyword of the sūrah, and it is a word English can only approximate. The root is *ʾ-l-f*, the same root that gives *alifa*, "to grow accustomed to, to become familiar with, to take to habitually," and *allafa*, "to bring together, to unite, to compose into a whole." From this root comes *ulfa*, affectionate familiarity, and *taʾlīf*, the composing of a book or the reconciling of hearts. The Qur'an uses a verb from this family in one of its most politically charged verses, describing how God joined the hearts of the Madinan Helpers and the Makkan Emigrants:

> *\"And He brought their hearts together (allafa bayna qulūbihim). If you had spent all that is in the earth, you could not have brought their hearts together, but God brought them together.\"* (Qur'an 8:63)

*Īlāf*, then, sits at the meeting point of three meanings, all of which the commentators have heard in this verse. First, it is *familiarity*: the Quraysh had grown used to a pattern of life — departure, trade, return, profit — until the pattern felt like nature rather than gift. Second, it is *covenanted security*: the historians and the tafsīr tradition report that *īlāf* and the related word *ilf* were used as names for the grants of safe-conduct that the Quraysh obtained from the surrounding powers. Third, it is *unification*: the tribe itself had been gathered, after periods of dispersion, into a people whose internal agreements and external treaties made long-distance commerce possible. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both note this range. The translation "secure passage" is therefore not wrong; it is a narrowing of a richer noun. *Īlāf* is the whole condition in which a people move through a dangerous world as if the road belonged to them.

That condition is easy to miss precisely because it is habitual. A man who has taken the same winter road for twenty years no longer feels the road as a decision; he feels it as weather. A tribe that has held a sanctuary for generations no longer feels the sanctuary as a miracle; they feel it as real estate. The verse begins by naming the very thing that familiarity conceals. Before it tells the Quraysh what to do, it tells them what they have. The logic is the logic of all gratitude: you cannot thank what you have not noticed, and you will not notice what you have grown used to, unless someone breaks the spell of custom by calling the custom by its name.

**Who Were Quraysh, and Why Does God Address Them by Name?**

*Quraysh* is not a vague ethnic label. In the usage of the Arabs it designated a specific tribal confederation descending, in the most common genealogy, from Fihr ibn Mālik — called Quraysh — or, in another reckoning, from al-Naḍr ibn Kinānah. The name itself was explained in several ways: from *taqarrush*, gathering and trading, because they were a people of commerce; from a sea creature of strength, because they were hardy; from *taqrīsh*, investigation, because they were a people who looked into the affairs of the pilgrims and the markets. Whatever the etymology, by the sixth century of the Common Era they were the leading tribe of Makkah, holding the offices that surrounded the Kaʿbah: the *sidānah* and *ḥijābah* (custodianship and guardianship of the House), the *siqāyah* (provision of water to pilgrims), and the *rifādah* (provision of food to pilgrims). They were, in other words, a religious aristocracy whose wealth and whose piety-of-custom were the same fact.

The Qur'an names them here not to flatter them and not, in the first instance, to condemn them. It names them because a blessing that is not assigned cannot be claimed, and a duty that is not addressed cannot be refused. General truths about "people" allow every listener to assume that someone else is meant. A proper name closes that exit. When God says *Quraysh*, every man of that tribe who heard the recitation knew that the subject of the sentence was not humanity in the abstract. It was his caravan, his treaty, his House, his dinner. Ibn ʿAbbās, the Companion most associated with the interpretation of the Qur'an, and the generations who learned from him, treated this naming as part of the sūrah's force: it is a summons served on a particular door.

There is a further precision. The Prophet ﷺ is of Quraysh, and the Qur'an elsewhere makes that lineage a proof rather than a privilege — the Messenger is *from among themselves*, so that they cannot say the warning came from a stranger:

> *\"There has certainly come to you a Messenger from among yourselves. Grievous to him is what you suffer; he is concerned over you, and to the believers kind and merciful.\"* (Qur'an 9:128)

Sūrah Quraysh, revealed earlier, prepares that later description. The tribe is named so that, when the Messenger ﷺ stands up from among them, no one can pretend that God had not already been speaking to the household.

**The Grants of Security and the Map Around Makkah**

The source commentary records the historical content that the word *īlāf* gathered to itself. The Quraysh reportedly received grants of security from the surrounding powers: Byzantium to the north, Persia to the northeast, Abyssinia to the west across the sea, and Yemen to the south. In a peninsula without a central state, without a police force on the roads, and without a law of nations, such grants were the difference between a merchant and a corpse. Bedouin raiding was not a crime in the modern sense; it was a season. Tribes intercepted caravans as a form of livelihood. The Quraysh moved through that ecology with a protection that other traders did not enjoy, and the commentators, following Ibn Kathīr among others, connect that protection to their status as keepers of the Kaʿbah. Other tribes granted them safe passage because to molest the people of the House was to molest the House, and to molest the House was to offend the religious sentiment of the whole peninsula — a sentiment older than Islam, older than the idols that had been installed in the Kaʿbah, reaching back to the memory of Abraham.

The verse, read against that background, is doing something the Quraysh's own self-understanding refused to do. They explained their safety as the fruit of their prestige, their treaties, their diplomacy, their descent. The verse explains their safety as *īlāf* — a condition given, not a condition earned. Treaties are real; prestige is real; descent is real. The Qur'an does not deny secondary causes. It relocates them. Hāshim ibn ʿAbd Manāf may have secured the Syrian covenant; ʿAbd Shams may have dealt with Abyssinia; Nawfal with Persia; al-Muṭṭalib with Yemen — the reports of the four brothers and their four directions are part of the historical memory the commentators preserve. But a covenant is only as strong as the One who keeps the road open after the ink is dry. The Year of the Elephant had already shown what happens to a well-planned expedition when the Lord of the House decides otherwise. Verse 1 invites the Quraysh to read their ordinary Tuesdays in the light of that extraordinary year.

**Familiarity as the Enemy of Gratitude**

The psychology of the verse is as important as its history. Human beings do not chiefly fail in gratitude because they deny that gifts exist. They fail because gifts that arrive on schedule stop feeling like gifts. The Quraysh's *īlāf* was scheduled: winter, summer, departure, return. A scheduled mercy is the mercy most likely to be reclassified as entitlement. This is why the Qur'an so often uses the verb *kafara* — to cover, to conceal, to be ungrateful — of people who are not theoretical atheists but practical forgetters. They have not reasoned God out of the universe; they have simply stopped seeing Him in the universe they have gotten used to.

An analogy makes the mechanism plain. A man who narrowly avoids a collision on a dangerous mountain road thanks God with his whole body; his hands are still shaking. The same man, three years later, driving that road every week, no longer thanks anyone. The road has not become safer. He has become less awake. Taqwā, in the Qur'anic sense, is in part the refusal to let repetition anesthetize the gift. It is the inner equivalent of driving a dangerous path with both hands on the wheel — not because one is terrified, but because one has not granted oneself the fiction that the cliff has moved. Sūrah Quraysh 106:1 is an alarm against that fiction. It says to a tribe of expert travelers: you have taken this road so often that you think it is yours. It is not. It is *īlāf*. It is a familiarity you were permitted.

**The Logic of Naming a People in Revelation**

Why should revelation name a tribe at all? The Qur'an more often addresses "mankind," "the believers," "the Children of Israel," "those who disbelieve." A proper name this specific, attached to a living commercial community, has a double function that later readers — who are not Quraysh — must not miss.

For the first audience, the name is a summons, as already noted. For every later audience, the name is a mirror. The Quraysh are not in the Qur'an as a closed historical file. They are in the Qur'an as the type of a people who possess sanctuary, trade, reputation, and a sacred center, and who are therefore in more danger, not less, of missing the Giver. Every later city that lives off a shrine, a port, a capital, a university, a currency, or a tourist season is living inside this verse's pattern. The particulars change; the structure does not. There is a House — some center that gathers people and wealth. There is *īlāf* — a customary security that makes the gathering possible. There is a temptation to explain the custom by the tribe's own cleverness. And there is a four-verse sūrah that will not accept that explanation.

Ibn Taymiyyah's broader principle is useful here, though he is not writing a line-by-line commentary on this chapter in the way al-Ṭabarī is: acknowledged *rubūbiyyah* (God's lordship in creating, providing, and protecting) is the premise from which *ulūhiyyah* (God's exclusive right to worship) follows. Verse 1 is the premise being laid down. It does not yet say "worship." It says, in effect, "You have been provided for and protected." The command will come in verse 3, and it will be valid only because verses 1 and 2 are true. A religion that commanded worship without first pointing at food and safety would be commanding in the dark. This verse turns on the light.

**What a Later Believer Does with a Verse Addressed to Someone Else**

The practical question for a reciter who is not a sixth-century Makkan merchant is not "How do I become Quraysh?" It is "Where is my *īlāf*?" Every life has one. It may be a passport that lets a person cross borders other people die trying to cross. It may be a salary that arrives on the same day each month. It may be a neighborhood whose doors can be left unlocked, or a country whose skies are not contested, or a body that has not yet failed, or a language in which contracts are honored. These are not small things. They are the conditions under which a human being plans a winter and a summer. The verse trains the reciter to find those conditions and to rename them. Not "my luck." Not "my grind." Not "the system, for all its faults." *Īlāf* — a permitted familiarity with a dangerous world.

Once the renaming has happened, the rest of the sūrah can land. Without it, verses 3 and 4 will sound like a generic call to piety, detachable from any real economy. With it, they sound like what they are: the only adequate response to the fact that one's life is, at bottom, a protected journey one did not invent.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:2

> **their secure passage in the journey of winter and of summer;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Verse Repeats the Word**

The second verse does not introduce a new noun. It repeats *īlāf* and then specifies it: *īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayf* — "their *īlāf*, the journey of the winter and of the summer." The grammarians discussed the relationship between the two verses with care. Some read verse 2 as a substitution (*badal*) for verse 1, unpacking the first *īlāf* by naming its content. Some read it as an explanatory apposition: the *īlāf* of Quraysh *is* their two seasonal journeys. Some treated the second occurrence as a *tawkīd*, a confirmation, the way Arabic restates a word when the speaker will not let the hearer slide past it. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī both dwell on this restatement. It is not padding. Verse 1 named the condition; verse 2 names the calendar. Blessing is thereby moved from the realm of mood into the realm of dates.

This is a characteristically Qur'anic move. The Book rarely leaves a favor in the abstract. It points at nights, days, rains, fruits, cattle, ships, spouses, children, the alternation of sun and moon. Gratitude, in this pedagogy, is not a feeling one tries to generate. It is a recognition one is taught to attach to particular, datable events. "Winter" and "summer" are such events. Every Qurayshī child knew them. The verse takes the two most ordinary words in a merchant's year and makes them theological.

**Two Seasons, Two Roads, One Provider**

The historical content is well attested in the commentary tradition. In the winter the Quraysh traveled south to Yemen. Yemen was warmer in those months, and it was the receiving-point for spices, incense, leather, and other commodities coming from India and the African coasts. In the summer they traveled north to Syria (al-Shām), where the climate was cooler, where agricultural produce — grain, oil, wine, cloth — could be bought, and where the markets of Byzantine provincial life rewarded a desert caravan that arrived intact. The two journeys were not tourism. They were the metabolic cycle of a city that could not feed itself.

Makkah stood in what Abraham had called "an uncultivated valley" (14:37). There was no river. There was no reliable tillage. There was a well — Zamzam — whose story the tradition traces to Hagar and Ishmael, and there was a House that drew pilgrims, and there was this double movement of camels. Take away the winter road or the summer road and the city starves, or it becomes a shrine without a people, or it becomes a people without a shrine. Ibn Kathīr, among others, emphasizes that the respect the Quraysh received as keepers of the Kaʿbah compelled other tribes to grant them safe passage and thus contributed to their wealth. The religious office and the commercial office were not two careers. They were one fact with two faces.

The verse therefore refuses a split that later secular habits of mind take for granted: the split between "the sacred" and "the economy." The Quraysh's sacredness *was* their economy, and their economy *was* the form their sacredness took in the world. When the verse names the winter and summer journeys as *īlāf*, it is not changing the subject from religion to trade. It is identifying trade as the medium in which God's care for this people had become visible. A theology that cannot find God in a caravan will not find Him in a sanctuary either, because the same Lord runs both.

**The Barren Valley That Became a Market of Nations**

Pause on the geographical absurdity, because the verse's argument depends on it. Cities that become trade capitals usually have a reason a geographer can point to: a harbor, a river-crossing, a grain hinterland, a mine, a mountain pass that cannot be avoided. Makkah had a well and a cubical House in a fold of volcanic hills several days' march from the Red Sea. By every ordinary calculation it should have remained a local cult-site. That it became, by the sixth century, a node in a network linking Yemen, Syria, Abyssinia, and the Persian sphere is not explained by the quality of its soil. It is explained, the Qur'an insists, by the prayer of Abraham and the protection of God.

Abraham had asked for two things in that valley: security and fruit.

> *\"And when Abraham said, 'My Lord, make this a secure city, and provide its people with fruits — those among them who believe in God and the Last Day.'\"* (Qur'an 2:126)

The fruits did not grow there. They *arrived* there, on the backs of animals, in the winter from the south and in the summer from the north. Verse 2 is, among other things, the historical form of 2:126. What Abraham asked, the Quraysh received. What they received, they were now being asked to trace back. The tragedy the later verses will circle is that a people can live inside an answered prayer and call it their business model.

Al-Qurṭubī and the historians of Makkah preserve the memory that the great-grandfather of the Prophet ﷺ, Hāshim ibn ʿAbd Manāf, was instrumental in regularizing these journeys and in securing the covenants that made them routine. The reports of the four sons of ʿAbd Manāf dividing the directions of diplomacy — Hāshim toward Syria, ʿAbd Shams toward Abyssinia, Nawfal toward Persia, al-Muṭṭalib toward Yemen — belong to this file. The tradition does not need to certify every detail of that division for the verse to stand. What the verse needs, and what history abundantly supplies, is the fact of a people whose year was two roads.

**Riḥlah: A Journey That Is a Way of Life**

The word for the journey is *riḥlah*, from *raḥala*, to depart, to saddle up, to move off with one's mount. It is not a word for a stroll or a commute. It carries the sense of a serious going-forth, the kind that requires packing, provision, guards, and a date of hoped-for return. In later Arabic a scholar's *riḥlah* would mean his voyage in search of knowledge; in this verse the merchants' *riḥlah* is a voyage in search of livelihood. The two uses are not as far apart as they seem. Both are acts of leaving home under a risk, for a good that cannot be obtained by sitting still.

That risk is the suppressed half of the verse. Winter and summer journeys through the Ḥijāz, the Tihāmah, and the Syrian marches were not safe by nature. They were made safe. Bandits, rival tribes, thirst, lost wells, political change at the far end of the route, the simple death of camels — any of these could erase a year's profit and a family's men. The Quraysh's achievement, as they saw it, was to have reduced that chaos to a timetable. The verse's achievement is to have renamed the timetable. *Īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayf*: their familiarity with the winter journey and the summer journey. Familiarity is what risk looks like after God has been kind for a long time.

**Seasonal Labor and the Theology of Supply**

There is a further layer, easy to miss if one thinks of the Quraysh only as aristocrats. A caravan is a social organism. It includes the wealthy who own the goods, the riders who guard them, the hired men who tend the animals, the families who wait, the creditors who financed the load, and the pilgrims who, in season, swell the same roads. When the verse names the two journeys, it names a whole local economy. Provision in the Qur'an is rarely a private miracle. It is a web.

> *\"Have they not seen that We have made a secure sanctuary, while people are snatched away all around them? Do they then believe in falsehood and show ingratitude for the blessing of God?\"* (Qur'an 29:67)

This verse of Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt is the closest Qur'anic parallel to 106:2, and Ibn Kathīr explicitly relates the two. The secure sanctuary is why the journeys work. People are snatched away *all around them* — the ordinary Arabian condition of raid and capture — and the Quraysh are the exception. 29:67 asks whether they will thank the exception by believing in idols. 106:2 supplies the concrete picture of what the exception looked like: a winter departure, a summer departure, and a return.

The logical principle is not obscure. If your city is a fortress of safety in a landscape of kidnapping, and if your year is a pair of profitable roads in a landscape of failed ones, then the cause of that pattern is the most important theological fact in your life. Getting the cause wrong is not a small error in piety. It is a misreading of the only data you have. The Quraysh had data: two seasons, two directions, one House. Verse 2 lays the data on the table and waits.

**What a Modern Economy Still Owes This Verse**

The winter-and-summer structure is ancient, but it is not obsolete. Modern life has not abolished seasons; it has hidden them behind glass and logistics. Food still moves. Fuel still moves. Medicine still moves. A city that does not grow its own grain still eats because someone traveled, someone shipped, someone signed a treaty that is now called a trade agreement, someone kept a strait open, someone did not fire on a truck. The people who eat have, as a rule, never met the people who moved the food. That distance is precisely the distance in which *īlāf* becomes invisible.

The verse's contemporary work is to shorten that distance in the mind. When a reciter stands in a lighted kitchen in a safe building and opens a package whose contents grew in another climate, he is standing inside 106:2, whether he knows the word *riḥlah* or not. The package is a summer journey that someone else took. The lock on the door is a sanctuary-status that someone else — and, the sūrah says, Someone else — maintains. To live this verse is not to romanticize camels. It is to recover, in a supply-chain world, the ability to see a journey in a meal.

Practically, that recovery has several forms. It looks like saying *al-ḥamdu lillāh* when food is present, not as a formula but as a correct attribution. It looks like refusing the lie that one's salary is self-generated, as if the road from labor to livelihood were not a road other people can lose. It looks like treating public safety — the fact that a child can walk to school — as a named blessing rather than a default setting. It looks like the opposite of the consumer's trance, in which goods simply *are*, on shelves, as if shelves were trees.

The Prophet ﷺ compressed a similar accounting into a short, authentic statement that the commentators on later verses of this sūrah often recall, and that belongs already here because verse 2 is about the conditions of an ordinary day:

> *\"Whoever among you wakes up secure in his dwelling, healthy in his body, having his food for the day, it is as though the whole world has been gathered for him.\"* (al-Tirmidhī 2346; Ibn Mājah 4141; graded ḥasan)

Secure dwelling, healthy body, food for the day: the hadith names in the life of one person what the verse names in the life of a tribe. Winter and summer are the tribal scale of "food for the day." *Īlāf* is the tribal scale of "secure in his dwelling." The sūrah is about to say what such a person, and such a tribe, must do. Verse 2 makes sure they cannot claim they were not given enough to owe it.

**The Year as a Witness**

One last function of the verse is evidentiary. In Islamic law and theology, a witness is someone who was present. Winter and summer, as they return, are present. They testify annually. A Qurayshī who denied God's care had to deny it in December and in July, on the road south and on the road north, against the evidence of profit that he himself counted at the end of each *riḥlah*. The verse turns the commercial calendar into a witness-stand. This is why the sūrah needs no long narrative. The hearer's own year is the narrative. God does not, in these four verses, tell the Quraysh a story they do not know. He reads them a story they have been living and have mis-titled.

That is a method of argument worth learning. It does not begin from a metaphysical puzzle. It begins from a ledger. It says: look at your seasons. Then it will say: worship the Lord of this House. The second command is only as strong as the first look. Verse 2 is that look, held long enough that looking away would itself be a choice.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:3

> **so let them worship the Lord of this House,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The "So" That Converts Blessing into Duty**

The third verse begins with *fa-*: *falyaʿbudū rabba hādhā al-bayt* — "so let them worship the Lord of this House." In Arabic the particle *fa-* is not a decorative "and then." It is the particle of consequence, sequence, and causation. What follows it is presented as the necessary next step of what preceded it. Verses 1 and 2 were not an interlude of economic history. They were the premises of an imperative. Because there is *īlāf*, because there is a winter road and a summer road, therefore worship. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qurṭubī all mark this *fa-* as the hinge of the sūrah. Remove it and you have two observations and a pious suggestion. Keep it and you have a proof.

The logic is the logic of honest accounting. A gift creates a relation. If the gift is small, the relation may be courtesy. If the gift is the entire condition of a people's life — their food, their safety, their prestige, their House — the relation is servitude in the proper sense: the willing acknowledgment that one is not self-made. The Qur'an does not treat worship as an arbitrary extra, a ritual surcharge added to an otherwise complete human life. It treats worship as the only truthful sentence a provided-for creature can speak. Anything less is a kind of fraud — using the gift while cutting the name of the Giver off the account.

Ibn Taymiyyah's distinction, already invoked in the introduction, is here enacted in a single grammatical movement. Verses 1 and 2 are *rubūbiyyah*: God as Lord who manages, provides, protects, and orders the seasons. Verse 3 is *ulūhiyyah*: God as the only One who may be worshipped. The sūrah's claim is that the second follows from the first as *fa-* follows from its sentence. A people who confess with their caravans that they are provided for, and then bow to idols with their foreheads, are not holding two opinions. They are contradicting themselves. The verse exists to make that contradiction audible.

**Not the House, but the Lord of the House**

The object of the command is carefully chosen. The Quraysh are not told, in the first instance, "worship God" under the most universal Name, though that is the meaning. They are told to worship *rabba hādhā al-bayt* — "the Lord of this House." *This* House: the Kaʿbah, standing within sight of the first audience, pointed at with a demonstrative as if the reciter were turning the hearer's face toward the stone cuboid in the valley. The House is not the object of worship. The Lord of the House is. That distinction is the whole of Makkan reform in one phrase.

Pre-Islamic Quraysh did not, as a rule, deny the Creator. The Qur'an itself records their confession:

> *\"And if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say, 'God.'\"* (Qur'an 29:61; cf. 31:25; 39:38; 43:87)

Their error was not atheism. It was mediation. They worshipped the Lord of the House *through* the idols installed in the House, and then, by a slide as old as religion, they worshipped the intermediaries and the House in ways that belonged only to the Lord. The verse cuts the slide. It does not say "honor this House." Honor for the House is assumed; the House is "this House," already known, already sacred. It says: worship its *Rabb*. The custodians of a sanctuary are the people most at risk of confusing the sanctuary with the One it was built to face.

Abraham, who raised the foundations of this House with Ishmael, had already drawn the same line in a prayer the Qur'an preserves:

> *\"My Lord, make this city secure, and keep me and my sons away from worshipping idols. My Lord, they have led many people astray. So whoever follows me belongs to me, and whoever disobeys me — indeed You are Forgiving, Merciful.\"* (Qur'an 14:35–36)

The builder of the House feared the House's future. He feared that his descendants would do exactly what Quraysh, by the sixth century, had done: fill Abraham's cuboid with 360 idols and call the arrangement piety. Verse 3 is Abraham's prayer returning as a command to Abraham's children. The Lord of this House is the Lord Abraham named. The idols are the thing Abraham asked to be kept from. The sūrah, in four verses, is a family letter.

**What "Worship" Means When the Worshipper Is Already Religious**

*Falyaʿbudū* — "let them worship" — uses the same root as *ʿibādah*, worship, and *ʿabd*, servant. The command is not "let them become religious." The Quraysh were already religious. They performed ḥajj. They performed ʿumrah. They honored the sacred months. They watered pilgrims. They slaughtered at the House. They recited poetry at the fairs of ʿUkāẓ in the shadow of their rites. The Prophet ﷺ himself, before revelation, participated in the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl, a pact of justice among Qurayshī clans, and in the rebuilding of the Kaʿbah. Verse 3 is not addressed to a secular people who have never heard of God. It is addressed to a liturgical people who have misassigned the liturgy.

This is why the verse is harder, not easier, than a call to the irreligious. A man with no rites can be taught rites. A man whose rites are already pointed at the wrong object must be told that his piety is the problem. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early commentators, as recorded by al-Ṭabarī, understood the verse in this way: the Quraysh are being commanded to direct to God alone the worship they have been scattering among rivals. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the imperative is both a summons to *tawḥīd* and a rebuke of *shirk*. The same bow, the same House, the same pilgrimage — and a different addressee. Islam did not, in Makkah, invent a sanctuary. It purified one.

The Prophet ﷺ later stated the definition of the religion in a sentence that is, in effect, this verse expanded into a lifetime:

> *\"I have been commanded to fight the people until they testify that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is the Messenger of God, establish the prayer, and give the zakāt. If they do that, they have protected from me their blood and their property, except by the right of Islam, and their reckoning is with God.\"* (al-Bukhārī 25; Muslim 22)

The first half of that testimony — *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — is what *falyaʿbudū rabba hādhā al-bayt* demands. The Lord of this House has no partner in the House.

**Rabb: The Nurturer Whose Right Is Exclusive**

The verse could have said *Allāh*. It says *Rabb*. The choice continues the argument of verses 1 and 2. *Rabb*, as the commentators on 1:2 of the Fātiḥah never tire of explaining, is not merely "lord" in the feudal sense. It is the owner, the master, and — most characteristically — the one who rears a thing through its stages toward its completion. *Tarbiyah*, upbringing, comes from this root. The *Rabb* of this House is the One who raised this House's people: who answered Abraham, who brought water from Zamzam, who turned a barren valley into a pilgrimage market, who turned back the elephant, who opened the winter road and the summer road. Worship is owed to Him under this Name because this Name is the Name under which they have already been living.

The Qur'an elsewhere makes the inference inescapable by putting it in a sequence of questions:

> *\"Say: Who provides for you from the heaven and the earth? Or who owns hearing and sight? And who brings the living out of the dead and brings the dead out of the living? And who directs the affair? They will say, 'God.' So say: Then will you not be reverent?\"* (Qur'an 10:31)

"Then will you not be reverent?" is the same *fa-* as *falyaʿbudū*. Confession of provision, followed by the demand of worship, is a Qur'anic habit of argument. Sūrah Quraysh is that habit in its shortest form. It does not ask "Who provides?" because the providing has already been described. It goes straight to "so worship."

**This House as a Direction for the Whole Earth**

*Hādhā al-bayt* — "this House" — is local in the mouth of the first reciter and universal in the life of the religion that followed. The Kaʿbah would become, within a few years of this revelation, the *qiblah* of every prayer on earth:

> *\"So turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque. And wherever you are, turn your faces toward it.\"* (Qur'an 2:144)

The command of 106:3 therefore outgrew its first street. The Lord of this House is the Lord toward whose House every believing body now turns five times a day. To worship Him is not, for later Muslims, to stand in Makkah only. It is to let Makkah stand in the middle of one's day, wherever one is. The verse's demonstrative — *this* House — becomes, in the prayer, a geographical theology: there is a point on earth that is not God, and that must never be worshipped, and that is nevertheless the earthly sign around which worship of God is arranged. The Quraysh were told to worship the Lord of the House they could see. Their descendants in faith are told to worship the Lord of the House they may never see except once, or never, and still face.

The ethics of that facing are already present in 106:3. If the House has a Lord, then the House is not a tribal possession. Quraysh thought of themselves as the owners of the sanctuary. The verse's grammar quietly dispossesses them. The House has a *Rabb*. They are, at most, its neighbors. When, years later, the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah as a conqueror and struck the idols down, he was not seizing a tribal asset. He was returning a House to its Lord, which is what this verse had asked from the beginning.

It is reported, in the histories and in the commentary on Sūrah al-Fīl, that on the day of the conquest he ﷺ said words that bind 105 and 106 together in action:

> *\"Indeed, God restrained the elephant from Makkah, and He has given His Messenger and the believers authority over it. And He has restored its sanctity today just as its sanctity was yesterday.\"*

The elephant was restrained for the House. Authority was given for the House. Sanctity was restored to the House. At no point does the House become a god. At every point the Lord of the House is the actor. Verse 3 is the sentence that makes that history intelligible in advance.

**Worship as the Only Honest Accounting**

Why worship, and not merely "give thanks"? Thanks (*shukr*) is included in worship, but worship is larger. Thanks can be a word. Worship is a redirection of the whole life — prayer, sacrifice, vow, hope, fear, law, and love — toward the One who deserves them. The Quraysh could have thanked God with their tongues, as they sometimes did, and kept the idols for the needs they thought God was too high to mind: a lost camel, a disputed inheritance, a caravan's luck. The verse will not accept that division of labor. *Falyaʿbudū*: let them worship — let them give to the Lord of this House the entire portfolio they had distributed among His rivals.

The psychological honesty of this demand deserves to be stated plainly. Human beings do not like to owe everything to one source, because a single source can call in the whole debt. Multiple patrons can be played against each other; a pantheon is, among other things, a diversification strategy. *Tawḥīd* closes the portfolio. It says: you have one Patron, so you have one claim upon you. That is why the Quraysh resisted — not because the existence of a Creator was news, but because the exclusivity of worship would have ended the religious economy of intercession, tribal pride, and negotiated piety in which they were the brokers. Verse 3 threatens a monopoly, and the monopolist is God.

For a modern reciter the idols are less often stone. They are the diversified patrons of a later age: career, audience, state, market, self-image, the family's name. The verse's test travels well. After one has numbered one's winter and one's summer — one's salary, passport, health, children, city — the question is not "Do you believe in a Creator?" The question is "Whom do you actually serve with what He gave?" Worship, in Ibn Taymiyyah's later definition, is "a comprehensive name for everything God loves and is pleased with, of words and deeds, inward and outward." Under that definition, 106:3 is not only a command to pray toward a House. It is a command to stop serving, with God's own gifts, anyone but God.

**From Comfort to Kneeling: Why the Command Comes After the Gift**

Notice the order, because the order is mercy. God did not first demand worship from a starving, terrified clan and then, if they obeyed, reward them with trade. He gave the trade, gave the security, gave the House, gave the memory of the elephant, and then said *falyaʿbudū*. The gifts precede the command so that the command may be recognized as justice rather than extortion. A tyrant demands service before bread. The Lord of this House gives bread — and safety from fear, as the next verse will say — and then asks for the worship that the bread has already made reasonable.

This order also explains why ingratitude is, in the Qur'an, not a manners problem but a metaphysical one. To receive first and refuse afterward is to treat the Giver as a vending machine: take the goods, walk away. The parable that the source commentary rightly places over verses 3 and 4 — and that will be read more fully with verse 4 — is already implied here:

> *\"God sets forth a parable: a town secure and at peace, its provision coming to it abundantly from every side. Then it was ungrateful for the blessings of God, so God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear for what they had been making.\"* (Qur'an 16:112)

The town in the parable is the template; Makkah is the instance under review. Verse 3 is the last clear invitation before the parable's second half becomes biography. Worship now, while the table is set. The sūrah still has one verse in which to name what is on the table.

**Living the Imperative When the House Is Far**

A believer who recites this verse thousands of miles from Makkah still has a "this House." He faces it in prayer. He is asked, five times a day, to perform the *fa-* of this sūrah: to let the recognition of provision pass over into worship before the recognition cools. The practical form is almost embarrassingly concrete. One eats, then one prays. One returns from work, then one stands. One is safe in a bed, then one rises before dawn and speaks to the Lord of the House that one's sleeping body was turned toward. The verse is not asking for a mood. It is asking for a sequence — the same sequence the sūrah itself is: gift, then worship.

And because the verse says *let them worship*, not "let them feel," it can be obeyed on days when the heart is dry. The Quraysh were not asked to manufacture an emotion about the Kaʿbah. They were asked to give the rites, the hope, the slaughter, and the law to the One who owned the rites all along. A later Muslim, whose *īlāf* is a paycheck and a locked door, obeys the same verse by giving the day's first and last word to the same Lord, and by refusing to park, in the middle of the day, any rival that demands what only He is owed.

## Sūrah Quraysh 106:4

> **Who relieved them of hunger and made them safe from fear.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hunger and Fear: The Two Prisons of the Human Creature**

The sūrah ends by identifying the Lord of the House through two acts: *alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa āmanahum min khawf* — "who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear." The relative clause is not extra description. It is the proof of identity. If someone asks "Which Lord?" — for the Quraysh had many candidates around the Kaʿbah — the verse answers: the One who did these two things. Idols do not feed. Ancestors do not secure. Seasonal luck does not deserve a relative pronoun of this kind. The grammar is a filter. It lets through only the Agent who actually acted.

The two acts are not random. They correspond to the two most primitive threats under which a human being, or a city, can be crushed: want of food and want of safety. A person who is hungry cannot think. A person who is afraid cannot rest. A society that is both hungry and afraid is not a society; it is a panic spread over ground. Every political philosophy, every economic system, every parent walking a child to sleep, is in one way or another trying to solve these two problems. The verse claims that, for this people, both problems were solved by God. The claim is historical before it is mystical. Makkah had been hungry — a barren valley, as Abraham said. Makkah had been afraid — a small town in a peninsula of raids, and then a target for Abrahah's elephant. Both conditions were lifted. The lifting is what verse 4 names.

Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī read the feeding as the fruit of the two journeys in verse 2, and the security as the fruit of the sanctuary and of the destruction of the elephant in the previous sūrah. The four verses of Quraysh and the five of al-Fīl thus close a circle: protection from the army, protection on the road, bread on the table, sleep in the house. The Lord of this House is the One whose résumé, in this chapter, is entirely practical.

**The Verb That Makes Hunger a Past**

*Aṭʿamahum min jūʿ* — "He fed them against hunger," or "He fed them out of hunger." The preposition *min* here has been read as "from," "out of," or "against": He delivered them from a state of hunger by feeding them. The verb is in the perfect tense. The feeding is a completed act, a settled fact, not a hope. God is not promising the Quraysh that He might feed them if they worship. He is reminding them that He already has. The imperative of verse 3 stands on the perfects of verse 4. Worship the One who *did* this.

Hunger in the Arabian memory was not a metaphor. Years of drought, the failure of a caravan, the cutting of a route, a siege — these were living possibilities. The Qur'an elsewhere lets Ismāʿīl's valley speak its poverty through Abraham's tongue, and it lets the whole peninsula's scarcity stand as the dark background against which Makkah's provision shines. When the verse says *aṭʿamahum*, it is not saying that the Quraysh never missed a meal. It is saying that, as a people, they were moved from the condition of a hungry shrine-clan to the condition of a fed commercial tribe, and that the move was God's.

Food, in the Qur'an, is never theologically silent. It is one of the primary signs:

> *\"Let man look at his food: how We poured down water in showers, then split the earth in clefts, and caused to grow therein grain, and grapevines and fresh vegetation, and olives and date-palms, and gardens dense with foliage, and fruit and pasture — as enjoyment for you and your livestock.\"* (Qur'an 80:24–32)

> *\"It is He who made the earth tame for you, so walk in its slopes and eat of His provision. And to Him is the resurrection.\"* (Qur'an 67:15)

Sūrah Quraysh adds a local clause to that universal file. The food of Quraysh did not grow in Makkah's slopes. It came from Yemen and Syria because God tamed not only the earth but the roads. Feeding, here, includes logistics. The Lord who is identified as the Feeder is the Lord of rain and of treaties, of grain and of the fear that kept bandits off a particular tribe's camels. A narrow piety that finds God in the date and not in the caravan that brought the date has not yet read this verse.

**Safety as a Divine Act, Not a Municipal Achievement**

The second predicate is *āmanahum min khawf* — "He made them safe from fear," or "He granted them security against fear." The root is *ʾ-m-n*, the same root as *īmān* (faith) and *amānah* (trust). *Amn* is safety, security, the condition in which the soul is not bracing. *Khawf* is fear, the condition in which the soul is bracing all the time. To be moved from *khawf* to *amn* is to be given back one's nights.

Makkah's security had several layers, and the verse holds them all. There was the legal-religious layer: the Ḥaram, the sacred precinct in which fighting was forbidden and the sacred months in which bloodshed was suspended. There was the political layer: the prestige of Quraysh and the treaties of *īlāf*. There was the miraculous layer: the Year of the Elephant, when security was not a custom but a spectacle. There was the social layer: a townsman of Makkah could travel where a man of a lesser clan could not. Ibn Kathīr, connecting this verse to 29:67, underlines the contrast: people are snatched away all around them, and they are not. The exception is the sign.

The Qur'an swears by this security as if it were one of the great created things:

> *\"By the fig and the olive, and by Mount Sinai, and by this secure city.\"* (Qur'an 95:1–3)

A city so secure that God names it in an oath beside Sinai is a city whose safety cannot be treated as a civic accomplishment. Verse 4 takes the oath's object and assigns it an Author. *He* made them safe. The passive comfort of a quiet street is, in this theology, an active verb with a Divine subject.

**The Parable of the Secure Town**

The source commentary is right to place over this verse the parable of 16:112–113, and the verse-by-verse method requires that the parable be read here in full, because it is the shadow this sūrah casts forward:

> *\"God sets forth a parable: a town secure and at peace, its provision coming to it abundantly from every side. Then it was ungrateful for the blessings of God, so God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear for what they had been making. A messenger from among themselves had certainly come to them, but they denied him; so the punishment seized them while they were wrongdoers.\"* (Qur'an 16:112–113)

The correspondences are too tight to be accidental. A town secure — *āmanahum min khawf*. Provision from every side — *aṭʿamahum min jūʿ*, and the two directions of verse 2. Ingratitude for the blessings of God — the refusal of verse 3. A messenger from among themselves — Muhammad ﷺ, of Quraysh. Hunger and fear returning as a "garment" — the inversion of verse 4, the two gifts worn now as two punishments. The parable is what Sūrah Quraysh becomes if verse 3 is refused. The sūrah is the parable spoken in time to prevent its fulfillment.

History, as the later Makkan years unfolded, showed both the warning and the mercy. There was hunger: the boycott in the ravine of Abū Ṭālib, when the believing clan of Hāshim was cut off from the markets the sūrah had named. There was fear: persecution, torture, the migration to Abyssinia, the expulsion toward Madinah. And then, after the conquest, there was a restoration in which the House was cleansed and the city was made secure again, this time under the worship verse 3 had demanded. The parable did not fail. It was applied, lifted, and applied again, as the people chose.

Abraham's prayer in 2:126 had already foreseen that provision and security would be morally sorted:

> *\"My Lord, make this a secure city, and provide its people with fruits — those among them who believe in God and the Last Day.\" He said: \"And whoever disbelieves, I will grant him enjoyment for a little; then I will compel him to the punishment of the Fire, and what an evil destination.\"* (Qur'an 2:126)

Verse 4 of Sūrah Quraysh is the "enjoyment for a little" as it looked in the sixth century: full markets, quiet nights. The verse does not say that the enjoyment is eternal. It says that it is real, that it has an Author, and that the Author is the One to be worshipped before the "little" runs out.

**The Two Blessings People Consume Without Noticing**

The Prophet ﷺ named, in a hadith already cited at verse 2 and now due its full weight, the personal scale of this verse:

> *\"Whoever among you wakes up secure in his dwelling, healthy in his body, having his food for the day, it is as though the whole world has been gathered for him.\"* (al-Tirmidhī 2346; Ibn Mājah 4141)

And in another authentic report, he ﷺ identified the two gifts most often wasted:

> *\"There are two blessings in which many people are cheated: health and free time.\"* (al-Bukhārī 6412)

Health and time, food and safety: the revelation and the sunnah keep pointing at the same neglected floor of human life. People write poetry about love and power. They rarely write poetry about the fact that they ate breakfast without scanning the street for raiders. Verse 4 is that rare poetry, spoken as theology. It insists that the floor is not floor; it is gift. A man who has not thanked God for the absence of hunger and the absence of fear has not yet begun the accounting this sūrah requires, no matter how subtle his other devotions.

The psychology is the same one verse 1 named as familiarity. Hunger, when it is gone long enough, stops feeling like a deliverance and starts feeling like a right. Fear, when it is gone long enough, stops feeling like a lifted weight and starts feeling like "how things are." The verse uses two *min*s — *min jūʿ*, *min khawf* — that keep the old conditions visible. He fed them *out of* hunger. He secured them *out of* fear. The preposition will not let the old state be deleted from the file. Gratitude needs the memory of the thing one was saved from. A people with no memory of hunger will treat bread as furniture. A people with no memory of fear will treat safety as climate. The Qur'an, here, is doing the work of memory for them.

**Ingratitude's Garment: When the Blessing Is Worn as Its Opposite**

The image in 16:112 is worth sitting with because it is the dark twin of 106:4. Hunger and fear become a *libās*, a garment. A garment is what you wear all day, what touches the skin, what you cannot leave at home. When God "makes a town taste the garment of hunger and fear," He does not merely reduce calories and raise crime. He clothes a people in the two lacks they had forgotten, so that the lacks become their atmosphere. The justice of this is almost mathematical. The two gifts of 106:4, denied as gifts, return as their opposites, denied as accidents. What was not attributed to God in plenty is attributed to God in loss, because loss is a theist-maker. The sūrah would rather the attribution happen in plenty.

This is not a crude prosperity-theology. The Qur'an does not teach that every hungry person is being punished, or that every safe person is being approved. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions went hungry. Makkah's believers were the ones who suffered the boycott. The verse is aimed at a *collective* condition of provision and security that had become the basis of a tribe's pride, and at the specific ingratitude of directing the worship owed for those gifts to other than the Giver. Individual affliction in the life of the pious has another literature in the Qur'an — trial, purification, degree. Sūrah Quraysh is not that literature. It is the literature of a well-fed, well-guarded people being told that their table and their locks are exhibits in a case for *tawḥīd*.

**Aqīdah in a Relative Clause**

Doctrinally, verse 4 is an identification of God by His acts (*afʿāl*), which is one of the three great avenues of knowing Him in Islamic theology — the others being His names and His attributes. The verse does not here name Him *al-Razzāq* (the Provider) or *al-Muʾmin* (the Giver of security), though both Names stand in the background. It points at two deeds. The deeds are enough. A God who feeds and secures is a God who knows need, who answers Abraham, who intervenes in sieges and in seasons, who is not the remote first cause of the philosophers but the near Lord of this House. Ibn Taymiyyah's school would later insist that God's acts in history are to be affirmed as they are revealed, without emptying them into metaphor. This verse is such an act-statement. He fed. He made safe. The perfect tense is part of the creed.

Human responsibility sits beside those perfects. God fed them; they must worship. God made them safe; they must not hide behind the safety as if it were a right to disbelieve. The verse does not teach compulsion of the will. The Quraysh could — and many did — refuse verse 3. What they could not do was honestly refuse verse 4's description. The food was there. The fear was gone. Free will, in this sūrah, is not the freedom to invent facts. It is the freedom to respond to facts already on the ground. That is why the sūrah is so short. It does not need to establish the facts. It needs only to name them and to name the response.

The Hereafter is not mentioned in these four verses, and that silence is itself a design. Some people are moved by the Day of Judgment, as in the Fātiḥah's fourth verse. Some are moved by the argument from creation, as in the later Makkan sūrahs that point at camels and sky. Sūrah Quraysh moves a person by his pantry and his unlocked door. If the Afterlife feels far, the next meal does not. If the sky feels abstract, last night's sleep does not. The sūrah meets the human being at the lowest, most undeniable layer of need, and builds a theology there. A faith that can be verified at breakfast is a faith that has nowhere left to hide.

**How to Wear Food and Safety Without Losing Them**

The practical end of the verse is not anxiety — as if one should eat in terror of a returning famine — but attribution and duty. Attribution: when food is present, name the Feeder. When the night is quiet, name the Securer. The Muslim's table-manners are, in this light, a small recitation of 106:4. The Prophet ﷺ taught:

> *\"O boy, mention the Name of God, eat with your right hand, and eat from what is nearest to you.\"* (al-Bukhārī 5376; Muslim 2022)

And when the meal is done:

> *\"Whoever eats food and says, 'Praise be to God who fed me this and provided it for me without any power or might from me,' his past sins will be forgiven.\"* (al-Tirmidhī 3458; Abū Dāwūd 4023; graded ḥasan)

These are not table-ornaments. They are verse 4 made into a sentence a child can say. *Without any power or might from me* is the confession the Quraysh would not make. The winter journey felt like their power. The treaties felt like their might. The hadith strips both, kindly, at the edge of a plate.

Duty follows attribution. A people fed and secured are a people who can now feed and secure. The *siqāyah* and *rifādah* — the Qurayshī offices of watering and feeding pilgrims — were already a dim version of this duty, mixed with pride. Islam would later purify it into *zakāt*, *ṣadaqah*, the feeding of the orphan, the guest, the prisoner, the wayfarer. Safety, likewise, becomes a trust to be extended: the Ḥaram's peace is a model for a believer's neighborhood, a merchant's contract, a nation's treatment of the unarmed. The gifts of verse 4 are not given to be hoarded behind a tribal wall. They are given to be recognized, thanked, and passed on under the Name of the One who first passed them.

In a modern city the verse reads with an almost painful clarity. Grocery lights, paid on time, create the illusion that hunger is a problem other countries have. Police sirens in another district create the illusion that fear is a problem other zip codes have. Verse 4 will not allow the illusion to become a creed. If you ate, He fed. If you slept, He made safe — through whatever secondary causes He employed, including the farmer, the truck, the officer, the locksmith, the diplomat, the rain. The secondary causes are real. They are also not the relative pronoun's antecedent. The antecedent is the Lord of this House.

**The Sūrah's Last Word Is a Condition, Not a Compliment**

Sūrah Quraysh does not end with a promise of Paradise or a threat of Fire. It ends with a description of two gifts. The description is the warning. If these are the grounds of worship, then the withdrawal of these is the first earthly consequence of refusing worship, as 16:112 states, and the Last Day is the final one. The sūrah leaves that inference to the hearer. It has done enough: it has named the tribe, named the journeys, named the House, named the Lord, named the bread, named the quiet night. A person who can hear those six namings and still walk away has not suffered from lack of evidence. He has suffered from the specific blindness this chapter was sent to cure — the blindness of the well-provided.

The cure, if it takes, is a small daily conversion of facts into worship. Winter and summer, or their modern equivalents, are noticed. The House is faced. The Lord of the House is served. Hunger remembered makes bread sweet. Fear remembered makes safety a prayer. And the four verses, recited in a few seconds, do the work they were given to do in the seventh century and have not finished doing: they stop a fed and guarded human being in the middle of his ordinary life and tell him, without cruelty and without flattery, who it was that got him this far.
