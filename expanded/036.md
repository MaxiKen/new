# Sūrah Yā Sīn (Chapter 36) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Makkan Proclamation at the Heart of Faith**

Sūrah Yā Sīn is a Makkan chapter of eighty-three verses. Its dominant setting is the struggle of revelation against settled denial: it vindicates Muhammad ﷺ as a messenger, displays a community that rejected envoys and the courageous believer who defended them, reads resurrection through the revived earth and ordered heavens, and ends by answering the objection that decayed bones cannot live again. Its movement is deliberate. Revelation announces; history warns; creation demonstrates; the trumpet gathers; Paradise and Hell disclose the two ends; and the final verses return the reader to God's unrestricted creative command.

Reports calling Yā Sīn “the heart of the Qur'an,” promising tenfold reward for it, or assigning particular worldly outcomes to its recitation are widely quoted, but their chains are disputed or weak. Scholarly reverence does not need uncertain attribution. The report, “Recite Yā Sīn over your dying,” is recorded by Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah and has also been disputed in grading. Muslims may recite Qur'an for blessing while avoiding confident claims the Prophet ﷺ cannot securely be shown to have made. The chapter's established excellence lies first in its being God's speech and in the immense truths its text plainly carries.

**One Argument Developed Through Eighty-Three Signs**

The chapter binds prophethood and resurrection together. If the Wise Qur'an truly comes from the Mighty, Merciful Lord, its warning of return must be heard. If dead soil lives, embryos become articulate adversaries, green wood yields fire, and the heavens were created by command, restoring bones cannot rationally be called impossible. Thus nature is not offered as decoration. Each ordinary phenomenon becomes evidence in a cumulative case.

The commentary below treats every verse independently, including verses grouped in the source. Direct Qur'anic and Prophetic quotations are blockquoted. Classical attributions are stated cautiously: where reports differ, possibility is distinguished from certainty; where revelation speaks of God's attributes, they are affirmed in a manner befitting Him, without resemblance to creation and without speculative description.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:1

> **Yā. Sīn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Separated Letters**

The sūrah opens with two letters of the Arabic alphabet recited by name: *yāʾ* and *sīn*. They belong to the class known as the separated letters — *al-muqaṭṭaʿāt* — which stand at the beginning of twenty-nine of the Qur'an's one hundred and fourteen sūrahs. The commentators at 2:1 note that in recitation the names of the letters are used rather than their sounds, which is why they are transliterated here as they are recited.

The majority position on their meaning is that it is known only to God. That is not a confession of ignorance about the text but a claim about it: these letters are not words, and no reading of them as words is compelled. The commentators record the readings that follow without requiring any of them, and the sūrah itself does not gloss them.

**The Reading 'O Human Being'**

Some allow that *yāʾ sīn* could be an abbreviation meaning O human being (Q). On this interpretation the *yāʾ* is the vocative particle O, used in many Qur'anic verses, and the *sīn* abbreviates *unsayn*, the diminutive of *insān* — human being. The commentators note that in this context the diminutive O little human being functions as a term of endearment, and is read as God's address to the Prophet Muhammad.

The reading has a consequence for what follows. If the opening is an address to the Prophet, then the oath at v2 and the affirmation at v3 attach to a person already named, and the sequence is vocative, oath, declaration. The commentators at vv 3–4 note exactly this: those verses affirm the Prophet's mission and thus support the interpretations that see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself.

**A Name Given to the Prophet**

Others say that Yā Sīn is a name given to the Prophet by God whose exact meaning is unknown (Q). For this reason it is sometimes used in the Islamic world as a name for a male. The commentators cite the report from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (IA, Q): I heard the Messenger of God say, Verily God has named me by seven names in the Quran — Muhammad, Aḥmad, Ṭā Hā, Yā Sīn, thou enwrapped, thou who art covered, and servant of God — with the cross-references 3:144, 33:40, 47:2 and 48:29 for Muhammad; 61:6 for Aḥmad; 20:1 for Ṭā Hā; 73:1 and 74:1 for the two enwrapped addresses; and 72:19 for servant of God.

The list places Yā Sīn among names the Qur'an itself supplies, which is what allows it to be used as a personal name. The report is preserved as a report; the commentators do not present it as settling the meaning of the letters, only as one account of how the sequence came to be understood as a name.

**The Other Readings**

Two further interpretations are recorded. Other commentators take Yā Sīn to be a name of the Qur'an itself. Al-Qushayrī identifies it with the Day of the Covenant — *yawm al-mīthāq* — when God made a covenant with all the children of Adam, as at 7:172.

The four readings are not competing claims about a single fact so much as different accounts of what kind of thing the opening is: an address, a name of the Prophet, a name of the Book, or an allusion to a primordial event. The commentators set them side by side. What they share is the assumption that the letters carry significance; what divides them is the direction of that significance, and the verse does not decide.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not explain the letters, and does not indicate which of the recorded readings is intended. It does not connect them to the oath that follows, and does not say whether they are an address, a title, or something else.

The verse also does not distinguish Yā Sīn from the separated letters at the head of the other twenty-eight sūrahs. The commentators treat it within that class, and the majority position — that the meaning is known only to God — applies to all of them equally. The particular readings offered here are offered for this sequence because the sūrah's own content, at vv 3–4, appears to support one of them.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:2

> **By the Wise Quran,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by the Book**

By the Wise Quran. The sūrah swears by the Qur'an before making its declaration, and the thing sworn by is characterised by a single adjective. The oath form is familiar from the Makkan sūrahs, where God swears by created things and by revelation alike, and the effect is to establish the ground on which what follows will rest.

What follows is the affirmation of the Prophet's mission at v3. The oath by the Qur'an and the declaration about the messenger are therefore joined: the Book is the warrant for the claim about the man. The commentators note at vv 3–4 that those verses affirm the Prophet's mission, and the oath here is what the affirmation is sworn upon.

**Ḥakīm as Determined**

*Ḥakīm*, here translated Wise, can also mean determined or made firm — *muḥkam* — as at 11:1, where the Qur'an is described as a Book whose signs have been determined, *uḥkimat* (Q). The commentators cite Ibn Kathīr for the consequence: that the Qur'an is *muḥkam* indicates that falsehood comes not upon it from before it or from behind it, as at 41:42.

On this reading the adjective describes the Book's resistance to alteration. Its signs have been fixed, and nothing can be inserted into it or removed from it. The commentators do not treat this as the only sense of *ḥakīm*, but as one of its established senses, and the cross-reference to 11:1 supplies the same root used of the same Book.

**Ḥakīm as Making Wise**

*Ḥakīm* could also mean something that makes wise — *muḥkim* — indicating that the Qur'an teaches the truth. The commentators note that Ibn Kathīr takes the use of *ḥakīm* here as an allusion to the Qur'an as a revealed book, and that the Qur'an is described as wise elsewhere, at 10:1, 31:2, and 43:3–4.

The two readings differ in direction. On the first the adjective describes what the Book is: settled, unalterable. On the second it describes what the Book does: it makes the one who follows it wise. The commentators preserve both, and the word's range admits both without strain, since a book whose signs are fixed can also be a book that instructs.

**The Adjective and the Sūrah's Argument**

The choice of *ḥakīm* is not incidental to what the sūrah will do. Yā Sīn's central movement is an argument about resurrection, conducted with the disbelievers of Makkah, and the argument proceeds by reasoning from what is already known to what is being denied. A book described as wise is a book that argues.

The commentators do not draw this connection explicitly at v2, and the verse does not claim it. What the verse establishes is the character of the authority being invoked. The sūrah then uses that authority in the way the adjective suggests — not by assertion alone but by putting cases, drawing analogies, and asking questions that the hearer must answer.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what is being sworn to. It supplies the oath and stops, leaving the declaration to the next verse. It does not explain in what respect the Qur'an is wise, and does not choose between the readings of *ḥakīm* recorded by the commentators.

The verse also does not say who swears. The oath form is unattributed in the Arabic, and the commentators do not supply an attribution here. The sūrah's later verses make clear that the speaker is God, but this verse leaves the oath standing on its own, which is characteristic of the form.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:3

> **truly thou art among the message bearers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Affirmation of the Mission**

Truly thou art among the message bearers. The declaration that the oath at v2 was sworn upon is now made. The emphatic construction leaves no room for qualification: the Prophet is among those who bear a message, and the sūrah states it as settled.

The commentators note that these verses affirm the Prophet's mission, and that the affirmation supports the interpretations which see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself. The connection is what gives the opening its shape: if the letters address the Prophet, then the oath and the declaration are addressed to him too, and the three verses form a single movement of naming, swearing, and confirming.

**Among, Not Alone**

The verse says among the message bearers, not that he is the message bearer. The preposition places him within a company, and the company is one the Qur'an describes at length elsewhere. Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb, and Moses have all been named in the preceding sūrahs as bearers of the same kind of message to the same kind of resistance.

The placement matters for the argument the sūrah will make. A messenger among messengers is not an innovation, and the opposition he faces is not unprecedented. The sūrah's account of the people of the town at vv 13–29 depends on this: what happens there has happened before, and the outcome is known.

**The Sequence of the Opening**

Read in order, the three verses move from an address to a warrant to a claim. Yā Sīn — if it is an address — names the one being spoken to. By the Wise Quran supplies the authority. Truly thou art among the message bearers states what that authority establishes.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence as such, and the verses do not announce their structure. What they achieve is that the Prophet's mission is not asserted at the opening of the sūrah but sworn to, and sworn to by the Book that constitutes the mission. The affirmation is therefore self-supporting in a way a bare declaration would not be.

**The Address and Its Recipient**

The declaration is in the second person singular and is addressed to the Prophet. The sūrah's opening is therefore not a general statement about prophethood but a particular statement to one man, and the commentators' note that these verses affirm his mission takes it that way.

This is consistent with the Makkan context the commentators describe for the sūrah. It was revealed in the early part of the middle Makkan period, when the opposition was intensifying and the Prophet's standing was being contested. An affirmation of the mission is a response to a contest, and the verse's emphatic form is what a response to a contest looks like.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the message is, and does not summarise it. It affirms that the Prophet bears one and leaves the content to the sūrah that follows. It does not name the other message bearers, and does not say how many there were.

The verse also does not say that the mission will succeed in the sense of being accepted. It states the fact of the mission rather than its outcome, and the sūrah's own narrative at vv 13–29 shows a community that rejected its messengers. The affirmation concerns the sending, not the reception.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:4

> **upon a straight path,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Upon a Straight Path**

The mission affirmed at v3 is now qualified: it is upon a straight path. The commentators direct the reader to 1:6 for the phrase, where guide us upon the straight path is understood as a prayer for perseverance in following that path and thus for continued aid (Ṭ), and for being made firm in following the way of truth (Ṭs).

The glosses at 1:6 add that the request for guidance implies seeking to be led to God Himself, and thus a desire for intimacy with Him, nearness to Him (Q), knowledge of Him, and love for Him. The path is therefore not merely a route but a relationship, and the commentators read it that way in the sūrah where the phrase is prayed rather than described.

**The Path and the Message**

The attachment of the path to the mission is what the verse contributes. The Prophet is among the message bearers, and his is upon a straight path. The commentators do not gloss the connection, but the phrase does two things at once: it characterises the message and distinguishes it from other claims.

A straight path is one without deviation, and the Qur'an uses the phrase to contrast the way of the messengers with the ways of those who opposed them. The sūrah's later verses will show the alternative in practice — a town that killed its messengers, and a people who said our auguring ill is upon yourselves. The path named here is what those alternatives are measured against.

**Straightness in the Qur'an**

The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an and is not local to this sūrah. The commentators' cross-reference to 1:6 places it in the daily prayer, where it is asked for rather than asserted. The difference between the two uses is significant: in al-Fātiḥah the believer asks to be guided upon it, and here the Prophet is declared to be upon it.

The commentators note at 1:6 that for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, their Lord guides them by their faith, citing 10:9 (Ṭs). The guidance is therefore continuous rather than a single bestowal, and the path is something one remains upon rather than something one enters once. The Prophet's being upon a straight path is a standing condition, not an achievement.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not define the straight path, and does not enumerate what it consists in. It attaches the phrase to the mission and leaves the content to the Qur'an's wider use of it, which the commentators supply by cross-reference rather than by explanation.

The verse also does not say that others are not upon it. It describes the Prophet's mission and does not exclude anyone else. The sūrah's later verses distinguish those who follow the messengers from those who reject them, but this verse makes no such division; it states where the mission lies and moves on.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:5

> **a revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful**

The oath at v2 and the affirmation at vv 3–4 are now given their source. The message is a revelation, and it comes from God described by two names. The commentators note that revelation can be read as the object of an implied verb — We revealed it as a revelation — in which case it refers back to the Wise Quran, or in the nominative, meaning It is a revelation.

Both readings are preserved. The grammatical ambiguity matters less than what both share: the message is not the Prophet's own composition but something sent to him. The sūrah has moved in four verses from an address to a warrant to a claim to a source, and this verse names the source.

**The Two Readings of What Is Revealed**

Most commentators say that revelation in either reading refers to the Qur'an, although some say it refers to the Prophet (Q, R), in which case the pronoun in the elided phrase would be rendered he rather than it. The commentators support this by noting that the Prophet is also said to be sent down, as at 65:10–11: God has certainly sent down unto you a reminder: a Messenger reciting unto you the clear signs of God.

The reading is not arbitrary. If the revelation is the Prophet himself, then the sūrah's opening — which the commentators take as possibly addressing him by name — closes its first movement by identifying him as the thing sent. Both readings are recorded without preference, and the commentators say plainly that both are possible.

**The Mighty and the Merciful**

The verse names God as the Mighty, the Merciful. The commentators observe that the juxtaposition of these two names here and in other verses — especially throughout Sūrah 26 — is seen as an allusion to God being both just and merciful, vengeful toward those who oppose Him and merciful toward those who obey Him.

The pairing is not a compromise between two attributes but a statement about how both operate. Might without mercy would make the warning at v6 a bare threat; mercy without might would make it empty. The sūrah needs both to say what it says next: a warner has been sent to a people who were never warned, and the Word has come due for most of them.

**The Sequence of the Opening Five Verses**

The commentators note at vv 3–4 that those verses affirm the Prophet's mission and thus support the interpretations which see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself. Verse 5 completes that movement by supplying the origin of the mission. What is sworn by at v2, affirmed at v3, qualified at v4, is now attributed.

The order is deliberate. The authority is invoked before the claim is made, and the source is named after the claim rather than before it. The sūrah does not begin with a statement about God but with an address and an oath, and arrives at the divine names only once the messenger's standing has been established.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the readings of revelation, and does not say whether what is sent down is the Book or the man. It does not explain the two names, and does not say in what proportion might and mercy operate.

The verse also does not say to whom the revelation is sent. That is supplied by the previous verse — a people whose fathers were not warned — and this verse concerns its origin rather than its destination. The sūrah separates the two questions and answers them in turn.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:6

> **that thou mayest warn a people whose fathers were not warned; so they were heedless.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A People Whose Fathers Were Not Warned**

That thou mayest warn a people whose fathers were not warned; so they were heedless. The verse states the purpose of the sending and identifies the recipients by an absence: no warner had come to their fathers. The commentators note that this is similar to 32:3, which states that the Qur'an was sent so that the Prophet might warn a people to whom no warner had come before.

The absence is confirmed elsewhere. At 34:44 God says of the Arabs: We have not given them any books that they study; nor have We sent them a warner before. The commentators are careful to qualify this: although the Arabs had been sent messengers before in the intervening generations from the time of Ishmael — who was both a prophet and the progenitor of the Arabs, as Isaac was of the Jews — the pure monotheism of Abraham had become lost, and from the point of view of the Arabs the religion of their fathers was pagan.

**The Loss Between Ishmael and Muhammad**

The qualification is what makes the verse historically precise rather than merely rhetorical. There had been messengers, but the message had not survived. The commentators describe a lineage that began with Abraham's monotheism and arrived at paganism, and they treat the intervening loss as the reason a new warner was needed.

This explains the phrase whose fathers were not warned. It does not mean the fathers had never been addressed; it means the address had not reached them intact. The heedlessness named at the end of the verse is therefore not original ignorance but inherited loss, and the commentators' account of the generations from Ishmael is what fills in that distinction.

**An Ominous Tone**

The commentators note that read in connection with the following verse and the context of the entire Qur'an, there is an ominous tone to this verse. The reason is given: as other verses state, Thy Lord never destroys towns until He sends a messenger to their mother city to recite unto them Our signs (28:59), and Never did We destroy a town save that it had warners (26:208); see also 6:131 and 10:13.

The ominousness lies in what the sending implies. A warner is sent before destruction, not after, and the sending is therefore a precondition of the punishment rather than a mere announcement. The sūrah's own narrative at vv 13–29 shows a town that rejected its messengers and was destroyed by a single cry, and this verse is what makes that outcome legible as a consequence.

**An Alternative Reading**

The commentators record that some propose the verse should be read of that of which their fathers were warned. On that reading the verse says the opposite of the standard one: the people are being warned of what their fathers were warned of, and the warning is a continuation rather than a new thing.

The reading is preserved without endorsement. The commentators do not say which is correct, and the verse's wording admits both. What differs between them is whether the Arabs stand outside the prophetic tradition or inside it, and the commentators' own note about the generations from Ishmael sits more comfortably with the alternative than with the standard reading.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how long the absence of warning lasted, and does not date it. It does not say whether all the fathers were unwarned or only the recent ones, and does not name the messengers who came before.

The verse also does not say that the warning will be accepted. It states the purpose of the sending — that thou mayest warn — and the next verse reports that the Word has come due for most of them because they do not believe. The purpose and the outcome are stated in adjacent verses without reconciliation.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:7

> **The Word has indeed come due for most of them, for they do not believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Has Come Due**

The Word has indeed come due for most of them, for they do not believe. The commentators explain the phrase: it translates *ḥaqqa al-qawlu ʿalā aktharihim*, which could also be rendered the Word has indeed proved true against most of them. Both renderings are given, and the difference between them is slight — a word that comes due is a word whose time has arrived, and a word that proves true is a word whose content has been confirmed.

The verse supplies its own reason in the second clause: for they do not believe. The commentators connect this explicitly to what precedes — they do not believe in the warning referred to in v6, in the Wise Quran of v2, or that the Prophet Muhammad is among the message bearers at v3. The three things the sūrah has just established are the three things refused.

**What the Word Is**

The Word most likely refers to the Word of punishment, with the commentators citing 39:71 and 22:18. It indicates that they will be in Hell, for as 40:6 states: Likewise did the Word of thy Lord come due for those who disbelieve, that they are the inhabitants of the Fire.

The identification is supported by a second cross-reference. The Word that comes due is seen by some as God's threat at 32:13: But the Word from Me comes due: I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together. The commentators note similar threats at 11:119 and 38:84–5, and the recurrence across the Qur'an is what allows the phrase here to be read without further explanation.

**An Alternative: the Word as the Qur'an**

The commentators record a second reading. The Word could also be a reference to the Qur'an itself, for it is thought that when one turns away from revelation, it proves one's true nature. In support they cite a famous ḥadīth: The Quran is a proof, either for you or against you.

This reading changes the grammar of the verse. On the first reading the Word is a decree that falls upon them from outside; on the second it is the Book they have rejected, which becomes evidence against them by their rejection of it. The commentators preserve both, and the ḥadīth supplies the principle that makes the second intelligible — revelation does not merely inform but testifies.

**Most of Them**

The verse says most of them, not all of them. The qualification is significant and the commentators do not gloss it away. Some of the people addressed did believe, and the sūrah's own narrative at vv 20–27 includes a man who came from the farthest part of the city urging his people to follow the messengers.

The distinction between most and all is what keeps the verse from being a blanket condemnation. The Word has come due for those who do not believe, and the reason is given as their disbelief rather than as their belonging to a particular group. The sūrah's later verses show the exception, and this verse leaves room for it by its wording.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the Word comes due — in this world, in the Hereafter, or both. It does not name the punishment, and does not describe it. It does not say whether the decree can be reversed by later belief.

The verse also does not explain why they do not believe. The reason is supplied at the next two verses, which describe shackles upon their necks and barriers before and behind them, and the commentators read those verses as describing the condition of unbelief rather than merely predicting its consequence.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:8

> **Truly We have put shackles upon their necks, and they are up to their chins, so that they are forced up.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shackles Upon Their Necks**

Truly We have put shackles upon their necks, and they are up to their chins, so that they are forced up. The commentators explain the word precisely: shackles translates *aghlāl*, specifically indicating iron shackles that bind the hands in cuffs that are attached to an iron ring around the neck.

The detail is what makes the image work. The hands are not merely bound but bound to the neck, so that they cannot be extended. The commentators note that they — the pronoun in they are up to their chins — is thus taken by most to indicate the hands that are bound to their necks (Q, Ṭs), and that the phrase is related to 17:29: And let not thine hand be shackled to thy neck (IK, Q, Ṭ), which is a warning against miserliness.

**The Link to Miserliness**

The connection to 17:29 is instructive. There the shackled hand is an image of stinginess — the hand that cannot be extended to give. Here the same image describes those who reject the messenger, and the commentators cite the parallel without elaborating the moral.

The implication is that the rejection described in these verses is not only intellectual. A hand shackled to the neck cannot reach outward, and the commentators at 17:29 read that as avarice. Applied here, the image suggests a closure that is moral as well as cognitive — an inability to extend oneself toward what is offered.

**This Life or the Next**

The commentators record two readings of when this occurs. The binding of their hands to their necks is taken by some to indicate their state on the Day of Resurrection (Ṭs) or in the Hereafter, as at 40:71: They will be dragged, with shackles and chains around their necks (Q).

Others say it refers to the state of the idolaters in this life, meaning that those who turn away from the Prophet's call are like those whose hands are shackled to their necks so that they cannot extend them toward what is good (IK, Ṭ, Ṭs). In this interpretation their chins being forced up alludes to their arrogance in the face of the truth (Q). The two readings are preserved without preference, and the verse's present tense admits both.

**The Forced-Up Chin**

The detail of the chin is the verse's most specific contribution. So that they are forced up describes a posture, and the commentators read it as arrogance. A head forced upward cannot look down, and the image is of someone unable to see what is at his feet.

This connects to the next verse, which describes barriers before and behind and a veiling, so that they see not. The commentators read the two verses together — the forced-up chin and the barriers are two descriptions of one condition. The posture prevents looking down, the barriers prevent looking forward or back, and the veiling prevents seeing at all.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who put the shackles there in the sense of a human agent. It says We have put, and the commentators do not gloss the agency further. It does not say whether the shackles can be removed, and does not offer a condition for their removal.

The verse also does not say that the binding is unjust. The previous verse gave the reason — they do not believe — and this verse describes the condition that follows. The commentators' readings differ on whether the description is literal or figurative and on whether it belongs to this life or the next, but neither reading treats the binding as arbitrary.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:9

> **And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and veiled them; so they see not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Barrier Before and Behind**

And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and veiled them; so they see not. The commentators give the sense directly: this is interpreted to mean We have placed a barrier between them and the truth, or We have blinded them to the truth (IK), in light of 10:96–97: Truly those for whom the Word of thy Lord has come due will not believe, though every sign should come unto them, till they see the painful punishment.

The cross-reference supplies the point. It is not that evidence is lacking but that evidence no longer reaches them. The commentators state the consequence plainly: understood this way, it means they cannot benefit from guidance no matter what the Prophet does.

**The Counsel That Follows**

The commentators connect this verse forward to v76, where God counsels the Prophet: Let not their speech grieve thee. The connection is the reason the counsel is needed. If the barrier is real and the veiling complete, then the Prophet's exertion cannot remove it, and grief over the outcome is grief over something outside his charge.

This is the practical function of the verse within the sūrah. It is not a statement about fate offered for its own sake but an explanation of why the messenger's task is limited to delivery. The commentators at vv 10–11 make the same point in other words: these verses remind the Prophet that he cannot guide those whom God has not guided and should simply leave them to God.

**The Two Barriers**

Some say the barrier before them refers to their being deluded in the life of this world, and the barrier behind them refers to their denying the life of the Hereafter (Q). On this reading the two barriers are not spatial but temporal: one closes off the present and the other closes off what follows it.

The reading makes the image systematic. A person enclosed by a barrier ahead and a barrier behind has nowhere to go, and the commentators take that as the description of a condition — attachment to this world and denial of the next — rather than of a place. The veiling completes it: they cannot see either direction.

**The Incident Reported**

Others maintain that vv 8–9 refer to a specific incident. The commentators relate that a leader from the tribe of Quraysh saw the Prophet praying and went to smash his head with a rock; as he moved his hand forward to cast it, his hand returned to his neck and the rock was stuck in it (Q, Ṭs). Then another man took the rock with the same purpose, but when he did so he could hear the Prophet and not see him. When a third man went to smash the Prophet's head, he saw before him a tremendous camel that was about to swallow him (Q).

The commentators are careful about how this report relates to the verse. These particular examples are not taken to contravene the more general implications, but rather to be manifestations of particular forms of denial and disbelief represented by the acts described. The incident is offered as an illustration, not as the verse's exclusive referent.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the barriers were placed, and does not say whether they were placed before or after the warning was delivered. It does not say whether they can be removed, and does not offer a condition for their removal.

The verse also does not say that the veiling is unjust. The previous verses gave the sequence — the warning was sent, they did not believe, the Word came due — and this verse describes the condition that followed. The commentators' several readings differ on whether the description is of this life or the next, but none treats the barriers as arbitrary.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:10

> **It is the same for them whether thou warnest them or warnest them not; they do not believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Warning Makes No Difference**

It is the same for them whether thou warnest them or warnest them not; they do not believe. The verse states the futility of the warning in terms that leave no room for degree: the two courses are equivalent in their effect. The commentators note that the last phrase could also be read they will not believe, and cite 2:6 as a parallel.

The alternative reading differs in tense rather than in substance. Whether they do not believe or will not believe, the warning does not change the outcome. The commentators cite further parallels where warnings and guidance are of no avail to disbelievers: 7:193, 26:136, and 63:6.

**The Limit on the Prophet**

The commentators explain the purpose of the verse. These verses remind the Prophet that he cannot guide those whom God has not guided and should simply leave them to God, citing 35:8: Truly God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will; so let not thy soul be expended in regrets over them. Truly God knows that which they do.

The counsel is about the distribution of responsibility rather than about the value of the warning. The Prophet's task remains delivery, and the verse does not withdraw it; what it withdraws is the expectation that delivery determines reception. The phrase let not thy soul be expended in regrets is what the commentators take the verse to be for.

**The Connection to the Previous Verses**

Read after vv 8–9, this verse is the conclusion of an argument rather than a new claim. The shackles and the barriers describe a condition; this verse states what the condition means for the messenger's task. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the sūrah's own movement is clear.

The verse is therefore not a general statement about disbelievers but a statement about these — the people whose fathers were not warned, for whom the Word has come due, and who are described as shackled and veiled. The futility named here is the futility of warning a people already in that condition.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that warning is therefore unnecessary. It says the warning makes no difference to them, and the sūrah continues to command delivery. The next verse identifies those whom the warning does reach.

The verse also does not say why they do not believe. The reasons were given in the preceding verses, and this verse states only the outcome. It does not say whether any of them will later believe, and the phrase most of them at v7 leaves that question open.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:11

> **Thou only warnest whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. So give such a one glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whom the Warning Reaches**

Thou only warnest whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. After the statement that the warning makes no difference to some, the verse identifies those to whom it does make a difference. The commentators note that the Reminder is a reference to the Qur'an itself (IK, Q, R, Ṭ), and direct the reader to 36:69 for the Qur'an as Reminder.

The restriction is significant. The verse does not say the Prophet warns only those who will believe, as though the outcome were known in advance; it says he warns whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate. The warning is effective in those disposed to receive it, and the disposition is described rather than predetermined.

**Fearing the Compassionate Unseen**

The commentators gloss the phrase: fears the Compassionate unseen (cf. 50:33) can be interpreted as a reference to worshipping God while God remains unseen, or to worshipping God in seclusion (Q, Ṭs). Both readings are preserved.

The two differ in what they make the unseen modify. On the first, God is the one unseen, and the fear is reverence for a God who is not visible. On the second, the worshipper is unseen, and the fear is exercised where no one observes. The commentators record both, and the verse's wording admits either. What they share is that the fear is not performed for an audience.

**Forgiveness and a Generous Reward**

So give such a one glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward. The commentators explain that a generous reward here means Paradise or the Garden, and direct the reader to 67:12. The two things promised are distinct: forgiveness concerns what is removed, and the reward concerns what is given.

The verse addresses the Prophet and instructs him to deliver the good news. The instruction matters because the preceding verse described a warning that has no effect. Here the same messenger is told to give glad tidings, and the difference is not in the message but in the recipients.

**The Two Verses Together**

Verses 10 and 11 form a pair that the commentators treat together. The first states that the warning is of no avail to some; the second states whom it reaches. Neither verse is complete without the other, and the commentators' note on both — that they remind the Prophet he cannot guide those God has not guided — applies to the pair.

The pairing also answers a question the sūrah raises at v7, where the Word comes due for most of them. Most is not all, and this verse describes the remainder. The sūrah does not reconcile the two by saying that the remainder are exempt from the warning; it says the warning reaches them because of what they already do.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how one comes to follow the Reminder. It describes the disposition of those reached rather than explaining its origin. It does not enumerate what following consists in beyond the two things named.

The verse also does not say that the glad tidings are conditional on continued following. It instructs the Prophet to give them to such a one, and leaves the question of perseverance to the sūrah's later treatment of the Garden and its inhabitants.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:12

> **Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth and that which they have left behind. And We have counted all things in a clear registry.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**We Give Life to the Dead**

Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth and that which they have left behind. The commentators explain that this verse follows upon the counsel to the Prophet to deliver the warning and leave the disbelievers to their own devices, since the price of their wrongdoing will come due upon the Resurrection.

The phrase We give life to the dead carries four recorded readings. It can be seen as a reference to the Resurrection; to God's bringing the earth back to life, as at 57:17: Know that God revives the earth after its death (IK); to bringing people from idolatry to faith (Z); or to the revivification, through knowledge and truth, of hearts made dead through heedlessness and ignorance (Aj, IK). All four are preserved.

**What Is Recorded**

That which they have sent forth refers to the deeds that will testify for or against them in the Hereafter, with the commentators citing 5:80, 75:13, and 82:5. The second term is more specific: that which they have left behind literally means their footsteps or their traces.

The commentators supply the occasion. It is said to refer to one of the tribes of Madinah, the Banū Salimah, who were settled far from the Prophet's mosque and desired to relocate closer to it. This verse was then revealed, and the Prophet said to them: Your footprints are recorded — that is, you receive reward for walking to the mosque. Why then do you want to move? (IK, Q, Ṭ, W). More broadly it is understood as a reference to the traces of good deeds that one leaves behind (Aj, Ṭ).

**The Three That Remain**

The commentators connect the phrase to a well-known ḥadīth: When the son of Adam dies, all his deeds come to an end except three — knowledge that is beneficial, a righteous child who prays for him, or ongoing charity that he leaves behind (IK); see 75:13.

The ḥadīth supplies the principle behind the verse's second term. Deeds sent forth are complete when the doer dies; traces left behind continue to accrue. The commentators treat the Banū Salimah report as the particular instance and the ḥadīth as the general rule, and both are cited without either being preferred.

**A Clear Registry**

And We have counted all things in a clear registry. The word rendered registry is *imām*, and the commentators record two readings. It is taken by some to allude to the inscription of all things from the beginning of time to its end on the Preserved Tablet (85:22; Aj, Ṭb, Ṭs), which is the *imām* — the leader — of all other books (Aj), and which is said to comprise all of God's Decrees (Ṭb, Ṭs); for the inscription of God's Decrees, see 68:1.

Others say *imām* refers to the book or registry in which all human actions are recorded (Aj, IK, Q, Ṭs); see 17:71 and 18:49. The commentators also note that this is one of the central verses cited by Shiite theologians as Qur'anic support for the Shiite understanding of Divinely ordained leadership in the form of the imamate after the death of the Prophet. In this vein al-Qummī relates a saying from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib: I, by God, am the clear *imām*; I clarify truth from falsehood. I inherited it from the Messenger of God (Qm, Ṭb). The three readings are recorded side by side.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the readings of either term. It does not say when the recording occurs, and does not describe the registry. It does not say whether the traces recorded are only good ones or both good and bad.

The verse also does not connect the recording to a judgement explicitly. It states that the dead are given life and that deeds and traces are recorded, and leaves the reckoning to the verses that follow. The commentators' note that the price of wrongdoing comes due upon the Resurrection supplies that connection from the verse's position in the argument rather than from its wording.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:13

> **And set forth for them as a parable the people of the town, when the message bearers came unto it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Set Forth a Parable**

And set forth for them as a parable the people of the town, when the message bearers came unto it. The commentators explain the command: the Prophet is here instructed to provide the Quraysh with an example of the fate that befell those who treated previous message bearers as they were treating him. The parable is therefore addressed to a present situation through a past one.

The commentators note that the first phrase could also be rendered Set forth for them a parable, the people of the town. The two renderings differ in whether the people are the parable or its subject, and the commentators record both without preferring either. What both share is that the account is offered as an example rather than as history for its own sake.

**The Town**

Most identify the town as Antioch (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). The commentators are equally clear about the limit of that identification: it cannot be verified (IK). The name is given because the tradition supplies it, not because the verse does.

The sūrah itself does not name the town, and the commentators do not treat the identification as load-bearing. What matters for the parable is the pattern — messengers sent, messengers denied, a town destroyed — and the pattern does not depend on the location. The identification is recorded because it was held, and flagged because it is uncertain.

**Message Bearers or Prophets**

The commentators distinguish the word used here. The verse says *mursalīn* rather than *rusul* — message bearers rather than messengers — and most commentators view them as apostles sent to the town by Jesus. On this interpretation, that God is said to have sent them indicates that Jesus was commanded by God to send them (Q, Ṭs).

A second opinion holds that the message bearers are prophets (Aj, Ṭs) rather than apostles sent by a prophet. The commentators say this interpretation seems more likely, and give their reason: the Prophet Muhammad is referred to as a *mursal* — a message bearer — at v3 of this very sūrah and at 2:252, and other prophets are referred to as *mursalūn* in over a dozen verses. The word is therefore established usage for prophets, not only for those sent by them.

**The Sequence of the Sending**

The commentators supply the order of events. After the first two were sent, the people of the town beat and imprisoned them (Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). Some say this punishment was ordered by the king (Aj), after which a third message bearer — whom some identify as Jonas — was sent.

The detail explains the structure of the next verse, where two are sent and then strengthened with a third. The commentators treat the imprisonment as the reason for the reinforcement, and the identification of the third as Jonas is recorded as what some say rather than as settled. The sūrah does not name any of the three.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name the town, the message bearers, or the period. It does not say how many people were in the town or what they believed before the messengers came. It does not say whether the messengers were sent to the whole town or to its rulers.

The verse also does not say what the outcome will be. It announces the parable and leaves the narrative to the verses that follow. The commentators' note that the Prophet was commanded to give the Quraysh an example supplies the purpose from the sūrah's context rather than from the verse's wording.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:14

> **When We sent two unto them, they denied them. So We strengthened them with a third, and they said, “Verily, we have been sent unto you!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two, Then a Third**

When We sent two unto them, they denied them. So We strengthened them with a third. The verse compresses the sequence the commentators describe: two were sent, the people denied them, and a third was sent to strengthen the first two. The commentators note that after the first two were sent the people beat and imprisoned them, and that the third came afterwards.

The reinforcement is the verse's notable feature. The denial does not end the mission; it brings a further sending. The sūrah's pattern elsewhere is warning, refusal, destruction, and here the pattern is interrupted by a strengthening before the outcome arrives.

**Verily, We Have Been Sent Unto You**

And they said, Verily, we have been sent unto you. The commentators note that this phrase here and at v16 could also be rendered we are message bearers unto you, and that the second statement at v16 is more emphatic than this first one because it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The first declaration is therefore the plainer of the two. The commentators treat the difference in emphasis as deliberate, and the sequence of the two verses as a single claim made twice with increasing force. The repetition is not redundancy; it is what a claim sounds like when it is refused and then repeated.

**Who Speaks**

The verse attributes the sending to God: When We sent two unto them. The commentators explain that on the reading which takes the message bearers as apostles sent by Jesus, the fact that God is said to have sent them indicates that Jesus was commanded by God to send them (Q, Ṭs). On the alternative reading they are prophets sent directly.

Both readings preserve the divine attribution. What differs is whether the sending is direct or mediated, and the commentators record both. The verse's own wording does not decide, and the identification of the message bearers at v13 leaves the question open.

**The Denial and Its Grounds**

The verse says they denied them without giving the reason. The reason comes at the next verse, where the people say You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. The denial is therefore not a refusal to engage but a specific objection, and the sūrah states it in the following verse rather than here.

The commentators connect that objection to a recurring pattern across the Qur'an, where communities reject prophets because they are human. The denial at v14 is thus the first instance of an objection the Qur'an records many times, and the sūrah's narrative is one case of a general pattern.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how the strengthening occurred — whether the third brought new evidence, new authority, or simply reinforcement in number. It does not say whether the two who were imprisoned were released.

The verse also does not say that the third succeeded where the first two failed. It states the strengthening and the declaration, and the outcome comes later in the narrative. The commentators do not gloss the verse as a success or a failure at this point.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:15

> **They said, “You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. You are but lying.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**You Are But Human Beings Like Us**

They said, You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. You are but lying. The objection has two parts: the messengers are human, and therefore nothing has been sent. The commentators note that in other passages many communities reject prophets because they are human, claiming that if God had wanted to send a message, He would have chosen an angel as His message bearer or messenger.

The reasoning is stated at 17:94: And naught hindered men from believing when guidance came unto them, save that they said, Has God sent a human being as a messenger? The commentators cite further instances at 6:8–9 and 6:50, 11:12, 14:10, 23:33, 41:14, and 64:6. The objection is therefore not particular to this town but a recurring response to prophecy.

**The Same Objection Against the Prophet**

The commentators note that in other verses this same objection is attributed to those who opposed the Prophet Muhammad: And they say, What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why is there not an angel sent down unto him to be a warner with him? (25:7).

The parallel is what makes the parable do its work. The Quraysh are hearing an account of a town that made their own argument, and the sūrah does not need to draw the comparison because the wording is recognisably the same. The commentators' cross-reference supplies the identification explicitly.

**The Compassionate Has Not Sent Down Anything**

The second half of the objection is a denial of revelation rather than of the messengers. The people do not say the messengers are mistaken about their own experience; they say nothing has been sent down at all. The charge of lying follows from that denial rather than from any particular claim.

The sūrah's opening answers this directly. Verse 5 described a revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful, and the verse uses the same divine name — the Compassionate — that the people here deny has sent anything. The commentators do not draw the connection explicitly, but the sūrah's structure supplies it.

**The Objection and Its Failure**

The commentators do not record a reply from the messengers to the humanity objection at this point. The next verse repeats the claim of the mission with greater emphasis rather than answering the argument. The messengers do not dispute that they are human; they reaffirm that they were sent.

This is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of the objection elsewhere. At 17:94 the objection is quoted as the thing that hindered belief, not as a position that is then refuted point by point. The sūrah treats it as a refusal rather than as an argument requiring an answer.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how many of the people made the objection or whether it was unanimous. It does not record any individual dissent from it, though the sūrah later describes a man who came from the farthest part of the city urging his people to follow the messengers.

The verse also does not say what kind of messenger the people would have accepted. They say the Compassionate has not sent down anything, which denies the sending rather than specifying an alternative. The commentators supply the angel objection from parallel verses rather than from this one.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:16

> **They said, “Our Lord knows. Verily we have indeed been sent unto you!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Our Lord Knows**

They said, Our Lord knows. Verily we have indeed been sent unto you. The messengers answer the charge of lying by appealing to God's knowledge rather than by producing evidence. The commentators note that the second statement of their mission is more emphatic than the first at v14, because it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The appeal is significant. The messengers do not dispute the town's account of what they have observed; they locate the question where it cannot be settled by observation. Our Lord knows is a claim about what is verifiable to God, and the reaffirmation that follows rests on it.

**The Increased Emphasis**

The commentators treat the difference between this verse and v14 as deliberate. The first declaration was Verily, we have been sent unto you; this one adds Our Lord knows and intensifies the affirmation. The reason given is that it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The sequence therefore records an argument that does not progress by new evidence but by repetition under pressure. The messengers have nothing further to offer except the claim itself, stated more firmly. The commentators do not read this as a weakness in the mission but as the appropriate response to a refusal that has not engaged the claim.

**The Appeal and the Charge**

The charge at v15 was You are but lying. The answer is Our Lord knows. The two are matched: a charge about truthfulness is answered by an appeal to the one who knows the truth. The commentators do not elaborate the exchange, but the sūrah's placement of the two verses makes the pairing clear.

The appeal also shifts the burden. The messengers do not attempt to prove the sending to the town; they state that God knows it. The town's denial is therefore left standing as a denial, and the verse does not offer a resolution that would satisfy it.

**The Verse in the Narrative**

This verse is the last word from the messengers before the narrative moves to the man from the farthest part of the city at v20. The commentators note at v17 that the messengers are only instructed to deliver the message and to guide those who heed it, and are not responsible for those who deny it.

The reaffirmation is therefore the completion of the delivery. Having stated the mission twice, the messengers have done what they were sent to do, and the sūrah's account of them ends with the claim standing against the denial. What follows concerns the town's response rather than the messengers' task.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not provide evidence for the sending, and does not offer a sign. It does not say what God's knowledge entails for the town, and does not threaten punishment here.

The verse also does not say how the messengers came to know they were sent. It reports their claim and their appeal, and leaves the origin of the mission to the account at vv 13–14, where the sending is attributed to God.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:17

> **And naught is incumbent upon us save the clear proclamation.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naught Is Incumbent Save the Clear Proclamation**

And naught is incumbent upon us save the clear proclamation. The messengers state the limit of their charge, and the verse closes their speech in the narrative. The commentators explain: like the Prophet Muhammad, the message bearers are only instructed to deliver the message and to guide those who heed it; they are not responsible for those who deny it.

The statement is a boundary rather than a concession. The messengers are not saying the mission is unimportant but that its scope is defined: proclamation is theirs, reception is not. The commentators direct the reader back to vv 10–11, where the same limit was stated for the Prophet — that he warns only whomsoever follows the Reminder.

**The Principle Across the Qur'an**

The commentators note that the prophets' responsibility being limited to delivering the message is emphasized in many passages, and cite 3:20, 5:92, 5:99, 13:40, 16:35, 16:82, 24:54, 29:18, 42:48, and 64:12. The recurrence is what allows the verse to state the principle in a single clause without argument.

The consistency matters for the parable's function. The Quraysh hearing this account are hearing a principle they have already encountered applied to the Prophet himself. The messengers in the town say what the Prophet says, and the sūrah's command at v13 to set forth the parable depends on that identity.

**Clear Proclamation**

The proclamation is qualified as clear. The commentators do not gloss the adjective here, but the sūrah's own vocabulary supplies it: the Qur'an was sworn by at v2 as Wise, described at v5 as a revelation, and named at v11 as the Reminder. The proclamation is clear because what is proclaimed has already been characterised.

The clarity is also what makes the denial culpable. At v9 the commentators described a people who cannot benefit from guidance no matter what the Prophet does, and at vv 10–11 the warning was said to make no difference to them. The clear proclamation is what distinguishes their refusal from ignorance.

**The End of the Messengers' Speech**

This is the last thing the messengers say in the sūrah. They declared the mission at v14, reaffirmed it at v16 under the charge of lying, and now state the limit of their responsibility. The commentators do not mark the closure explicitly, but the narrative moves to the town's response at v18 and then to the man from the outskirts at v20.

The sequence is complete: declaration, reaffirmation, statement of scope. The messengers have done what they were sent to do, and the verse says so in the plainest terms. What follows is not their task.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what happens to those who deny. It states the messengers' limit and leaves the consequence to the narrative that follows. It does not threaten punishment here, and does not predict the town's fate.

The verse also does not say that guidance is impossible for the deniers. It says the messengers are not responsible for them, which is a statement about the messengers rather than about the deniers' capacity. The commentators' cross-reference to vv 10–11 preserves the distinction.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:18

> **They said, “Truly we augur ill of you. If you cease not, we shall certainly stone you, and a painful punishment will certainly befall you from us.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly We Augur Ill of You**

The town answers the messengers with an omen and a threat in a single breath: they see ill in the messengers' arrival, and if the mission does not stop, stoning will follow and a painful punishment will come from the town itself. The commentators give the sense of the first phrase: this is the response of the disbelievers to the message bearers, meaning We see an ill omen or foretell a punishment that will befall you.

The augury is therefore not a vague unease but a prediction. The town attributes its own misfortune to the messengers' presence, and the commentators read the phrase as combining two things — an omen and a foretelling — in one statement.

**The Threat**

The second half is explicit: if you cease not, we shall certainly stone you. The commentators note at vv 13–14 that after the first two message bearers were sent, the people of the town beat and imprisoned them, and that some say this punishment was ordered by the king (Aj). The threat here continues that pattern.

The demand is cessation rather than silence. The town does not ask the messengers to leave or to stop speaking in public; it asks them to cease, which the commentators take as a demand that the mission end. The penalty attached is stoning, and the verse adds a painful punishment from us, which the commentators read as part of the same foretelling.

**The Omen and Its Logic**

The commentators cite 17:13 in connection with the next verse: And for every man We have fastened his omen upon his neck, and We shall bring it forth for him on the Day of Resurrection; see also 7:131 and 27:47. The cross-reference identifies the omen as belonging to the person rather than to the occasion.

This is what the messengers' reply at v19 turns on. The town locates its ill fortune in the messengers; the verse at 17:13 locates each person's omen upon his own neck. The sūrah's answer is therefore not that there is no omen but that it has been misattributed.

**The Demand and the Preceding Denial**

The threat follows the denial at v15 and the messengers' reaffirmation at v16. The commentators note that the second declaration was more emphatic because it was a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection. The town's response to the reaffirmation is not a further argument but a threat.

The sequence records an exchange that has stopped being an exchange. The messengers repeated their claim, and the town answered with violence rather than with reasons. The commentators do not gloss this transition, but the sūrah's placement of the two verses makes it plain.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say whether the threat was carried out. It reports the demand and the penalty attached to refusal, and the narrative moves on. The commentators' note about the beating and imprisonment at vv 13–14 concerns an earlier stage.

The verse also does not say what misfortune the town believed the messengers had brought. The augury is stated without content, and the commentators gloss it as a foretelling of punishment rather than as a diagnosis of a particular calamity.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:19

> **They said, “Your auguring ill is upon yourselves though you have been reminded? Nay, you are a prodigal people!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Your Auguring Ill Is Upon Yourselves**

They said, Your auguring ill is upon yourselves though you have been reminded? Nay, you are a prodigal people! The commentators explain the reply: the messengers respond that the punishment the disbelievers foretell will befall them because of their disbelief. The omen is returned to those who pronounced it.

The commentators note that this rebounding indicates the reflexive nature of good and evil deeds, which is emphasized throughout the Qur'an. They cite the pattern at length: if one spends in charity, one spends it for oneself, as at 2:272 — Whatever good you spend, it is for yourselves, when you spend only seeking the Face of God. Conversely, whosoever is miserly is only miserly unto himself (47:38); if one tries to deceive, one only deceives oneself (2:9); if one tries to lead astray, one only goes astray (3:69; 4:113); and if some devise a plot against God and His Messenger, God will devise a plot against them (52:42); see 4:111.

**The Three Readings of the Clause**

The commentators record three readings of the phrase rendered though you have been reminded. In the most widespread reading, *in dhukkirtum* is read *a-in dukkirtum*, meaning Even if you are reminded. In this reading the ellipsis indicates a rhetorical elision that constitutes the object of the interrogative and serves as a rebuke, as if to say: Do you augur ill and disbelieve, even when you have been reminded?

In another canonical reading, *a-in dhukkirtum* is read *ayna dhukkirtum*, meaning now that you have been reminded — *al-ān dhukkirtum* (Q, Ṭ). Another reading is *an dhukkirtum*, meaning because you have been reminded (Z). All three are canonical, and the commentators record them without preferring one. The sense differs: on the first the reminder is conceded and dismissed, on the second it is the occasion of the augury, on the third its cause.

**A Prodigal People**

Nay, you are a prodigal people. The commentators direct the reader to 10:12 for the meaning of the phrase. The word rendered prodigal denotes excess beyond proper bounds, and the charge is not that the town is mistaken but that it is extravagant in its error.

The accusation follows the return of the omen. Having said that the ill augury belongs to the town itself, the messengers characterise the town as excessive. The commentators do not elaborate the connection, but the sequence moves from a correction to a diagnosis.

**The Omen Upon the Neck**

The commentators' cross-reference to 17:13 supplies the principle behind the reply: And for every man We have fastened his omen upon his neck, and We shall bring it forth for him on the Day of Resurrection; see also 7:131 and 27:47. The omen is not a quality of events but a record attached to the person.

This is what makes the messengers' answer decisive rather than merely contradictory. The town said the messengers were an ill omen; the principle at 17:13 says each person carries his own. The reply is therefore not a counter-superstition but a correction of where the omen resides.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what punishment will befall the town, and does not predict its form. It returns the foretelling without specifying it, and the destruction comes later in the narrative.

The verse also does not say that the town cannot be reminded further. The three readings differ on the status of the reminder already given, and none states that further reminder is impossible. The commentators record the readings and leave the question open.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:20

> **And from the outskirts of the city, a man came running. He said, “O my people! Follow the message bearers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Man Came Running**

And from the outskirts of the city, a man came running. He said, O my people! Follow the message bearers! The commentators note that the man is usually identified by the name Ḥabīb al-Najjār. The identification is recorded as what is usually held rather than as settled, and the verse itself does not name him.

The detail of his coming from the outskirts is worth noting. He was not among those who had heard the messengers in the city centre and refused them; he came running from the edge. The commentators' accounts differ on what brought him, and the verse supplies only the movement.

**The Three Accounts of Him**

The commentators record three accounts. Some claim that he worshipped God in seclusion, and when the news of the message bearers came, he proclaimed his faith (Aj). Another interpretation says that Ḥabīb al-Najjār followed the first two message bearers after they healed his son (Aj, Q).

The third is a saying attributed to the Prophet: The foremost among the people are three who did not disbelieve in God for the blinking of an eye — ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, the companion of Yā Sīn, and the believer among the people of Pharaoh, at 40:28–29. They are the truthful — *ṣiddīq* (Sy, Th, Ṭs). All three accounts are preserved without preference, and they differ on how he came to believe rather than on whether he did.

**The Address to His People**

He said, O my people! Follow the message bearers! The address is to his own people, and the commentators do not gloss it further. The man identifies with the town he is addressing and urges it toward the messengers it has just threatened to stone.

The placement is what gives the verse its force. Verse 18 recorded the town's threat; verse 19 the messengers' reply; and now a member of the town speaks against his own. The commentators' note at v17 that the messengers are not responsible for those who deny does not exclude the possibility that one of them will believe, and this verse supplies it.

**The Exception to Most of Them**

At v7 the sūrah said the Word has come due for most of them. The qualifier most left room for an exception, and this verse is that exception appearing in the narrative. The commentators do not draw the connection explicitly, but the sūrah's structure supplies it.

The man's belief also answers the town's objection at v15 — that the messengers were but human beings like us. Here is a human being like them who followed, and the sūrah places his appearance immediately after the messengers have stated the limit of their responsibility. What they could not compel, one of the town chose.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name the man. The identification as Ḥabīb al-Najjār comes from the commentators, who record it as usual rather than certain. It does not say his age, his standing, or his occupation, though the name recorded for him has been read as indicating a trade.

The verse also does not say how many followed his urging. It records his appeal and moves to his argument in the next verses. The outcome for the town is given later, and the sūrah does not say whether his intervention changed it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:21

> **Follow those who ask not of you any reward and who are guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Follow Those Who Ask No Reward**

Follow those who ask not of you any reward and who are guided. The man from the outskirts gives his people a reason to follow the messengers, and the reason is not doctrinal. It is about what the messengers have not asked for.

The commentators explain the principle: a mark of prophethood is to ask for nothing from the people, except that they follow the message the prophets are sent to deliver. The absence of a demand is therefore not incidental to the mission but a sign of it, and the man offers it as evidence rather than as a moral observation.

**The Prophetic Precedent**

The commentators supply the precedent at length. As Noah says: And if you turn your backs, I have not asked of you any reward. My reward lies only with God, and I am commanded to be among those who submit (10:72). Thus the Prophet Muhammad is commanded at 6:90, 38:86, and 42:23: Say, I ask not of you any reward for it — see also 26:109, 127, 145, 164, and 180.

The recurrence across the Qur'an is what makes the man's argument available to him. He is not inventing a test; he is applying one that the prophets themselves have stated. The sūrah's own account of the messengers at vv 16–17 recorded their claim and the limit of their charge, and this verse adds the condition under which the charge was accepted.

**And Who Are Guided**

The second half of the verse adds a qualification: the messengers are guided. The commentators do not gloss the phrase separately, and the man does not argue for it. It stands beside the absence of reward as a second mark.

The two marks differ in kind. The first is observable — the messengers have asked for nothing, and the town can verify it. The second is a claim about their state. The man offers both without distinguishing them, and the commentators do not separate them either.

**The Argument and Its Audience**

The man addresses his own people, and the commentators do not gloss the address. What he offers is the kind of argument that works on people who have not considered the doctrine: the messengers want nothing from you, and they are guided. The first half requires no belief to assess.

This is consistent with his position in the narrative. He came running from the outskirts, and the commentators record differing accounts of how he came to believe. His appeal to his people is framed for those who have not yet made his own journey, and the reward question is where such an appeal would start.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what reward the messengers do receive. It states that they ask none of the people, and the commentators supply from Noah's words that the reward lies with God rather than with the addressed community.

The verse also does not say that asking for reward disqualifies a claimant. It offers the absence of a demand as a mark of prophethood, and does not state the converse. The commentators' note that this is a mark of prophethood is positive rather than exclusive.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:22

> **Why should I not worship Him who originated me, and unto Whom you will be returned?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Him Who Originated Me**

Why should I not worship Him who originated me, and unto Whom you will be returned? The man's argument turns on two facts about himself: he was originated by God, and he will return to God. The commentators explain the first term precisely: originated me translates *faṭaranī*, which derives from the same root as *fiṭrah*, or primordial nature.

The root connects the verse to a larger claim. The commentators cite 30:30: Set thy face to religion as a *ḥanīf*, in the primordial nature from God upon which He originated mankind — there is no altering the creation of God; that is the upright religion, but most of mankind know not. The same verb is used of the same act, and the word for the nature is built from it.

**Worship and the Primordial Nature**

The commentators draw the conclusion directly. Read in light of 30:30 and 51:56 — And I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me — this verse implies that the worship of God is central to the primordial nature with which human beings were created.

This is what makes the man's question rhetorical rather than open. Why should I not worship Him is not a request for reasons but a statement that no reason against it survives. If worship belongs to the nature one was originated with, then the question answers itself, and the commentators' reading makes that explicit.

**Unto Whom You Will Be Returned**

The second half shifts from the man to his audience: and unto Whom you will be returned. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, but the sūrah supplies the context. Verse 12 stated Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth, and the commentators there explained that the verse follows the counsel to leave the disbelievers to their devices since the price of their wrongdoing comes due upon the Resurrection.

The man's argument therefore has two terms, and both concern God's relation to human beings: origination at the beginning and return at the end. What falls between them is the worship he is asking about, and the verse places it inside that span.

**The Form of the Question**

The verse is a question directed at himself but posed publicly. The commentators do not gloss its form, and the sūrah does not mark it as addressed to anyone in particular. It follows the command to follow the messengers at v21 and precedes the rejection of other gods at v23.

The sequence is an argument in three moves: follow them, because I have no reason not to worship the One who made me, and taking other gods would leave me in manifest error. The question at v22 is the middle term, and its rhetorical form is what allows it to carry the weight without further support.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the return occurs, and does not describe it. It does not say what will happen at the return, and does not threaten punishment here.

The verse also does not say that his people were originated by the same God, though it implies it by addressing them about their return. The man states his own origination explicitly and their return as a shared fact, and the commentators do not elaborate the difference.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:23

> **Shall I take gods apart from Him? If the Compassionate desired harm for me, their intercession would avail me naught, nor would they save me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shall I Take Gods Apart From Him**

Shall I take gods apart from Him? The commentators give the sense: the question means Shall I take gods other than God, worship them, and leave the worship of the One Who is worthy of worship? (Sh). The gloss expands the question into its three parts — taking, worshipping, and abandoning — and identifies what makes it absurd.

The question follows directly from the previous verse. If worship belongs to the nature one was originated with, and if the One who originated is the One to whom one returns, then taking other gods requires abandoning the worship that the first verse established as central.

**Their Intercession Would Avail Me Naught**

If the Compassionate desired harm for me, their intercession would avail me naught, nor would they save me. The man states the practical consequence of taking other gods, and the commentators supply the doctrine behind it: the Qur'an indicates that angels and prophets will be able to intercede on the Day of Judgment, but that intercession is entirely by the Will of God.

The commentators cite 20:109: On that Day intercession will be of no benefit, save that of those whom the Compassionate has granted leave and with whose word He is content. Thus 2:255 asks rhetorically, Who is there who may intercede with Him save by His Leave? They direct the reader to 2:48 and 2:255 for the fuller treatment of intercession.

**The Argument Against Other Gods**

The man's reasoning is not that other gods do not exist but that they cannot help. The verse supposes the Compassionate desiring harm for him and asks what the other gods could do. The answer is that their intercession would avail nothing and they would not save him.

The commentators' note that intercession is entirely by God's Will is what makes the argument work. If intercession depends on permission, then a god who cannot grant permission cannot intercede, and taking such gods adds nothing to one's position. The man is not disputing their existence but their usefulness.

**The Verse in the Man's Speech**

This is the second of the man's three arguments. Verse 22 asked why he should not worship the One who originated him; verse 23 asks whether he should take others; verse 24 states what the alternative would make him. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the movement is from a positive ground to a rejected alternative to a consequence.

The three verses together are a complete case, and the man delivers it in front of the people who have just threatened to stone the messengers. The commentators record his identification as Ḥabīb al-Najjār and the differing accounts of his belief, but the argument itself stands without them.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that intercession is impossible. It says the intercession of other gods would not avail, and the commentators' note preserves the Qur'anic position that intercession exists but is by God's leave alone.

The verse also does not say what harm the Compassionate might desire. The supposition is hypothetical and is offered to test the usefulness of other gods rather than to assert that harm is intended. The commentators do not gloss the conditional as a prediction.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:24

> **Truly would I then be in manifest error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly Would I Then Be in Manifest Error**

Truly would I then be in manifest error. The man states the consequence of the alternative he rejected at v23. The commentators explain the phrase: manifest error — *ḍalāl mubīn* — is employed throughout the Qur'an to indicate one whose wrongdoing makes him stray from the path of truth (Ṭ).

The commentators add two further readings of the phrase. It can also be understood to mean error that makes manifest or makes clear. It is also read by some as *ḍalāl mubayyan* (Bḍ), which would then indicate error that has been made manifest or made clear. All three are recorded without preference.

**The Two Directions of the Phrase**

The readings differ in which direction the manifestness runs. On the commentators' first gloss the error is manifest to others — it is visible, obvious, not hidden. On the alternative reading the error itself makes manifest, which suggests it discloses something rather than merely being disclosed.

The commentators do not choose between these, and the phrase admits both. What they share is that the error is not concealed. The man is not describing a private mistake but a condition that would be evident, and the sūrah's use of the phrase elsewhere carries the same sense.

**The Conclusion of the Argument**

The verse closes the man's case. Verse 22 established the ground for worshipping the One who originated him; verse 23 rejected the alternative of other gods on the ground that they cannot intercede; verse 24 states what taking them would make him. The three move from ground to rejection to consequence.

The commentators do not gloss the verse as a conclusion, but its position and its brevity give it that function. After two questions the man makes a statement, and the statement is about himself rather than about his people. He does not say you would be in manifest error; he says truly would I then be.

**The Self-Accusation and the Appeal**

The man's argument has been about himself throughout: Him who originated me, if the Compassionate desired harm for me, would I then be. The commentators do not gloss this as a rhetorical strategy, but the effect is that he has not accused his people of anything.

This is consistent with the address at v20 — O my people! — and with the verse that follows, where he says Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me. The case is presented as his own reasoning rather than as a charge, and the commentators record the differing readings of who that final appeal is addressed to.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the error consists in beyond the taking of other gods. It states the condition and leaves its content to the previous verse.

The verse also does not say that his people are in that error. It describes what the man would be if he took the alternative, and the sūrah's account of the town's fate comes later. The commentators do not read the verse as a verdict on the people.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:25

> **Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly I Believe in Your Lord**

Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me. The man's declaration is the climax of his speech, and its form is notable: he speaks of your Lord rather than my Lord. The God he has just argued for at vv 22–24 is named as theirs.

The commentators do not gloss the possessive, but the sūrah's structure supplies its force. The man has been addressing his own people since v20, and the argument he has made has been about what is true for him and for them alike. Naming the Lord as theirs is the conclusion of that argument rather than a new claim.

**The Two Readings of So Listen to Me**

The commentators record two readings of the final appeal. According to some, so listen to me is a call to the people to follow God and His message bearers. Others say it is a call to the message bearers, asking them to bear witness for the man before God (IK).

The two readings differ on who is being addressed, and the verse's wording admits both. On the first the man is still speaking to the town, continuing the appeal that began at v20. On the second he turns to the messengers and asks them to testify for him. The commentators preserve both without preferring either.

**The Declaration After the Argument**

The man has made his case in three moves: the ground for worshipping the One who originated him, the rejection of other gods who cannot intercede, and the consequence of taking them. This verse is the statement that follows, and it is a statement about himself.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the movement from argument to declaration is what gives the verse its weight. He has not asked his people to accept his reasoning; he has announced that he believes, and then asked them to listen. The order matters: the belief comes first.

**The Verse Before the Outcome**

This is the last thing the man says in the narrative. The next verse records what was said to him, and the commentators supply what intervened: some say that after his declaration to the people, the man was stoned and killed (Aj, IK, Ṭs).

The sūrah does not narrate the killing. It moves from the declaration to the invitation into the Garden, and the commentators fill the gap. The verse's placement is therefore immediately before the outcome, and what the man asked for — that his people listen — is left unanswered in the text.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say whether his people listened. It records the appeal and moves on, and the sūrah's account of the town's fate comes at vv 28–29 without reference to any response to him.

The verse also does not say when he came to believe. The commentators record differing accounts — that he worshipped in seclusion and proclaimed his faith on hearing of the messengers, or that he followed the first two after they healed his son — and the verse supplies neither.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:26

> **It was said unto him, “Enter the Garden!” He said, “Would that my people knew**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Enter the Garden**

It was said unto him, Enter the Garden! The invitation comes without narrative transition, and the commentators supply what the verse omits. Some say that after his declaration to the people at v25, the man was stoned and killed (Aj, IK, Ṭs). Then he was told — by God, or by the angels and the righteous — Enter the Garden! (Aj, R).

The commentators also record the alternative: others maintain that God took him alive to heaven. The two accounts differ on whether he died before the invitation, and the verse does not decide. What both agree on is that the invitation came and that it came to him.

**Who Speaks**

The verse says it was said without naming the speaker. The commentators offer three possibilities: God, the angels, or the righteous (Aj, R). The passive construction is what allows all three, and the commentators do not choose.

The ambiguity is characteristic of the sūrah's treatment of this narrative. The town is unnamed, the messengers are unnamed, and the man is named only in the commentators. The invitation is given in the same register — stated as a fact without attribution.

**The Immediacy of the Invitation**

The commentators' account places the invitation immediately after the declaration, and on the majority reading immediately after the killing. The man asked his people to listen, and what follows is not their response but his entry.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence as a consolation, but the sūrah's placement gives it that shape. The man's appeal goes unanswered in the narrative, and the next thing recorded is his admission to the Garden. The commentators' note that some say he was stoned while praying for his people to be guided supplies the contrast.

**The Garden and the Sūrah's Argument**

The man's entry is the sūrah's first concrete instance of the reward promised at v11, where the Prophet is told to give glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward to whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. The commentators there explained that a generous reward means Paradise or the Garden.

The connection is not stated in the text, but the sūrah's structure supplies it. The promise at v11 is general and the case at v26 is particular. The man followed the messengers and feared the Compassionate, and the reward named in the abstract is here shown being given.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how he entered, and does not describe the Garden. It records the invitation and leaves the entry to be inferred. It does not say whether he entered alone.

The verse also does not say whether his people witnessed any of this. The next verse has him wishing they knew, which implies they did not, and the commentators do not gloss the difference between what he saw and what they saw.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:27

> **how my Lord forgave me and placed me among the honored.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Would That My People Knew**

He said, Would that my people knew how my Lord forgave me and placed me among the honored. The man's first words in the Garden are a wish about those who killed him, and the commentators supply the context that makes the wish significant.

According to Qatādah — who died in 117/726 and is described by the commentators as one of the foremost commentators from the third generation of Muslim scholars — the people began to stone him while he was praying for God to guide them, and as they continued to stone him, he continued to pray for them, even while suffering a gruesome death at their hands (IK).

**The Prayer That Did Not Stop**

Qatādah's account is what gives the wish its shape. The man was praying for his people's guidance while they killed him, and the prayer did not stop when the stoning began. The wish in the Garden continues it: he wants them to know what he has been given.

The commentators record this as Qatādah's account rather than as the verse's statement. The verse itself says only that he wished his people knew, and the commentators supply the continuity between the prayer during the stoning and the wish afterwards.

**How My Lord Forgave Me**

The commentators note that this verse could also mean for what my Lord forgave me and that He placed me among the honored (Ṭs), which may imply the bad deeds that were forgiven or the good deed or deeds for which God forgave him his sins.

The alternative reading changes the sense of the preposition. On the standard reading the man wishes his people knew the manner of the forgiveness; on the alternative he wishes they knew the reason for it. The commentators preserve both, and neither identifies the specific deeds in question.

**Placed Among the Honored**

The second half names what he was given: a place among the honored. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the sūrah does not describe the company. What the verse states is that the forgiveness and the placement are joined, and that the man reports them together.

The wish is therefore about both. He does not wish his people knew that he was forgiven, or that he was honored, but how the Lord did both. The commentators' note on the alternative reading keeps the two connected — the forgiveness and the placement are reported as one thing.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what his people would have done had they known. The wish is stated without a consequence, and the sūrah does not supply one. It does not say whether any of them came to know later.

The verse also does not say that the man forgave his people. It records his wish that they knew what he had been given, and Qatādah's account of his prayer for their guidance is recorded by the commentators rather than stated in the verse.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:28

> **And after him We did not send down a host from Heaven against his people; nor would We send down.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Host From Heaven**

The verse states what was *not* sent: no heavenly host was dispatched against the man's people after him, and none would be. The negation is the verse's whole content, and the commentators record two readings of what the phrase means.

The verse is taken by many as a reference to the punishment that God inflicted upon his people for rejecting the message bearers and for killing — or seeking to kill — Ḥabīb al-Najjār. On this reading it indicates that God wiped them out with a single cry, at v29, rather than sending angels against them (IK, Q).

**The Alternative: No Further Revelation**

Others say that a host from Heaven refers to the angels of revelation (Ṭs), meaning that God did not send another revelation after having sent the message bearers (Ṭ). On this reading the verse is not about the punishment but about the cessation of sending.

The commentators say which reading they think fits better, and give their reason. The second interpretation is more consistent with this story in its function as a warning to the Quraysh, for it implies that accepting the revelation sent to the Prophet Muhammad is their only opportunity and they will not receive another. The preference is stated as an interpretive judgement rather than as a fact about the verse.

**Why the Reasoning Matters**

The commentators' preference connects the parable to its audience. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town as a parable for the Quraysh, and the commentators explained that the purpose was to give an example of the fate of those who treated previous message bearers as they were treating him.

On the first reading the parable warns of destruction. On the second it warns that no further messenger will come. The commentators take the second as better suited to the Quraysh's situation, and the sūrah's command at v13 is what makes that suitability relevant.

**The Final Phrase**

The commentators note that the last phrase — nor would We send down — could also be rendered nor what We used to send down (Ka, Ṭs). The alternative reading makes the negation concern a past practice rather than a future intention.

The difference is subtle but real. On the standard reading the verse says God would not send; on the alternative it says God does not send what He used to send. The commentators record both, and the second sits more naturally with the reading that takes the host as the angels of revelation.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the punishment was. It states what was not sent, and the cry comes at the next verse. It does not say how many were destroyed or when.

The verse also does not say that the people were destroyed for killing the man specifically. The commentators describe the punishment as for rejecting the message bearers and for killing or seeking to kill Ḥabīb al-Najjār, and the verse itself gives no reason.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:29

> **It was but a single cry. Then, behold, they were extinguished.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Cry**

It was but a single cry. Then, behold, they were extinguished. The commentators' note on this verse is brief and states its point directly: the verse indicates that the destruction for having disbelieved was instant.

The brevity of the commentary matches the brevity of the verse. There is no elaboration to give because the verse supplies none — one cry, and the people are extinguished. The commentators do not describe the cry, and the sūrah does not say where it came from.

**Extinguished**

The word rendered extinguished is drawn from the vocabulary of putting out a flame. The commentators do not gloss it, and the verse does not elaborate. What the image carries is finality: a fire that is extinguished is not diminished or weakened but ended.

The sūrah uses the same economy elsewhere. At v8 the disbelievers were described as shackled and at v9 as veiled, and the commentators read those verses as describing a condition. Here the condition ends in an event, and the event is described in two words.

**The Sequence With the Previous Verse**

Verse 28 stated what was not sent — no host from Heaven. This verse states what was sent instead, and the contrast is the point. The commentators explain at v28 that on the majority reading the verse indicates God wiped them out with a single cry rather than sending angels against them (IK, Q).

The two verses therefore work as a pair. The negation at v28 prepares for the cry at v29, and the commentators' reading makes the connection explicit. The punishment was not a military destruction but a single sound, and the sūrah's economy in describing it is part of the claim.

**The Instantaneity and the Warning**

The commentators note at v28 that the reading which takes the host as the angels of revelation is more consistent with the story's function as a warning to the Quraysh, since it implies that accepting the revelation sent to the Prophet Muhammad is their only opportunity. The instantaneity of the destruction supports that warning.

A punishment that comes in a single cry leaves no interval in which to respond. The sūrah's parable was commanded at v13 precisely so that the Quraysh would see what happened to a town that mocked its messengers, and the speed of the outcome is what makes the parable urgent rather than merely illustrative.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how many were extinguished, and does not say whether any survived. It does not describe the cry, and does not say whether the believers among them were affected. The commentators' note that the man had entered the Garden implies he was not, but the verse does not connect the two.

The verse also does not say that the destruction was the final word. The next verse moves to a lament over the servants, and v32 states that each group will be arraigned. The instantaneity described here concerns this world, and the sūrah's later verses concern what follows.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:30

> **Alas for the servants! Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alas for the Servants**

Alas for the servants! Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him. The commentators note the parallel at 15:11 and record two readings of the exclamation. It is interpreted by most to indicate amazement at the extreme injustice disbelievers do to themselves and the punishment they will suffer (IK, Q).

On this reading the lament is directed at the disbelievers, and its content is the harm they have done to themselves. The commentators connect this to the reflexive principle they described at v19 — that good and evil rebound upon their doer — and the exclamation is what that principle sounds like when it is observed.

**The Alternative: the Servants as Prophets**

Others take servants as a reference to the prophets, in which case the verse expresses God's lament for the hardships they endure when mocked by disbelievers (Q, Ṭs). On this reading the exclamation is not about the disbelievers' self-harm but about what the messengers suffer.

The two readings differ on who is being lamented, and the commentators preserve both. The verse's wording admits either, since the word rendered servants is general. What both readings share is that the lament concerns the pattern stated in the second half of the verse: every messenger was mocked.

**Never a Messenger But They Mocked Him**

The second half states the pattern without exception. Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him. The commentators do not gloss the universality of the claim, and the sūrah's own parable supplies one instance of it.

The pattern is what connects the parable to the Quraysh. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town as an example for those treating him as they were treating him. This verse generalises: it was not only that town, and it is not only him.

**The Verse's Position**

The verse stands between the end of the parable at v29 and the argument from the destroyed generations at v31. The commentators do not gloss its placement, but the movement is from a particular case to a general statement to a question about the evidence.

The lament is therefore a hinge. The parable has shown one town mocking its messengers and being extinguished; this verse says the mockery was universal; and the next verse asks whether the hearers have not considered how many generations were destroyed. The exclamation is what carries the reader from the story to the argument.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who speaks. The exclamation is unattributed in the Arabic, and the commentators' two readings differ on whether it expresses amazement at the disbelievers or lament for the prophets without settling who utters it.

The verse also does not say that the mocking always ended in destruction. It states the pattern of mockery, and the destruction comes at v31 as a question rather than as a claim. The sūrah separates the two.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:31

> **Have they not considered how many generations before them We destroyed, such that they return not unto them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Have They Not Considered**

Have they not considered how many generations before them We destroyed, such that they return not unto them? The verse is a question rather than a statement, and the commentators note that its pronouns admit three readings: they and them can refer to the former peoples who were destroyed, to the inhabitants of Makkah who deny the prophethood of the Prophet Muhammad, or to both.

The commentators do not choose between the three, and the verse's wording supports each. The ambiguity is productive rather than confusing: on any of the readings the question asks the hearer to look at what happened to those who came before.

**How Many Generations**

The verse does not give a number. It says how many, which puts the quantity in question form and leaves the hearer to supply it. The sūrah's own parable at vv 13–29 supplies one instance, and the Qur'an elsewhere supplies many more.

The commentators do not enumerate the generations, and the verse does not require it. What the question asks is not for a count but for consideration — the act of looking at the record and drawing what follows from it.

**Such That They Return Not**

The clause that they return not unto them states the finality of the destruction. The commentators do not gloss it, and the verse does not elaborate. What it excludes is any return of the destroyed generations to those who came after.

The finality is what makes the question urgent. If the destroyed generations could return, the record would be revisable. Since they cannot, what happened to them is settled, and the hearer's consideration of it is consideration of a closed case.

**The Question and Its Audience**

The commentators' note that the pronouns may refer to the inhabitants of Makkah connects the verse to the parable's purpose. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town for the Quraysh, and this verse asks them directly whether they have considered the generations.

The question form is what suits that audience. A statement could be denied; a question about what they already know cannot. The commentators do not gloss the rhetoric, but the sūrah's use of questions elsewhere — at v22 in the man's speech, and at v78 later — follows the same pattern.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say why the generations were destroyed. It asks whether the hearers have considered how many were destroyed, and the reason is supplied by the surrounding verses rather than by this one.

The verse also does not say that the same will happen to the hearers. It asks a question about the past and leaves the application to the reader. The commentators' note that the pronouns may refer to the Makkans supplies the connection without the verse stating it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:32

> **Each of them shall be a group arraigned before Us.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Each of Them Shall Be a Group Arraigned**

Each of them shall be a group arraigned before Us. The commentators explain the scope: this verse and v53 indicate that all human beings, believers and disbelievers alike, will be arraigned on the Day of Judgment.

The arraignment is collective. The verse says each of them shall be a group, and the commentators read this as the gathering of all human beings rather than as an individual reckoning. The grouping is by community, and the sūrah's parable has just shown one such community destroyed as a unit.

**The Apparent Tension**

The commentators note a difficulty and resolve it. Other verses indicate that only the disbelievers will be arraigned unto the punishment, citing 30:16 and 34:38. It appears that these verses indicate two arraignments: the first is the gathering of all human beings referred to here and at v53; the second, which comes after the gathering of all human beings, is the Reckoning the disbelievers will endure before they are cast into Hell.

The resolution is not imposed on the text but drawn from comparing it. The commentators identify two distinct events and assign the verses to them, which preserves both sets of statements without making either false. The reading is presented as what appears to be the case rather than as settled.

**The Gathering and the Reckoning**

The distinction matters for what the sūrah is claiming. The gathering is universal and the reckoning is not. At v12 the commentators explained that We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth, and at v103 of Sūrah Hūd the Day is described as one on which mankind shall be gathered together.

The arraignment here is the gathering, and the commentators' second arraignment is what follows for some. The verse itself does not distinguish the two, and the commentators supply the distinction from the wider Qur'anic usage.

**Before Us**

The arraignment is before Us, and the commentators do not gloss the phrase. What it establishes is that the grouping is not merely a gathering but an appearance, and the sūrah's earlier verses have prepared for it.

At v7 the commentators explained that the Word has come due for most of them, and at v12 that deeds and traces are recorded. The arraignment is where the record is presented, and the verse's brevity leaves the procedure to those earlier statements.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what happens at the arraignment. It states that each group will be arraigned, and the commentators supply the two-stage reading from other verses rather than from this one.

The verse also does not say how the groups are constituted. It says each of them shall be a group, and the commentators do not specify whether the grouping is by community, by period, or by allegiance. The sūrah's parable suggests community, but the verse does not state it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Subdued for Food and Travel**

Large animals submit to smaller human beings, permitting transport and nourishment. This ordinary asymmetry is itself a sign, not merely a biological fact taken for granted.

**A Word Carried by the Arabic**

In this verse, dhallalnāhā means We made them tractable and serviceable.

**The Qur'an Explains the Qur'an**

The meaning belongs within the Qur'an's wider teaching; compare Qur'an 22:36.

**Subdued for Food and Travel: The Wider Scriptural Argument**

> *“Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create their like? Yes indeed; and He is the Knowing Creator.”* (Qur’an 36:81)

Ibn ʿAbbās and later commentators report a Meccan objector bringing a decayed bone, commonly identified as Ubayy ibn Khalaf, and challenging resurrection. The Qur'an answers at several levels: the questioner forgot his origin; first creation establishes power; fire from green wood shows apparently opposed states emerging by God's arrangement; and the heavens establish the greater-scale premise. The argument culminates not in a disclosed mechanism but effective command—God's will meets no resistance, fatigue, missing information, or shortage of means.

**The Verse Brought into Daily Life**

Benefit does not authorize cruelty. The Giver of usefulness also judges how the gift is handled.
