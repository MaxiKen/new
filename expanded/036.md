# Sūrah Yā Sīn (Chapter 36) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Makkan Proclamation at the Heart of Faith**

Sūrah Yā Sīn is a Makkan chapter of eighty-three verses. Its dominant setting is the struggle of revelation against settled denial: it vindicates Muhammad ﷺ as a messenger, displays a community that rejected envoys and the courageous believer who defended them, reads resurrection through the revived earth and ordered heavens, and ends by answering the objection that decayed bones cannot live again. Its movement is deliberate. Revelation announces; history warns; creation demonstrates; the trumpet gathers; Paradise and Hell disclose the two ends; and the final verses return the reader to God's unrestricted creative command.

Reports calling Yā Sīn “the heart of the Qur'an,” promising tenfold reward for it, or assigning particular worldly outcomes to its recitation are widely quoted, but their chains are disputed or weak. Scholarly reverence does not need uncertain attribution. The report, “Recite Yā Sīn over your dying,” is recorded by Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah and has also been disputed in grading. Muslims may recite Qur'an for blessing while avoiding confident claims the Prophet ﷺ cannot securely be shown to have made. The chapter's established excellence lies first in its being God's speech and in the immense truths its text plainly carries.

**One Argument Developed Through Eighty-Three Signs**

The chapter binds prophethood and resurrection together. If the Wise Qur'an truly comes from the Mighty, Merciful Lord, its warning of return must be heard. If dead soil lives, embryos become articulate adversaries, green wood yields fire, and the heavens were created by command, restoring bones cannot rationally be called impossible. Thus nature is not offered as decoration. Each ordinary phenomenon becomes evidence in a cumulative case.

The commentary below treats every verse independently, including verses grouped in the source. Direct Qur'anic and Prophetic quotations are blockquoted. Classical attributions are stated cautiously: where reports differ, possibility is distinguished from certainty; where revelation speaks of God's attributes, they are affirmed in a manner befitting Him, without resemblance to creation and without speculative description.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:1

> **Yā. Sīn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Separated Letters**

The sūrah opens with two letters of the Arabic alphabet recited by name: *yāʾ* and *sīn*. They belong to the class known as the separated letters — *al-muqaṭṭaʿāt* — which stand at the beginning of twenty-nine of the Qur'an's one hundred and fourteen sūrahs. The commentators at 2:1 note that in recitation the names of the letters are used rather than their sounds, which is why they are transliterated here as they are recited.

The majority position on their meaning is that it is known only to God. That is not a confession of ignorance about the text but a claim about it: these letters are not words, and no reading of them as words is compelled. The commentators record the readings that follow without requiring any of them, and the sūrah itself does not gloss them.

**The Reading 'O Human Being'**

Some allow that *yāʾ sīn* could be an abbreviation meaning O human being (Q). On this interpretation the *yāʾ* is the vocative particle O, used in many Qur'anic verses, and the *sīn* abbreviates *unsayn*, the diminutive of *insān* — human being. The commentators note that in this context the diminutive O little human being functions as a term of endearment, and is read as God's address to the Prophet Muhammad.

The reading has a consequence for what follows. If the opening is an address to the Prophet, then the oath at v2 and the affirmation at v3 attach to a person already named, and the sequence is vocative, oath, declaration. The commentators at vv 3–4 note exactly this: those verses affirm the Prophet's mission and thus support the interpretations that see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself.

**A Name Given to the Prophet**

Others say that Yā Sīn is a name given to the Prophet by God whose exact meaning is unknown (Q). For this reason it is sometimes used in the Islamic world as a name for a male. The commentators cite the report from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (IA, Q): I heard the Messenger of God say, Verily God has named me by seven names in the Quran — Muhammad, Aḥmad, Ṭā Hā, Yā Sīn, thou enwrapped, thou who art covered, and servant of God — with the cross-references 3:144, 33:40, 47:2 and 48:29 for Muhammad; 61:6 for Aḥmad; 20:1 for Ṭā Hā; 73:1 and 74:1 for the two enwrapped addresses; and 72:19 for servant of God.

The list places Yā Sīn among names the Qur'an itself supplies, which is what allows it to be used as a personal name. The report is preserved as a report; the commentators do not present it as settling the meaning of the letters, only as one account of how the sequence came to be understood as a name.

**The Other Readings**

Two further interpretations are recorded. Other commentators take Yā Sīn to be a name of the Qur'an itself. Al-Qushayrī identifies it with the Day of the Covenant — *yawm al-mīthāq* — when God made a covenant with all the children of Adam, as at 7:172.

The four readings are not competing claims about a single fact so much as different accounts of what kind of thing the opening is: an address, a name of the Prophet, a name of the Book, or an allusion to a primordial event. The commentators set them side by side. What they share is the assumption that the letters carry significance; what divides them is the direction of that significance, and the verse does not decide.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not explain the letters, and does not indicate which of the recorded readings is intended. It does not connect them to the oath that follows, and does not say whether they are an address, a title, or something else.

The verse also does not distinguish Yā Sīn from the separated letters at the head of the other twenty-eight sūrahs. The commentators treat it within that class, and the majority position — that the meaning is known only to God — applies to all of them equally. The particular readings offered here are offered for this sequence because the sūrah's own content, at vv 3–4, appears to support one of them.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:2

> **By the Wise Quran,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by the Book**

By the Wise Quran. The sūrah swears by the Qur'an before making its declaration, and the thing sworn by is characterised by a single adjective. The oath form is familiar from the Makkan sūrahs, where God swears by created things and by revelation alike, and the effect is to establish the ground on which what follows will rest.

What follows is the affirmation of the Prophet's mission at v3. The oath by the Qur'an and the declaration about the messenger are therefore joined: the Book is the warrant for the claim about the man. The commentators note at vv 3–4 that those verses affirm the Prophet's mission, and the oath here is what the affirmation is sworn upon.

**Ḥakīm as Determined**

*Ḥakīm*, here translated Wise, can also mean determined or made firm — *muḥkam* — as at 11:1, where the Qur'an is described as a Book whose signs have been determined, *uḥkimat* (Q). The commentators cite Ibn Kathīr for the consequence: that the Qur'an is *muḥkam* indicates that falsehood comes not upon it from before it or from behind it, as at 41:42.

On this reading the adjective describes the Book's resistance to alteration. Its signs have been fixed, and nothing can be inserted into it or removed from it. The commentators do not treat this as the only sense of *ḥakīm*, but as one of its established senses, and the cross-reference to 11:1 supplies the same root used of the same Book.

**Ḥakīm as Making Wise**

*Ḥakīm* could also mean something that makes wise — *muḥkim* — indicating that the Qur'an teaches the truth. The commentators note that Ibn Kathīr takes the use of *ḥakīm* here as an allusion to the Qur'an as a revealed book, and that the Qur'an is described as wise elsewhere, at 10:1, 31:2, and 43:3–4.

The two readings differ in direction. On the first the adjective describes what the Book is: settled, unalterable. On the second it describes what the Book does: it makes the one who follows it wise. The commentators preserve both, and the word's range admits both without strain, since a book whose signs are fixed can also be a book that instructs.

**The Adjective and the Sūrah's Argument**

The choice of *ḥakīm* is not incidental to what the sūrah will do. Yā Sīn's central movement is an argument about resurrection, conducted with the disbelievers of Makkah, and the argument proceeds by reasoning from what is already known to what is being denied. A book described as wise is a book that argues.

The commentators do not draw this connection explicitly at v2, and the verse does not claim it. What the verse establishes is the character of the authority being invoked. The sūrah then uses that authority in the way the adjective suggests — not by assertion alone but by putting cases, drawing analogies, and asking questions that the hearer must answer.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what is being sworn to. It supplies the oath and stops, leaving the declaration to the next verse. It does not explain in what respect the Qur'an is wise, and does not choose between the readings of *ḥakīm* recorded by the commentators.

The verse also does not say who swears. The oath form is unattributed in the Arabic, and the commentators do not supply an attribution here. The sūrah's later verses make clear that the speaker is God, but this verse leaves the oath standing on its own, which is characteristic of the form.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:3

> **truly thou art among the message bearers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Affirmation of the Mission**

Truly thou art among the message bearers. The declaration that the oath at v2 was sworn upon is now made. The emphatic construction leaves no room for qualification: the Prophet is among those who bear a message, and the sūrah states it as settled.

The commentators note that these verses affirm the Prophet's mission, and that the affirmation supports the interpretations which see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself. The connection is what gives the opening its shape: if the letters address the Prophet, then the oath and the declaration are addressed to him too, and the three verses form a single movement of naming, swearing, and confirming.

**Among, Not Alone**

The verse says among the message bearers, not that he is the message bearer. The preposition places him within a company, and the company is one the Qur'an describes at length elsewhere. Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb, and Moses have all been named in the preceding sūrahs as bearers of the same kind of message to the same kind of resistance.

The placement matters for the argument the sūrah will make. A messenger among messengers is not an innovation, and the opposition he faces is not unprecedented. The sūrah's account of the people of the town at vv 13–29 depends on this: what happens there has happened before, and the outcome is known.

**The Sequence of the Opening**

Read in order, the three verses move from an address to a warrant to a claim. Yā Sīn — if it is an address — names the one being spoken to. By the Wise Quran supplies the authority. Truly thou art among the message bearers states what that authority establishes.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence as such, and the verses do not announce their structure. What they achieve is that the Prophet's mission is not asserted at the opening of the sūrah but sworn to, and sworn to by the Book that constitutes the mission. The affirmation is therefore self-supporting in a way a bare declaration would not be.

**The Address and Its Recipient**

The declaration is in the second person singular and is addressed to the Prophet. The sūrah's opening is therefore not a general statement about prophethood but a particular statement to one man, and the commentators' note that these verses affirm his mission takes it that way.

This is consistent with the Makkan context the commentators describe for the sūrah. It was revealed in the early part of the middle Makkan period, when the opposition was intensifying and the Prophet's standing was being contested. An affirmation of the mission is a response to a contest, and the verse's emphatic form is what a response to a contest looks like.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the message is, and does not summarise it. It affirms that the Prophet bears one and leaves the content to the sūrah that follows. It does not name the other message bearers, and does not say how many there were.

The verse also does not say that the mission will succeed in the sense of being accepted. It states the fact of the mission rather than its outcome, and the sūrah's own narrative at vv 13–29 shows a community that rejected its messengers. The affirmation concerns the sending, not the reception.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:4

> **upon a straight path,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Upon a Straight Path**

The mission affirmed at v3 is now qualified: it is upon a straight path. The commentators direct the reader to 1:6 for the phrase, where guide us upon the straight path is understood as a prayer for perseverance in following that path and thus for continued aid (Ṭ), and for being made firm in following the way of truth (Ṭs).

The glosses at 1:6 add that the request for guidance implies seeking to be led to God Himself, and thus a desire for intimacy with Him, nearness to Him (Q), knowledge of Him, and love for Him. The path is therefore not merely a route but a relationship, and the commentators read it that way in the sūrah where the phrase is prayed rather than described.

**The Path and the Message**

The attachment of the path to the mission is what the verse contributes. The Prophet is among the message bearers, and his is upon a straight path. The commentators do not gloss the connection, but the phrase does two things at once: it characterises the message and distinguishes it from other claims.

A straight path is one without deviation, and the Qur'an uses the phrase to contrast the way of the messengers with the ways of those who opposed them. The sūrah's later verses will show the alternative in practice — a town that killed its messengers, and a people who said our auguring ill is upon yourselves. The path named here is what those alternatives are measured against.

**Straightness in the Qur'an**

The phrase recurs throughout the Qur'an and is not local to this sūrah. The commentators' cross-reference to 1:6 places it in the daily prayer, where it is asked for rather than asserted. The difference between the two uses is significant: in al-Fātiḥah the believer asks to be guided upon it, and here the Prophet is declared to be upon it.

The commentators note at 1:6 that for those who believe and perform righteous deeds, their Lord guides them by their faith, citing 10:9 (Ṭs). The guidance is therefore continuous rather than a single bestowal, and the path is something one remains upon rather than something one enters once. The Prophet's being upon a straight path is a standing condition, not an achievement.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not define the straight path, and does not enumerate what it consists in. It attaches the phrase to the mission and leaves the content to the Qur'an's wider use of it, which the commentators supply by cross-reference rather than by explanation.

The verse also does not say that others are not upon it. It describes the Prophet's mission and does not exclude anyone else. The sūrah's later verses distinguish those who follow the messengers from those who reject them, but this verse makes no such division; it states where the mission lies and moves on.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:5

> **a revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful**

The oath at v2 and the affirmation at vv 3–4 are now given their source. The message is a revelation, and it comes from God described by two names. The commentators note that revelation can be read as the object of an implied verb — We revealed it as a revelation — in which case it refers back to the Wise Quran, or in the nominative, meaning It is a revelation.

Both readings are preserved. The grammatical ambiguity matters less than what both share: the message is not the Prophet's own composition but something sent to him. The sūrah has moved in four verses from an address to a warrant to a claim to a source, and this verse names the source.

**The Two Readings of What Is Revealed**

Most commentators say that revelation in either reading refers to the Qur'an, although some say it refers to the Prophet (Q, R), in which case the pronoun in the elided phrase would be rendered he rather than it. The commentators support this by noting that the Prophet is also said to be sent down, as at 65:10–11: God has certainly sent down unto you a reminder: a Messenger reciting unto you the clear signs of God.

The reading is not arbitrary. If the revelation is the Prophet himself, then the sūrah's opening — which the commentators take as possibly addressing him by name — closes its first movement by identifying him as the thing sent. Both readings are recorded without preference, and the commentators say plainly that both are possible.

**The Mighty and the Merciful**

The verse names God as the Mighty, the Merciful. The commentators observe that the juxtaposition of these two names here and in other verses — especially throughout Sūrah 26 — is seen as an allusion to God being both just and merciful, vengeful toward those who oppose Him and merciful toward those who obey Him.

The pairing is not a compromise between two attributes but a statement about how both operate. Might without mercy would make the warning at v6 a bare threat; mercy without might would make it empty. The sūrah needs both to say what it says next: a warner has been sent to a people who were never warned, and the Word has come due for most of them.

**The Sequence of the Opening Five Verses**

The commentators note at vv 3–4 that those verses affirm the Prophet's mission and thus support the interpretations which see Yā Sīn as a reference to the Prophet himself. Verse 5 completes that movement by supplying the origin of the mission. What is sworn by at v2, affirmed at v3, qualified at v4, is now attributed.

The order is deliberate. The authority is invoked before the claim is made, and the source is named after the claim rather than before it. The sūrah does not begin with a statement about God but with an address and an oath, and arrives at the divine names only once the messenger's standing has been established.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the readings of revelation, and does not say whether what is sent down is the Book or the man. It does not explain the two names, and does not say in what proportion might and mercy operate.

The verse also does not say to whom the revelation is sent. That is supplied by the previous verse — a people whose fathers were not warned — and this verse concerns its origin rather than its destination. The sūrah separates the two questions and answers them in turn.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:6

> **that thou mayest warn a people whose fathers were not warned; so they were heedless.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A People Whose Fathers Were Not Warned**

That thou mayest warn a people whose fathers were not warned; so they were heedless. The verse states the purpose of the sending and identifies the recipients by an absence: no warner had come to their fathers. The commentators note that this is similar to 32:3, which states that the Qur'an was sent so that the Prophet might warn a people to whom no warner had come before.

The absence is confirmed elsewhere. At 34:44 God says of the Arabs: We have not given them any books that they study; nor have We sent them a warner before. The commentators are careful to qualify this: although the Arabs had been sent messengers before in the intervening generations from the time of Ishmael — who was both a prophet and the progenitor of the Arabs, as Isaac was of the Jews — the pure monotheism of Abraham had become lost, and from the point of view of the Arabs the religion of their fathers was pagan.

**The Loss Between Ishmael and Muhammad**

The qualification is what makes the verse historically precise rather than merely rhetorical. There had been messengers, but the message had not survived. The commentators describe a lineage that began with Abraham's monotheism and arrived at paganism, and they treat the intervening loss as the reason a new warner was needed.

This explains the phrase whose fathers were not warned. It does not mean the fathers had never been addressed; it means the address had not reached them intact. The heedlessness named at the end of the verse is therefore not original ignorance but inherited loss, and the commentators' account of the generations from Ishmael is what fills in that distinction.

**An Ominous Tone**

The commentators note that read in connection with the following verse and the context of the entire Qur'an, there is an ominous tone to this verse. The reason is given: as other verses state, Thy Lord never destroys towns until He sends a messenger to their mother city to recite unto them Our signs (28:59), and Never did We destroy a town save that it had warners (26:208); see also 6:131 and 10:13.

The ominousness lies in what the sending implies. A warner is sent before destruction, not after, and the sending is therefore a precondition of the punishment rather than a mere announcement. The sūrah's own narrative at vv 13–29 shows a town that rejected its messengers and was destroyed by a single cry, and this verse is what makes that outcome legible as a consequence.

**An Alternative Reading**

The commentators record that some propose the verse should be read of that of which their fathers were warned. On that reading the verse says the opposite of the standard one: the people are being warned of what their fathers were warned of, and the warning is a continuation rather than a new thing.

The reading is preserved without endorsement. The commentators do not say which is correct, and the verse's wording admits both. What differs between them is whether the Arabs stand outside the prophetic tradition or inside it, and the commentators' own note about the generations from Ishmael sits more comfortably with the alternative than with the standard reading.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how long the absence of warning lasted, and does not date it. It does not say whether all the fathers were unwarned or only the recent ones, and does not name the messengers who came before.

The verse also does not say that the warning will be accepted. It states the purpose of the sending — that thou mayest warn — and the next verse reports that the Word has come due for most of them because they do not believe. The purpose and the outcome are stated in adjacent verses without reconciliation.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:7

> **The Word has indeed come due for most of them, for they do not believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Has Come Due**

The Word has indeed come due for most of them, for they do not believe. The commentators explain the phrase: it translates *ḥaqqa al-qawlu ʿalā aktharihim*, which could also be rendered the Word has indeed proved true against most of them. Both renderings are given, and the difference between them is slight — a word that comes due is a word whose time has arrived, and a word that proves true is a word whose content has been confirmed.

The verse supplies its own reason in the second clause: for they do not believe. The commentators connect this explicitly to what precedes — they do not believe in the warning referred to in v6, in the Wise Quran of v2, or that the Prophet Muhammad is among the message bearers at v3. The three things the sūrah has just established are the three things refused.

**What the Word Is**

The Word most likely refers to the Word of punishment, with the commentators citing 39:71 and 22:18. It indicates that they will be in Hell, for as 40:6 states: Likewise did the Word of thy Lord come due for those who disbelieve, that they are the inhabitants of the Fire.

The identification is supported by a second cross-reference. The Word that comes due is seen by some as God's threat at 32:13: But the Word from Me comes due: I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together. The commentators note similar threats at 11:119 and 38:84–5, and the recurrence across the Qur'an is what allows the phrase here to be read without further explanation.

**An Alternative: the Word as the Qur'an**

The commentators record a second reading. The Word could also be a reference to the Qur'an itself, for it is thought that when one turns away from revelation, it proves one's true nature. In support they cite a famous ḥadīth: The Quran is a proof, either for you or against you.

This reading changes the grammar of the verse. On the first reading the Word is a decree that falls upon them from outside; on the second it is the Book they have rejected, which becomes evidence against them by their rejection of it. The commentators preserve both, and the ḥadīth supplies the principle that makes the second intelligible — revelation does not merely inform but testifies.

**Most of Them**

The verse says most of them, not all of them. The qualification is significant and the commentators do not gloss it away. Some of the people addressed did believe, and the sūrah's own narrative at vv 20–27 includes a man who came from the farthest part of the city urging his people to follow the messengers.

The distinction between most and all is what keeps the verse from being a blanket condemnation. The Word has come due for those who do not believe, and the reason is given as their disbelief rather than as their belonging to a particular group. The sūrah's later verses show the exception, and this verse leaves room for it by its wording.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the Word comes due — in this world, in the Hereafter, or both. It does not name the punishment, and does not describe it. It does not say whether the decree can be reversed by later belief.

The verse also does not explain why they do not believe. The reason is supplied at the next two verses, which describe shackles upon their necks and barriers before and behind them, and the commentators read those verses as describing the condition of unbelief rather than merely predicting its consequence.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:8

> **Truly We have put shackles upon their necks, and they are up to their chins, so that they are forced up.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shackles Upon Their Necks**

Truly We have put shackles upon their necks, and they are up to their chins, so that they are forced up. The commentators explain the word precisely: shackles translates *aghlāl*, specifically indicating iron shackles that bind the hands in cuffs that are attached to an iron ring around the neck.

The detail is what makes the image work. The hands are not merely bound but bound to the neck, so that they cannot be extended. The commentators note that they — the pronoun in they are up to their chins — is thus taken by most to indicate the hands that are bound to their necks (Q, Ṭs), and that the phrase is related to 17:29: And let not thine hand be shackled to thy neck (IK, Q, Ṭ), which is a warning against miserliness.

**The Link to Miserliness**

The connection to 17:29 is instructive. There the shackled hand is an image of stinginess — the hand that cannot be extended to give. Here the same image describes those who reject the messenger, and the commentators cite the parallel without elaborating the moral.

The implication is that the rejection described in these verses is not only intellectual. A hand shackled to the neck cannot reach outward, and the commentators at 17:29 read that as avarice. Applied here, the image suggests a closure that is moral as well as cognitive — an inability to extend oneself toward what is offered.

**This Life or the Next**

The commentators record two readings of when this occurs. The binding of their hands to their necks is taken by some to indicate their state on the Day of Resurrection (Ṭs) or in the Hereafter, as at 40:71: They will be dragged, with shackles and chains around their necks (Q).

Others say it refers to the state of the idolaters in this life, meaning that those who turn away from the Prophet's call are like those whose hands are shackled to their necks so that they cannot extend them toward what is good (IK, Ṭ, Ṭs). In this interpretation their chins being forced up alludes to their arrogance in the face of the truth (Q). The two readings are preserved without preference, and the verse's present tense admits both.

**The Forced-Up Chin**

The detail of the chin is the verse's most specific contribution. So that they are forced up describes a posture, and the commentators read it as arrogance. A head forced upward cannot look down, and the image is of someone unable to see what is at his feet.

This connects to the next verse, which describes barriers before and behind and a veiling, so that they see not. The commentators read the two verses together — the forced-up chin and the barriers are two descriptions of one condition. The posture prevents looking down, the barriers prevent looking forward or back, and the veiling prevents seeing at all.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who put the shackles there in the sense of a human agent. It says We have put, and the commentators do not gloss the agency further. It does not say whether the shackles can be removed, and does not offer a condition for their removal.

The verse also does not say that the binding is unjust. The previous verse gave the reason — they do not believe — and this verse describes the condition that follows. The commentators' readings differ on whether the description is literal or figurative and on whether it belongs to this life or the next, but neither reading treats the binding as arbitrary.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:9

> **And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and veiled them; so they see not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Barrier Before and Behind**

And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and veiled them; so they see not. The commentators give the sense directly: this is interpreted to mean We have placed a barrier between them and the truth, or We have blinded them to the truth (IK), in light of 10:96–97: Truly those for whom the Word of thy Lord has come due will not believe, though every sign should come unto them, till they see the painful punishment.

The cross-reference supplies the point. It is not that evidence is lacking but that evidence no longer reaches them. The commentators state the consequence plainly: understood this way, it means they cannot benefit from guidance no matter what the Prophet does.

**The Counsel That Follows**

The commentators connect this verse forward to v76, where God counsels the Prophet: Let not their speech grieve thee. The connection is the reason the counsel is needed. If the barrier is real and the veiling complete, then the Prophet's exertion cannot remove it, and grief over the outcome is grief over something outside his charge.

This is the practical function of the verse within the sūrah. It is not a statement about fate offered for its own sake but an explanation of why the messenger's task is limited to delivery. The commentators at vv 10–11 make the same point in other words: these verses remind the Prophet that he cannot guide those whom God has not guided and should simply leave them to God.

**The Two Barriers**

Some say the barrier before them refers to their being deluded in the life of this world, and the barrier behind them refers to their denying the life of the Hereafter (Q). On this reading the two barriers are not spatial but temporal: one closes off the present and the other closes off what follows it.

The reading makes the image systematic. A person enclosed by a barrier ahead and a barrier behind has nowhere to go, and the commentators take that as the description of a condition — attachment to this world and denial of the next — rather than of a place. The veiling completes it: they cannot see either direction.

**The Incident Reported**

Others maintain that vv 8–9 refer to a specific incident. The commentators relate that a leader from the tribe of Quraysh saw the Prophet praying and went to smash his head with a rock; as he moved his hand forward to cast it, his hand returned to his neck and the rock was stuck in it (Q, Ṭs). Then another man took the rock with the same purpose, but when he did so he could hear the Prophet and not see him. When a third man went to smash the Prophet's head, he saw before him a tremendous camel that was about to swallow him (Q).

The commentators are careful about how this report relates to the verse. These particular examples are not taken to contravene the more general implications, but rather to be manifestations of particular forms of denial and disbelief represented by the acts described. The incident is offered as an illustration, not as the verse's exclusive referent.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the barriers were placed, and does not say whether they were placed before or after the warning was delivered. It does not say whether they can be removed, and does not offer a condition for their removal.

The verse also does not say that the veiling is unjust. The previous verses gave the sequence — the warning was sent, they did not believe, the Word came due — and this verse describes the condition that followed. The commentators' several readings differ on whether the description is of this life or the next, but none treats the barriers as arbitrary.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:10

> **It is the same for them whether thou warnest them or warnest them not; they do not believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Warning Makes No Difference**

It is the same for them whether thou warnest them or warnest them not; they do not believe. The verse states the futility of the warning in terms that leave no room for degree: the two courses are equivalent in their effect. The commentators note that the last phrase could also be read they will not believe, and cite 2:6 as a parallel.

The alternative reading differs in tense rather than in substance. Whether they do not believe or will not believe, the warning does not change the outcome. The commentators cite further parallels where warnings and guidance are of no avail to disbelievers: 7:193, 26:136, and 63:6.

**The Limit on the Prophet**

The commentators explain the purpose of the verse. These verses remind the Prophet that he cannot guide those whom God has not guided and should simply leave them to God, citing 35:8: Truly God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will; so let not thy soul be expended in regrets over them. Truly God knows that which they do.

The counsel is about the distribution of responsibility rather than about the value of the warning. The Prophet's task remains delivery, and the verse does not withdraw it; what it withdraws is the expectation that delivery determines reception. The phrase let not thy soul be expended in regrets is what the commentators take the verse to be for.

**The Connection to the Previous Verses**

Read after vv 8–9, this verse is the conclusion of an argument rather than a new claim. The shackles and the barriers describe a condition; this verse states what the condition means for the messenger's task. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the sūrah's own movement is clear.

The verse is therefore not a general statement about disbelievers but a statement about these — the people whose fathers were not warned, for whom the Word has come due, and who are described as shackled and veiled. The futility named here is the futility of warning a people already in that condition.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that warning is therefore unnecessary. It says the warning makes no difference to them, and the sūrah continues to command delivery. The next verse identifies those whom the warning does reach.

The verse also does not say why they do not believe. The reasons were given in the preceding verses, and this verse states only the outcome. It does not say whether any of them will later believe, and the phrase most of them at v7 leaves that question open.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:11

> **Thou only warnest whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. So give such a one glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whom the Warning Reaches**

Thou only warnest whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. After the statement that the warning makes no difference to some, the verse identifies those to whom it does make a difference. The commentators note that the Reminder is a reference to the Qur'an itself (IK, Q, R, Ṭ), and direct the reader to 36:69 for the Qur'an as Reminder.

The restriction is significant. The verse does not say the Prophet warns only those who will believe, as though the outcome were known in advance; it says he warns whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate. The warning is effective in those disposed to receive it, and the disposition is described rather than predetermined.

**Fearing the Compassionate Unseen**

The commentators gloss the phrase: fears the Compassionate unseen (cf. 50:33) can be interpreted as a reference to worshipping God while God remains unseen, or to worshipping God in seclusion (Q, Ṭs). Both readings are preserved.

The two differ in what they make the unseen modify. On the first, God is the one unseen, and the fear is reverence for a God who is not visible. On the second, the worshipper is unseen, and the fear is exercised where no one observes. The commentators record both, and the verse's wording admits either. What they share is that the fear is not performed for an audience.

**Forgiveness and a Generous Reward**

So give such a one glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward. The commentators explain that a generous reward here means Paradise or the Garden, and direct the reader to 67:12. The two things promised are distinct: forgiveness concerns what is removed, and the reward concerns what is given.

The verse addresses the Prophet and instructs him to deliver the good news. The instruction matters because the preceding verse described a warning that has no effect. Here the same messenger is told to give glad tidings, and the difference is not in the message but in the recipients.

**The Two Verses Together**

Verses 10 and 11 form a pair that the commentators treat together. The first states that the warning is of no avail to some; the second states whom it reaches. Neither verse is complete without the other, and the commentators' note on both — that they remind the Prophet he cannot guide those God has not guided — applies to the pair.

The pairing also answers a question the sūrah raises at v7, where the Word comes due for most of them. Most is not all, and this verse describes the remainder. The sūrah does not reconcile the two by saying that the remainder are exempt from the warning; it says the warning reaches them because of what they already do.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how one comes to follow the Reminder. It describes the disposition of those reached rather than explaining its origin. It does not enumerate what following consists in beyond the two things named.

The verse also does not say that the glad tidings are conditional on continued following. It instructs the Prophet to give them to such a one, and leaves the question of perseverance to the sūrah's later treatment of the Garden and its inhabitants.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:12

> **Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth and that which they have left behind. And We have counted all things in a clear registry.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**We Give Life to the Dead**

Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth and that which they have left behind. The commentators explain that this verse follows upon the counsel to the Prophet to deliver the warning and leave the disbelievers to their own devices, since the price of their wrongdoing will come due upon the Resurrection.

The phrase We give life to the dead carries four recorded readings. It can be seen as a reference to the Resurrection; to God's bringing the earth back to life, as at 57:17: Know that God revives the earth after its death (IK); to bringing people from idolatry to faith (Z); or to the revivification, through knowledge and truth, of hearts made dead through heedlessness and ignorance (Aj, IK). All four are preserved.

**What Is Recorded**

That which they have sent forth refers to the deeds that will testify for or against them in the Hereafter, with the commentators citing 5:80, 75:13, and 82:5. The second term is more specific: that which they have left behind literally means their footsteps or their traces.

The commentators supply the occasion. It is said to refer to one of the tribes of Madinah, the Banū Salimah, who were settled far from the Prophet's mosque and desired to relocate closer to it. This verse was then revealed, and the Prophet said to them: Your footprints are recorded — that is, you receive reward for walking to the mosque. Why then do you want to move? (IK, Q, Ṭ, W). More broadly it is understood as a reference to the traces of good deeds that one leaves behind (Aj, Ṭ).

**The Three That Remain**

The commentators connect the phrase to a well-known ḥadīth: When the son of Adam dies, all his deeds come to an end except three — knowledge that is beneficial, a righteous child who prays for him, or ongoing charity that he leaves behind (IK); see 75:13.

The ḥadīth supplies the principle behind the verse's second term. Deeds sent forth are complete when the doer dies; traces left behind continue to accrue. The commentators treat the Banū Salimah report as the particular instance and the ḥadīth as the general rule, and both are cited without either being preferred.

**A Clear Registry**

And We have counted all things in a clear registry. The word rendered registry is *imām*, and the commentators record two readings. It is taken by some to allude to the inscription of all things from the beginning of time to its end on the Preserved Tablet (85:22; Aj, Ṭb, Ṭs), which is the *imām* — the leader — of all other books (Aj), and which is said to comprise all of God's Decrees (Ṭb, Ṭs); for the inscription of God's Decrees, see 68:1.

Others say *imām* refers to the book or registry in which all human actions are recorded (Aj, IK, Q, Ṭs); see 17:71 and 18:49. The commentators also note that this is one of the central verses cited by Shiite theologians as Qur'anic support for the Shiite understanding of Divinely ordained leadership in the form of the imamate after the death of the Prophet. In this vein al-Qummī relates a saying from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib: I, by God, am the clear *imām*; I clarify truth from falsehood. I inherited it from the Messenger of God (Qm, Ṭb). The three readings are recorded side by side.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the readings of either term. It does not say when the recording occurs, and does not describe the registry. It does not say whether the traces recorded are only good ones or both good and bad.

The verse also does not connect the recording to a judgement explicitly. It states that the dead are given life and that deeds and traces are recorded, and leaves the reckoning to the verses that follow. The commentators' note that the price of wrongdoing comes due upon the Resurrection supplies that connection from the verse's position in the argument rather than from its wording.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:13

> **And set forth for them as a parable the people of the town, when the message bearers came unto it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Set Forth a Parable**

And set forth for them as a parable the people of the town, when the message bearers came unto it. The commentators explain the command: the Prophet is here instructed to provide the Quraysh with an example of the fate that befell those who treated previous message bearers as they were treating him. The parable is therefore addressed to a present situation through a past one.

The commentators note that the first phrase could also be rendered Set forth for them a parable, the people of the town. The two renderings differ in whether the people are the parable or its subject, and the commentators record both without preferring either. What both share is that the account is offered as an example rather than as history for its own sake.

**The Town**

Most identify the town as Antioch (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). The commentators are equally clear about the limit of that identification: it cannot be verified (IK). The name is given because the tradition supplies it, not because the verse does.

The sūrah itself does not name the town, and the commentators do not treat the identification as load-bearing. What matters for the parable is the pattern — messengers sent, messengers denied, a town destroyed — and the pattern does not depend on the location. The identification is recorded because it was held, and flagged because it is uncertain.

**Message Bearers or Prophets**

The commentators distinguish the word used here. The verse says *mursalīn* rather than *rusul* — message bearers rather than messengers — and most commentators view them as apostles sent to the town by Jesus. On this interpretation, that God is said to have sent them indicates that Jesus was commanded by God to send them (Q, Ṭs).

A second opinion holds that the message bearers are prophets (Aj, Ṭs) rather than apostles sent by a prophet. The commentators say this interpretation seems more likely, and give their reason: the Prophet Muhammad is referred to as a *mursal* — a message bearer — at v3 of this very sūrah and at 2:252, and other prophets are referred to as *mursalūn* in over a dozen verses. The word is therefore established usage for prophets, not only for those sent by them.

**The Sequence of the Sending**

The commentators supply the order of events. After the first two were sent, the people of the town beat and imprisoned them (Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). Some say this punishment was ordered by the king (Aj), after which a third message bearer — whom some identify as Jonas — was sent.

The detail explains the structure of the next verse, where two are sent and then strengthened with a third. The commentators treat the imprisonment as the reason for the reinforcement, and the identification of the third as Jonas is recorded as what some say rather than as settled. The sūrah does not name any of the three.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name the town, the message bearers, or the period. It does not say how many people were in the town or what they believed before the messengers came. It does not say whether the messengers were sent to the whole town or to its rulers.

The verse also does not say what the outcome will be. It announces the parable and leaves the narrative to the verses that follow. The commentators' note that the Prophet was commanded to give the Quraysh an example supplies the purpose from the sūrah's context rather than from the verse's wording.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:14

> **When We sent two unto them, they denied them. So We strengthened them with a third, and they said, “Verily, we have been sent unto you!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two, Then a Third**

When We sent two unto them, they denied them. So We strengthened them with a third. The verse compresses the sequence the commentators describe: two were sent, the people denied them, and a third was sent to strengthen the first two. The commentators note that after the first two were sent the people beat and imprisoned them, and that the third came afterwards.

The reinforcement is the verse's notable feature. The denial does not end the mission; it brings a further sending. The sūrah's pattern elsewhere is warning, refusal, destruction, and here the pattern is interrupted by a strengthening before the outcome arrives.

**Verily, We Have Been Sent Unto You**

And they said, Verily, we have been sent unto you. The commentators note that this phrase here and at v16 could also be rendered we are message bearers unto you, and that the second statement at v16 is more emphatic than this first one because it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The first declaration is therefore the plainer of the two. The commentators treat the difference in emphasis as deliberate, and the sequence of the two verses as a single claim made twice with increasing force. The repetition is not redundancy; it is what a claim sounds like when it is refused and then repeated.

**Who Speaks**

The verse attributes the sending to God: When We sent two unto them. The commentators explain that on the reading which takes the message bearers as apostles sent by Jesus, the fact that God is said to have sent them indicates that Jesus was commanded by God to send them (Q, Ṭs). On the alternative reading they are prophets sent directly.

Both readings preserve the divine attribution. What differs is whether the sending is direct or mediated, and the commentators record both. The verse's own wording does not decide, and the identification of the message bearers at v13 leaves the question open.

**The Denial and Its Grounds**

The verse says they denied them without giving the reason. The reason comes at the next verse, where the people say You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. The denial is therefore not a refusal to engage but a specific objection, and the sūrah states it in the following verse rather than here.

The commentators connect that objection to a recurring pattern across the Qur'an, where communities reject prophets because they are human. The denial at v14 is thus the first instance of an objection the Qur'an records many times, and the sūrah's narrative is one case of a general pattern.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how the strengthening occurred — whether the third brought new evidence, new authority, or simply reinforcement in number. It does not say whether the two who were imprisoned were released.

The verse also does not say that the third succeeded where the first two failed. It states the strengthening and the declaration, and the outcome comes later in the narrative. The commentators do not gloss the verse as a success or a failure at this point.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:15

> **They said, “You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. You are but lying.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**You Are But Human Beings Like Us**

They said, You are but human beings like us, and the Compassionate has not sent down anything. You are but lying. The objection has two parts: the messengers are human, and therefore nothing has been sent. The commentators note that in other passages many communities reject prophets because they are human, claiming that if God had wanted to send a message, He would have chosen an angel as His message bearer or messenger.

The reasoning is stated at 17:94: And naught hindered men from believing when guidance came unto them, save that they said, Has God sent a human being as a messenger? The commentators cite further instances at 6:8–9 and 6:50, 11:12, 14:10, 23:33, 41:14, and 64:6. The objection is therefore not particular to this town but a recurring response to prophecy.

**The Same Objection Against the Prophet**

The commentators note that in other verses this same objection is attributed to those who opposed the Prophet Muhammad: And they say, What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets? Why is there not an angel sent down unto him to be a warner with him? (25:7).

The parallel is what makes the parable do its work. The Quraysh are hearing an account of a town that made their own argument, and the sūrah does not need to draw the comparison because the wording is recognisably the same. The commentators' cross-reference supplies the identification explicitly.

**The Compassionate Has Not Sent Down Anything**

The second half of the objection is a denial of revelation rather than of the messengers. The people do not say the messengers are mistaken about their own experience; they say nothing has been sent down at all. The charge of lying follows from that denial rather than from any particular claim.

The sūrah's opening answers this directly. Verse 5 described a revelation of the Mighty, the Merciful, and the verse uses the same divine name — the Compassionate — that the people here deny has sent anything. The commentators do not draw the connection explicitly, but the sūrah's structure supplies it.

**The Objection and Its Failure**

The commentators do not record a reply from the messengers to the humanity objection at this point. The next verse repeats the claim of the mission with greater emphasis rather than answering the argument. The messengers do not dispute that they are human; they reaffirm that they were sent.

This is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of the objection elsewhere. At 17:94 the objection is quoted as the thing that hindered belief, not as a position that is then refuted point by point. The sūrah treats it as a refusal rather than as an argument requiring an answer.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how many of the people made the objection or whether it was unanimous. It does not record any individual dissent from it, though the sūrah later describes a man who came from the farthest part of the city urging his people to follow the messengers.

The verse also does not say what kind of messenger the people would have accepted. They say the Compassionate has not sent down anything, which denies the sending rather than specifying an alternative. The commentators supply the angel objection from parallel verses rather than from this one.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:16

> **They said, “Our Lord knows. Verily we have indeed been sent unto you!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Our Lord Knows**

They said, Our Lord knows. Verily we have indeed been sent unto you. The messengers answer the charge of lying by appealing to God's knowledge rather than by producing evidence. The commentators note that the second statement of their mission is more emphatic than the first at v14, because it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The appeal is significant. The messengers do not dispute the town's account of what they have observed; they locate the question where it cannot be settled by observation. Our Lord knows is a claim about what is verifiable to God, and the reaffirmation that follows rests on it.

**The Increased Emphasis**

The commentators treat the difference between this verse and v14 as deliberate. The first declaration was Verily, we have been sent unto you; this one adds Our Lord knows and intensifies the affirmation. The reason given is that it is a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection.

The sequence therefore records an argument that does not progress by new evidence but by repetition under pressure. The messengers have nothing further to offer except the claim itself, stated more firmly. The commentators do not read this as a weakness in the mission but as the appropriate response to a refusal that has not engaged the claim.

**The Appeal and the Charge**

The charge at v15 was You are but lying. The answer is Our Lord knows. The two are matched: a charge about truthfulness is answered by an appeal to the one who knows the truth. The commentators do not elaborate the exchange, but the sūrah's placement of the two verses makes the pairing clear.

The appeal also shifts the burden. The messengers do not attempt to prove the sending to the town; they state that God knows it. The town's denial is therefore left standing as a denial, and the verse does not offer a resolution that would satisfy it.

**The Verse in the Narrative**

This verse is the last word from the messengers before the narrative moves to the man from the farthest part of the city at v20. The commentators note at v17 that the messengers are only instructed to deliver the message and to guide those who heed it, and are not responsible for those who deny it.

The reaffirmation is therefore the completion of the delivery. Having stated the mission twice, the messengers have done what they were sent to do, and the sūrah's account of them ends with the claim standing against the denial. What follows concerns the town's response rather than the messengers' task.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not provide evidence for the sending, and does not offer a sign. It does not say what God's knowledge entails for the town, and does not threaten punishment here.

The verse also does not say how the messengers came to know they were sent. It reports their claim and their appeal, and leaves the origin of the mission to the account at vv 13–14, where the sending is attributed to God.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:17

> **And naught is incumbent upon us save the clear proclamation.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naught Is Incumbent Save the Clear Proclamation**

And naught is incumbent upon us save the clear proclamation. The messengers state the limit of their charge, and the verse closes their speech in the narrative. The commentators explain: like the Prophet Muhammad, the message bearers are only instructed to deliver the message and to guide those who heed it; they are not responsible for those who deny it.

The statement is a boundary rather than a concession. The messengers are not saying the mission is unimportant but that its scope is defined: proclamation is theirs, reception is not. The commentators direct the reader back to vv 10–11, where the same limit was stated for the Prophet — that he warns only whomsoever follows the Reminder.

**The Principle Across the Qur'an**

The commentators note that the prophets' responsibility being limited to delivering the message is emphasized in many passages, and cite 3:20, 5:92, 5:99, 13:40, 16:35, 16:82, 24:54, 29:18, 42:48, and 64:12. The recurrence is what allows the verse to state the principle in a single clause without argument.

The consistency matters for the parable's function. The Quraysh hearing this account are hearing a principle they have already encountered applied to the Prophet himself. The messengers in the town say what the Prophet says, and the sūrah's command at v13 to set forth the parable depends on that identity.

**Clear Proclamation**

The proclamation is qualified as clear. The commentators do not gloss the adjective here, but the sūrah's own vocabulary supplies it: the Qur'an was sworn by at v2 as Wise, described at v5 as a revelation, and named at v11 as the Reminder. The proclamation is clear because what is proclaimed has already been characterised.

The clarity is also what makes the denial culpable. At v9 the commentators described a people who cannot benefit from guidance no matter what the Prophet does, and at vv 10–11 the warning was said to make no difference to them. The clear proclamation is what distinguishes their refusal from ignorance.

**The End of the Messengers' Speech**

This is the last thing the messengers say in the sūrah. They declared the mission at v14, reaffirmed it at v16 under the charge of lying, and now state the limit of their responsibility. The commentators do not mark the closure explicitly, but the narrative moves to the town's response at v18 and then to the man from the outskirts at v20.

The sequence is complete: declaration, reaffirmation, statement of scope. The messengers have done what they were sent to do, and the verse says so in the plainest terms. What follows is not their task.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what happens to those who deny. It states the messengers' limit and leaves the consequence to the narrative that follows. It does not threaten punishment here, and does not predict the town's fate.

The verse also does not say that guidance is impossible for the deniers. It says the messengers are not responsible for them, which is a statement about the messengers rather than about the deniers' capacity. The commentators' cross-reference to vv 10–11 preserves the distinction.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:18

> **They said, “Truly we augur ill of you. If you cease not, we shall certainly stone you, and a painful punishment will certainly befall you from us.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly We Augur Ill of You**

The town answers the messengers with an omen and a threat in a single breath: they see ill in the messengers' arrival, and if the mission does not stop, stoning will follow and a painful punishment will come from the town itself. The commentators give the sense of the first phrase: this is the response of the disbelievers to the message bearers, meaning We see an ill omen or foretell a punishment that will befall you.

The augury is therefore not a vague unease but a prediction. The town attributes its own misfortune to the messengers' presence, and the commentators read the phrase as combining two things — an omen and a foretelling — in one statement.

**The Threat**

The second half is explicit: if you cease not, we shall certainly stone you. The commentators note at vv 13–14 that after the first two message bearers were sent, the people of the town beat and imprisoned them, and that some say this punishment was ordered by the king (Aj). The threat here continues that pattern.

The demand is cessation rather than silence. The town does not ask the messengers to leave or to stop speaking in public; it asks them to cease, which the commentators take as a demand that the mission end. The penalty attached is stoning, and the verse adds a painful punishment from us, which the commentators read as part of the same foretelling.

**The Omen and Its Logic**

The commentators cite 17:13 in connection with the next verse: And for every man We have fastened his omen upon his neck, and We shall bring it forth for him on the Day of Resurrection; see also 7:131 and 27:47. The cross-reference identifies the omen as belonging to the person rather than to the occasion.

This is what the messengers' reply at v19 turns on. The town locates its ill fortune in the messengers; the verse at 17:13 locates each person's omen upon his own neck. The sūrah's answer is therefore not that there is no omen but that it has been misattributed.

**The Demand and the Preceding Denial**

The threat follows the denial at v15 and the messengers' reaffirmation at v16. The commentators note that the second declaration was more emphatic because it was a reaffirmation in the face of denial and rejection. The town's response to the reaffirmation is not a further argument but a threat.

The sequence records an exchange that has stopped being an exchange. The messengers repeated their claim, and the town answered with violence rather than with reasons. The commentators do not gloss this transition, but the sūrah's placement of the two verses makes it plain.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say whether the threat was carried out. It reports the demand and the penalty attached to refusal, and the narrative moves on. The commentators' note about the beating and imprisonment at vv 13–14 concerns an earlier stage.

The verse also does not say what misfortune the town believed the messengers had brought. The augury is stated without content, and the commentators gloss it as a foretelling of punishment rather than as a diagnosis of a particular calamity.

---
## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:19

> **They said, “Your auguring ill is upon yourselves though you have been reminded? Nay, you are a prodigal people!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Your Auguring Ill Is Upon Yourselves**

They said, Your auguring ill is upon yourselves though you have been reminded? Nay, you are a prodigal people! The commentators explain the reply: the messengers respond that the punishment the disbelievers foretell will befall them because of their disbelief. The omen is returned to those who pronounced it.

The commentators note that this rebounding indicates the reflexive nature of good and evil deeds, which is emphasized throughout the Qur'an. They cite the pattern at length: if one spends in charity, one spends it for oneself, as at 2:272 — Whatever good you spend, it is for yourselves, when you spend only seeking the Face of God. Conversely, whosoever is miserly is only miserly unto himself (47:38); if one tries to deceive, one only deceives oneself (2:9); if one tries to lead astray, one only goes astray (3:69; 4:113); and if some devise a plot against God and His Messenger, God will devise a plot against them (52:42); see 4:111.

**The Three Readings of the Clause**

The commentators record three readings of the phrase rendered though you have been reminded. In the most widespread reading, *in dhukkirtum* is read *a-in dukkirtum*, meaning Even if you are reminded. In this reading the ellipsis indicates a rhetorical elision that constitutes the object of the interrogative and serves as a rebuke, as if to say: Do you augur ill and disbelieve, even when you have been reminded?

In another canonical reading, *a-in dhukkirtum* is read *ayna dhukkirtum*, meaning now that you have been reminded — *al-ān dhukkirtum* (Q, Ṭ). Another reading is *an dhukkirtum*, meaning because you have been reminded (Z). All three are canonical, and the commentators record them without preferring one. The sense differs: on the first the reminder is conceded and dismissed, on the second it is the occasion of the augury, on the third its cause.

**A Prodigal People**

Nay, you are a prodigal people. The commentators direct the reader to 10:12 for the meaning of the phrase. The word rendered prodigal denotes excess beyond proper bounds, and the charge is not that the town is mistaken but that it is extravagant in its error.

The accusation follows the return of the omen. Having said that the ill augury belongs to the town itself, the messengers characterise the town as excessive. The commentators do not elaborate the connection, but the sequence moves from a correction to a diagnosis.

**The Omen Upon the Neck**

The commentators' cross-reference to 17:13 supplies the principle behind the reply: And for every man We have fastened his omen upon his neck, and We shall bring it forth for him on the Day of Resurrection; see also 7:131 and 27:47. The omen is not a quality of events but a record attached to the person.

This is what makes the messengers' answer decisive rather than merely contradictory. The town said the messengers were an ill omen; the principle at 17:13 says each person carries his own. The reply is therefore not a counter-superstition but a correction of where the omen resides.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what punishment will befall the town, and does not predict its form. It returns the foretelling without specifying it, and the destruction comes later in the narrative.

The verse also does not say that the town cannot be reminded further. The three readings differ on the status of the reminder already given, and none states that further reminder is impossible. The commentators record the readings and leave the question open.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:20

> **And from the outskirts of the city, a man came running. He said, “O my people! Follow the message bearers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Man Came Running**

And from the outskirts of the city, a man came running. He said, O my people! Follow the message bearers! The commentators note that the man is usually identified by the name Ḥabīb al-Najjār. The identification is recorded as what is usually held rather than as settled, and the verse itself does not name him.

The detail of his coming from the outskirts is worth noting. He was not among those who had heard the messengers in the city centre and refused them; he came running from the edge. The commentators' accounts differ on what brought him, and the verse supplies only the movement.

**The Three Accounts of Him**

The commentators record three accounts. Some claim that he worshipped God in seclusion, and when the news of the message bearers came, he proclaimed his faith (Aj). Another interpretation says that Ḥabīb al-Najjār followed the first two message bearers after they healed his son (Aj, Q).

The third is a saying attributed to the Prophet: The foremost among the people are three who did not disbelieve in God for the blinking of an eye — ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, the companion of Yā Sīn, and the believer among the people of Pharaoh, at 40:28–29. They are the truthful — *ṣiddīq* (Sy, Th, Ṭs). All three accounts are preserved without preference, and they differ on how he came to believe rather than on whether he did.

**The Address to His People**

He said, O my people! Follow the message bearers! The address is to his own people, and the commentators do not gloss it further. The man identifies with the town he is addressing and urges it toward the messengers it has just threatened to stone.

The placement is what gives the verse its force. Verse 18 recorded the town's threat; verse 19 the messengers' reply; and now a member of the town speaks against his own. The commentators' note at v17 that the messengers are not responsible for those who deny does not exclude the possibility that one of them will believe, and this verse supplies it.

**The Exception to Most of Them**

At v7 the sūrah said the Word has come due for most of them. The qualifier most left room for an exception, and this verse is that exception appearing in the narrative. The commentators do not draw the connection explicitly, but the sūrah's structure supplies it.

The man's belief also answers the town's objection at v15 — that the messengers were but human beings like us. Here is a human being like them who followed, and the sūrah places his appearance immediately after the messengers have stated the limit of their responsibility. What they could not compel, one of the town chose.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name the man. The identification as Ḥabīb al-Najjār comes from the commentators, who record it as usual rather than certain. It does not say his age, his standing, or his occupation, though the name recorded for him has been read as indicating a trade.

The verse also does not say how many followed his urging. It records his appeal and moves to his argument in the next verses. The outcome for the town is given later, and the sūrah does not say whether his intervention changed it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:21

> **Follow those who ask not of you any reward and who are guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Follow Those Who Ask No Reward**

Follow those who ask not of you any reward and who are guided. The man from the outskirts gives his people a reason to follow the messengers, and the reason is not doctrinal. It is about what the messengers have not asked for.

The commentators explain the principle: a mark of prophethood is to ask for nothing from the people, except that they follow the message the prophets are sent to deliver. The absence of a demand is therefore not incidental to the mission but a sign of it, and the man offers it as evidence rather than as a moral observation.

**The Prophetic Precedent**

The commentators supply the precedent at length. As Noah says: And if you turn your backs, I have not asked of you any reward. My reward lies only with God, and I am commanded to be among those who submit (10:72). Thus the Prophet Muhammad is commanded at 6:90, 38:86, and 42:23: Say, I ask not of you any reward for it — see also 26:109, 127, 145, 164, and 180.

The recurrence across the Qur'an is what makes the man's argument available to him. He is not inventing a test; he is applying one that the prophets themselves have stated. The sūrah's own account of the messengers at vv 16–17 recorded their claim and the limit of their charge, and this verse adds the condition under which the charge was accepted.

**And Who Are Guided**

The second half of the verse adds a qualification: the messengers are guided. The commentators do not gloss the phrase separately, and the man does not argue for it. It stands beside the absence of reward as a second mark.

The two marks differ in kind. The first is observable — the messengers have asked for nothing, and the town can verify it. The second is a claim about their state. The man offers both without distinguishing them, and the commentators do not separate them either.

**The Argument and Its Audience**

The man addresses his own people, and the commentators do not gloss the address. What he offers is the kind of argument that works on people who have not considered the doctrine: the messengers want nothing from you, and they are guided. The first half requires no belief to assess.

This is consistent with his position in the narrative. He came running from the outskirts, and the commentators record differing accounts of how he came to believe. His appeal to his people is framed for those who have not yet made his own journey, and the reward question is where such an appeal would start.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what reward the messengers do receive. It states that they ask none of the people, and the commentators supply from Noah's words that the reward lies with God rather than with the addressed community.

The verse also does not say that asking for reward disqualifies a claimant. It offers the absence of a demand as a mark of prophethood, and does not state the converse. The commentators' note that this is a mark of prophethood is positive rather than exclusive.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:22

> **Why should I not worship Him who originated me, and unto Whom you will be returned?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Him Who Originated Me**

Why should I not worship Him who originated me, and unto Whom you will be returned? The man's argument turns on two facts about himself: he was originated by God, and he will return to God. The commentators explain the first term precisely: originated me translates *faṭaranī*, which derives from the same root as *fiṭrah*, or primordial nature.

The root connects the verse to a larger claim. The commentators cite 30:30: Set thy face to religion as a *ḥanīf*, in the primordial nature from God upon which He originated mankind — there is no altering the creation of God; that is the upright religion, but most of mankind know not. The same verb is used of the same act, and the word for the nature is built from it.

**Worship and the Primordial Nature**

The commentators draw the conclusion directly. Read in light of 30:30 and 51:56 — And I did not create jinn and mankind, save to worship Me — this verse implies that the worship of God is central to the primordial nature with which human beings were created.

This is what makes the man's question rhetorical rather than open. Why should I not worship Him is not a request for reasons but a statement that no reason against it survives. If worship belongs to the nature one was originated with, then the question answers itself, and the commentators' reading makes that explicit.

**Unto Whom You Will Be Returned**

The second half shifts from the man to his audience: and unto Whom you will be returned. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, but the sūrah supplies the context. Verse 12 stated Truly We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth, and the commentators there explained that the verse follows the counsel to leave the disbelievers to their devices since the price of their wrongdoing comes due upon the Resurrection.

The man's argument therefore has two terms, and both concern God's relation to human beings: origination at the beginning and return at the end. What falls between them is the worship he is asking about, and the verse places it inside that span.

**The Form of the Question**

The verse is a question directed at himself but posed publicly. The commentators do not gloss its form, and the sūrah does not mark it as addressed to anyone in particular. It follows the command to follow the messengers at v21 and precedes the rejection of other gods at v23.

The sequence is an argument in three moves: follow them, because I have no reason not to worship the One who made me, and taking other gods would leave me in manifest error. The question at v22 is the middle term, and its rhetorical form is what allows it to carry the weight without further support.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say when the return occurs, and does not describe it. It does not say what will happen at the return, and does not threaten punishment here.

The verse also does not say that his people were originated by the same God, though it implies it by addressing them about their return. The man states his own origination explicitly and their return as a shared fact, and the commentators do not elaborate the difference.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:23

> **Shall I take gods apart from Him? If the Compassionate desired harm for me, their intercession would avail me naught, nor would they save me.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shall I Take Gods Apart From Him**

Shall I take gods apart from Him? The commentators give the sense: the question means Shall I take gods other than God, worship them, and leave the worship of the One Who is worthy of worship? (Sh). The gloss expands the question into its three parts — taking, worshipping, and abandoning — and identifies what makes it absurd.

The question follows directly from the previous verse. If worship belongs to the nature one was originated with, and if the One who originated is the One to whom one returns, then taking other gods requires abandoning the worship that the first verse established as central.

**Their Intercession Would Avail Me Naught**

If the Compassionate desired harm for me, their intercession would avail me naught, nor would they save me. The man states the practical consequence of taking other gods, and the commentators supply the doctrine behind it: the Qur'an indicates that angels and prophets will be able to intercede on the Day of Judgment, but that intercession is entirely by the Will of God.

The commentators cite 20:109: On that Day intercession will be of no benefit, save that of those whom the Compassionate has granted leave and with whose word He is content. Thus 2:255 asks rhetorically, Who is there who may intercede with Him save by His Leave? They direct the reader to 2:48 and 2:255 for the fuller treatment of intercession.

**The Argument Against Other Gods**

The man's reasoning is not that other gods do not exist but that they cannot help. The verse supposes the Compassionate desiring harm for him and asks what the other gods could do. The answer is that their intercession would avail nothing and they would not save him.

The commentators' note that intercession is entirely by God's Will is what makes the argument work. If intercession depends on permission, then a god who cannot grant permission cannot intercede, and taking such gods adds nothing to one's position. The man is not disputing their existence but their usefulness.

**The Verse in the Man's Speech**

This is the second of the man's three arguments. Verse 22 asked why he should not worship the One who originated him; verse 23 asks whether he should take others; verse 24 states what the alternative would make him. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the movement is from a positive ground to a rejected alternative to a consequence.

The three verses together are a complete case, and the man delivers it in front of the people who have just threatened to stone the messengers. The commentators record his identification as Ḥabīb al-Najjār and the differing accounts of his belief, but the argument itself stands without them.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that intercession is impossible. It says the intercession of other gods would not avail, and the commentators' note preserves the Qur'anic position that intercession exists but is by God's leave alone.

The verse also does not say what harm the Compassionate might desire. The supposition is hypothetical and is offered to test the usefulness of other gods rather than to assert that harm is intended. The commentators do not gloss the conditional as a prediction.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:24

> **Truly would I then be in manifest error.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly Would I Then Be in Manifest Error**

Truly would I then be in manifest error. The man states the consequence of the alternative he rejected at v23. The commentators explain the phrase: manifest error — *ḍalāl mubīn* — is employed throughout the Qur'an to indicate one whose wrongdoing makes him stray from the path of truth (Ṭ).

The commentators add two further readings of the phrase. It can also be understood to mean error that makes manifest or makes clear. It is also read by some as *ḍalāl mubayyan* (Bḍ), which would then indicate error that has been made manifest or made clear. All three are recorded without preference.

**The Two Directions of the Phrase**

The readings differ in which direction the manifestness runs. On the commentators' first gloss the error is manifest to others — it is visible, obvious, not hidden. On the alternative reading the error itself makes manifest, which suggests it discloses something rather than merely being disclosed.

The commentators do not choose between these, and the phrase admits both. What they share is that the error is not concealed. The man is not describing a private mistake but a condition that would be evident, and the sūrah's use of the phrase elsewhere carries the same sense.

**The Conclusion of the Argument**

The verse closes the man's case. Verse 22 established the ground for worshipping the One who originated him; verse 23 rejected the alternative of other gods on the ground that they cannot intercede; verse 24 states what taking them would make him. The three move from ground to rejection to consequence.

The commentators do not gloss the verse as a conclusion, but its position and its brevity give it that function. After two questions the man makes a statement, and the statement is about himself rather than about his people. He does not say you would be in manifest error; he says truly would I then be.

**The Self-Accusation and the Appeal**

The man's argument has been about himself throughout: Him who originated me, if the Compassionate desired harm for me, would I then be. The commentators do not gloss this as a rhetorical strategy, but the effect is that he has not accused his people of anything.

This is consistent with the address at v20 — O my people! — and with the verse that follows, where he says Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me. The case is presented as his own reasoning rather than as a charge, and the commentators record the differing readings of who that final appeal is addressed to.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the error consists in beyond the taking of other gods. It states the condition and leaves its content to the previous verse.

The verse also does not say that his people are in that error. It describes what the man would be if he took the alternative, and the sūrah's account of the town's fate comes later. The commentators do not read the verse as a verdict on the people.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:25

> **Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Truly I Believe in Your Lord**

Truly I believe in your Lord, so listen to me. The man's declaration is the climax of his speech, and its form is notable: he speaks of your Lord rather than my Lord. The God he has just argued for at vv 22–24 is named as theirs.

The commentators do not gloss the possessive, but the sūrah's structure supplies its force. The man has been addressing his own people since v20, and the argument he has made has been about what is true for him and for them alike. Naming the Lord as theirs is the conclusion of that argument rather than a new claim.

**The Two Readings of So Listen to Me**

The commentators record two readings of the final appeal. According to some, so listen to me is a call to the people to follow God and His message bearers. Others say it is a call to the message bearers, asking them to bear witness for the man before God (IK).

The two readings differ on who is being addressed, and the verse's wording admits both. On the first the man is still speaking to the town, continuing the appeal that began at v20. On the second he turns to the messengers and asks them to testify for him. The commentators preserve both without preferring either.

**The Declaration After the Argument**

The man has made his case in three moves: the ground for worshipping the One who originated him, the rejection of other gods who cannot intercede, and the consequence of taking them. This verse is the statement that follows, and it is a statement about himself.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the movement from argument to declaration is what gives the verse its weight. He has not asked his people to accept his reasoning; he has announced that he believes, and then asked them to listen. The order matters: the belief comes first.

**The Verse Before the Outcome**

This is the last thing the man says in the narrative. The next verse records what was said to him, and the commentators supply what intervened: some say that after his declaration to the people, the man was stoned and killed (Aj, IK, Ṭs).

The sūrah does not narrate the killing. It moves from the declaration to the invitation into the Garden, and the commentators fill the gap. The verse's placement is therefore immediately before the outcome, and what the man asked for — that his people listen — is left unanswered in the text.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say whether his people listened. It records the appeal and moves on, and the sūrah's account of the town's fate comes at vv 28–29 without reference to any response to him.

The verse also does not say when he came to believe. The commentators record differing accounts — that he worshipped in seclusion and proclaimed his faith on hearing of the messengers, or that he followed the first two after they healed his son — and the verse supplies neither.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:26

> **It was said unto him, “Enter the Garden!” He said, “Would that my people knew**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Enter the Garden**

It was said unto him, Enter the Garden! The invitation comes without narrative transition, and the commentators supply what the verse omits. Some say that after his declaration to the people at v25, the man was stoned and killed (Aj, IK, Ṭs). Then he was told — by God, or by the angels and the righteous — Enter the Garden! (Aj, R).

The commentators also record the alternative: others maintain that God took him alive to heaven. The two accounts differ on whether he died before the invitation, and the verse does not decide. What both agree on is that the invitation came and that it came to him.

**Who Speaks**

The verse says it was said without naming the speaker. The commentators offer three possibilities: God, the angels, or the righteous (Aj, R). The passive construction is what allows all three, and the commentators do not choose.

The ambiguity is characteristic of the sūrah's treatment of this narrative. The town is unnamed, the messengers are unnamed, and the man is named only in the commentators. The invitation is given in the same register — stated as a fact without attribution.

**The Immediacy of the Invitation**

The commentators' account places the invitation immediately after the declaration, and on the majority reading immediately after the killing. The man asked his people to listen, and what follows is not their response but his entry.

The commentators do not gloss the sequence as a consolation, but the sūrah's placement gives it that shape. The man's appeal goes unanswered in the narrative, and the next thing recorded is his admission to the Garden. The commentators' note that some say he was stoned while praying for his people to be guided supplies the contrast.

**The Garden and the Sūrah's Argument**

The man's entry is the sūrah's first concrete instance of the reward promised at v11, where the Prophet is told to give glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward to whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. The commentators there explained that a generous reward means Paradise or the Garden.

The connection is not stated in the text, but the sūrah's structure supplies it. The promise at v11 is general and the case at v26 is particular. The man followed the messengers and feared the Compassionate, and the reward named in the abstract is here shown being given.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how he entered, and does not describe the Garden. It records the invitation and leaves the entry to be inferred. It does not say whether he entered alone.

The verse also does not say whether his people witnessed any of this. The next verse has him wishing they knew, which implies they did not, and the commentators do not gloss the difference between what he saw and what they saw.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:27

> **how my Lord forgave me and placed me among the honored.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Would That My People Knew**

He said, Would that my people knew how my Lord forgave me and placed me among the honored. The man's first words in the Garden are a wish about those who killed him, and the commentators supply the context that makes the wish significant.

According to Qatādah — who died in 117/726 and is described by the commentators as one of the foremost commentators from the third generation of Muslim scholars — the people began to stone him while he was praying for God to guide them, and as they continued to stone him, he continued to pray for them, even while suffering a gruesome death at their hands (IK).

**The Prayer That Did Not Stop**

Qatādah's account is what gives the wish its shape. The man was praying for his people's guidance while they killed him, and the prayer did not stop when the stoning began. The wish in the Garden continues it: he wants them to know what he has been given.

The commentators record this as Qatādah's account rather than as the verse's statement. The verse itself says only that he wished his people knew, and the commentators supply the continuity between the prayer during the stoning and the wish afterwards.

**How My Lord Forgave Me**

The commentators note that this verse could also mean for what my Lord forgave me and that He placed me among the honored (Ṭs), which may imply the bad deeds that were forgiven or the good deed or deeds for which God forgave him his sins.

The alternative reading changes the sense of the preposition. On the standard reading the man wishes his people knew the manner of the forgiveness; on the alternative he wishes they knew the reason for it. The commentators preserve both, and neither identifies the specific deeds in question.

**Placed Among the Honored**

The second half names what he was given: a place among the honored. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the sūrah does not describe the company. What the verse states is that the forgiveness and the placement are joined, and that the man reports them together.

The wish is therefore about both. He does not wish his people knew that he was forgiven, or that he was honored, but how the Lord did both. The commentators' note on the alternative reading keeps the two connected — the forgiveness and the placement are reported as one thing.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what his people would have done had they known. The wish is stated without a consequence, and the sūrah does not supply one. It does not say whether any of them came to know later.

The verse also does not say that the man forgave his people. It records his wish that they knew what he had been given, and Qatādah's account of his prayer for their guidance is recorded by the commentators rather than stated in the verse.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:28

> **And after him We did not send down a host from Heaven against his people; nor would We send down.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Host From Heaven**

The verse states what was *not* sent: no heavenly host was dispatched against the man's people after him, and none would be. The negation is the verse's whole content, and the commentators record two readings of what the phrase means.

The verse is taken by many as a reference to the punishment that God inflicted upon his people for rejecting the message bearers and for killing — or seeking to kill — Ḥabīb al-Najjār. On this reading it indicates that God wiped them out with a single cry, at v29, rather than sending angels against them (IK, Q).

**The Alternative: No Further Revelation**

Others say that a host from Heaven refers to the angels of revelation (Ṭs), meaning that God did not send another revelation after having sent the message bearers (Ṭ). On this reading the verse is not about the punishment but about the cessation of sending.

The commentators say which reading they think fits better, and give their reason. The second interpretation is more consistent with this story in its function as a warning to the Quraysh, for it implies that accepting the revelation sent to the Prophet Muhammad is their only opportunity and they will not receive another. The preference is stated as an interpretive judgement rather than as a fact about the verse.

**Why the Reasoning Matters**

The commentators' preference connects the parable to its audience. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town as a parable for the Quraysh, and the commentators explained that the purpose was to give an example of the fate of those who treated previous message bearers as they were treating him.

On the first reading the parable warns of destruction. On the second it warns that no further messenger will come. The commentators take the second as better suited to the Quraysh's situation, and the sūrah's command at v13 is what makes that suitability relevant.

**The Final Phrase**

The commentators note that the last phrase — nor would We send down — could also be rendered nor what We used to send down (Ka, Ṭs). The alternative reading makes the negation concern a past practice rather than a future intention.

The difference is subtle but real. On the standard reading the verse says God would not send; on the alternative it says God does not send what He used to send. The commentators record both, and the second sits more naturally with the reading that takes the host as the angels of revelation.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the punishment was. It states what was not sent, and the cry comes at the next verse. It does not say how many were destroyed or when.

The verse also does not say that the people were destroyed for killing the man specifically. The commentators describe the punishment as for rejecting the message bearers and for killing or seeking to kill Ḥabīb al-Najjār, and the verse itself gives no reason.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:29

> **It was but a single cry. Then, behold, they were extinguished.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Cry**

It was but a single cry. Then, behold, they were extinguished. The commentators' note on this verse is brief and states its point directly: the verse indicates that the destruction for having disbelieved was instant.

The brevity of the commentary matches the brevity of the verse. There is no elaboration to give because the verse supplies none — one cry, and the people are extinguished. The commentators do not describe the cry, and the sūrah does not say where it came from.

**Extinguished**

The word rendered extinguished is drawn from the vocabulary of putting out a flame. The commentators do not gloss it, and the verse does not elaborate. What the image carries is finality: a fire that is extinguished is not diminished or weakened but ended.

The sūrah uses the same economy elsewhere. At v8 the disbelievers were described as shackled and at v9 as veiled, and the commentators read those verses as describing a condition. Here the condition ends in an event, and the event is described in two words.

**The Sequence With the Previous Verse**

Verse 28 stated what was not sent — no host from Heaven. This verse states what was sent instead, and the contrast is the point. The commentators explain at v28 that on the majority reading the verse indicates God wiped them out with a single cry rather than sending angels against them (IK, Q).

The two verses therefore work as a pair. The negation at v28 prepares for the cry at v29, and the commentators' reading makes the connection explicit. The punishment was not a military destruction but a single sound, and the sūrah's economy in describing it is part of the claim.

**The Instantaneity and the Warning**

The commentators note at v28 that the reading which takes the host as the angels of revelation is more consistent with the story's function as a warning to the Quraysh, since it implies that accepting the revelation sent to the Prophet Muhammad is their only opportunity. The instantaneity of the destruction supports that warning.

A punishment that comes in a single cry leaves no interval in which to respond. The sūrah's parable was commanded at v13 precisely so that the Quraysh would see what happened to a town that mocked its messengers, and the speed of the outcome is what makes the parable urgent rather than merely illustrative.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how many were extinguished, and does not say whether any survived. It does not describe the cry, and does not say whether the believers among them were affected. The commentators' note that the man had entered the Garden implies he was not, but the verse does not connect the two.

The verse also does not say that the destruction was the final word. The next verse moves to a lament over the servants, and v32 states that each group will be arraigned. The instantaneity described here concerns this world, and the sūrah's later verses concern what follows.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:30

> **Alas for the servants! Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alas for the Servants**

Alas for the servants! Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him. The commentators note the parallel at 15:11 and record two readings of the exclamation. It is interpreted by most to indicate amazement at the extreme injustice disbelievers do to themselves and the punishment they will suffer (IK, Q).

On this reading the lament is directed at the disbelievers, and its content is the harm they have done to themselves. The commentators connect this to the reflexive principle they described at v19 — that good and evil rebound upon their doer — and the exclamation is what that principle sounds like when it is observed.

**The Alternative: the Servants as Prophets**

Others take servants as a reference to the prophets, in which case the verse expresses God's lament for the hardships they endure when mocked by disbelievers (Q, Ṭs). On this reading the exclamation is not about the disbelievers' self-harm but about what the messengers suffer.

The two readings differ on who is being lamented, and the commentators preserve both. The verse's wording admits either, since the word rendered servants is general. What both readings share is that the lament concerns the pattern stated in the second half of the verse: every messenger was mocked.

**Never a Messenger But They Mocked Him**

The second half states the pattern without exception. Never did a messenger come unto them, but that they mocked him. The commentators do not gloss the universality of the claim, and the sūrah's own parable supplies one instance of it.

The pattern is what connects the parable to the Quraysh. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town as an example for those treating him as they were treating him. This verse generalises: it was not only that town, and it is not only him.

**The Verse's Position**

The verse stands between the end of the parable at v29 and the argument from the destroyed generations at v31. The commentators do not gloss its placement, but the movement is from a particular case to a general statement to a question about the evidence.

The lament is therefore a hinge. The parable has shown one town mocking its messengers and being extinguished; this verse says the mockery was universal; and the next verse asks whether the hearers have not considered how many generations were destroyed. The exclamation is what carries the reader from the story to the argument.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who speaks. The exclamation is unattributed in the Arabic, and the commentators' two readings differ on whether it expresses amazement at the disbelievers or lament for the prophets without settling who utters it.

The verse also does not say that the mocking always ended in destruction. It states the pattern of mockery, and the destruction comes at v31 as a question rather than as a claim. The sūrah separates the two.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:31

> **Have they not considered how many generations before them We destroyed, such that they return not unto them?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Have They Not Considered**

Have they not considered how many generations before them We destroyed, such that they return not unto them? The verse is a question rather than a statement, and the commentators note that its pronouns admit three readings: they and them can refer to the former peoples who were destroyed, to the inhabitants of Makkah who deny the prophethood of the Prophet Muhammad, or to both.

The commentators do not choose between the three, and the verse's wording supports each. The ambiguity is productive rather than confusing: on any of the readings the question asks the hearer to look at what happened to those who came before.

**How Many Generations**

The verse does not give a number. It says how many, which puts the quantity in question form and leaves the hearer to supply it. The sūrah's own parable at vv 13–29 supplies one instance, and the Qur'an elsewhere supplies many more.

The commentators do not enumerate the generations, and the verse does not require it. What the question asks is not for a count but for consideration — the act of looking at the record and drawing what follows from it.

**Such That They Return Not**

The clause that they return not unto them states the finality of the destruction. The commentators do not gloss it, and the verse does not elaborate. What it excludes is any return of the destroyed generations to those who came after.

The finality is what makes the question urgent. If the destroyed generations could return, the record would be revisable. Since they cannot, what happened to them is settled, and the hearer's consideration of it is consideration of a closed case.

**The Question and Its Audience**

The commentators' note that the pronouns may refer to the inhabitants of Makkah connects the verse to the parable's purpose. At v13 the Prophet was commanded to set forth the people of the town for the Quraysh, and this verse asks them directly whether they have considered the generations.

The question form is what suits that audience. A statement could be denied; a question about what they already know cannot. The commentators do not gloss the rhetoric, but the sūrah's use of questions elsewhere — at v22 in the man's speech, and at v78 later — follows the same pattern.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say why the generations were destroyed. It asks whether the hearers have considered how many were destroyed, and the reason is supplied by the surrounding verses rather than by this one.

The verse also does not say that the same will happen to the hearers. It asks a question about the past and leaves the application to the reader. The commentators' note that the pronouns may refer to the Makkans supplies the connection without the verse stating it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:32

> **Each of them shall be a group arraigned before Us.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Each of Them Shall Be a Group Arraigned**

Each of them shall be a group arraigned before Us. The commentators explain the scope: this verse and v53 indicate that all human beings, believers and disbelievers alike, will be arraigned on the Day of Judgment.

The arraignment is collective. The verse says each of them shall be a group, and the commentators read this as the gathering of all human beings rather than as an individual reckoning. The grouping is by community, and the sūrah's parable has just shown one such community destroyed as a unit.

**The Apparent Tension**

The commentators note a difficulty and resolve it. Other verses indicate that only the disbelievers will be arraigned unto the punishment, citing 30:16 and 34:38. It appears that these verses indicate two arraignments: the first is the gathering of all human beings referred to here and at v53; the second, which comes after the gathering of all human beings, is the Reckoning the disbelievers will endure before they are cast into Hell.

The resolution is not imposed on the text but drawn from comparing it. The commentators identify two distinct events and assign the verses to them, which preserves both sets of statements without making either false. The reading is presented as what appears to be the case rather than as settled.

**The Gathering and the Reckoning**

The distinction matters for what the sūrah is claiming. The gathering is universal and the reckoning is not. At v12 the commentators explained that We give life to the dead and record that which they have sent forth, and at v103 of Sūrah Hūd the Day is described as one on which mankind shall be gathered together.

The arraignment here is the gathering, and the commentators' second arraignment is what follows for some. The verse itself does not distinguish the two, and the commentators supply the distinction from the wider Qur'anic usage.

**Before Us**

The arraignment is before Us, and the commentators do not gloss the phrase. What it establishes is that the grouping is not merely a gathering but an appearance, and the sūrah's earlier verses have prepared for it.

At v7 the commentators explained that the Word has come due for most of them, and at v12 that deeds and traces are recorded. The arraignment is where the record is presented, and the verse's brevity leaves the procedure to those earlier statements.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what happens at the arraignment. It states that each group will be arraigned, and the commentators supply the two-stage reading from other verses rather than from this one.

The verse also does not say how the groups are constituted. It says each of them shall be a group, and the commentators do not specify whether the grouping is by community, by period, or by allegiance. The sūrah's parable suggests community, but the verse does not state it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:33

> **A sign unto them is the dead earth: We revive it and bring forth grain therefrom, that they may eat thereof.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dead Earth**

A sign unto them is the dead earth: We revive it and bring forth grain therefrom, that they may eat thereof. The commentators mark a structural turning point here: these verses begin what can be seen as the second section of the sūrah. The first section ran from the oath through the parable of the town; this one argues from creation.

The commentators describe the method: the verses employ a common Qur'anic rhetorical device by which the wonders of creation are cited as signs of God's Oneness, Perfection, Wisdom, and Omnipotence, and they direct the reader to 3:190–91 and 41:53 for the device in its fuller form.

**Three Meanings of a Sign**

The commentators record that the phrase a sign unto them can have three meanings: it is something to be contemplated; it is a blessing upon human beings; or it is a warning to human beings (Q). All three are preserved.

The three readings are not alternatives so much as layers. The dead earth revived is something to look at, and looking at it discloses a blessing, and the blessing carries a warning for those who do not see it. The commentators do not rank them, and the verse's wording admits all three at once.

**Revival as Evidence of Resurrection**

The commentators state the argument directly: God's ability to revive the earth is cited as evidence of His ability to resurrect. They supply an extensive list of parallels — 7:57, 16:65, 22:5, 29:63, 30:19, 30:24, 30:50, 35:9, 41:31, 41:39, 43:11, 45:5, and 50:9–11.

The recurrence is what makes the argument available. The sūrah does not need to establish the analogy because the Qur'an has established it repeatedly. What this verse contributes is a particular instance: the dead earth, revived, producing grain that is eaten.

**The Second Reading: the Hardened Heart**

The commentators add that as at v12, the verse can also be seen as an allusion to the revivification, through faith and knowledge, of a heart that has been hardened. The cross-reference matters: at v12 the commentators recorded that We give life to the dead could mean the revivification of hearts made dead through heedlessness and ignorance (Aj, IK).

On this reading the dead earth is not only a proof of bodily resurrection but a picture of what happens to a person who receives the message. The sūrah's earlier verses described people who were shackled and veiled; this verse describes the opposite movement, and the commentators connect the two through the shared vocabulary of life and death.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how the earth is revived. It states the revival and the grain, and the next verse supplies the springs. It does not say when this happens or how often.

The verse also does not say that those who eat will be grateful. The question comes at v35 — Will they not then give thanks? — and the commentators note there that people's lack of gratitude is a common Qur'anic theme. The sign is given without a guarantee of reception.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:34

> **And We place gardens of date palms and grapevines therein and make springs flow forth,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Gardens and Springs**

And We place gardens of date palms and grapevines therein and make springs flow forth. The verse adds two things to the revived earth: planted gardens and flowing water. The commentators explain the specification: gardens of date palms and grapevines are named because they were thought by Arabs to be the best of fruits, and they can thus be seen as an allusion to all forms of plant life by which people are nourished (Q).

The commentators supply the cross-references at 2:266 and 23:19, where the same pairing appears. The choice of examples is therefore culturally specific and generalising at once — the best fruits the audience knew, standing for all nourishment.

**The Order of the Verse**

The gardens are placed and the springs are made to flow, and the order is not incidental. The commentators note at v35 that its fruit refers to the fruit produced by plants through the water from the springs (Q). The water precedes the fruit causally, and the verse arranges the two accordingly.

The sequence is earth, gardens, springs — and at v35 the eating. Each step depends on the one before, and the commentators' gloss makes the dependency explicit. The verse is describing a system rather than listing blessings.

**The Sign and Its Layers**

The commentators' three meanings of a sign apply here as they did at v33. The gardens are something to be contemplated; they are a blessing upon human beings; and they are a warning. The commentators do not separate the layers, and the verse does not choose between them.

What makes the warning legible is the sūrah's larger argument. The God who places gardens and makes springs flow is the God who will resurrect, and the commentators state that connection at v33. The blessing is therefore also evidence, and the evidence carries an obligation.

**Creation as Argument**

The commentators place these verses within a wider Qur'anic practice of citing the wonders of creation as signs of God's Oneness, Perfection, Wisdom, and Omnipotence. The sūrah is not making an original argument but joining an established one.

This is why the verse can be so brief. It names gardens, palms, vines, and springs, and does not argue for their significance. The commentators supply the significance from the Qur'an's settled usage, and the verse relies on the reader already knowing it.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who plants the gardens. It says We place gardens, and the commentators do not gloss whether the placing is the creation of the plants or the provision of the conditions for them. Verse 35 raises the question of human labour directly.

The verse also does not say where the springs come from. It states that they are made to flow, and the sūrah's earlier verse at v33 named the revived earth as the context. The mechanism is not described.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:35

> **that they may eat of its fruit and of that which their hands have worked. Will they not then give thanks?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Of Its Fruit and of Their Hands' Work**

That they may eat of its fruit and of that which their hands have worked. Will they not then give thanks? The commentators record two readings of the first term. Its fruit refers to the fruit produced by plants through the water from the springs (Q). Or it could be rendered His fruit, meaning the fruit that God has produced (Aj, IK).

The second reading shifts the possessor from the earth to God, and the commentators preserve both. The difference matters for the second half of the verse, which concerns what human hands have worked.

**The Positive Reading of Human Labour**

The commentators note that as translated, the verse can be read as a positive affirmation of eating the fruits of one's own labour, and they cite a ḥadīth in support: No one eats any food that is better than that from the work of his own hand.

On this reading the verse places divine provision and human work side by side without subordinating the second to the first. The eating is of both, and the ḥadīth supplies the valuation — the food from one's own hand is the best there is. The commentators do not treat this reading as secondary.

**The Alternative: Their Hands Did Not Make It**

The commentators record a second reading of the same phrase. That which their hands have worked could also be rendered though their hands did not make it (IK, Q, R, Ṭ), a reference to the fact that all fruits are ultimately produced by God, citing 27:60 and 56:64.

This reading reverses the sense of the conjunction. Where the first reading joins God's fruit and human work, the second contrasts them: they eat fruit that their hands did not make. The commentators preserve both, and the difference between them is whether human labour is a genuine contribution or an appearance.

**Will They Not Then Give Thanks**

The verse ends with a question, and the commentators supply its context: people's lack of gratitude is a common Qur'anic theme, as at 2:243 — Truly God is Possessed of Bounty for mankind, but most of mankind do not give thanks — with further instances at 10:60, 12:38, 22:66, 25:50, and 40:61.

The question is therefore not open. The commentators' note that most of mankind do not give thanks tells the reader what the expected answer is, and the sūrah's earlier verses have shown a people who did not. The question is a rebuke in the form of an invitation.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what thanks consists in. It asks whether they will give it, and does not enumerate its forms. It does not say what follows if they do not.

The verse also does not resolve the two readings of human labour. It states the eating and asks the question, and the commentators' differing glosses leave the status of human work undecided. The question about gratitude is posed on either reading.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:36

> **Glory be unto Him, Who has created the pairs, all of them, from what the earth makes grow, and from themselves, and from what they know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Glory Be Unto Him**

The verse opens with an ascription of glory to the One who created every pair — from what the earth grows, from human beings themselves, and from what they do not know. The commentators explain the opening phrase: Glory be indicates both God's being beyond all that the idolaters associate with Him and amazement at what they say.

The two senses are preserved together. The phrase is a denial of association and an expression of astonishment, and the commentators do not choose between them. The verse that follows the argument from creation opens by removing the associations that the argument was meant to displace.

**The Pairs**

The commentators record two readings of the word rendered pairs. It translates *azwāj*, which can also mean kinds in the sense of species, thus indicating the many different kinds of things that God has created (Q). It can also be seen as a reference to the creation of all things in pairs, as at 51:49: And of all things We created pairs, that haply you may remember (IK).

The two readings differ in what the pairing is. On the first it is variety — many kinds, each distinct. On the second it is complementarity — each thing having its counterpart. The commentators preserve both, and the cross-reference at 51:49 supports the second explicitly.

**Three Sources of the Pairs**

The verse names three sources: what the earth makes grow, themselves, and what they know not. The commentators gloss the second: from themselves could also mean from their souls, in which case it can be seen as a reference to the male and the female, as at 53:45 — And that He creates the two, *zawjayn*, male and female — which are said to be mates, *azwāj*, created from a single soul, citing 4:1, 6:98, 7:189, and 39:6.

The third source is glossed as an allusion to the unseen realm or to the many things in the created world that humans have not experienced, as at 16:8: And He creates that which you know not. The three sources therefore run from the visible and familiar, through the human, to the unknown.

**The Unknown as Part of the Argument**

The inclusion of what they know not is significant. The argument from creation usually proceeds from what is observed, and this verse extends it to what is not. The commentators' cross-reference to 16:8 places the phrase within a settled usage.

The effect is that the pairing is not limited to what can be inspected. The sūrah's earlier argument from the dead earth relied on observation; this verse adds a domain that observation cannot reach, and the commentators read it as the unseen realm as well as unexperienced things in the visible world.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not enumerate the pairs, and does not say how many kinds there are. It does not explain the pairing, and does not choose between the commentators' two readings of *azwāj*.

The verse also does not say what follows from the pairing. It states the creation and opens with Glory be, and leaves the consequence — the removal of association — to the phrase's own force rather than to an argument.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:37

> **And a sign unto them is the night: We strip the day therefrom, and behold, they are in darkness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night as a Sign**

And a sign unto them is the night: We strip the day therefrom, and behold, they are in darkness. The commentators place this verse within a settled Qur'anic practice: the alternation of night and day is cited throughout the Qur'an as a sign of God's Power and Perfection, as are the sun and the moon.

The commentators cite 39:5 for the same theme: He rolls the night up into the day and rolls the day up into the night, and He made the sun and the moon subservient, each running for a term appointed. They direct the reader to 10:6 and 17:12 for the relationship between night and day treated more fully.

**Strip**

The commentators give the etymology of the central verb. Strip translates *naslakha*, which originally means to remove the skin from an animal. The word is therefore drawn from butchery, and the commentators supply the origin without commenting on its effect.

The image is more violent than the translation suggests. The day is not withdrawn or faded but stripped, as a skin is stripped from what it covers, and what remains is darkness. The commentators do not elaborate the metaphor, but the etymology is what they offer in place of elaboration.

**Behold, They Are in Darkness**

The verse ends with an abrupt result: behold, they are in darkness. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the sūrah does not elaborate. What the word behold carries is suddenness — the darkness is not approached but arrives.

The suddenness matches the verb. If the day is stripped rather than withdrawn, then the darkness is not a gradual transition but an exposure. The commentators' note that the alternation is a sign of God's Power and Perfection applies to the speed as well as to the regularity.

**The Sequence of the Creation Verses**

This verse is the second in the sūrah's second section, which the commentators mark as beginning at vv 33–34 with the argument from the wonders of creation. The dead earth came first, then the night, and the sun and moon follow at vv 38–40.

The order moves from the earth to the sky. The commentators note at vv 33–34 that the device is to cite the wonders of creation as signs of God's Oneness, Perfection, Wisdom, and Omnipotence, and the sequence here follows that device through successive domains.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say why the day is stripped, and does not attribute the darkness to anything other than the stripping. It does not say how often this occurs, though the alternation is the subject.

The verse also does not connect the darkness to the sūrah's earlier imagery of veiling at v9. The commentators do not draw that connection, and the verse does not invite it explicitly, though the shared vocabulary of sight and darkness is available to the reader.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:38

> **And the sun runs to a dwelling place of its own. That is the decree of the Mighty, the Knowing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sun Runs to a Dwelling Place**

And the sun runs to a dwelling place of its own. That is the decree of the Mighty, the Knowing. The commentators record three readings of the verse, and the first is a report. It is reported that a Companion of the Prophet, Abū Dharr, said: The Prophet asked me at sunset, Do you know where the sun goes when it sets? I replied, God and His Messenger know best.

The report continues: He said, It travels till it prostrates itself underneath the Throne and asks permission to rise again, and it is permitted. Then it is about to prostrate itself, but its prostration will not be accepted, and it will ask permission to go on its course, but it will not be permitted and will instead be ordered to return whence it has come, and so it will rise in the west (IK).

**The Eschatological Reading**

The commentators state the consequence of the report: in light of this account, the verse alludes to the sun's final dwelling place on the Day of Judgment. The prostration that will not be accepted and the rising in the west are both end-of-time events, and the dwelling place is where the sun's course terminates.

On this reading the verse is not about the daily setting but about the last one. The commentators do not present this as the only reading, but as what the Abū Dharr report implies.

**The Astronomical Reading**

Others see the verse as a reference to the sun following its winter and summer orbits until each solstice, when it reaches its northernmost or southernmost extreme before reversing direction (IK). Dwelling place then indicates the solstice.

The commentators add an observation about the word: in this interpretation it bears a close resemblance to the word solstice, which derives from the Latin *solstitium*, meaning sun-stopping. The comparison is offered as a parallel in another language rather than as an etymology, and the commentators do not claim a connection between the two.

**The Third Reading: No Dwelling Place**

The commentators record a variant of the verse itself. Another reading — And the sun runs; it has no dwelling place, *lā mustaqarra lahā* — was reportedly preferred by some of the Prophet's Companions, such as the famous Qur'an scholars Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās (IK, Ṭ, Ṭs).

This reading reverses the sense. Where the standard text gives the sun a dwelling place, the variant denies it one. The commentators record the preference of two Companions known for Qur'anic scholarship without endorsing it, and the difference between the readings is not marginal — it changes what the verse asserts.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the three readings. It states the running and the decree, and the commentators supply the alternatives. It does not describe the dwelling place, and does not say where it is.

The verse also does not explain the two names at the end. It calls the decree that of the Mighty, the Knowing, and does not elaborate either. The commentators do not gloss the pairing here, though they note at v5 that the juxtaposition of Might and Mercy elsewhere alludes to God being both just and merciful.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:39

> **And for the moon, We have decreed mansions, till it returns like an old palm stalk.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mansions for the Moon**

And for the moon, We have decreed mansions, till it returns like an old palm stalk. The commentators explain the reference directly: this verse concerns the waxing and waning of the moon through the twenty-eight stations — *manāzil* — of a single lunar month (Q).

The number is specific and the commentators supply it. Twenty-eight stations in a lunar month, each a mansion the moon occupies in turn, and the verse describes the whole cycle as decreed. The word rendered mansions is the same one used elsewhere for the stages of a journey.

**Like an Old Palm Stalk**

The commentators explain the comparison precisely. Like an old palm stalk is a reference to the appearance of what remains from a date cluster after its fruit has been removed and it has withered, when it resembles the thin crescent moon in shape, width, and color (Q, Z).

The gloss identifies three points of resemblance — shape, width, and colour — and the commentators name all three. The image is agricultural and specific: not a stick or a branch but the stalk left after the dates have been taken, dried and curved. The comparison is drawn from what the audience would have handled.

**The Waning and the Cycle**

The verse describes the end of the cycle: till it returns like an old palm stalk. The commentators' note that the stations number twenty-eight in a single lunar month makes the return cyclical rather than terminal. The moon wanes to the stalk and then begins again.

The commentators do not gloss the cycle explicitly, but the reference to the month supplies it. What the verse names is the point of greatest diminishment, and the comparison to the withered stalk is what makes that point visible.

**The Verse in the Sequence**

The sun came at v38 and the moon at v39, and the next verse states that neither overtakes the other. The commentators treat the three verses as a unit concerning the celestial bodies, and at v40 they explain that the orbits are essential for navigating space and calculating time.

The sequence therefore moves from the sun's course to the moon's stations to the relation between them. The commentators do not gloss the arrangement, but the sūrah's argument from creation proceeds by successive instances, and this is the second and third.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name the twenty-eight stations, and does not describe them. It states the decree and the comparison, and the commentators supply the number.

The verse also does not say what the moon's stations are for. The purpose is given at v40 and in the commentators' cross-reference to 2:189, where the stations are linked to the pilgrimage. This verse describes the cycle without stating its use.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:40

> **It befits not the sun to overtake the moon, nor the night to outstrip the day. Each glides in an orbit.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Neither Overtakes the Other**

It befits not the sun to overtake the moon, nor the night to outstrip the day. Each glides in an orbit. The commentators note the parallel at 21:33 and explain the point: the sun, moon, and other celestial bodies move in separate measurable orbits that are essential for navigating space and calculating time.

The separation is what the verse asserts. Neither body overtakes the other, and neither period outstrips the other. The commentators do not gloss the negation as a prohibition but as a description of how the orbits are arranged.

**What Depends on the Orbits**

The commentators state the consequence of the arrangement. Without the orbits human beings would lose their bearings and not be able to continue their lives on earth; more specifically, they would not be able to perform the religious rites required of them.

The two consequences are ordered from general to particular. Navigation and timekeeping come first, and the religious rites second. The commentators do not treat the second as an afterthought; the specification is what makes the first concrete, and the rites are the practices that depend on the reckoning.

**Guidance and Mercy**

The commentators draw the conclusion: in this way the celestial bodies can be seen as manifestations of God's Guidance and Mercy. They cite 10:5: He it is Who made the sun a radiance, and the moon a light, and determined for it stations, that you might know the number of years and the reckoning of time — see also 17:12.

The cross-reference at 2:189 makes the connection to worship explicit: They ask thee about the new moons. Say, They are markers of time for mankind and for the ḥajj. The commentators note that in this way the sun and the moon serve humanity in this life.

**The Limit of the Service**

The commentators add a qualification: though on the Day of Judgment the sun and the moon are brought together, citing 75:9. The service the bodies render is bounded by this world, and the verse at 75:9 marks where it ends.

The qualification is consistent with the sūrah's larger movement. The argument from creation establishes what God has arranged for human life, and the sūrah's earlier verses establish that the arrangement is not permanent. The commentators supply the cross-reference that makes the boundary explicit.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe the orbits, and does not say how they are measured. It states that each glides in an orbit, and the commentators supply the function rather than the mechanics.

The verse also does not say what would happen if one did overtake the other. It states that neither does, and the commentators explain what depends on that rather than describing the alternative.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:41

> **And a sign unto them is that We carried their progeny in the full-laden Ark.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Their Progeny in the Full-Laden Ark**

And a sign unto them is that We carried their progeny in the full-laden Ark. The verse returns to the sign formula used at vv 33 and 37, and the commentators identify the referent: according to most commentators, this verse refers to Noah's Ark, by which God preserved one generation of humanity and thereby all of humanity (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs, Z).

The commentators' gloss supplies what makes the Ark a sign for this audience. It was not merely a vessel but the means by which one generation was preserved and, through it, all subsequent generations. The word progeny is therefore doing real work: those carried were the ancestors of everyone who came after.

**The Alternative Reading**

The commentators record a second interpretation. Others take the verse as a reference to past generations being carried in the wombs of women, which are here likened to the full-laden Ark (Q, Z).

On this reading the Ark is a comparison rather than a referent. The verse would be about the carrying of generations in the womb, and the Ark would supply the image of a laden vessel preserving what is inside it. The commentators preserve both readings without preferring either, and the verse's wording admits both.

**The Sign and Its Audience**

The verse says a sign unto them, and the commentators do not specify who them is here. The sūrah's earlier uses of the formula at vv 33 and 37 were part of the argument from creation addressed to those who deny the resurrection.

The Ark functions differently from the dead earth and the night. Those were observable and recurring; the Ark was a single event in the past. The commentators' note that it preserved all of humanity is what makes it available as a sign to a later audience — they are descended from those carried.

**The Verse in the Sequence**

The creation signs ran from the earth at v33 through the night, sun, moon, and orbits at vv 37–40. This verse moves from what is continually observable to what happened once. The commentators do not gloss the shift, but the sūrah's argument broadens.

The next verse continues the theme of carrying, and the commentators there record readings that extend the reference from Noah's Ark to ships generally and to beasts of burden. The two verses together move from a particular vessel to the general provision of transport.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not name Noah, and does not recount the flood. It states the carrying and leaves the narrative to the Qur'an's other accounts, which the commentators do not cross-reference here.

The verse also does not say how many were carried. It says their progeny, and the commentators supply the claim that one generation was preserved and thereby all of humanity. The verse itself does not quantify.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:42

> **And We created for them the like thereof upon which they ride.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Like Thereof**

And We created for them the like thereof upon which they ride. The commentators record three readings of what the likeness is. The verse is taken by many as a reference to other ships that are similar to Noah's Ark (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs).

On this reading the verse extends the sign from a single vessel to a class of vessels. What was done once for Noah's generation is done continually, and the commentators treat the likeness as one of kind rather than of appearance.

**The Ships of the Desert**

The commentators note that many understand the verse as a reference to camels, which were known as the ships of the desert (IK, Q, Ṭ, Ṭs, Z). The reading is not marginal — the commentators say many hold it, and they supply the Arabic idiom that makes it natural.

The comparison is functional. A camel carries its rider across terrain where no ship can go, and the idiom was established enough for the commentators to cite it without explanation. On this reading the verse addresses an audience whose daily transport was not a ship.

**All Beasts of Burden**

Others say that it is a reference to all beasts of burden (Q, Ṭ, Ṭs). This reading is the most general, and it subsumes the camel reading while extending beyond it.

The three readings therefore run from ships, to camels, to all carrying animals. The commentators preserve all three without preferring any, and the verse's phrase the like thereof is general enough to carry each.

**Created for Them**

The verse says We created for them, and the commentators do not gloss the phrase. What it establishes is that the provision is intentional rather than incidental — the likeness was created for the purpose of carrying.

This connects to the commentators' treatment of v40, where they explained that the orbits of the celestial bodies are essential for navigating space and calculating time and can be seen as manifestations of God's Guidance and Mercy. The provision of transport is the same kind of provision, and the verse states it in the same register.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the three readings, and does not describe what the likeness consists in. It states the creation and the riding, and the commentators supply the referents.

The verse also does not say who them is. The previous verse spoke of their progeny in the Ark, and this verse continues with them, but the commentators do not specify whether the reference is to Noah's descendants or to human beings generally.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:43

> **And if We will, We drown them, such that they would have none to call upon and would not be saved,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**If We Will, We Drown Them**

And if We will, We drown them, such that they would have none to call upon and would not be saved. The commentators state the point directly: this verse indicates that God could drown people at any moment (IK).

The conditional is what carries the claim. The verse does not say God will drown them but that He could, and the commentators gloss it as a reminder that one's life is always in God's Hands. The possibility is present at every moment, not only at sea.

**The Pattern of Forgetting**

The commentators connect the verse to a recurring Qur'anic theme. They cite other passages that speak of those who remember God when they are on the verge of drowning, yet forget Him when danger is no longer imminent, and direct the reader to 10:22–23 and 17:66–67.

The pattern is what makes the verse a rebuke rather than a threat. The danger produces remembrance and the safety produces forgetting, and the commentators treat this as a settled description of human behaviour rather than as an accusation against a particular group.

**None to Call Upon**

The commentators record two readings of the phrase. They would have none to call upon for help or protection. This phrase could also be rendered None would call upon them, indicating that they are completely forgotten.

The two readings reverse the direction. On the first the drowning have no one to appeal to; on the second no one appeals for them. The commentators support the second with several verses that speak of the disbelievers being forgotten — 7:51, 9:67, 20:126, 32:14, 45:34, and 59:19 — and preserve both readings.

**Would Not Be Saved**

The verse closes with and would not be saved. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the verse does not elaborate. What it excludes is rescue from any source, and the preceding phrase has already removed the possibility of appeal.

The two clauses together describe a complete isolation: no one to call upon and no saving. The commentators' note that life is always in God's Hands is the positive counterpart — the isolation is not a general condition but what obtains when the one Hand is not appealed to.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that God wills the drowning. It states the possibility and leaves the will unexercised. It does not say when or where, and does not restrict the drowning to those at sea.

The verse also does not say why they would not be saved. It states the outcome and the next verse supplies the exception — save as a mercy from Us — which the commentators treat as the qualification on the whole statement.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:44

> **save as a mercy from Us and an enjoyment for a while.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Save as a Mercy From Us**

Save as a mercy from Us and an enjoyment for a while. The exception qualifies the previous verse's statement that they would not be saved. The commentators explain the first term: for a while indicates that God will postpone the disbelievers' punishment, thus giving them time until the moment of their death to repent or to prove their true nature.

The two purposes are joined by or rather than and, and the commentators do not choose between them. The respite allows repentance, and it also discloses what the person actually is. The commentators cite 3:178 in support: And let not those who disbelieve suppose that the respite We grant them is good for them. We only grant them respite that they may increase in sin, and theirs shall be a humiliating punishment.

**The Respite and Its Ambiguity**

The cross-reference at 3:178 complicates the mercy named here. The respite is described as a mercy in this verse and as something that increases sin in that one, and the commentators cite both without reconciling them.

What the two share is that the respite is not a reward. It is time, and what the time produces depends on the one given it. The commentators' note that the respite allows repentance or the proving of true nature is precisely this ambiguity stated as a pair of outcomes.

**An Enjoyment for a While**

The commentators explain the second term: in this context, enjoyment for a while indicates the ephemeral delights of this world, which are naught but the enjoyment of delusion, citing 3:185 and 57:20, and which stand in stark contrast to the rewards of the next life, with cross-references at 3:14, 4:77, 9:38, 13:26, 20:131, 28:60, 40:39, and 42:36.

The commentators' phrase enjoyment of delusion is what gives the term its weight. The enjoyment is real, and the delusion is about its duration. The word while is therefore the operative term, and the commentators' extensive cross-referencing shows how settled the contrast is across the Qur'an.

**The Verse in the Argument**

The verse closes a sequence that began at v41 with the Ark as a sign. The carrying was a mercy, the provision of transport a mercy, and the non-drowning a mercy with a limit. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the three verses move from a past deliverance to a present provision to a present reprieve.

The limit is what connects the sequence to the sūrah's larger argument. The respite ends at death, and the sūrah's earlier verses have described the arraignment at v32 and the destruction of previous generations at v31. The enjoyment is for a while, and the while is bounded.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how long the while is. It states the limit as death rather than as a duration, and the commentators do not supply a measure.

The verse also does not say that the mercy is withdrawn at death. It states the exception and its term, and leaves what follows to the sūrah's later treatment of the Day. The commentators' note that the respite gives time to repent or to prove true nature leaves both outcomes open.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:45

> **And when it is said unto them, “Be mindful of that which is before you and of that which is behind you, that haply you may receive mercy.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Be Mindful of What Is Before and Behind**

The exhortation put to them is to be mindful of what lies ahead of them and what lies behind, so that they might receive mercy. The commentators record three readings of the two phrases, and the differences between them are substantial.

The first reading takes that which is before you as a reference to the Hereafter, which is to come and for which one must work, and that which is behind you as a reference to this world, by which one should not be deluded (Q, Ṭs). On this reading the two phrases are temporal and the exhortation is about orientation — work for what is coming and do not be taken in by what has passed.

**The Apparent and the Hidden**

According to others, the first phrase refers to what is apparent and the second to what is hidden (Q). The commentators cite 34:9 in support: Have they not considered that which is before them and that which is behind them of the sky and the earth? If We will, We cause the earth to engulf them or fragments from the sky to fall upon them. Truly in that is a sign for every penitent servant.

On this reading the phrases are spatial rather than temporal, and the mindfulness is about the encompassing created order rather than about past and future. The cross-reference at 34:9 supplies the same pairing applied to sky and earth.

**The Sins Past and to Come**

The third reading is attributed to two named authorities. According to Mujāhid and Ibn ʿAbbās, the former refers to the sins one has committed (IK, Q), and the latter to the sins one will commit (Q), meaning that one should be mindful of God so as to avoid sin in the future and repent for the sins of the past (Ṭs).

This reading reverses the temporal direction of the first. Where the first reading puts the Hereafter before and this world behind, this one puts past sins before and future sins behind. The commentators preserve all three without preferring any, and the verse's wording is general enough to carry each.

**The Unfinished Conditional**

The commentators note a structural feature of the verse. The phrase they turn away from it — which appears at the start of the next verse — could be placed at the end of this verse as an implied response to the conditional phrase when it is said unto them (Q, Ṭs).

On this reading the verse is complete in itself: when it is said to them, be mindful, they turn away. The sūrah's division between the two verses is then a matter of arrangement rather than of sense, and the commentators record the alternative placement without endorsing it.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not choose between the three readings of the two phrases, and does not say who gives the exhortation. It uses the passive it is said, and the commentators do not supply a speaker.

The verse also does not say that mercy will follow. It says that haply you may receive mercy, and the commentators do not gloss the qualification. The hope is stated without a guarantee, and the next verse records the turning away.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:46

> **Never did a sign from among the signs of their Lord come unto them but that they turned away from it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Never a Sign But They Turned Away**

Never did a sign from among the signs of their Lord come unto them but that they turned away from it. The commentators note the parallel at 6:4 and record that the verse can be understood as a reference to the signs mentioned in the preceding verses, to the whole of the created order, to the whole of revelation, or to all three, as all are said to be signs for those who possess intellect, citing 3:190.

The breadth of the possible referents is what makes the verse comprehensive. The sūrah has just spent thirteen verses citing signs — the dead earth, the night, the sun, the moon, the orbits, the Ark, the provision of transport — and this verse says that every one of them was turned away from.

**The Covering and the Deafness**

The commentators cite 18:57 in support: And who does greater wrong than one who has been reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away from them and forgets that which his hands have sent forth? Surely We have placed coverings over their hearts, such that they understand not, and in their ears a deafness. Even if thou callest them to guidance, they will never be rightly guided. They add 32:22 as a further parallel.

The cross-reference supplies what the verse states without explanation. The turning away is not a failure of perception but a condition imposed, and the commentators connect it to the sūrah's earlier imagery at vv 8–9 of shackles and barriers.

**Veiled, Not Blind**

The commentators make a distinction that the verse itself does not draw. It is not that these people cannot see the signs, but that God veils them due to their arrogance and disbelief, as at 7:146: I shall turn away from My signs those who wax arrogant upon the earth without right.

The distinction matters for culpability. The veiling is a response to arrogance rather than an original condition, and the commentators cite 7:146 to establish the sequence. The signs remain signs; what has changed is the capacity to receive them.

**Even If They Saw Every Sign**

The commentators add a final statement: even if they were to see every sign, they would not believe in them. This closes the question the verse might otherwise leave open — whether more evidence would help.

The statement connects to the sūrah's earlier verses. At v9 the commentators explained that the barrier means they cannot benefit from guidance no matter what the Prophet does, and at vv 10–11 that the warning makes no difference to them. This verse generalises the claim from the Prophet's exertion to the signs themselves.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not enumerate the signs, and does not say how many came. It states the pattern of turning away and leaves the instances to the preceding verses.

The verse also does not say that the turning away is irreversible. It describes what has happened whenever a sign came, and the commentators' note about the veiling being due to arrogance implies a cause that could in principle change, though the verse does not say so.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:47

> **And when it is said unto them, “Spend of that wherewith God has provided you,” those who disbelieve say to those who believe, “Are we to feed one whom, if God willed, He would feed him? You are in naught but manifest error.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Spend of What God Has Provided**

The command put to them is to give out of what God has already given them, and the disbelievers answer the believers with a question: are we to feed someone God would have fed if He had willed it? The commentators note that although there are several different accounts identifying the subject of this verse, they all agree that it responds to those who scoffed at the injunction to spend in the way of God — to feed the poor — found throughout the Qur'an.

The agreement across differing accounts is what the commentators emphasise. The occasion may be disputed, but the target is not: the verse answers an objection to charitable giving, and the objection takes the form of a question about God's ability.

**The Account of Abū Bakr**

The commentators relate the occasion in detail. It is said that some disbelievers among the Quraysh said to Abū Bakr, who used to feed the destitute among the Muslims from his own wealth: O Abū Bakr, do you claim that God is able to feed these people? He replied, Yes. To which they replied, So why is it that he does not feed them?

The exchange continues. He said, Some people are tried with poverty, others with wealth. The poor are commanded to be patient, and the wealthy are commanded to give. So they replied, O Abū Bakr, surely you are in error. Do you claim that God is able to feed these people, yet He does not feed them, then you feed them? Then this verse was revealed, as were 92:5–6 (Q).

**The Shape of the Objection**

The argument the commentators record is worth setting out, because the verse answers it without restating it. If God is able to feed the poor and does not, then either He is unwilling or the feeding is not His business. If the feeding is not His business, then a human being who feeds them is doing what God has declined to do, and the objection concludes that this is an error.

Abū Bakr's reply in the account supplies the missing premise: poverty and wealth are both trials, and the command to give is part of the trial of wealth. The commentators do not gloss the reply as the answer to the objection, but the verse's command to spend presupposes it.

**You Are in Naught but Manifest Error**

The charge is turned back on the believers in the verse's final phrase. The commentators note at v24 that manifest error — *ḍalāl mubīn* — is employed throughout the Qur'an to indicate one whose wrongdoing makes him stray from the path of truth (Ṭ), and that it can also be read as error that makes manifest or, on another reading, error that has been made manifest (Bḍ).

The same phrase the man from the outskirts applied to himself at v24 is here applied by the disbelievers to the believers. The commentators do not draw the parallel, but the sūrah's repetition of the phrase makes it available, and the two uses point in opposite directions.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not answer the objection in its own words. It records the command to spend and the reply of the disbelievers, and leaves the rebuttal to the reader. The commentators supply the occasion and Abū Bakr's reasoning from the report rather than from the verse.

The verse also does not say how much is to be spent or to whom. It says spend of that wherewith God has provided you, and the commentators note that the injunction to feed the poor is found throughout the Qur'an without enumerating its terms here.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:48

> **And they say, “When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**When Will This Promise Come to Pass**

And they say, When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful? The commentators note the parallels at 10:48, 21:38, 27:71, 34:29, and 67:25, and explain the occasion: the disbelievers are said to have mocked the Prophet for not knowing exactly when the Hour and the Day of Resurrection, about which he warned them, would come to pass, for they expected a prophet to be able to see the future.

The expectation is what the commentators identify as the target. The objection is not that the promise is implausible but that the one making it cannot date it, and the commentators treat the expectation of foreknowledge as a mistaken criterion for prophethood.

**The Answer at 7:187**

The commentators supply the response from elsewhere in the Qur'an. In answer to this expectation, 7:187 states: They question thee about the Hour, when it will set in. Say, Knowledge thereof lies only with my Lord. None save He shall manifest it at its proper time. Heavy shall it weigh upon the heavens and the earth. It shall not come upon you but suddenly. They question thee as if thou knew it well. Say, Knowledge thereof lies only with God, but most of mankind know not.

The answer does not supply a date but relocates the knowledge. The commentators cite the verse at length because it addresses the expectation directly: the question is asked as if the Prophet knew, and the answer is that he does not, and that this is not a deficiency.

**This Promise**

The commentators record a further point about the phrase. According to some, the disbelievers referred to the Resurrection as this promise because they thought they would be the ones to be rewarded with good. Thus Ibn Kathīr glosses this verse with 42:18: Those who believe not in it seek to hasten it.

The reading makes the question ironic in a particular way. The demand for the promise to come is not a challenge from those who fear it but from those who expect to benefit from it. The commentators preserve this as what some say, and Ibn Kathīr's gloss supplies the cross-reference that supports it.

**The Question and the Next Verse**

The verse is a question, and the sūrah answers it at the next verse rather than in this one. The commentators explain at v49 that a single cry refers to the first blast of the trumpet, which will level all that is on the earth, and that it will come while they are oblivious, disputing, bargaining, eating and drinking.

The answer therefore does not give a date but a description. The question asked when, and the response says suddenly and without warning. The commentators' note that it comes while they are engaged in the affairs of the world is what makes the description an answer.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not answer the question. It records the demand and leaves the response to the following verse and to the cross-reference at 7:187.

The verse also does not say that the questioners are mistaken to ask. It reports the question and the condition attached to it — if you are truthful — and the commentators identify the mistaken expectation from the occasion rather than from the verse's wording.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:49

> **They await naught but a single cry that will seize them while they dispute among themselves,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Single Cry While They Dispute**

They await naught but a single cry that will seize them while they dispute among themselves. The commentators identify the cry: it refers to the first blast of Seraphiel's trumpet, and they direct the reader to 39:68 for the fuller account. The blast will level all that is on the earth.

The identification settles what the sūrah leaves unnamed. Verse 48 recorded the demand for a date, and this verse supplies the event instead. The commentators do not gloss the substitution, but the sequence answers the question by describing what will happen rather than when.

**While They Are Oblivious**

The commentators describe the circumstance in detail: the cry will come while they are oblivious to it, disputing, bargaining, eating and drinking, and engaging in other affairs of the world.

The list is deliberately ordinary. The commentators do not describe a moment of crisis or anticipation but of routine — commerce, meals, argument. The seizure occurs inside the ordinary rather than at its edge, and the enumeration is what makes that plain.

**Disputing Among Themselves**

The verse specifies that they are disputing among themselves, and the commentators include disputing first in their list of occupations. The dispute is not identified, and the commentators do not say what it concerns.

The context supplies a candidate. The preceding verses recorded the disbelievers' objections — the question about feeding the poor at v47 and the demand for a date at v48. The dispute named here may be a continuation of those, and the commentators leave the identification open.

**The Answer to the Demand for a Date**

The commentators connect this verse to the question at v48. There the disbelievers asked when the promise would come to pass; here the answer is that it will come suddenly, during ordinary activity. The commentators at v52 make the connection explicit, noting that the last sentence of that verse answers the question posed at v48.

The answer therefore does not supply the requested information. It replaces a date with a description, and the description is of an event that cannot be anticipated. The commentators do not gloss this as a refusal, but the sūrah's structure makes it one.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how long the cry lasts, and does not describe its sound. It does not say whether anyone is exempt from the seizure, and does not distinguish believers from disbelievers here.

The verse also does not say what follows the seizure. The next verse describes the impossibility of any final act, and v51 describes the second blast. The commentators supply the interval between the two from other sources rather than from this verse.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:50

> **and then they can make no bequest, nor return to their people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Bequest and No Return**

And then they can make no bequest, nor return to their people. The commentators explain the point: the Hour will come upon people in an instant, so that they cannot attend to any affairs of this world, thus indicating that one should be ready for death at every moment.

The two things named are both ordinary closing acts. A bequest settles what one leaves behind, and a return to one's people is the most basic of homecomings. The verse says neither will be possible, and the commentators take this as the measure of the suddenness.

**The Alternative Reading of Bequest**

The commentators record a second sense of the phrase. Make no bequest could also mean do not exhort or enjoin, or give no advice, which would then imply that there must be no delay in following the command to exhort one another to truth, and exhort one another to patience, citing 103:3.

On this reading the verse is not only about the impossibility of settling affairs but about the impossibility of giving final warning. The commentators connect it to the Qur'anic command to mutual exhortation, and the implication they draw is that the exhortation must happen before rather than at the end.

**Ready at Every Moment**

The commentators draw the practical conclusion: the verse indicates that one should be ready for death at every moment. The reasoning is that if the Hour comes during ordinary activity, as v49 described, then there is no moment at which preparation can be postponed to.

This connects to the sūrah's earlier treatment of the respite. At v44 the commentators explained that for a while indicates God postponing the punishment and giving time until death to repent or to prove one's true nature. The respite is bounded by death, and this verse describes the boundary arriving without warning.

**Nor Return to Their People**

The second impossibility is the more personal. A return to one's people is what a traveller expects, and the verse removes it. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the sūrah does not elaborate.

The pairing of the two impossibilities covers both directions. The bequest concerns what one sends forward, and the return concerns where one goes back. The verse excludes both, and the commentators' note that no affairs of this world can be attended to is the general statement of which these are the instances.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who them is. It continues from the previous verse, where the cry seizes those who dispute, and the commentators do not restrict the reference.

The verse also does not say what happens after. It states the two impossibilities and stops. The next verse describes the trumpet and the rushing forth from the graves, and the commentators supply the interval between the two blasts from other sources.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:51

> **And the trumpet will be blown. Then, behold, they will rush forth from their graves unto their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Blast**

And the trumpet will be blown. Then, behold, they will rush forth from their graves unto their Lord. The commentators identify this as the second blast from Seraphiel's trumpet, which will herald the Resurrection. The first was described at v49, and the commentators direct the reader to 39:68–70 for the relation between the two cries or blasts.

The two blasts are distinct in function. The first levels what is on the earth, as the commentators explained at v49; the second raises the dead. The sūrah names the trumpet without numbering it, and the commentators supply the sequence.

**The Interval of Forty Years**

The commentators record a specific tradition: between the two blasts there is said to be an interval of forty years (Āl, IK, Q, Ṭ), and they cite 70:43 as a further reference.

The interval is presented as what is said rather than as what the verse states. The commentators attribute it to four authorities and do not endorse it, and the verse itself says nothing about a gap. The tradition is preserved because it was widely held, not because the text requires it.

**Rushing Forth**

The verse describes the rising as a rush. The commentators do not gloss the verb, and the sūrah does not elaborate. What the word carries is speed and direction — the movement is toward their Lord, and it is not deliberate.

The destination is named, and the commentators do not comment on it. The rushing is unto their Lord, which is the arraignment described at v32, where the commentators explained that all human beings, believers and disbelievers alike, will be arraigned on the Day of Judgment.

**Behold, and the Suddenness**

The word behold recurs from v37, where the night was stripped and they were in darkness. The commentators do not gloss the particle, and the sūrah uses it at points of abrupt transition.

The commentators at v53 cite 16:77 in connection with the parallel verse: that it is but a single cry is said to imply that the matter of the Hour is as the blinking of an eye, or nearer still. The suddenness is therefore a settled feature of the Qur'an's description, and behold is the particle that marks it.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who blows the trumpet. The commentators name Seraphiel from tradition, and the verse uses the passive. It does not describe the graves, and does not say how the rising occurs.

The verse also does not say what happens on arrival. It states the rushing and the destination, and leaves the arraignment to v53, where the commentators direct the reader back to v32.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:52

> **They will say, “Oh, woe unto us! Who has raised us from our place of sleep?” “This is that which the Compassionate did promise; and the message bearers spoke true.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Oh, Woe Unto Us**

They will say, Oh, woe unto us! Who has raised us from our place of sleep? The commentators record an alternate reading of the first phrase: Oh, woe unto us! Who has raised us — *waylanā man baʿathanā* — is understood by some to mean Oh, woe unto us for our having been raised, *waylanā min baʿthinā* (Āl, Q).

The two readings differ on whether the woe is directed at the raising or exclaimed at it. On the standard reading the question is genuine; on the alternative it is a lament about the fact of having been raised. The commentators preserve both without preferring either.

**Our Place of Sleep**

The commentators note a further variant: from our place of sleep could also be read from our sleep (Āl). The difference is between a location and a state, and the commentators record both.

The description of the grave as a place of sleep is what makes the question intelligible. Those who ask have experienced the interval as sleep rather than as death, and the commentators' note about the forty-year interval at v51 supplies the duration that such a sleep might cover.

**The Answer**

This is that which the Compassionate did promise; and the message bearers spoke true. The commentators explain the function of this sentence: it is an answer to the question posed by the disbelievers in v48, as if to say that it does not matter when the promise will come to pass, only that people must be prepared for it.

The connection is what gives the verse its force. At v48 the demand was for a date; here the answer is that the promise has come and the date was never the point. The commentators draw the implication explicitly, and it is the sūrah's answer to the objection recorded earlier.

**Who Speaks the Answer**

The commentators record two readings of the speaker. The answer can be seen as a continuation of what the disbelievers say to themselves, or as words of the angels addressed to them (IK).

The two readings differ substantially. On the first the disbelievers acknowledge the truth of the messengers in the moment of their own ruin; on the second the acknowledgement is put to them by another. The commentators preserve both, and the verse's wording does not mark a change of speaker.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that the acknowledgement benefits those who make it. It records the words and leaves their effect unstated. The commentators do not gloss it as a repentance, and the sūrah does not connect it to mercy.

The verse also does not say what happens next. It records the exchange and moves to the arraignment at v53. The commentators' note that the answer addresses the question at v48 is about the argument rather than about the fate of those who speak.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:53

> **There shall be but a single cry. Then, behold, they will all be arraigned before Us!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**But a Single Cry**

There shall be but a single cry. Then, behold, they will all be arraigned before Us! The commentators note the parallel at 79:13–14 and explain the significance: that it is but a single cry is said to imply that the matter of the Hour is as the blinking of an eye, or nearer still, citing 16:77.

The singularity is the point. The verse does not describe a process but a single sound followed immediately by the arraignment, and the commentators' cross-reference supplies the Qur'an's own measure of the interval — the blink of an eye, or less.

**The Two Cries Distinguished**

The sūrah has now named a cry three times. At v29 the people of the town were extinguished by a single cry; at v49 the first blast of the trumpet seizes the disputing; here a single cry brings the arraignment. The commentators distinguish the blasts at v51, identifying the second as the one that heralds the Resurrection.

The cry at v53 is not the same event as the cry at v49. The commentators at v49 identified that one as the first blast, which levels what is on the earth, and at v51 the second as the one that raises the dead. The arraignment follows the raising, and the commentators direct the reader to v32 for the term arraigned.

**All Arraigned**

The commentators at v32 explained that this verse and that one indicate all human beings, believers and disbelievers alike, will be arraigned on the Day of Judgment, and that other verses indicate only the disbelievers will be arraigned unto the punishment, citing 30:16 and 34:38. They resolved the tension by identifying two arraignments: the gathering of all, and the Reckoning the disbelievers endure before being cast into Hell.

The cross-reference is what makes this verse intelligible. The word all here matches the universal gathering at v32, and the commentators' two-stage reading explains how the same term can elsewhere apply only to some.

**Behold, Again**

The particle behold recurs from vv 37 and 51. The commentators do not gloss it, and the sūrah uses it at each abrupt transition — the darkness after the stripping of the day, the rushing forth from the graves, and here the arraignment.

The repetition is what gives the three verses their shared rhythm. Each describes a sudden change of state, and each marks it with the same word. The commentators' note at 16:77 that the matter of the Hour is as the blinking of an eye applies to all three.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what happens at the arraignment. It states the cry and the assembly, and the commentators supply the procedure from v32 and from other verses rather than from this one.

The verse also does not say who sounds the cry. The commentators name Seraphiel at vv 49 and 51 from tradition, and this verse uses no agent at all. The absence is consistent with the suddenness the commentators identify.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:54

> **This day no soul will be wronged in any way, and you will not be recompensed, save for that which you used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Soul Will Be Wronged**

This day no soul will be wronged in any way, and you will not be recompensed, save for that which you used to do. The commentators are careful about what the verse does not mean: it does not mean that none shall be punished. Rather, it indicates that the punishment the disbelievers receive is because they have wronged themselves by having disbelieved and turned away from God's signs.

The distinction is between being wronged and being punished. The verse denies the first and the commentators supply the second from the surrounding verses. The punishment is real, and it is not an injustice.

**The Principle Across the Qur'an**

The commentators note that this idea is mentioned throughout the Qur'an, and cite 3:117: God wrongs them not, but themselves do they wrong; and 10:44: Truly God does not wrong human beings in the least, but rather human beings wrong themselves.

The recurrence is what allows the verse to state the principle without argument. The commentators add 39:69: Judgment will be made between them in truth, and they shall not be wronged, with further parallels at 45:22 and 46:19. The formula is settled enough that the sūrah can use it in a single line.

**Even the Worst Punished**

The commentators make the point explicitly: in this respect, even those who receive the worst of punishments are not wronged in the sense of having been treated unjustly. The claim is not that the punishment is light but that it is deserved.

This is the same principle the sūrah applied to the destroyed towns. At v31 the commentators noted that they and them can refer to the former peoples who were destroyed or to the inhabitants of Makkah, and the sūrah's parable at vv 13–29 showed a town extinguished after mocking its messengers. The principle stated here is what makes that extinction intelligible as justice.

**Save for That Which You Used to Do**

The second half of the verse states the measure of recompense. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the verse does not elaborate. What it establishes is that the recompense corresponds to the deeds, and the sūrah's earlier verse at v12 described the recording of what people sent forth and what they left behind.

The connection between the two verses is what the commentators' reading supplies. The record at v12 and the recompense at v54 are the same deeds viewed from two ends, and the correspondence between them is what makes the punishment not a wrong.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that all will be punished. It denies wronging and states the measure, and the commentators note that the punishment concerns those who disbelieved and turned away.

The verse also does not say how the measure is calculated. It says recompense corresponds to what was done, and leaves the reckoning to the arraignment described at v53 and to the commentators' two-stage reading at v32.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:55

> **Truly the inhabitants of the Garden on that Day will be busy rejoicing,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Busy Rejoicing**

Truly the inhabitants of the Garden on that Day will be busy rejoicing. The commentators explain what the busyness consists in: this verse implies that the inhabitants of the Garden are busy with the delights of Paradise and spiritual realities (IK, Q) rather than preoccupied by the concerns of this world.

The word busy is notable because it is ordinarily a term for labour and distraction. The commentators read it as describing absorption rather than distraction, and the contrast they draw is with the concerns of this world rather than with idleness.

**The Contrast With the Arraignment**

The verse follows the arraignment at v53 and the statement of justice at v54. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the sūrah's structure supplies the contrast: the arraignment is universal, and the occupation of the Garden's inhabitants is what some are doing during it.

The commentators at v32 explained that the two arraignments are the gathering of all and the Reckoning the disbelievers endure. This verse describes what the others are doing, and the busyness with delight is what occupies the place the Reckoning occupies for the rest.

**Levels of the Garden**

The commentators record a further distinction. According to some, those in different levels of Paradise are occupied with different delights (Q). The occupation is therefore not uniform, and the commentators treat the variety as following from the levels.

This connects to the sūrah's earlier promise at v11, where the Prophet was told to give glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward to whomsoever follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate unseen. The commentators there explained that a generous reward means Paradise or the Garden, and this verse begins the description of it.

**The Highest Delight**

The commentators name the highest of the delights: it involves being absorbed in the vision of God (R). The attribution is to a single authority, and the commentators do not elaborate what the vision consists in.

The reading gives the word spiritual realities in the commentators' earlier gloss its specific content. The busyness with delight has a hierarchy, and its top is not a created pleasure but the vision of God. The commentators record this as R's reading rather than as the verse's statement.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe the delights, and does not enumerate the levels. It states the busyness and the rejoicing, and the commentators supply the content from other sources.

The verse also does not say how long the occupation lasts. It places it on that Day, and the sūrah's later verses describe the Garden as an abode, but this verse does not state a duration.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:56

> **they and their spouses reclining upon couches in the shade.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Reclining Upon Couches**

They and their spouses reclining upon couches in the shade. The commentators note the parallel at 43:70 and explain the posture: those in Paradise are reclining upon couches, indicating that they are content and at peace, citing R on 18:31 and 55:54.

The reclining is therefore not merely a position but a sign. The commentators read it as indicating contentment, and the cross-references supply the same image in the Qur'an's other descriptions of the Garden.

**The Couches as Thrones**

The commentators record a further reading: some also view the couches as thrones, indicating that they have attained a high rank. The reading connects the furniture to status rather than to rest.

The two readings are not opposed. A throne is a kind of seat, and reclining upon one combines the contentment of the first reading with the rank of the second. The commentators preserve both without reconciling them explicitly.

**Spouses Made Pure**

The commentators explain the companions: believers are to be rewarded with spouses made pure, citing 2:25, 3:15, and 4:57. They record that this can be understood as an allusion to spiritual beings particular to Paradise, to spouses of this world who have also attained to Paradise, or to both.

The three readings differ on who the spouses are, and the commentators preserve all three. The verse itself says their spouses without qualification, and the commentators' cross-references supply the description pure without identifying the referent.

**In the Shade**

The commentators direct the reader to 56:30 for the connotations of the Qur'an's use of shade. The word is not merely a description of temperature but carries established associations in the Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden.

The commentators do not elaborate those connotations here, and the verse does not explain them. What the phrase contributes to this verse is a setting — the reclining is in shade, and the shade is part of what makes the posture one of contentment rather than mere rest.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe the couches or the shade, and does not say where they are. It states the posture, the company, and the setting, and the commentators supply the significance from parallel verses.

The verse also does not say that all the inhabitants of the Garden have spouses. It describes them and their spouses reclining, and the commentators' note about spouses made pure concerns the reward generally rather than this verse's scope.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:57

> **Therein they have fruit and whatsoever they call for.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fruit and Whatsoever They Call For**

Therein they have fruit and whatsoever they call for. The commentators begin with a comparison: according to several aḥādīth, the fruits of Paradise are far superior to those of this world. The superiority is not specified beyond the claim, and the commentators do not elaborate it.

The verse pairs the fruit with a much broader provision. What they have is not a list but an open category — whatsoever they call for — and the commentators treat the fruit as an instance rather than as the whole of what is available.

**The Report of the Outstretched Hand**

The commentators relate a specific account. There are several accounts in which the Prophet is said to have stretched forth his hand as if reaching for something and, when asked about it, responded: I was shown the Garden and wanted to have a bunch of fruit from it. Had I taken it, you would have eaten from it as long as the world remains.

The report is offered as evidence for the superiority of the fruit. What it adds is the counterfactual — the fruit would have remained edible for the duration of the world — and the commentators cite it without endorsing the account's details.

**Calling, Willing, Desiring**

The commentators extend the provision considerably. The inhabitants of Paradise will receive whatsoever they call for, citing 41:31 and 44:55, and whatsoever they will or desire, with an extensive list of parallels: 16:31, 25:16, 39:34, 41:31, 42:22, 43:71, 50:35, 52:22, 56:20–24, and 77:41–44.

The three terms — calling, willing, desiring — cover the range from expressed request to unexpressed inclination. The commentators' extensive cross-referencing shows how settled the claim is across the Qur'an, and the verse's own phrase whatsoever they call for is the narrowest of the three.

**The Barrier for the Disbelievers**

The commentators draw the contrast: whereas for the disbelievers a barrier is set between them and that which they desire, citing 34:54. The same vocabulary of desire is used in both cases, and the difference is whether the desire is met.

The contrast connects to the sūrah's earlier imagery. At v9 the commentators described a barrier before and behind the disbelievers, and at v8 shackles that prevent the hands from being extended. The barrier at 34:54 is the same structure applied to desire rather than to sight.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not enumerate what they call for, and does not describe the fruit beyond naming it. The commentators supply the superiority claim and the report from other sources rather than from the verse.

The verse also does not say whether everything called for is granted. It states that they have whatsoever they call for, and the commentators' list of parallels supports the claim without qualification, but the verse itself does not address a request that is refused.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:58

> **“Peace!” a word from a Lord most Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Peace, a Word From a Lord Most Merciful**

Peace! a word from a Lord most Merciful. The commentators record a saying attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās: God Himself is peace upon the people of the Garden (IK), perhaps referring to Peace — *al-Salām* — as one of the Names of God.

The reading makes the greeting not a message from God but God's own presence as peace. The commentators qualify it with perhaps, and cite 33:44 as a parallel: Their greeting on the day they meet Him will be Peace (IK).

**The Ḥadīth of the Light**

The commentators relate a longer account. According to a ḥadīth: While the people of the Garden are in their bliss, a light shines upon them. Then they lift their heads, and it is God beholding them from above. Then He says, Peace be upon you, O people of the Garden. And that is the meaning of His saying, Peace! a word from a Lord most Merciful.

The account continues: So He looks at them and they look at Him, and they are not distracted by anything from that bliss so long as they are looking upon Him, until He is unveiled to them and His Light and His Blessing remain upon them in their abodes (IK, Q). The commentators cite it as the interpretation of this verse rather than as an independent claim.

**The Connection to the Vision of God**

The commentators noted at v55 that according to some, those in different levels of Paradise are occupied with different delights (Q), the highest of which involves being absorbed in the vision of God (R). The ḥadīth at this verse describes that vision.

The two verses therefore describe the same thing from two angles. Verse 55 names the absorption as the highest delight, and this verse supplies the greeting that accompanies it. The commentators do not draw the connection explicitly, but both cite the vision.

**The Busyness and Its Interruption**

At v55 the commentators explained that the inhabitants are busy with the delights of Paradise and spiritual realities rather than preoccupied by the concerns of this world. The ḥadīth at this verse describes an interruption of that busyness: they lift their heads.

The account is precise about what happens to the busyness. They are not distracted by anything from that bliss so long as they are looking upon Him. The interruption of the delights is therefore not a loss of bliss but its intensification, and the commentators preserve the account without commenting on the relation.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say who speaks the word. The commentators attribute it to God from the Ibn ʿAbbās saying and the ḥadīth, and the verse describes it as a word from a Lord most Merciful without naming the speaker.

The verse also does not describe the unveiling. The ḥadīth mentions it as the point at which His Light and Blessing remain upon them in their abodes, and the commentators cite the account without elaborating what the unveiling is.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:59

> **Stand apart this Day, O guilty ones!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Stand Apart This Day**

Stand apart this Day, O guilty ones! The commentators explain the reference: this verse is an allusion to the separation of believers and disbelievers on the Day of Judgment. The command is addressed to the guilty, and the separation is what it effects.

The verse follows the description of the Garden's inhabitants at vv 55–58 and turns abruptly to the others. The commentators do not gloss the transition, but the sūrah's structure supplies it — the arraignment at v53 was universal, and this verse begins the division.

**The Day of Division**

The commentators supply the name the separation gives the Day: because of this separation it is also referred to as the Day of Division. They cite 37:21, 44:40, 77:13–14 and 77:38, and 78:17, with further references at 10:28 and 30:14.

The naming is what connects this verse to a wider Qur'anic vocabulary. The Day is known by several names, and the commentators identify this one from the separation the verse describes. The cross-references show the name is established rather than local.

The commentators at v32 explained that this verse and that one indicate all human beings will be arraigned, and that other verses indicate only the disbelievers will be arraigned unto the punishment. This verse belongs to the second. The command to stand apart is addressed to the guilty, and the separation is what distinguishes them from those described in the preceding verses. The commentators' two-stage reading is what makes the sequence coherent.

**O Guilty Ones**

The address names those commanded. The commentators do not gloss the term, and the verse does not enumerate what the guilt consists in. The sūrah's earlier verses supply it — disbelief at v7, turning away from the signs at v46, and the refusal to spend at v47.

The direct address is notable. The preceding verses described the Garden's inhabitants in the third person; this verse speaks to the others in the second. The commentators do not gloss the shift, but it marks the point at the description becomes an address.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say where they are to stand apart, and does not describe the separation. It gives the command and leaves the arrangement to the verses that follow.

The verse also does not say that the separation is permanent. It states the command for that Day, and the sūrah's later verses describe the abodes of the two groups, but this verse does not address duration.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:60

> **Did I not enjoin upon you, O Children of Adam, that you not worship Satan—truly he is a manifest enemy unto you—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Did I Not Enjoin Upon You**

Did I not enjoin upon you, O Children of Adam, that you not worship Satan — truly he is a manifest enemy unto you. The commentators explain the central verb: enjoin translates *aʿhad*, which is from the same root as *ʿahd*, meaning vow, pact, or covenant.

The etymology is what gives the verse its force. The enjoining is not a new command but the invocation of an existing pact, and the question Did I not presupposes an affirmative answer. The commentators do not gloss the rhetorical form, but the root supplies its ground.

**The Primordial Covenant**

The commentators connect the word to a specific event. In this sense it relates to the covenant that all human beings made with God before coming into this world, for in acknowledging that God is their Lord, they acknowledge that only He is worthy of worship; see 7:172.

The cross-reference is to the Day of the Covenant, which the commentators also mentioned at v1 in connection with al-Qushayrī's reading of Yā Sīn. The sūrah therefore opens and closes its argument with the same covenant, and the commentators supply the connection through the root of the verb.

**A Manifest Enemy**

The commentators note that the description of Satan as a manifest enemy is recurrent, and cite 2:168, 2:208, 6:142, 7:22, 12:5, 17:53, 28:15, and 43:62. The recurrence is what allows the verse to state it in a parenthetical without argument.

The enemy is described as manifest, and the commentators do not gloss the adjective. What the extensive cross-referencing shows is that the enmity is established across the Qur'an rather than asserted here for the first time. The verse invokes a settled fact rather than proving one.

**The Address to the Children of Adam**

The verse addresses the Children of Adam, and the commentators' note about the covenant explains why. The covenant was made by all human beings before coming into this world, and the address is therefore to everyone present at it.

This connects to the sūrah's earlier verse at v22, where the man from the outskirts asked why he should not worship Him who originated him. The commentators there explained that originated translates *faṭaranī*, from the same root as *fiṭrah*, and cited 30:30 for the primordial nature. The covenant and the origination are the same ground approached from two directions.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe the worship of Satan, and does not say what it consists in. It prohibits it and gives the reason — the enmity — without enumerating its forms.

The verse also does not say that the covenant was forgotten. It asks whether the enjoining occurred, and the next verse states what was enjoined. The commentators' note about the primordial covenant supplies the occasion without saying that it was lost to memory.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:61

> **and that you worship Me? This is a straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**And That You Worship Me**

And that you worship Me? This is a straight path. The verse completes the enjoining begun at v60, and the two prohibitions and commands form a single pair. The commentators treat vv 60–61 under one note, and the pairing is what their shared explanation covers.

The enjoining is therefore not a prohibition alone. The commentators explained at v60 that enjoin translates *aʿhad*, from the same root as *ʿahd*, meaning vow, pact, or covenant. The covenant has both a negative and a positive term, and this verse states the positive one.

**The Covenant's Two Terms**

The commentators connect the word to the covenant that all human beings made with God before coming into this world, for in acknowledging that God is their Lord, they acknowledge that only He is worthy of worship; see 7:172. The acknowledgement of lordship and the acknowledgement of worthiness of worship are one act.

The two verses of this passage therefore restate the covenant's structure. Not worshipping Satan and worshipping God are not two separate obligations but the two sides of the single acknowledgement made before entering the world.

**A Straight Path**

The commentators direct the reader to 1:6 for the phrase straight path. The cross-reference is to the opening sūrah's petition, and the commentators do not elaborate the connection.

What the phrase contributes here is a description of the enjoining. The command to worship God is not presented as a burden but as a path, and the adjective straight distinguishes it from the deviation implied by the worship of Satan at v60. The commentators supply the cross-reference without glossing the metaphor.

**The Question Form**

The verse is framed as the second half of a question begun at v60: Did I not enjoin upon you. The form presupposes an affirmative answer, and the commentators do not gloss it as rhetorical, but the structure requires it.

The question is addressed to the guilty ones commanded to stand apart at v59. The address therefore moves from command to remembrance, and the commentators' note about the primordial covenant is what makes the remembrance intelligible — the enjoining occurred before the world.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe the straight path, and does not say how it is followed. It names it and leaves the description to the cross-reference at 1:6.

The verse also does not say that the covenant was forgotten. It asks whether the enjoining occurred, and the next verse supplies the consequence of having ignored it. The commentators' note about the covenant before the world does not address its retention or loss.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:62

> **For indeed he has led many among you astray. Did you not understand?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Has Led Many Astray**

For indeed he has led many among you astray. Did you not understand? The commentators gloss the question directly: this verse asks, Did you not understand the fundamental difference between worshipping God and worshipping Satan? (IK).

The gloss supplies what the verse leaves unstated. The question Did you not understand is not about a fact but about a distinction, and the commentators identify the distinction as the fundamental difference between the two worships.

**Many Among You**

The verse quantifies the deviation: he has led many among you astray. The commentators do not gloss the word many, and the verse does not give a number. What it establishes is that the deviation is not an individual matter but a collective one.

The address at v60 was to the Children of Adam, and the many among you are drawn from that address. The commentators' note about the covenant made by all human beings before coming into this world is what makes the collective framing coherent — the deviation is measured against a universal obligation.

**The Difference Between the Two Worships**

The commentators identify the difference as fundamental, and the surrounding verses supply its terms. Verse 60 stated that Satan is a manifest enemy; v61 stated that worshipping God is a straight path. The difference is therefore between enmity and guidance.

The commentators at v22 noted that originated translates *faṭaranī*, from the same root as *fiṭrah* or primordial nature, and cited 30:30. Read in light of that verse and 51:56, they said, the worship of God is central to the primordial nature with which human beings were created. The difference between the two worships is therefore not a matter of preference but of conformity to that nature.

**The Question's Place in the Address**

The verse follows the enjoining at vv 60–61 and precedes the naming of Hell at v63. The commentators do not gloss the sequence, but the question sits between the reminder of the covenant and the announcement of the consequence.

The question is therefore the pivot. It asks whether the difference was understood, and the next two verses answer by showing what follows from not having understood it. The commentators' gloss supplies the content of the difference without addressing the transition.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say what the fundamental difference consists in. The commentators supply it as the difference between worshipping God and worshipping Satan, and the verse asks only whether it was understood.

The verse also does not say that the many were deceived against their will. It states that he led them astray, and the question about understanding implies that the leading involved a failure on their part. The commentators do not address the relation between the two.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:63

> **This is the Hell that you were promised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**This Is the Hell You Were Promised**

This is the Hell that you were promised. The commentators give the sense of this verse and the next together: these verses say essentially, This is the Fire the prophets warned you about, but you denied, so taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny, citing 32:20 and 34:42.

The gloss supplies the speaker and the occasion. The verse itself states the identification without saying who speaks or to whom, and the commentators read it as an address to those who denied the warning.

**The Promise and Its Fulfilment**

The verse identifies what is present as what was promised. The commentators' gloss makes the connection explicit — the Fire the prophets warned about — and the sūrah's earlier verses supply the warnings.

At v48 the disbelievers demanded to know when the promise would come to pass, and the commentators there explained that according to some, the disbelievers referred to the Resurrection as this promise because they thought they would be the ones to be rewarded with good. This verse is the answer: the promise has come, and it is Hell rather than reward.

**The Cross-References**

The commentators cite 32:20 and 34:42 in support of the gloss. Both verses contain the same address — taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny — and the commentators treat them as parallels rather than as sources.

The recurrence of the formula is what allows this verse to state the identification without elaboration. The commentators supply the fuller form from the parallel verses, and the sūrah's own wording is the shorter of the two.

**The Sequence of the Address**

The verse follows the question at v62 and precedes the command at v64. The commentators treat vv 63–64 under one note, and the two verses form a single statement — the identification and the consequence.

The movement is from remembrance to arrival. Verses 60–62 recalled the covenant and asked whether the difference was understood; vv 63–64 announce that the consequence has arrived. The commentators' shared note treats them as one address rather than as two.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not describe Hell, and does not say who speaks. The commentators supply the prophets as the source of the warning from the parallel verses rather than from this verse's wording.

The verse also does not say that the promise was made to these particular addressees. It says you were promised, and the commentators' gloss identifies the warning as coming through the prophets without specifying its recipients.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:64

> **Burn therein today for having disbelieved!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Burn Therein Today**

Burn therein today for having disbelieved! The commentators treat this verse with the preceding one: these verses say essentially, This is the Fire the prophets warned you about, but you denied, so taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny, citing 32:20 and 34:42.

The command is the consequence of the identification. Verse 63 named the Hell, and this verse states what happens in it. The commentators do not separate the two, and their shared gloss covers both.

The verse gives the reason, and the reason is disbelief. The commentators at v54 explained that no soul will be wronged, and that the punishment the disbelievers receive is because they have wronged themselves by having disbelieved and turned away from God's signs.

The connection is what makes the command intelligible as justice rather than as retaliation. The commentators cited 3:117 and 10:44 at that verse for the principle that God does not wrong human beings but rather human beings wrong themselves. This verse states the reason in the same terms.

**Today**

The word today recurs from v54, where no soul will be wronged, and from v59, where the guilty were commanded to stand apart. The commentators do not gloss the term, and the sūrah uses it to mark the Day of Judgment.

The specificity is notable. The verse does not say forever or for a time but today, and the commentators do not address the duration. The Day is the frame, and the burning is located within it.

**The Address Completed**

The verse closes the address begun at v59. The sequence ran from the command to stand apart, through the enjoining recalled at vv 60–61, the question at v62, the identification at v63, to the command here. The commentators do not gloss the sequence as a whole, but the movement is continuous.

The command here is therefore the last thing said to them before their speech is removed.

The verse does not describe the burning, and does not say how long it lasts. It gives the command and the reason, and the commentators supply the fuller form from the parallel verses at 32:20 and 34:42.

The verse also does not say that the disbelief was the only ground. It names disbelief as the reason, and the sūrah's earlier verses supply the turning away from the signs and the refusal to spend. The verse does not enumerate them.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:65

> **On that Day We shall seal their mouths. Their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will bear witness to that which they used to earn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sealed Mouths**

The mouths are closed and the limbs are made to speak — the hands address God directly and the feet testify to what was earned. The commentators state the purpose: on the Day of Judgment people will no longer be able to make excuses and hide their sins with lies and duplicity.

The sealing is therefore not a punishment in itself but the removal of a possibility. The commentators identify the possibility as excuse-making, and the verse's structure supplies the replacement — the limbs speak in place of the mouth.

**The Wider Testimony of the Limbs**

The commentators set this verse alongside the Qur'an's other accounts. Hands and feet are also spoken of in 24:24 as testifying on the Day of Judgment, but there tongues are mentioned as well. According to 41:20, their ears, their eyes, and their skins will bear witness against them for that which they used to do.

The commentators note the difference: at 24:24 the tongues are included, whereas here the mouths are sealed. They direct the reader to 41:20–23 for a more extensive account of the manner in which one's members and faculties bear witness against one's soul, and they do not reconcile the two accounts.

**The Ḥadīth of the Contending Servant**

The commentators relate a famous ḥadīth reported by Anas ibn Mālik, who died around 91/709 and served the Prophet for many years. The Prophet laughed and said: Do you know at what I am laughing? ... At what a servant says to his Lord.

The exchange continues. The servant says: O Lord, will you not protect me from injustice? He replies: But of course. He says: I will not accept any witness against myself but myself. God replies: Today your soul suffices as a witness against you, as do the noble scribes. Then his mouth will be sealed and it will be said unto his limbs, Speak! So they will speak of his deeds (IK, Q).

**The Point of the Ḥadīth**

The account does not end with the testimony. Then he will be permitted to speak and will say to his limbs: Away with you! Be doomed! It was on your behalf that I contended. The commentators cite the whole ḥadīth, including this final turn.

The laughter the Prophet describes is at the servant's contention. He asked for no witness but himself, received his own limbs as witnesses, and then repudiated them. The commentators do not gloss the moral, but the sequence is what makes the sealing intelligible — the mouth's testimony was never the reliable one.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how the limbs speak, and does not describe the testimony. The commentators supply the manner from 41:20–23 and from the ḥadīth rather than from the verse.

The verse also does not say that the sealing is permanent. It describes the Day, and the ḥadīth records that the servant is later permitted to speak. The commentators preserve the account without addressing the relation between the sealing and the later permission.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:66

> **And had We willed, We would have blotted out their eyes. Then they would race to the path; yet how would they see?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Had We Willed, We Would Have Blotted Out Their Eyes**

The counterfactual concerns the eyes. Had God so willed, the eyes would have been blotted out, and the consequence would follow: they would hurry toward the path, and yet how could they see it? The commentators explain the phrase: blotted out their eyes indicates that God will make them blind to guidance and the straight path (Q, Ṭ), and they cite 4:47 as a parallel.

The reading is therefore not about physical sight. The commentators take the eyes as the capacity for guidance, and the blotting out as its removal. The cross-reference at 4:47 supports the reading without elaborating it.

**The Conditional**

The verse is conditional — had We willed — and the commentators do not gloss the form. What it establishes is that the blinding has not occurred, and that it remains within God's power. The verse states a possibility rather than an event.

This connects to the sūrah's earlier verse at v9, where the commentators explained that God placed shackles upon the disbelievers' necks up to their chins so that they cannot lower their gaze, and a barrier before and behind them so that they cannot benefit from guidance. The blinding described there has occurred; the blinding described here is what could have occurred instead.

**Racing to the Path**

The consequence the verse draws is a futile hurry toward the path, with the question of how they could see it left standing. The commentators do not gloss the phrase the path, and the sūrah has used the same term at v61, where worshipping God was called a straight path.

The rhetorical question is what carries the point. Racing to the path would be useless if the capacity to see it had been removed, and the commentators' gloss on the blinding supplies the reason. The verse does not say that they would fail to reach the path but that they would be unable to see it.

**The Pair of Counterfactuals**

This verse and the next form a pair. Both begin with had We willed, and both describe an alteration God could have made. The commentators treat them separately, but the shared form links them.

The two alterations differ in kind. This verse concerns sight; the next concerns the body's position. The commentators at v67 explain that in their places indicates that no matter where they may be, God can alter people's state instantly or destroy them if He wills. The pair therefore covers both perception and placement.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say why God did not will the blinding, and does not say that it will occur later. It states the possibility and its consequence, and leaves the will unexercised.

The verse also does not say who them is. It follows the address to the guilty ones at vv 59–64, and the commentators do not restrict the reference, though the context supplies it.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:67

> **And had We willed, We would have transformed them in their places. Then they could neither advance nor go back.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Transformed in Their Places**

And had We willed, We would have transformed them in their places. Then they could neither advance nor go back. The commentators record two readings of the phrase: in their places could also mean in their dwellings.

The two readings differ in specificity. One refers to position generally, the other to the home. The commentators note that in either reading the point is the same, and they supply it: no matter where they may be, God can alter people's state instantly or destroy them if He wills.

**The Transformation Into Animals**

The commentators record a further reading. This is seen by some as an allusion to God punishing disbelievers by transforming them into animals such as monkeys and pigs (Ṭs). They cite 4:47 as a further reference.

The reading is attributed to some rather than to all, and the commentators preserve it without endorsing it. The specific animals named are drawn from the Qur'an's other accounts of transformation, and the commentators supply them without citing the verses here.

**Neither Advance Nor Go Back**

The verse describes the consequence of the transformation as immobility. The commentators do not gloss the phrase, and the verse does not elaborate. What it excludes is movement in both directions, and the pairing is what makes the exclusion complete.

This connects to the sūrah's earlier imagery. At v9 the commentators described a barrier before the disbelievers and a barrier behind them, so that they are covered over and see not. The barriers there prevent movement of the gaze; the immobility here prevents movement of the body.

**The Power to Alter Instantly**

The commentators' gloss supplies what the verse implies: God can alter people's state instantly. The word instantly is not in the verse, and the commentators add it to make the claim explicit.

The claim connects to the sūrah's earlier treatment of divine power. At v38 the commentators noted that some understand the sun's running to its appointed time to refer to the end of the world, and at v68 they will explain the regression in old age as an argument for the power to resurrect. The transformation described here is the same power applied to the body's form.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that the transformation occurred, and does not say it will occur. Like the preceding verse it is conditional, and the commentators treat both as statements of possibility.

The verse also does not say what the transformation consists in. The commentators supply the animal reading as what some say, and the verse states only that they would be transformed and would be unable to move.

## Sūrah Yā Sīn 36:68

> **And whomsoever We give long life, We cause him to regress in creation. Do they not understand?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Long Life and Regression**

And whomsoever We give long life, We cause him to regress in creation. Do they not understand? The commentators explain the phrase: We cause him to regress in creation refers to physical and mental decline in old age. The regression is therefore not merely bodily but cognitive.

The verse states a general pattern rather than a punishment. The commentators do not gloss whomsoever as referring to a particular group, and the decline is presented as what accompanies long life wherever it occurs.

**The Argument From Decline**

The commentators identify the verse's function: here it is meant as an argument, as if to say, Does not the One who has power to do this have the power to resurrect you? The regression is therefore evidence rather than mere description.

The argument runs from the observed to the promised. What is seen is the reversal of development; what is promised is its further reversal. The commentators state the argument as a question, and the verse's own closing Do they not understand is the demand for the inference.

**The Parallel at 30:54**

The commentators cite a parallel: this is similar to 30:54 — God is He Who created you from weakness, then ordained strength after weakness, then ordained weakness and old age after strength.

The cross-reference supplies the full sequence. The verse at 30:54 names three stages, and the regression described here is the third. The commentators cite the verse without elaborating the sequence, and the parallel shows the pattern is established elsewhere in the Qur'an.

**The Stages as Signs**

The commentators add a further reference: see also 22:5, in which all of the stages of earthly life from conception to old age are cited as signs of God's Power. The stages are therefore themselves signs, and the commentators treat them as belonging to the same category as the created-order signs at vv 33–40.

The connection is what gives this verse its place in the sūrah. The earlier signs were external — the earth, the night, the sun, the moon, the orbits. This one is internal, drawn from the human life cycle. The commentators do not draw the contrast, but the cross-reference at 22:5 shows the stages functioning as signs elsewhere.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say how long the long life is, and does not describe the decline beyond calling it a regression. The commentators supply the physical and mental content from the cross-references rather than from the verse.

The verse also does not say that the regression is a punishment. It states the pattern and asks whether it is understood, and the commentators' reading of it as an argument for the power to resurrect makes it evidence rather than retribution.
