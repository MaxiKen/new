# Sūrat al-Maʿārij (Chapter 70) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Named for the Ways of Ascent**

Sūrat al-Maʿārij takes its name from its third verse, where God describes Himself as *Dhī l-Maʿārij* — "Lord of the ascending ways." The Arabic root ʿ-r-j (*ʿaraja*) means to rise, to climb, to ascend by degrees; a *miʿrāj* is a ladder or means of ascent, and *maʿārij* is its plural — many ways of rising, degree upon degree. The choice of this name is itself instructive: a sūrah that opens with a mocking demand for punishment and closes with the humiliated emergence of the mockers from their graves is named, at its very center, not for punishment but for *ascent*. The Qur'an thereby frames the entire drama of belief and disbelief as a question of direction: some souls rise toward their Lord through the disciplines He prescribes, and others sink under the weight of what they hoarded. The root is threaded through the Qur'an's cosmology: *"...then it ascends (taʿruju) to Him in a day whose measure is a thousand years"* (32:5); *"the angels and the Spirit descend therein"* (97:4); *"He knows what descends from heaven and what ascends (yaʿruju) to it"* (34:2). Whoever grasps that the whole created order is in motion — descending by command and ascending to account — already holds the key to this sūrah.

**Three Names, One Warning**

The sūrah carries two other traditional names. It is called *al-Mawāqiʿ*, "The Impending Things," from the first verse's warning of a punishment that is *wāqiʿ* — falling, inescapable, already on its descent path toward those it must reach. And it is called *Sāʾala*, "He Asked," from its very first word, the only sūrah of the Qur'an to open with a verb of questioning. The three names together map the sūrah's movement: a question is asked (Sāʾala) about something already falling (al-Mawāqiʿ), and the answer directs the questioner's gaze upward (al-Maʿārij) to the Lord who controls every ascent and every descent. Classical commentators such as al-Suyūṭī record all three titles; the multiplicity is not decoration but tri-focal vision — ask, be warned, and look up.

**A Middle Makkan Sūrah in the Shadow of al-Ḥāqqah**

Al-Maʿārij belongs to the middle Makkan period, that intense stretch of revelation in which the Qur'an pressed upon the Quraysh, season after season, the realities they most successfully avoided: resurrection, reckoning, and the Fire. In the standard chronologies it stands as the seventy-ninth sūrah revealed, immediately after al-Ḥāqqah (69) — and the linkage is more than accidental. Al-Ḥāqqah had ended its catalogue of Hell's terrors with the guilty one's own plea: *"Would that I had taken a path with the Messenger!"* (69:27), after the companions of the right hand are given their Book in their right hand (69:19–24), while the sinner is seized and dragged and burned (69:30–37). Ālūsī and others note that al-Maʿārij continues that same sermon, almost without drawing breath: the Resurrection and the Fire of 69:18–37 are picked up and carried forward into 70:8–18. What al-Ḥāqqah declared — *the Inevitable* — al-Maʿārij interrogates: *if it is inevitable, when?* The Prophet's adversaries demanded a date; the sūrah answers them with a cosmology, a psychology, and a moral program, and never once gives them the date.

**The Architecture of the Sūrah**

The sūrah's forty-four verses fall into five movements, each flowing into the next. First, the mocking question and the divine answer: a punishment is asked for, and God answers that it is descending by His decree, on a day whose measure is fifty thousand years — therefore *"be patient with beautiful patience"* (vv. 1–7). Second, the cosmic unveiling: the sky like molten metal, the mountains like carded wool, and the day when no friend asks after his friend and no ransom is accepted (vv. 8–14). Third, the Fire itself, personalized — a fire that *calls* to those who turned their backs and hoarded (vv. 15–18). Fourth, the sūrah's heart and most famous teaching: the diagnosis of the human being as *halūʿ* — anxious, fretful, withholding — and the exception of those who pray, give, believe, fear, guard their chastity, keep their trusts and testimony, and are mindful of prayer; these are *"in Gardens, honored"* (vv. 19–35). Fifth, the return to the mockers who scramble around the Prophet in crowds, hoping for Paradise without price, and the oath by the Lord of the easts and the wests that God can replace them utterly; the sūrah closes on the Day they were promised, eyes humbled, abasement overwhelming them (vv. 36–44). The design is a ring: it opens with a punishment none can avert (v. 2) and closes with the Day that punishment was promised (v. 44) — the question of verse 1 answered, in the end, not with an argument but with an appointment.

**The Heart of the Matter: A Diagnosis and Eight Remedies**

What has made al-Maʿārij endure in the memory of believers is the passage at its center. Three verses (19–21) deliver the most unsparing psychological x-ray in the Qur'an: man was created *halūʿ* — gripped by anxious greed; when evil touches him he becomes *jazūʿ* — fretful, impatient; when good touches him he becomes *manūʿ* — withholding, begrudging. This is not a description of a tribe or an era; it is the factory setting of the human soul, acknowledged in the very grammar (*khuliqa*, "he was created") as something constitutive. Then — with the single word *illā*, "except" — the sūrah names the community of the excepted who escape the diagnosis. Al-Rāzī observed that the exception implies that what follows is the cure: the eight responsibilities of vv. 22–34 are set out as a therapy, one mark and one medicine at a time — prayer at the beginning and prayer at the end, the rights of the poor, certainty of the Day of Recompense, fear of God, chastity, trustworthiness, upright testimony. The sūrah thus teaches that the anxious human being is not repaired by information but by *practice*: a life ordered around the prayer's rhythm, the wealth's shared due, and the covenant kept. Modern readers who speak of anxiety epidemics will find here a fourteen-century-old analysis that deserves their attention.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the forty-four verses as an independent discourse, in strict sequence, exactly as mandated: every verse is quoted individually, then expanded through Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAjībah and others), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the logic of the teaching, and its bearing upon modern life. Verses the source commentary treats in groups (for example vv. 10–11, 12–14, or the long pericope of vv. 22–35) are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing has been compressed because of the chapter's length; the sūrah that teaches patience is read patiently, from *"A questioner asked"* to *"That is the Day they have been promised."*

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:1

> **A questioner asked about an impending punishment**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Question That Opens a Whole Sūrah**

The Qur'an begins this chapter with a construction designed to startle the attentive Arab ear: *saʾala sāʾilun* — literally, "asked an asker." In Arabic rhetoric this is the cognate accusative carried to its baldest form: the verb is followed immediately by an agent-noun from its own root, so that the act of questioning and the questioner are welded into a single unit of sound and sense. The effect is like a door slammed open: not "someone said something," but *a question happened*. The questioner is deliberately unnamed — a choice the commentators exploited. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record that the verse was understood to refer to a specific incident, but by leaving the man's name out, the Qur'an turns him into a type: every human being who has ever demanded of God a sign, a deadline, a proof on his own terms stands inside this opening verse. What he asks about is *"ʿadhābin wāqiʿ"* — "an impending punishment" — and the adjective does the work of a verdict.

**Wāqiʿ: The Grammar of Something Already Falling**

The word *wāqiʿ* is an active participle from the root w-q-ʿ, "to fall, to strike, to come to pass." Arabic uses the participle for an action unfolding in the present and continuing — a falling that has begun. The Qur'an names a whole sūrah *al-Wāqiʿah*, "The Inevitable Event" (56), and there defines it: *"when the Event falls (waqaʿat), nothing denying its occurrence shall call it a lie"* (56:1–2). So the questioner is not asking about something hypothetical; he is asking about something the Qur'an treats as already in motion, like an object that has left the hand and is mid-flight. Al-Qurṭubī savoringly notes the hidden irony: the man asks *when* it will fall, and the Qur'an answers with a word that says it *is* falling. The question "when?" already concedes "what." It is as if a man standing in the path of a collapsing wall should ask, not whether it will fall, but what time it expects to land.

**Two Readings of the Little Word *Bi***

The translation "asked *about*" rests on reading the preposition *bi* in its sense of inquiry — the man asked the Prophet *concerning* the punishment: who will receive it, and when? This is the reading of al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, and it fits the reports of the Makkan skeptics who taunted the Prophet with the very question the Qur'an quotes elsewhere: *"When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?"* (Qur'an 67:25; cf. 36:48). But many commentators read the *bi* differently, in its sense of purpose: "a supplicant supplicated *for*" — that is, a man actually *prayed* that the punishment come. On this reading the verse is a portrait of defiance pushed to its final, self-destructive edge: the skeptic is so certain the revelation is false that he invokes destruction upon himself as a wager. The Qur'an records that exact wager elsewhere:

> *"And they say: 'O God, if this be the truth from Thee, rain down stones upon us from the sky, or bring us a painful punishment.'"* (Qur'an 8:32)

Ibn Kathīr, al-Jalālayn, and al-Qurṭubī connect 70:1 directly to this verse, and al-Wāḥidī's reports of the circumstances of revelation identify the man: al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, one of the Quraysh's chief tormentors of the Prophet, who is said to have spoken these words of 8:32 with his own mouth. Others — al-Qurṭubī among them — name Abū Jahl instead. Either way, the wager was accepted. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Wāḥidī record the bitter sequel: the man who asked for the punishment met it at the Battle of Badr (2/624), where al-Naḍr was killed in captivity. He asked for stones from the sky; he received, instead, a day on which *"the two parties met"* (8:41) and the truth he had wagered against came for him sword in hand. The Qur'an had already given the general law of such wagers:

> *"And if God were to take mankind to task for what they earned, He would not leave upon the back of it a single creature; but He defers them to an appointed term. Then, when their term comes — truly God has ever seen His servants."* (Qur'an 35:45)

**Why God Does Not Answer Demands for Deadlines**

The deeper teaching of the verse lies in what God does *not* do. The questioner demanded a schedule, and the sūrah will speak of fifty thousand years, of molten skies, of ascents and angels — everything except a date. The Qur'an explains this divine reticence elsewhere with perfect clarity:

> *"They ask thee to hasten the punishment; but had it not been for an appointed term, the punishment would surely have come upon them; yet it shall come upon them suddenly, when they are unaware. They ask thee to hasten the punishment; and truly Hell encompasses the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 29:53–54)

The logic is both judicial and merciful. Judicially, a sentence is not announced to the accused in the hour of his contempt; it is entered into the record and executed at the appointed term. Mercifully, a fixed date would end all moral effort — no one repents on the eve of a known deadline out of love of God; he merely calculates. The interval between warning and fulfillment is precisely the space in which free moral choice operates. The Qur'an presses this point on the demanders themselves:

>*"Say: 'Have you considered, if His punishment comes upon you by night or by day — what portion of it would the guilty seek to hasten?'"* (Qur'an 10:50)

The question turns the mockery back on itself: if the punishment is so desirable to hasten, why would anyone flee it when it arrives? The demand was never a request for information; it was a performance of unbelief.

**The Psychology of Demanding a Sign**

The verse also rewards a psychological reading. Mockery of this kind — "Bring it on, then!" — is rarely confidence; it is more often the whistling of a man in the dark. The Prophet's contemporaries had watched him for years: a man they knew to be truthful before his mission suddenly claiming revelation, his followers growing, his recitation unlike any poetry they knew. The demand for an immediate catastrophe is the demand of a man who wants the question *settled* — and who has begun to suspect which way it will settle. This is why the Qur'an describes such demanders as being, in truth, not doubters but hopers-of-delay:

> *"God has decreed: 'Truly I and My messengers shall surely prevail.' ...Those who oppose God and His messenger shall be abased, just as those before them were abased."* (Qur'an 58:21–22)

A man who genuinely believed there is no Hereafter would simply laugh and walk away; the man who stays to demand its arrival has already conceded that it matters. In every generation there are those who taunt religion with the request for instant, measurable proof; the sūrah's reply is the same today as in Makkah: the proof is *wāqiʿ* — falling — and the demanders are being answered on a schedule they did not set. For the believer, the verse is a lesson in trusting God's timing: what is certain need not be soon, and what is soon need not be announced.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:2

> **upon the disbelievers which none can avert,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punishment That Has an Owner**

The second verse completes the first's sentence with surgical precision: the impending punishment is *lil-kāfirīn* — "for the disbelievers." The Arabic preposition *li* here denotes possession and rightful destination. The punishment is not described merely as something that will strike them, but as something that *belongs* to them — as a debt belongs to the one who incurred it. The Qur'an uses this possessive construction for both sides of the ledger: for Hell's inmates, *"as for those who are wretched, they shall be in the Fire"* (11:106); and for the righteous, *"their reward is with their Lord: Gardens of Eden beneath which rivers flow"* (98:8). The grammar teaches ʿaqīdah in miniature: heaven and hell are not places one wanders into; they are inheritances one earns — *"every soul is a pledge for what it earned"* (74:38), and each of us is already living in the antechamber of his permanent home.

**Kāfir: The One Who Covers**

The word rendered "disbeliever" is *kāfir*, from the root k-f-r whose base meaning is to cover. The Qur'an itself uses the root for a farmer covering seed with soil:

> *"...like vegetation after a rain whose growth pleases the sower (kuffār) — then it withers..."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The classical lexicons and the commentators draw on this agricultural sense: the *kāfir* is one who *covers* the truth — the truth that rises, again and again, within the soul, in conscience and in the created order, and which he pats down, soil over seed, so that nothing need grow from it. This is why the Qur'an can say of the pagans that they *"know it"* even while denying it (cf. 27:14: *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of wrong and arrogance"*). Disbelief in the Qur'an is rarely portrayed as intellectual error honestly reached; it is portrayed as a posture, a hand held over the eyes, a seed smothered at the moment of germination. That is why the punishment in this verse is *theirs by right*: not an arbitrary rage poured on honest perplexity, but the harvest that a covered field finally yields.

**Laysa Lahu Dāfiʿ: The Negation of Every Rescuer**

The verse's climax is its last word-group: *laysa lahu dāfiʿ* — "none is there for it, to repel it." The negation is categorical. The root d-f-ʿ means to push away, to ward off, to drive back; the participle *dāfiʿ* would be the one who could do the pushing. The sentence constructs a courtroom in the imagination and then, one by one, empties it of every possible defender. Not: "no strong defender," not "no defender except the generous"; simply — no defender at all. The Qur'an repeats this emptying elsewhere with terrible patience:

> *"Say: 'Who is there that can shield you from God, if He intends for you evil, or intends for you mercy?' And they will find for themselves, apart from God, neither protector nor helper."* (Qur'an 33:17)

> *"And fear a day when no soul shall avail another in anything; nor shall intercession be accepted from it; nor shall any ransom be taken from it; nor shall they be helped."* (Qur'an 2:48)

Ibn Kathīr notes that verses of this class were revealed precisely to demolish the Makkan assumption that the idols — and the ancestors' intercession, and the tribe's collective standing — would somehow absorb the blow on the Day. Each supposed shield is named and removed elsewhere in the Qur'an: the idols *"have no knowledge of it; they follow only conjecture"* (53:28); the forefathers *"bear their own burdens, and burdens upon their burdens"* (16:25); the angels intercede *"only for those with whom He is content"* (21:28). What is left standing, when every advocate has been dismissed from the court, is the soul itself, face to face with its Judge.

**Why a Punishment No One Can Avert Is Logically Necessary**

The certainty here is not arbitrary severity; it follows from what justice means. The Qur'an's argument, developed across many sūrahs, runs: if there is a God who is perfectly just, then there must be a reckoning in which every wrong is settled — otherwise the tyrant who died in his bed and his victim who died in his cell would simply have exchanged places without ever meeting the scale. And if that reckoning is to be just, it must be *unavertable by precisely those instruments* by which justice is defeated in this world. What defeats justice among men? Wealth (bribes), connections (intercession by privilege), power (force), and delay (statutes of limitation and death). Each of these the Qur'an names and negates for the final Day: *"An earth full of gold would not be accepted from any one of them, were he to offer it in ransom"* (3:91); intercession belongs *"only to whoever has permission from the Merciful"* (20:109); *"the Day they come forth, nothing of them is hidden from God"* (40:16); and *"no term shall be deferred when its appointed time has come"* — the Qur'an's words at 10:49: *"Say: I have no power to harm or benefit myself, save as God wills; for every people is an appointed term; when their term comes, they cannot put it off an hour, nor bring it forward."* A punishment that could be averted by wealth or influence would not be the punishment of a just God; it would be an enlarged version of the world's courts, where outcomes track power. Verse 2 is thus a guarantee written in negation: on that Day, power will not track wealth or status — only truth.

**The Believer's Consolation Hidden in a Warning**

Al-Qurṭubī observes a pastoral irony: verses that empty the disbeliever's courtroom fill the believer's. The same God from whom nothing can shield anyone is the God with whom nothing can sever the believer:

> *"Truly those who say, 'Our Lord is God,' and then stand upright — the angels descend upon them: 'Fear not, nor grieve; rejoice in the Garden you were promised. We are your protectors in the life of this world and in the Hereafter...'"* (Qur'an 41:30–31)

The negation *laysa lahu dāfiʿ* applies to punishment's *victims*, not to mercy's candidates. For those who turn to God before the term, the very inescapability of His decree becomes the deepest security: the same irresistibility that hunts the hardened wrongdoer is the irresistibility that guards every promise God has made to the repentant. In modern terms: the believers' hope rests not on finding a loophole in the system, but on the Judge Himself having offered pardon through repentance, faith, and the eight responsibilities this sūrah will shortly enumerate. The warning of verse 2 is therefore the shadow side of an invitation — flee to the only One whose decree none can avert, while the door He Himself has opened still stands open. The Prophet ﷺ taught his companions to hold both sides of this truth in one breath:

> *"Be mindful of God wherever you are, and follow a wrong deed with a good deed that will erase it, and treat people with beautiful character."* (al-Tirmidhī 1987, from Abū Dharr — graded ḥasan; the concise charter of repentance taught as the believer's daily equipment)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:3

> **from God, Lord of the ascending ways.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Lord of Every Climb**

The third verse answers the questioner of verse 1 by naming the source of the impending punishment: *mina llāhi dhī l-maʿārij* — "from God, Lord of the ascending ways." The juxtaposition is deliberate and severe. The punishment *descends* from the very One who owns every *ascent*. In one breath the Qur'an collapses the fantasy that underlies all defiance: the fantasy of an escape route upward. If a punishment were falling from a creature, one might outrun it, out-climb it, find a higher ground. But this One is *Dhī l-Maʿārij* — the Possessor of the ladders themselves. Ibn Kathīr states the plain sense: the *maʿārij* are the ways by which angels and souls ascend through the heavens, and none determines who may ascend, or how far, but God. There is no staircase to His territory that He does not own; there is no elevation above His reach. The verse performs in spatial terms what verse 2 performed in legal terms: *no defender* — and now, *no exit upward*.

**The Morphology of Maʿārij: Degrees Upon Degrees**

The word *maʿārij* is the plural of *miʿrāj*, a noun of instrument from the root ʿ-r-j: a means of ascending, a ladder, a stairway — and by extension, a rank or degree reached by climbing. Arabic builds ranks and stairs from the same root family: *daraja* (a step) yields *darajāt*, the "degrees" the Qur'an promises the believers: *"For all are degrees of what they have done, and your Lord is not unaware of what they do"* (6:132); *"God will raise those who believe, and those given knowledge, in degrees"* (58:11). Al-Qurṭubī develops this reading of our verse: the ascending ways are also the degrees of favor and blessing God bestows upon His creatures — every height any human being has ever reached in knowledge, virtue, power, or fortune is one rung of a ladder God built and holds. The verse therefore says two things at once to the defiant questioner: the punishment comes from the Owner of every height you hope to reach, and every height you have already reached was a loan from the same Hand. Pride is thus revealed as a bookkeeping error: the climber strutting on the summit of a ladder he did not build, kept aloft by the Owner who could withdraw it in a breath.

**"Or Have They a Ladder?"**

The Qur'an presses this same imagery on the mockers elsewhere with open irony. Of the claimants who insinuated that the revelation came from some hidden informant, it asks:

> *"Or do they possess the treasures of thy Lord? Or are they the controllers? Or have they a ladder (sullam) by which they listen? Then let their listener bring a clear authority."* (Qur'an 52:37–38)

The question is engineered to expose the absurdity: no one reaches God's council uninvited; no one eavesdrops on the command that descends. And in Sūrat al-Anʿām the same ladder is offered to the Prophet as a rhetorical consolation: if their rejection weighs on you, *"then seek a hole into the earth, or a ladder (sullam) into heaven, and bring them a sign"* (6:35) — as much as to say, the signs have already come, and no further altitude is available to you or to them except by God's leave. The ladders belong to the Lord of the ways of ascent; revelation descends by His choosing, and no one climbs to fetch it on demand. The questioner of verse 1 demanded control of the schedule; verse 3 answers that he does not even control the staircase.

**The Prophet's Own Ascent: The Miʿrāj Echo**

For the Muslim ear, *Dhī l-Maʿārij* carries a resonance no first hearer could have fully anticipated: the Prophet's Night Journey and Ascension, when he was carried *"from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque"* (17:1) and then raised through the heavens to a nearness no human being before or after has known: *"then he drew near and descended, and he was at two bows' length or nearer"* (53:8–9). The Qur'an itself connects that experience with the highest horizon and the greatest signs: *"The sight did not swerve, nor did it transgress; truly he saw some of the greatest signs of his Lord"* (53:17–18). The point for our verse is exact: the one human being who ever *did* ascend the ways of ascent did not climb them by his own power — he was *carried*, by the permission and grace of *Dhī l-Maʿārij* himself. Ascent in the Qur'an's economy is never conquest; it is invitation. The contrast with the questioner could not be sharper: al-Naḍr or Abū Jahl demanded that heaven come down to him on his terms, and was refused; the Prophet asked nothing of the kind, and heaven reached down and lifted him. Between those two postures — demanding and being raised — lies the entire spiritual geography of the sūrah.

**Climbing as a Way of Life**

The verse also plants, for the believer, one of the Qur'an's most hopeful metaphors: religion as ascent. The same root that names God's lordship over the heavens' staircases names the moral life He commands — a life of *taqwā* carried like provisions for a climb: *"And take provision — and the best of provision is godwariness (taqwā)"* (2:197). The Qur'an's classic images make the upward direction explicit: *"And God invites to the Abode of Peace, and guides whom He wills to a straight path"* (10:25); *"To Him ascends the good word, and the righteous deed He raises up"* (35:10). Every prayer, every act of charity, every kept trust is a rung. Al-Qurṭubī's reading — the ascending ways as degrees of divine favor — quietly assures the believer that the ladder is not a trick: God has set real rungs in the world, and really rewards the climb. The modern reader may hear in the image a rebuke to every shortcut culture: no hack, no bribe, no elevator of privilege substitutes for the step-by-step work of the soul. God is the Lord of the ways of ascent — which means the way up exists, and it is His gift that it exists at all; and it also means that the summons of this sūrah, later, to prayer, almsgiving, and trustworthiness is nothing less than the map of the first rungs.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:4

> **Unto Him ascend the angels and the Spirit on a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Cosmos in Constant Motion Upward**

The fourth verse widens the lens from the questioner's demand to the machinery of the universe: *taʿruju l-malāʾikatu wa-l-rūḥu ilayhi* — "the angels and the Spirit ascend unto Him." The verb is in the present tense, and the classical commentators heard in it a continuous, unbroken traffic: creation does not merely exist under God; it is forever *moving toward* Him — commands descending, deeds and angels ascending, as the Qur'an states elsewhere: *"He directs the command from the heaven to the earth; then it ascends to Him in a day whose measure is a thousand years"* (32:5). Sūrat al-Sabaʾ gives the two-way flow in one line: *"He knows what penetrates into the earth and what comes forth from it, and what descends from the heaven and what ascends therein; and He is the Merciful, the Forgiving"* (34:2). The image is of an empire administered in person: nothing waits in an inbox; everything rises to the throne. For the questioner who demanded that God act *now*, the verse delivers the crushing correction: God's administration is already, at this moment, processing the affairs of the worlds — the delay you mock is not idleness but the majesty of a scale of operation your calendar cannot hold, as the next phrase will now quantify.

**The Angels and the Spirit: Who Ascends**

"The Spirit" — *al-Rūḥ* — is identified by the majority of the commentators, following Ibn ʿAbbās as al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr report, with the Archangel Gabriel, the angel of revelation, whom the Qur'an names *"the Trustworthy Spirit"* (26:193) and *"holy spirit"* (16:102), and whom it singles out even among angels: *"a day when the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks; they speak not, save whom the Merciful has permitted, and who speak aright"* (78:38). The pairing is therefore meaningful: the angels ascend, and with them the greatest of them, the carrier of the very Book whose punishment the questioner mocked. Others, as al-Qurṭubī records, took *al-Rūḥ* as another angel of still greater rank than all the hosts, created for a purpose above theirs; and a third understanding reads the verse of the spirits of the dead, for the Qur'an speaks of the taking of the soul in terms of ascent and conveyance: *"God takes souls at the time of their death, and those that die not, during their sleep; He keeps those upon whom He has decreed death, and sends the others back for an appointed term"* (39:42). On this reading, every human death is also an ascent through the ways of the Lord of the ways of ascent — the same staircase of verse 3, traveled in the opposite direction by every soul that ever lived, which makes the questioner's date-demand look even smaller: the traffic between heaven and earth has never once stopped.

**Fifty Thousand Years: The Arithmetic of Awe**

Then comes the phrase that staggered the first hearers: the day of this ascending *kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alfa sanah* — "whose measure is fifty thousand years." Two families of interpretation arise in the classical commentaries. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, citing reports from the early generations, identify the day as the Day of Resurrection itself, whose length — for the disbelievers, in their anguish — is as fifty thousand years; and Ibn Kathīr transmits the Prophetic report that gives the verse its pastoral balance:

> *"He will lighten it for the believer until it seems to him lesser than the obligatory prayer he used to perform in the life of this world."* (transmitted in the commentaries of Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, citing the reports of al-Suyūṭī; al-Ṭabarī records its like — the Day that measures millennia to the lost is compressed to moments for those whom God honors)

Al-Qurṭubī and others also record the alternative cosmological reading: that the fifty thousand years measure the extent of the ascent — the distance through the realms above — so that the verse describes how far the traffic must climb to reach Him. Whichever reading one takes, the number's function is the same: to make the soul feel scale. Beside a Day of fifty thousand years, the demand "hasten it!" is revealed for what it is — a mayfly demanding that the mountain hurry. And the Qur'an deliberately places this measure beside another, shorter one: *"a day whose measure is a thousand years"* (32:5); *"a day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of your reckoning"* (22:47). The graduated scale teaches that time itself is a creature, flexible in God's hand, different in different corridors of His kingdom — an intuition that modern physics, with its discovery that time's flow is not absolute but varies with motion and gravity, has made newly thinkable for the modern reader, though the Qur'an's purpose is not physics but awe: the One who sets the clock of the cosmos is not run by ours.

**The Believer's Shortened Day**

The reports behind the lightened Day carry the verse's deepest pastoral teaching. The Prophet ﷺ also taught, in a ḥadīth graded ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"The poor will enter Paradise before the rich by half a day — and that is five hundred years."* (al-Tirmidhī 2353, from Abū Hurayrah; al-Tirmidhī declared it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ — the commentators note the arithmetic: half a day of the thousand-year Day of 32:5)

The two numbers — five hundred years for the poor's head start, fifty thousand for the Day's outer measure — are not in tension; they are tiers of the same architecture, reminding the believer that the Hereafter's timescape is ordered by justice, not chaos: the very people the world made to wait at its back doors will be first through its front gate. The psychological medicine here is precise. Human anxiety — which this sūrah will diagnose in verse 19 as *halūʿ* — is largely a disease of time: we cannot bear waiting, cannot bear the gap between promise and fulfillment. The cure the verse offers is not a shorter wait but a *bigger clock*: set your waiting inside the fifty-thousand-year Day, and the delays of your few decades shrink to what they truly are. The believer who stands in prayer, or buried treasure in charity, or keeps a difficult trust for years without recognition, is not being ignored; his file is ascending, with the angels and the Spirit, through a system whose scale is vaster than any impatience — and whose record, as the sūrah's end will insist, misses nothing.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:5

> **So be patient with beautiful patience.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command the Whole Sūrah Was Building Toward**

After the questioner's demand, the verdict that none can avert it, and the fifty-thousand-year Day, the sūrah turns from the mockers to the Messenger — and issues its first imperative: *fa-ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā* — "So be patient with beautiful patience." The construction is a cognate accusative with an adjective: patience, then a particular *kind* of patience, named and defined by its beauty. Ibn ʿAjībah reads the address as aimed first at the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself, commanded to endure the mockery of men like al-Naḍr and Abū Jahl without seeking their punishment ahead of its term; but the commentators agree the command radiates outward to every believer confronted with the disbelief of others — and, on some readings, even back onto the questioners themselves, who would need patience far more than they knew if they understood what they were asking for. What matters is the logic of the sequence: God has just disclosed the scale of the Day (v. 4), and *therefore* — *fa-* — be patient. Patience here is not stoic grit ground out of ignorance; it is the reasonable posture of a man who has been shown the calendar. The believer can wait precisely because he has been told how the story ends; it is the mockers, certain there is no ending, who are in a hurry.

**Sabrun Jamīl: What Makes Patience Beautiful**

The phrase is Jacob's phrase before it is Muhammad's. When his sons came to him with Joseph's bloodied shirt, the patriarch answered: *fa-ṣabrun jamīl* — *"Beautiful patience!"* (12:18), and he repeated it decades later when Benjamin was taken: *"Beautiful patience — and God's help is sought against what you describe"* (12:83), and once more in his blind old age: *"I shall complain of my anguish and grief to God"* (12:86) — a complaint made *to God*, not *about* Him, which the commentators took to be the very secret of the beauty. The classical tradition distilled the definition from these usages and from the early generations: beautiful patience is patience that has no complaint in it before creatures — no theatrical suffering, no indictment of the Decree — because the pain has already been carried where it belongs, to God. It is patience without panic, without self-display, and without an undertone of accusation; its face toward people is calm, and its voice is raised only in the private audience of supplication. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have said that beautiful patience is patience stripped of complaint; and the scholars add a further note: it is patience maintained at the *beginning* of the blow, not assembled afterward — for everyone is patient once the shock has passed, but the beautiful patience belongs to the first hour of the wound.

**Three Kinds of Patience the Command Covers**

The tradition — Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both organize the virtue this way — divides ṣabr into three territories, and all three are in play in this Makkan context. Patience in obedience: performing prayer, reciting the Qur'an, and calling to God under ridicule, year after year, without an audience to applaud. Patience in abstaining: holding back from the vengeance that was fully in the Prophet's power to incite, and from the despair that was fully in his human capacity to feel. And patience upon affliction: burying supporters in Makkah's hostile soil, enduring boycott and the deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib in the same Year of Sorrow, and still not permitting the message to curdle into grief-stricken rage. The command to beautiful patience is thus not one instruction but a whole regime of the heart. The Qur'an elsewhere ties the same knot between certainty and endurance: *"And be patient, and thy patience is only through God"* (16:127) — even patience itself is borrowed strength, sought rather than manufactured.

**Was the Command Abrogated? A Question the Commentators Settled**

Al-Jalālayn and al-Qurṭubī record a discussion: some held that the instruction to bear the disbelievers patiently was abrogated by the later revealed verses commanding fighting (such as 9:5) — that patience was Makkah's strategy and jihād Madinah's. But this reading did not carry the day. Al-Ṭabarī argues that the command to patience holds at all times and in all situations, and Ibn ʿAjībah classifies the verse as *muḥkam* — definitive and permanent, never abrogated. The majority position, which the later scholarship consolidated, is that abrogation applies to specific legal rulings whose circumstances the Qur'an itself superseded, not to standing moral principles; a community may at one phase be commanded to forgive and at another to defend itself, but the duty of the individual heart to master panic, complaint, and vengeance is never repealed. The proof is the Prophet's own biography after the conquest of Makkah: with the city that had tormented him for two decades at his mercy, he entered not as an avenger but declaring general amnesty — *"Go, you are free"* — beautiful patience having so matured into power that it did not need to be spent as fury. The verse thus remains the standing constitution of the believer under provocation: the scale of the Day (v. 4) makes patience rational; the beauty of the patience makes it exemplary; and its permanence makes it practice.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:6

> **Truly they see it as far off,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Psychology of Distance**

The sixth verse turns from the command to the Prophet back to the mockers, and delivers in six words an entire theory of motivated perception: *innahum yarawnahu baʿīdā* — "truly they see it as far off." The pronoun "it" (the Hour, the punishment, the Day just measured at fifty thousand years) is the object, and the verb is *yarauna* — they *see*. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both note the precision: the deniers do not merely *say* it is far; they *see* it as far. The Qur'an grants them the verb of vision deliberately, for it is diagnosing not an argument but a way of looking. Ibn ʿAjībah adds that "far off" means they regarded it as impossible, or at least as so distant that no present concern was owed it — the classic psychology of the deadline perceived as distant: everything urgent is near, and everything near is what gets our attention. The Qur'an elsewhere captures the same spiritual astigmatism in a single image: *"Nay, but you love the hasty life, and leave the Hereafter behind"* (75:20–21) — *tadhharūna*, "you leave behind," a verb of physical placement, as though the Last Day were an object they had deliberately set down at the far end of the room, out of eyeshot.

**Why Distance Is a Decision, Not a Discovery**

The verse's quiet genius is to treat "far" as something seen rather than measured. No denier of Makkah possessed evidence that the Hour was distant — by definition, an unannounced event has no known schedule, and the Qur'an insists the Prophet himself did not know its time: *"They ask thee about the Hour: when is its arrival? Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord; none can disclose it at its time but He... Say: I have no power to benefit or harm myself, save as God wills; and had I knowledge of the Unseen, I would have acquired much good, and no evil would touch me"* (7:187–188). Distance, then, was not a conclusion the deniers reached; it was a lens they chose, because of what the lens saved them from having to do. To see the Day as near is to be bound by it — every transaction, every relationship, every hoarded coin suddenly standing in its light. To see it as far is to be left alone with the present and its pleasures. The Qur'an names the mechanism without mercy: *"Nay, man is a witness against himself, though he puts forth his excuses"* (75:14–15). Modern psychology would call this motivated reasoning and temporal discounting — the well-documented human habit of shrinking the moral weight of whatever is far away, whether in space (distant famine) or time (distant death). The Qur'an described it fourteen centuries before the laboratories did, and located its center not in the eye but in the will.

**The Odd Arithmetic of Believers and Deniers**

The Prophet ﷺ taught a strange inversion of time-sense that belongs exactly here. He is authentically reported to have said:

> *"I have been sent, and the Hour is like these two" — and he joined his index and middle finger.* (al-Bukhārī 6504, from Sahl ibn Saʿd; Muslim 2950 from ʿĀʾishah — his own mission and the Hour held up together, finger beside finger)

For the deniers, a Day potentially thousands of years off was "far"; for the Prophet, the Day that could be millennia off was held up beside his own lifetime, close as two fingers of one hand. The difference is not arithmetic but allegiance: those who intend to meet the Day prepare for it now, and those who do not, postpone it now. The classical scholars drew the practical corollary that later Sufi teachers made into a maxim of spiritual method: *hold the Hour before you as though it were tomorrow*. The Qur'an's own command to that effect is direct: *"O you who believe, fear God as He should be feared, and die not save as those who have surrendered"* (3:102) — for the believer, the relevant distance to the Hour is always one lifetime at most, and a lifetime is not a length but a spend-rate. Verse 6 is thus a mirror held up to every reader in every age: when you read "they see it as far off," the useful question is not what the Quraysh saw, but what *your* expenditures say you see.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:7

> **but We see it as nigh.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Eyes, One Hour**

The seventh verse is the shortest sentence in the sūrah and its perfect hinge: *wa-narāhu qarībā* — "but We see it as nigh." Where the deniers "see it far off," God answers with the same verb of vision turned back from the other side of the divide — *We see* — and the same object with the opposite adjective: *near*. Al-Ṭabarī reads the "We" as God's plural of majesty, the first-person plural that Arabic uses to surround a king's singular decree with the dignity of his court: the same voice that said *"We created," "We sent down," "We shall gather"* says *"We see."* Ibn Kathīr records the alternative of others: that the "We" may indicate the believers, who fear the Day as though it stood at the door — a reading the verse richly deserves, for it is the mark of faith described elsewhere: *"the believers... certain that they shall meet their Lord"* (2:46). The Prophet ﷺ gave that certainty its most famous form of counsel:

> *"Be in the world as a stranger or a passer-by."* (al-Bukhārī 6416, from Ibn ʿUmar — whose completion of the counsel became a maxim of the pious: *"Take from your evening for your morning, and from your life for your death."*)

**Who Is Right About Distance?**

Set side by side, verses 6 and 7 present a contest of perception with an objectively decidable outcome — which is what makes the pairing so quietly devastating. The deniers' "far" and God's "near" cannot both be true measures, because distance to a fixed point is not a matter of taste. And the verse asserts, without argument, whose optics are the real ones: the Maker of the clock sees the remaining time as it is. The Qur'an's reasoning on this question is deployed elsewhere with full clarity:

> *"And they say: 'When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?' Say: 'For you the appointed day is one day, from which you cannot delay an hour, nor advance it.'"* (Qur'an 34:30–31)

> *"We have not created the heavens and the earth and what is between them, save in truth; and the Hour is surely coming..."* (Qur'an 15:85)

For every individual denier — and this is the point the sūrah's own endgame will enforce — the "Hour" is nearer than any cosmic date: it is his own death, an appointment *"when its term comes, they can neither delay it an hour nor bring it forward"* (cf. Qur'an 7:34; 10:49). And after death, the interval to the Resurrection, whether a millennium or fifty thousand years, will be experienced as a sleep between two wakings, for the Qur'an itself describes the raised dead as insisting: *"they will swear they tarried but an hour"* (30:55); and the People of the Cave, who slept three centuries, rose *"saying: How long have you tarried? They said: A day, or part of a day"* (18:19). Distance, in other words, is a property of *this* life's clock only. The moment that clock stops, the Hour converts instantly from "far" to "now" — which is why God's seeing it as near is not poetry but precision: measured from the vantage of the souls who will live through it, it is at most one lifespan away, and from God's vantage, what is a thousand years among His fifty-thousand-year Days? *"A day with thy Lord is as a thousand years of your reckoning"* (22:47).

**Living in the Light of the Near**

The devotional tradition built on this verse a whole orientation. If God sees the Day as near, then nearness is the truer fact, and the believer aligns his sight with God's sight rather than the market's. Ibn al-Qayyim, in his classical manual of the heart's stations, remarks that the servants are ranked by their proportion of this seeing: the heedless see the Hereafter as a distant country and this world as home; the intelligent see this world as a bridge and the Hereafter as home; and the portion of each soul's tranquility rises with the second sight. The practical fruit is concrete. The person who internalizes "We see it as nigh" is easy to identify in behavior: he reconciles quarrels before the night ends, pays the worker before his sweat dries — the Prophet ﷺ commanded, *"Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries"* (Ibn Mājah 2443, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī) — hastens repentance, and refuses to build on borrowed time: *"and let not the disbeliever reckon that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin"* (3:178). Verse 7 closes the sūrah's opening movement as it opened — with vision: the questioner demanded to see; the deniers see wrongly; God sees truly. From here the sūrah will simply unfold *what* it is that He sees so near: a sky like molten metal, mountains like wool, and a Fire with a voice.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:8

> **On a day when the sky is as molten lead,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First of the Unveilings**

With verse 8 the sūrah stops arguing and starts *showing*. The phrase *yawma takūnu* — "the day when it comes to be" — announces a scene, and the first thing in the scene is the sky, the most stable feature of every human landscape: *ka-l-muhl* — like molten lead. The force of the image was carefully documented by the commentators' lexicons. *Muhl*, as Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī gloss it, is the thick, foul residue left when oil has been boiled — the scum that rises and settles on a pot left too long on the fire; al-Qurṭubī adds the readings "oozing blood" and "pus." The Qur'an does not say the sky *becomes* lead or oil; it says it becomes *like* the residue of burning — like what is left when a thing has been consumed by heat. The verse is thereby a metaphysical statement about the sky itself: what we treat as the ceiling of permanence is, in the Qur'an's cosmology, a created thing with a term of its own, and on that Day its term expires in a spectacle of un-creation. The most familiar object of the human world becomes the most terrifying.

**The Qur'an's Rehearsal of Sky-Foldings**

Our verse stands in a sequence of images the Qur'an deploys across the Makkan sūrahs, each intensifying the last. The sky will be *"split"* (84:1); it will *"turn rose-red like paint"* (55:37); it will be *"rolled up"*: *"the Day We roll up the heaven as the rolling of scrolls for writings"* (21:104); and in our sūrah it hangs with the residue of its own burning. The variety is deliberate: each image attacks a different assumption of permanence — the scroll refutes the sky as a fixed vault, the paint refutes it as a solid firmament, the molten dregs refute it as the clean, incorruptible above. The psychological target is what the Qur'an elsewhere names: the denier's unexamined trust that the world's furniture is permanent — *"and they say: 'What is there besides our present life, dying and living, and destroyed only by Time?' And they have no knowledge of that; they only conjecture"* (45:24). Against that trust the Qur'an directs its most durable images, for it is speaking to a species whose eyes are lifted thousands of times a day to the one thing that has never visibly failed them. The sūrah's logic is shock therapy by verse: the demanders wanted to *see* the punishment (vv. 1, 6); very well — the vision begins, and it begins with the betrayal of the sky.

**Muhl at Table and in the Drink**

The word *muhl* returns in the Qur'an in two further scenes, and the repetition teaches how the vocabulary of Hell is built. The food of the sinful in Hell is *"like molten lead (muhl), boiling in the bellies as the boiling of scalding water"* (44:45–46) — the same residue that was the sky's death becomes the sinner's cuisine; the outer world's corruption and the body's corruption meet in one substance. And the drink of Hell is poured in the same vocabulary:

> *"...and if they ask for a drink, they will be given water like molten lead (muhl) that scalds their faces; how dreadful the drink, and how evil the resting-place."* (Qur'an 18:29)

The pattern is exact and intentional: the one who refused the pure water of revelation is given the residue of burning; the sky that refused to warn him becomes, in the end, the same substance as his food and his drink. The Qur'an's Hell is not a random furnace; it is the truth of every corrupted pleasure, served at last in its concentrated form.

**Reading Catastrophe Imagery Without Sensationalism**

The verse requires of the reader a disciplined imagination. The commentary tradition — al-Ṭabarī's generation above all — consistently handled these images as realities of the unseen whose *mode* God knows, not as symbols to be demythologized away nor as cinema to be savored. Their temper is instructive: describe what the Book describes, in the Book's own words, and let the description do its work on the heart. The purpose of the imagery is not horror for horror's sake; it is *proportion* — restoring to the word "consequences" a magnitude the comfortable present has drained from it. The Qur'an itself frames the Day with the image that says most: *"a Day that shall make children gray-haired"* (73:17). And the Prophet ﷺ, who taught his community to hold that Day in view, closed his sermons with the standing prayer of refuge:

> *"I seek refuge in God from Hell-Fire, and from the punishment of the grave, and from the trial of life and death, and from the evil of the trial of the False Messiah."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Jumuʿah, from Jābir — the Prophet's regular closing, making the doctrine of verses 8–14 a matter of weekly congregational practice)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:9

> **and the mountains are as carded wool,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Unveiling: Mountains Unspun**

If the sky is the ceiling of permanence, the mountains are its pillars, and verse 9 dissolves them next: *wa-takūnu l-jibālu ka-l-ʿihn* — "and the mountains become like carded wool." The lexicons describe *ʿihn* as wool or cotton in its first stage of preparation: teased and carded until its fibers separate into soft, flying tufts. The resurrection verse of Sūrat al-Qāriʿah gives the image its twin: *"and the mountains become like carded wool (ka-l-ʿihni l-manfūsh)"* (101:5) — with *manfūsh*, "fluffed and carded," making the process explicit; and Ibn Kathīr records the variant recitation *ka-l-ʿihni l-mufannal* — "like dyed wool of varied colors" — a reading the commentators treasured, for the mountains, red and white and black, scattered and carded, would indeed resemble heaps of dyed wool of different hues. The image is a feat of scale-inversion. In the Qur'an's own idiom the mountain is the emblem of immovability — *"as if they were pegs"* (78:7), driven down like tent-stakes of the earth — and the End is narrated as the unstaking: *"the Day the heaven sways with a wondrous swaying, and the mountains travel with a wondrous traveling"* (52:9–10); *"and the mountains are made to pass away as if they were a mirage"* (78:20); *"and you will see the mountains, supposing them fixed, passing as the passing of clouds"* (27:88); *"and they ask thee about the mountains, so say: 'My Lord will scatter them as dust'"* (20:105); *"and the mountains like heaps of shifting sand"* (73:14). Our verse completes the set: what was once driven into the earth like a peg becomes soft enough to be carded and blown away. The hardest matter in human experience acquires, on that Day, the physics of a textile.

**Why Wool? The Quiet Theology of the Image**

Every element of the comparison carries freight. Carded wool is not merely soft; it is *unspun*. Spinning is the act that gives fibers strength — thousands of weak strands twisted into a single unbreakable thread. The mountains, so the image whispers, were the earth's *spun* material: matter twisted together with such force that armies, earthquakes, and ages could not comb it apart. On the Day, the spinning is undone — the twist that held particle to particle is released, and the emblem of permanence becomes raw fluff awaiting a new hand. And wool is the material of *clothing* — the stuff that human hands card, spin, and weave. There is a quiet rebuke embedded in the comparison: what you called eternal was, from the first, only raw material in the hands of a Maker; and the Maker who spun it into solidity can comb it back into softness whenever He wills. Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both cite 101:5 alongside our verse, and both note the coupling in that sūrah of softened mountains with scattered, moth-like people (101:4): creation's two anchors — matter and mankind — are unmoored in the same breath, and both images teach the single lesson of *iqāmah*: nothing stands but what God holds standing.

**The Argument from Stability's End**

Verses 8–9 together constitute an argument, and it is worth stating its logic plainly, because it is the Qur'an's answer to the questioner of verse 1. The denier's unspoken syllogism ran: the world has always been stable; stable systems do not end; therefore the promised end will not come. The sūrah's counter-syllogism: stability is not self-sustaining but *held*; what is held is held by a Holder; and the Holder has announced a Day on which He lets go:

> *"We created not the heavens and the earth and what is between them in play. We created them not save in truth; but most of them know not. The Day of Separation is an appointed time for them all — a Day when no patron shall avail a client, nor shall they be helped — save whomsoever God has mercy on; truly He is the Mighty, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 44:38–42)

The modern reader possesses, in plate tectonics and stellar physics, accidental illustrations of the point the Qur'an was pressing in the seventh century: the mountain is not an eternal category but a temporary configuration — rock that rose and will level; the sky is not a vault but a thin shell of gas around a minor planet in an ageing cosmos. The Qur'an's claim is more radical than any slow decay, for it asserts not erosion but a *summons*: the sky and the mountains do not merely wear out; they answer, at the appointed hour, the command of their Lord — *"the Day the earth is replaced by another earth, and the heavens too, and all come forth before God, the One, the Irresistible"* (14:48). The questioner demanded a date; the sūrah gives him instead a cast list for the Day — his own sky and his own mountains among the actors — and lets him consider what part is left for him.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:10

> **and no loyal friend shall ask about a loyal friend,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah's First Word Comes Back Reversed**

Verse 10 contains the sūrah's most elegant structural irony, and the classical commentators paused on it. Al-Maʿārij opened with a questioner: *saʾala sāʾilun* — "a questioner asked." Now, ten verses later, on the Day that questioner asked about, the verb returns in the negative: *wa-lā yasʾalu ḥamīmun ḥamīmā* — "and no loyal friend asks about a loyal friend." The man who had questions for God will have none even for his best friend. The repetition of *ḥamīm* — friend, friend — in the same cognate construction as the opening's questioner-asked drives the symmetry home: the sūrah begins with a world full of questions and ends its vision with a world in which the only remaining question is each soul's own account. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both read the verse as a description of the totality of the Day's occupation: every ear will be filled with its own news, as the Qur'an says elsewhere with finality:

> *"But when there comes the Deafening Blast — the Day a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his spouse and his children — every man that Day shall have an affair to suffice him."* (Qur'an 80:33–37)

**Ḥamīm: The Warmest Word for a Friend**

The word the Qur'an chooses for "friend" here is not the ordinary *ṣadīq* (companion) or *khalīl* (intimate). *Ḥamīm* comes from the root ḥ-m-m, whose base is heat: something *ḥāmm* is burning-hot, and a *ḥamīm* is etymologically "the warm one" — the friend in whom you are wrapped as in a warm garment, a devoted friend, a protector, sometimes even a kinsman by marriage. The lexicographers note the Arab usage: a man's *ḥamīm* was the one whose loyalty was assumed without contract, whose help was owed without asking. In a tribal society where a man's survival depended on his web of ḥamīm-alliances, the verse announces the dissolution of the very structure of security: on the Day, the warmest bond of the warmest word goes cold. Each person becomes, in the Qur'an's crushing phrase, *"an affair that suffices him"* — sufficient to occupy, and insufficient to save.

**Why Even Friendship Must Be Suspended Then**

The suspension of all asking is not cruelty; it is the necessary condition of a judgment in which every case stands on its own. The Qur'an states the principle as a legal axiom:

> *"No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another; and man has nothing save what he strives for."* (Qur'an 53:38–39; cf. 6:164)

If friends could plead for friends, judgment would follow friendship rather than truth — the very defect of every patronage system this world has ever run. If friends could even *console* friends, the verdicts would be softened before they were received. So the Day is engineered, mercifully and terribly, as the one court in which no one can represent, distract, or accompany anyone: *"and We shall inherit from him what he speaks, and he shall come to Us alone"* (19:80). The Prophet ﷺ warned even the dearest human bond not to be leaned upon as a ladder:

> *"'Āʾishah said: A man came to the Prophet and said: 'O Messenger of God, who is most deserving of my good company?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Your mother.' He said: 'Then who?' He said: 'Your father.'"* (al-Bukhārī 5971; Muslim 2548 — the ranking is absolute, and yet the same Prophet taught that on the Day no mother bears another's burden)

The verse's practical teaching is thus a re-ordering of trust now, before the Day: love your ḥamīm warmly, do good to him, be his warmth in this world — but build your house on no man's plea. The Qur'an even measures the exact weight of human attachment against its use on that Day:

> *"Say: If your fathers, your sons, your brothers, your spouses, your clan, the wealth you have acquired, a trade you fear may slacken, and dwellings you love are dearer to you than God and His Messenger and striving in His way — then wait until God brings His command; God guides not the iniquitous people."* (Qur'an 9:24)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:11

> **[though] they will be made to see them. The guilty would wish to ransom himself from the punishment of that Day at the price of his children,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seen, but Not Spoken To**

Verse 11 begins with a paradox the commentators worked hard to resolve: *yubaṣṣarūnahum* — "they will be made to see them." The verb is in the intensive second-form: not merely *yarawna* (they see), but *yubaṣṣarūna* — they are given piercing sight, made to stare with clarified vision. Al-Ṭabarī offers the readings: though no one asks about anyone, all will still *see* one another — the friend visible and unavailable; the mere sight of the beloved becoming part of the punishment, as a drowning man sees the shore. He adds a second reading recorded in the tradition: that God shows the believers the states of the disbelievers — a sight that completes the believers' understanding — and al-Qurṭubī transmits a third: that the angels are shown the states of all human beings, each creature's record made visible to the cosmic court. What unites the readings is the theme of exposure: on that Day sight is perfected and speech is rationed. In this world, the guilty manage their image — friendships are curated, failures reframed, accomplices silenced or bribed. There, *yu-baṣ-ṣa-rū-na*: the intensive stem strips every veil, and each person sees the others as they truly were. The reunion of all mankind will be the most honest meeting in history.

**The Ransom List: Beginning with What He Loves Most**

Then the verse begins its terrible catalogue: *yawaddu l-mujrimu law yaftadī* — "the guilty one would wish to ransom himself." The verb *yawaddu* (he wishes) is conditional in force: not that it will be accepted, but that he will *wish* it. And the first price he offers is *bi-banīhi* — his children. The order of the list across verses 11–14 is descending in intimacy and ascending in scope: children, spouse, brother, the clan that sheltered him, then every soul on earth altogether. The classical commentators ask: why begin with the children? And their answer has the ring of psychological truth: a parent would sooner offer himself than his child — so the guilty man offers the child *first*, because at that point he is still negotiating as a man who believes some greater pain might purchase his escape; the sequence is the anatomy of desperation. The Qur'an knows the depth of parental love and names it as the very currency of the proposed transaction:

> *"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world; but enduring righteous deeds are better with thy Lord for reward, and better for hope."* (Qur'an 18:46)

**The Qur'an's Repeated Verdict on Ransom**

That no ransom will be taken is not announced here alone; the Qur'an returns to the theme with the insistence of a gavel falling:

> *"Truly those who disbelieve and die while they are disbelievers — an earth full of gold would not be accepted from any one of them, were he to offer it in ransom; theirs is a painful punishment, and they have no helpers."* (Qur'an 3:91)

> *"Were those who work evil to possess all that is on the earth, and the like of it besides it, they would surely offer it to ransom themselves from the evil of the punishment on the Day of Resurrection; but there will appear to them from God what they never reckoned."* (Qur'an 39:47)

> *"So this Day no ransom is taken from you, nor from those who disbelieved; your abode is the Fire; it is your patron — and an evil journey's end."* (Qur'an 57:15; cf. 2:48, 2:123; 10:54; 13:18)

Al-Ṭabarī notes the economic logic beneath the imagery: ransom works in systems where the judge benefits from payment, or where the injured party consents to compensation. On that Day, the Judge owns all treasure and cannot be enriched, and the injured party — God Himself, whose right the sinner violated — has already refused the price. The verdict belongs to the wronged Sovereign alone: *"Say: For whose sake is the entire earth and all therein, if you have knowledge? They will say: God's. Say: Will you not then remember?"* (23:84–85) — and what the sinner offers was never his to offer.

**Every Soul Its Own Case**

The verse completes the teaching of verse 10 and states the Qur'an's principle of ultimate individuality: *"and fear a Day when no soul shall avail another in anything"* (2:48). This was a revolution in the religious economy of the ancient world, where sacrifice could be transferred, priests could absorb guilt, and one creature's blood could be laid on another. Islam's assembly on that Day is a court of one-person cases: your prayer, your alms, your testimony, your guarded chastity from vv. 22–34 — yours, presented by you. The moral effect is double. For the complacent, it is the end of hiding: behind mother's prayer, father's name, spouse's virtue, the community's reputation — *"then We shall surely relate to them with knowledge, for We were not absent"* (7:7). For the anxious, it is strangely consoling: a judgment of pure personal merit cannot be rigged by anyone against you either — no enemy's slander can outweigh your record, and no tyrant's power can confiscate your deeds. The guilty man of verse 11, offering his children, his clan, and finally the whole earth, is the patron saint of every attempt to outsource one's own soul; and the Day's answer to him is the sūrah's own next word: *kallā* — no.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:12

> **his spouse, and his brother,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Intimates on the Altar of Escape**

Verse 12 extends the ransom list from the children of verse 11 to the two adults with whom a man shares his innermost life: *wa-ṣāḥibatihi* — "and his spouse" — *wa-akhīhi* — "and his brother." The word for spouse is built from the root ṣ-ḥ-b, companionship itself: a *ṣāḥibah* is literally "his companion," the one who has accompanied him through the nights and years. The Qur'an defines this bond at its most beautiful:

> *"And of His signs is that He created for you, from yourselves, spouses that you might find repose in them; and He set between you affection (mawaddah) and mercy (raḥmah); surely in that are signs for a people who reflect."* (Qur'an 30:21)

And at its most intimate: *"They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them"* (2:187) — each the other's covering, warmth, and adornment. In the world, the spouse knows what no one else knows: the sins, the weaknesses, the 3 a.m. confessions. On the Day of verse 12, the guilty man offers that person as a price — the garment offered to the fire in place of the body. The brother follows for the same reason: between brothers there is the shared house, the shared blood, the shared enemies — in the Arab's world, the brother was the man you stood beside in every feud. The Qur'an's resurrection scene of Sūrat ʿAbasa has already been quoted: *"the Day a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his spouse and his children"* (80:34–36). Here the flight is inverted into a bargain: not fleeing them, but *selling* them — and the commentators noted which is the darker image, for the fleeing man at least keeps his love intact.

**Love's True Currency Under Judgment**

The verse's psychological honesty is uncomfortable: it does not say the guilty man *stops loving* his spouse and brother; it says his terror outruns his love. This is precisely the corruption of love that the Qur'an diagnoses as *shirk's* domestic form — loving the creation more than the Creator, and being willing to sacrifice the creature to save oneself. The sūrah's remedy comes later, in the community of the excepted (vv. 22–35), whose loves are healed precisely because they are ordered: they love spouse and brother *within* the covenant, not *instead of* it. The Qur'an elsewhere shows what such rightly-ordered love looks like under pressure — the believer whose family mocks his faith is commanded kindness without obedience, and the believer whose spouse and children are a trial is promised better:

> *"O you who believe, surely among your spouses and your children are enemies to you; so beware of them. But if you pardon and overlook and forgive — God is Forgiving, Merciful. Your wealth and your children are but a trial; and God — with Him is a tremendous reward."* (Qur'an 64:14–15)

And the believing spouse becomes a teammate in the very deliverance this verse's guilty man tried to purchase alone:

> *"And the believers, men and women, are protecting friends of one another: they enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong... God has promised the believers, men and women, Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein — that is the supreme triumph."* (Qur'an 9:71–72)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:13

> **his kin who had sheltered him,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faṣīlah: The Branch That Held Him**

Verse 13 names the third price: *wa-faṣīlatihi allatī tuʾwīhi* — "and his clan (faṣīlah) that shelters him." The word deserves slow reading. From the root f-ṣ-l, "to split, to separate, to branch off," a *faṣīlah* is literally an offshoot — the branch of a tribe descending from one stock, the inner circle of kin who are one's own "section." Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī render it "his tribe, his clan"; Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī also record the more focused reading of Ibn ʿAbbās's school: one's mother — the womb-relation (aṣl al-faṣīlah) from whom one's own "branch" was first split. The relative clause is what gives the verse its Makkahn punch: *allatī tuʾwīhi* — "the one that gives him refuge," from the verb awā, to shelter, to harbor, to take in. In pre-Islamic Arabia the clan was survival itself: the man outside his clan's protection was an outlaw anyone might kill with impunity; the man inside it had, in the clan's collective honor, a wall. The Quraysh listener heard verse 13 as the bankruptcy of his civilization's oldest institution: on the Day, the wall that sheltered you from every enemy is offered by you to the fire as kindling.

**The Clan Cannot Cover What God Has Uncovered**

The deeper teaching is about the *kind* of protection a clan can actually give. A clan can avenge blood, defend a well, guarantee a debt, and shield a fugitive — from other men. It cannot stand between a soul and its Maker, because the wrongs at issue on that Day are not wrongs against the clan but against the God who sees in secret. The Qur'an marks the boundary of family protection with the story of Noah's son at the flood — the single most precise parallel to this verse in the whole Book, for it uses the same root of sheltering:

> *"...and Noah called to his son, who was apart: 'Embark with us, my son; be not with the disbelievers.' He said: 'I shall take refuge (sa-ʾāwī) on a mountain that will defend me from the water.' He said: 'Today there is no defender (lā ʿāṣima) from God's command, except for whom He has mercy.'"* (Qur'an 11:42–43)

Noah — a prophet, a father, the head of the only saved household on earth — could not stretch his clan's covering over one son who stood outside obedience. And Abraham, the intimate of God, learned the same boundary about his own father-figure: *"And Abraham's asking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him; but when it became clear to him that he was an enemy of God, he disavowed him; truly Abraham was soft of heart, clement"* (9:114). Even prophetic rank does not convert kinship into currency. On that Day, as the Qur'an says with terrible economy, *"close friends will be enemies to one another, except the righteous"* (43:67) — not because love dies, but because every covering is stripped except the covering of God: *"the Day on which neither wealth nor sons avail, save whoever comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89).

**Shelter Redefined for the Believer**

For the believer, verse 13 quietly relocates his security. If the clan's tent cannot cover him on that Day, where does one shelter? The Qur'an's answer is the promise that turns the very root of this verse into an attribute of God: *"Did He not find thee an orphan and shelter thee (fa-āwā)?"* (93:6) — the same verb that describes the clan's sheltering of its member describes God's sheltering of His Prophet when the clan of Quraysh had withdrawn every protection. The sūrah's later excepted ones (vv. 22–35) are, in the end, simply those who chose the better ḥamīm and the better faṣīlah: *"God is the Patron of the believers"* (2:257); *"God is the Patron of the righteous"* (45:19). The man who leans on the everlasting shelter needs no one to ransom him, because he is already inside the only wall that the Day cannot breach.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:14

> **and all who are on the earth, that it might save him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List Reaches Its Impossible Maximum**

Verse 14 completes the catalogue with a figure of speech that swallows the world: *wa-man fī l-arḍi jamīʿan* — "and everyone who is on the earth, all together." The grammar of escalation across vv. 11–14 has now reached its ceiling: my children (my future), my spouse (my present), my brother (my blood), my clan (my wall) — and now, in a final spasm, *everyone else alive, the entire human race*. The logic of the list is the logic of a man emptying his pockets to a highwayman, offering first what is most valuable and ending by throwing the whole wallet, the coat, and the car keys. The Qur'an describes the wish as *thumma yunjīhi* — "that it might then save him" — a subjunctive of forlorn hope: he stacks the whole of mankind on the scale, and the scale does not move. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr both stress that the verse is the measure not of the family's worth but of the punishment's weight: only a terror of that magnitude could make a human being contemplate such an exchange. The Qur'an gives the same disproportion from the other side — the reward of the righteous, and the one exchange that actually matters:

> *"No soul knows what is concealed for them of the comfort of the eyes, as a recompense for what they used to do."* (Qur'an 32:17)

> *"...and whosoever is removed far from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has triumphed; and the life of this world is naught but enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 3:185)

The exchange rate of that Day is simply not set in any currency the world mints.

**Why Everything Together Would Not Be Enough**

The theological reason no aggregate of creatures can ransom one soul is stated by the Qur'an as ownership: everything he is offering already belongs to the One he is offering it to. *"To God belongs all that is in the heavens and the earth"* (2:284); *"and to God belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, and to God is the journey's end"* (24:42). A defendant who offers the judge the judge's own courthouse has not paid anything; he has only added contempt to his crime. And the reason *no one else* can stand in is the reason given at verse 10 — *"no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another"* (53:38) — the Day's cases are tried one defendant at a time. The classical scholars drew out the corollary that modern readers most need: if nothing purchasable, lovable, or human avails then, then the *only* variable that remains is what God Himself has said avails — and He has named it plainly:

> *"Truly God has purchased from the believers their souls and their wealth, that the Garden be theirs..."* (Qur'an 9:111)

The currency of that Day is the surrendered self — not offered as a bribe, but given as a trust to the Purchaser; and vv. 22–35 of this very sūrah will itemize what that surrender looks like in practice. The sūrah has thus completed its first great arc: the questioner demanded the punishment (v. 1); the punishment was fixed and dated in divine terms (vv. 2–4); the Prophet was told to wait beautifully (v. 5); the Day was shown (vv. 6–14); and now, with every ransom refused and every shelter stripped, the text turns to the one thing that remains when everything else is gone: the Fire itself, which the guilty will meet not as strangers, but as something that has been calling their name.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:15

> **Nay, but it is a churning fire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā: The Door Slams on the Bargain**

The ransom catalogue of vv. 11–14 was one long subjunctive wish — he *would* offer, he *wishes* it *might* save. Verse 15 kills the wish in a single word: *kallā*. This small particle is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic voices in the middle Makkan sūrahs — a corrective interjection that means "No! Nay! Not at all! Stop!" and functions like the bang of a gavel: *enough of this*. With it, the entire economy of escape — the children, the spouse, the brother, the clan, the populated earth — is swept off the negotiating table in one motion. The classical readers also recorded an alternative vocalization of the following clause, noted by al-Qurṭubī, under which the verse reads not as denial but as terrible affirmation: "Truly it is Laẓā." On either reading the meaning converges: the transaction is void, and what remains standing over the bankrupt bargaining table is the thing itself — the Fire.

**Laẓā: A Proper Name, Not a Description**

The Qur'an does not merely say "a fire"; it names it: *innahā laẓā* — "it is Laẓā." Hell in the Qur'an is a place with a personal registry of names, each name an aspect of its reality: Jahannam (the Abyss), Saqar (the Scorching), Saʿīr (the Blazing), Jaḥīm (the Furnace), al-Ḥutamah (the Crusher), al-Ḥāwiyyah (the Pit), and here, Laẓā. The lexicographers derive it from a root whose sense al-Jalālayn record in a vivid verb: *talalaẓẓā* — it coils and churns its flames, the way a fierce animal coils before striking. Laẓā is fire considered not as light but as *motion* — flame that does not merely burn but lunges, swirls, and wraps around its fuel. The choice of this name, precisely here, is the Qur'an's answer to the ransom list: the guilty man proposed to hand over everyone dear to him; the Fire is described as a living hunger that seizes *him*, personally, by name — for the following verses will give it a voice, calling to those it recognizes. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both pause at the personification: the Qur'an's Fire is not an element but an instrument with assigned duties, *"a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, prepared for the disbelievers"* (2:24).

**Why the Qur'an Names Its Fire**

The naming matters beyond rhetoric. In the Qur'an's theology, Hell is not a metaphor for regret, nor an impersonal consequence flowing automatically like gravity; it is a *prepared* place, tailored — as its many names each emphasize a different aspect — to the many-sidedness of evil. The one who hoarded meets a Fire that is fed by what was hoarded (9:34–35, quoted below at v. 18); the one who tore society with slander meets the Crusher; the one who sank is met by the Abyss. Laẓā's specialty, per its position in this sūrah, is the seizure of the one who thought someone else could be seized in his place. The Qur'an elsewhere gives the Fire the same awful personification in motion:

> *"When it sees them from a place far off, they will hear its fury and its roaring; and when they are cast, bound together, into a narrow place of it, they will call out there for destruction. 'Call not today for one destruction, but call for many destructions!'"* (Qur'an 25:12–14)

The scene inverts verse 11: there, the guilty man wished for one great substitution; here, he wishes for one small annihilation, and is told to wish for many — the economy of exchange is closed in both directions.

**The Mercy Inside the Warning**

For all its severity, the verse belongs to a genre whose motive the Qur'an itself declares: *"And remind — for truly the reminder benefits the believers"* (51:55). The warning is an invitation to reflection before the fact: *"Say: I exhort you to one thing: that you rise up for God, in pairs and singly, and then reflect"* (34:46). A God who described the Fire not at all would leave His creatures navigating by appetite alone; the description is the compassion of a physician who insists on showing the patient the X-ray. And the Prophet ﷺ looped the memory of verses like this one through the community's most joyful devotion, so that the believer never rises to celebrate without remembering where celebration leads if it is abused:

> *"...Labbayk, O God, labbayk... truly the praise and the grace are Yours, and the kingdom; labbayk — and I seek refuge in You from Hell."* (al-Bukhārī 1549, from Ibn ʿUmar — the pilgrim's litany commanded by the Prophet ﷺ, closing with refuge from the Fire)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:16

> **ripping away the scalp,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nazzāʿah Lil-Shawā: The Grammar of Intensity**

The description of Laẓā continues in verse 16 with two more precisely chosen words. *Nazzāʿah* is an intensive active participle from the root n-z-ʿ, "to pull off, to strip away, to snatch": not a fire that merely singes, but one whose defining activity is *removal* — it is a plucker, a stripper. And its object is *al-shawā*, the scalp — the skin of the head, with its covering of hair. Ibn Kathīr glosses the image: the Fire burns away the scalp completely; al-Ṭabarī records the wider reading that it burns away the whole skin, the body's entire envelope; and Ibn Kathīr also transmits a spectrum of views about what, if anything, the burning spares — the bones, the fingertips, the facial features — from which the imagination is meant to recoil and return chastened. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, the great ascetic of the first generation after the Companions, gives the reading that rings most awfully: everything of the body will be burned away, *and only the heart will remain, and it will cry out* — the organ with which a man refused to feel will be the last thing left to feel.

**Why the Scalp First?**

The choice of the head as the point of attack is deliberate anatomy. The scalp is the body's crown — the seat of thought, of sight, of hearing; the part a helmet exists to protect; in every culture the head is what is bared or covered to signify dignity, and the face is what a human being shields first. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same point with the same body part when it describes the punishment of the hoarders: *"the Day it is heated in the Fire of Hell, and their foreheads and their sides and their backs are branded with it"* (9:35) — the brand is applied to the identity-bearing surfaces. And in our sūrah's own sequel the Fire *"calls to those who turned their backs"* (v. 17) — the back that was turned to God in this world, the head that was lifted in arrogance, are the very members the description fastens upon. There is an exact justice of correspondence in the Qur'an's eschatology: *"And those who earn evil deeds — the recompense of an evil deed is its like, and abasement will cover them"* (10:27). The instrument of punishment addresses itself, member by member, to the members that were used to sin: *"On the Day their tongues and their hands and their feet bear witness against them concerning what they used to do"* (24:24).

**The Body That Cannot Be Finally Distracted**

Verse 16 also completes a theological argument that began at verse 8. The denier's implicit creed was materialism: the sky and mountains are permanent, the body is the self, and death is the body's end — therefore no reckoning. The sūrah's answer has moved step by step: the sky burns (v. 8), the mountains blow away (v. 9), every social structure dissolves (vv. 10–14) — and now the body itself, the materialist's only certainty, is shown to be no refuge from what comes after it, for the Fire's first act is to unhouse the body from its own skin. The Qur'an states the underlying principle with the terrifying verses of Sūrat al-Nisāʾ:

> *"Surely those who disbelieve in Our signs — We shall surely cause them to burn in a Fire; as often as their skins are consumed, We will give them other skins in exchange, that they may taste the punishment. Truly God is Mighty, Wise."* (Qur'an 4:56)

The verse's logic is a mirror of the resurrection's logic: the God who can re-clothe the soul with skin for punishment is the same God who can re-clothe it for bliss — *"and they will say: 'Praise be to God, who removed from us sorrow; truly our Lord is Forgiving, Thankful — who of His bounty has made us dwell in the Abode, where no weariness touches us, nor touches us fatigue'"* (35:34–35). The skin — the organ that registers pain and pleasure at the boundary of the self — is the battleground on which both the warning and the promise are written. For the believer, the verse turns at last into counsel about this life: the scalp that Laẓā strips is carried today on shoulders that can bow in prayer; the skin that will taste then is a skin that can feel the discomfort of fasting now. The Prophet ﷺ used precisely this leverage of present sensation when he taught:

> *"The Fire has been veiled with desires, and the Garden has been veiled with hardships."* (al-Bukhārī 6487; Muslim 2822, from Abū Hurayrah — the two veils: the road to each of the two abodes passes through sensation now, sensation then)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:17

> **calling to those who turned their backs and fled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire Is Given a Voice**

Verse 17 deepens the personification begun in vv. 15–16: *tadʿū man adbara wa-tawallā* — "it calls to those who turned their backs and fled." The verb is d-ʿ-w — the ordinary Arabic word for calling, invoking, summoning; the same root that gives the believer's own *duʿāʾ*. The image is of a host who calls out to a guest — and the horror of the image is its hospitality inverted: in the Qur'an's scenes, the Fire receives its people like an estate receiving its heirs. Al-Ṭabarī explains the verse's logic: during the life of this world they turned away from obedience to God and fled from belief in His books and His messengers; then, on the Day, the Fire will *call them by their deeds* — summoning those who fled from the truth as a master summons runaway servants. Ibn Kathīr sharpens it: the Fire will pick out and claim those who turned their backs — it knows its own. The Qur'an gives this summons audible form elsewhere:

> *"When it sees them from a place far off, they will hear its fury and its roaring..."* (Qur'an 25:12)

> *"And when they are flung into a narrow place of it, bound together, they will call out there for destruction..."* (Qur'an 25:13; cf. 67:7–8: *"When they are cast into it, they will hear it drawing its breath as it boils; it would almost burst with rage")*

Laẓā does not burn its inhabitants anonymously. It *draws breath*, *roars*, *sees them from afar*, and *calls*. The theological statement inside the poetry is precise: punishment in the Qur'an is not an indifferent physical process like falling off a cliff; it is addressed, personal, and relational — the final, unavoidable confrontation between the soul and the truth it spent a lifetime declining.

**You Cannot Turn Your Back on What Calls Your Name**

The verse's two verbs of refusal deserve attention. *Adbara* — to turn one's back; *tawallā* — to turn away, withdraw, depart. They describe a posture rather than an argument: the denier of vv. 1–7 was never shown defeating the Prophet's case; he was shown walking away from it. And this, the sūrah now reveals, is precisely what the Fire exists to answer. The psychology is timeless: most rejection of God is not refutation but *avoidance* — changing the subject, scrolling past, staying busy, postponing the question to a day that never comes. The Qur'an anatomizes the pattern in the denier of Sūrat al-Qiyāmah: *"He denied the truth and turned away; then he went off to his family, swaggering"* (75:32–33). What avoidance secures in the short term — peace, amusement, the unexamined life — it loses in the end, because the appointment it avoids keeps its own calendar. The Qur'an describes the resurrection of these avoiders in an image of involuntary arrival:

> *"And leave Me with the deniers, the masters of ease — and grant them a brief respite. Truly with Us are fetters and a Blaze, and food that chokes, and a painful punishment — the Day the earth and the mountains quake, and the mountains become a slipping heap of sand. Truly We have sent to you a Messenger as a witness upon you, as We sent a messenger to Pharaoh... the Day the Caller calls to a thing whose sound is abhorrent. That Day they will come forth from the graves as if they were scattered locusts, craning their necks toward the Caller..."* (Qur'an 73:11–14 and 54:6–7 — the Caller whom no one can decline)

The lesson the verse writes into the soul: answer God's call now, in the days when answering is still free, and no other call will have power over you; refuse it now, and the day will come when the call you refused to hear is replaced by one you cannot refuse.

**The Two Calls and the One Who Answers Both**

Ibn ʿAjībah, reading as a spiritual master, notes the symmetry of the two calls in the Qur'an: God calls to faith in this world — *"And God calls to the Abode of Peace, and guides whom He wills to a straight path"* (10:25) — and to Paradise in the next: *"And the dwellers of the Garden will call to the dwellers of the Fire: 'We have found what our Lord promised us true; have you found what your Lord promised true?' They will say: Yes"* (7:44). The Fire's call in v. 17 is the third call, and the only compulsory one: the first call could be declined, the second overheard, but the third gathers. The verse is therefore, for the believer, a map of spiritual priorities: organize life so that when the compulsory call comes, it finds you already answering the first — *"And obey God and the Messenger, that you may be shown mercy"* (3:132) — for the Prophet ﷺ promised: *"Whoever obeys me, he will enter the Garden; and whoever disobeys me — he has refused."* (al-Bukhārī 7280, from Abū Hurayrah)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:18

> **who amassed and hoarded.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jamaʿa Fa-Awʿā: The Sin Named in Bookkeeping Terms**

Verse 18 completes the Fire's call by naming the specific trait of the people it summons: *jamaʿa fa-awʿā* — "he amassed and hoarded." The Arabic is two verbs in sequence, and the sequence is the diagnosis. *Jamaʿa* — he gathered: income, herds, coin, whatever accumulates. Then *fa-awʿā* — "then he contained": from the root w-ʿ-y, the noun of which (*wiʿāʾ*) is a vessel, a container. He turned himself into a warehouse. The *fa* of consequence tells the whole story: gathering is not itself the crime; the crime is the *sealing* — money taken in and never released, wealth treated as terminus rather than transit. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr both read the verse against the refusal of the zakāh: the man who amasses and containers his wealth is the man in whose wealth *"is an acknowledged due"* (vv. 24–25 below) that he declined to acknowledge. The Qur'an indicts the same figure in two twin sūrahs, with the vocabulary shifted from storing to counting:

> *"Woe to every backbiting slanderer, who amasses wealth and counts it repeatedly, thinking that his wealth makes him immortal!"* (Qur'an 104:1–3)

And with the verdict spelled out in fire:

> *"...And those who hoard gold and silver and spend it not in the way of God — give them tidings of a painful punishment, the Day when it is heated in the Fire of Hell, and their foreheads and their sides and their backs are branded with it: 'This is what you hoarded for yourselves; so taste what you were hoarding!'"* (Qur'an 9:34–35)

The irony of 9:35 is exact: the metal he refused to release becomes the brand that marks him; the container becomes the contents.

**Why Hoarding Wages War on the Soul and the City**

The classical scholars did not read this verse as private advice about thrift; they read it as social legislation. Zakāh is, in the jurists' architecture, a *right embedded in wealth itself* — the source's rendering of v. 24, "an acknowledged due," is the exact sense: the poor hold a share in every rich man's surplus by divine law, not by his mood. The hoarder therefore commits two thefts at once: from God, whose distribution he intercepts and dams; and from the community, whose circulating medium he drains. The Prophet ﷺ gave the warning its most unforgettable form:

> *"No owner of gold or silver who fails to give its due — but on the Day of Resurrection plates will be heated for him in the Fire of Hell, and his side and forehead and back will be branded with them; each time they cool, they will be reheated, on a Day whose measure is fifty thousand years — until judgment is rendered among the servants."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 987, from Abū Hurayrah — whose closing phrase, "a day whose measure is fifty thousand years," ties this sermon directly to v. 4 of our sūrah)

And the hoarded wealth itself will rise against its keeper:

> *"Whoever God gives wealth to, and he does not pay its zakāh — his wealth will be made to appear to him on the Day of Resurrection as a bald serpent with two dark spots above its eyes; it will coil around him on the Day of Resurrection, seize his jawbones, and say: 'I am your wealth; I am your treasure.'"* (al-Bukhārī 1403, from Abū Hurayrah)

Economically, the verse states a principle modern readers can verify: wealth that circulates is a circulatory system; wealth that is contained becomes a clot. The Islamic law of inheritance — which shatters every estate across a wide net of claimants each generation — and the law of zakāh — which taxes the *stored* surplus yearly — are both engineered against the formation of permanent containers. Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, counseling a man who had built a granary, is remembered to have asked him whether he had built it for the grain or for the Fire — a question this verse arms every conscience with.

**What the Hoarder Was Really Trying to Buy**

Beneath the economics lies the psychology, and it is here that v. 18 joins the sūrah's main artery. Verses 10–14 showed a man on the Day willing to trade everything — children, spouse, clan, the earth entire — for deliverance: the ultimate panic buyer. Verse 18 explains where he learned the strategy: he spent his whole life believing that stockpiles create safety. The hoarder's container is an essay in applied theology — a material confession of what he trusts — and the sūrah has already dismantled its premise piece by piece: the sky melts, the mountains blow, every human shelter is struck from the docket, no ransom is accepted. What he amassed could not buy off the Day; indeed, in the Qur'an's scenes it becomes the fuel and the instrument of the Day. The believing alternative is not poverty but *flow*: the excepted ones of vv. 22–35 are people of prayer *and* of acknowledged dues — wealth possessed, but never contained; held in the hand like water in an open palm, which, Ibn ʿAjībah notes, can neither be lost to freezing (miserliness) nor burn the holder (attachment). The Prophet ﷺ gave the whole matter its one-line charter:

> *"Charity never decreased anything of wealth."* (Muslim 2588, from Abū Hurayrah)

> *"The son of Adam says: 'My wealth, my wealth' — but what of your wealth is yours, save what you ate and consumed, or wore and wore out, or gave in charity and sent ahead? All else departs and leaves him behind."* (Muslim 2958 — the inventory that leaves the container empty)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:19

> **Truly man was created anxious;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns Its X-Ray on the Reader**

With verse 19 the sūrah executes its most dramatic pivot. Eighteen verses have been aimed at the deniers — their demand, their Day, their Fire. Now, without warning, the beam turns inward, onto every listener: *inna l-insāna khuliqa halūʿā* — "truly man was created anxious." The word *insān* is not "the disbeliever"; the source notes that although some commentators confined it to the disbelievers (al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī among them), al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī read the following exception — "save those who pray" (v. 22) — as proof that the general reading is intended: what is described is the human being as such, the raw material, and only the disciplined religious life lifts a person out of the diagnosis. This is one of the Qur'an's most important anthropological statements, standing beside its siblings: *"Man was created of haste"* (21:37); *"And man prays for evil as he prays for good; and man is ever hasty"* (17:11); *"Man wearies not of praying for good"* (41:49). Together they sketch the species: a creature of appetite and urgency, optimized for short horizons, prone to panic and greed — and then the Qur'an, in each location, immediately begins the work of re-formation.

**Halūʿ: A Single Word for a Whole Syndrome**

The word *halūʿ* is built on the faʿūl pattern that Arabic uses for intensity and habitual character. The lexicographers and the commentators — al-Qurṭubī among the fullest — assemble its meaning from the verses that define it: it denotes the one who cannot bear affliction without panic, and cannot receive blessing without clutching — a compound of severe anxiety, greed, and covetousness; Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported in the tafsīr tradition, glosses it as the one who is impatient in hardship and stingy in ease. The choice of a single word is itself a marvel of compression: modern psychology would need a paragraph — loss aversion, scarcity mindset, catastrophizing, attachment, fear of missing out — and the Qur'an carries it all in two syllables. The syndromic quality is the point: anxiety and greed are not two vices but one vice seen from two sides. The man who *fears* not-having is the same man who *refuses* giving; the wound beneath both is the same unbelief in Providence — the suspicion that he is on his own, that what comes will not be replaced, that the universe is scarce and no one is minding the store.

**Khuliqa: The Grammar of Compassionate Diagnosis**

The verb is passive — *"he was created"* — and the commentators drew out its theology. This is not a verdict of condemnation but a statement of design: the frailty is factory-installed. Al-Rāzī notes the mercy in the grammar: God does not say man *chose* anxiety; He says man was *created* with it — which simultaneously (a) removes the grounds for arrogance in the disciplined, who conquered only what they were given the means to conquer, (b) removes the grounds for despair in the struggling, who are fighting their own constitution rather than a unique corruption, and (c) explains the entire apparatus of the religion as *therapy*: the prayer at fixed hours, the alms at fixed rates, the fast, the pilgrimage — a scaffold erected around a healing soul, exactly as a splint is erected around a setting bone. Islamic anthropology thereby steers between two Western errors the modern reader will recognize: not the doctrine of total depravity (the fitrah is sound, and the soul can be brought to rest — *"O soul at rest, return to thy Lord, content, contenting"* (89:27–28)), and not the doctrine of natural perfection (the soul *commands* to evil — *"and I do not acquit my soul; truly the soul commands to evil, save that on which my Lord has mercy"* (12:53)). Ibn al-Qayyim organizes the tradition's map of the soul into exactly these stations: the soul commanding evil (ammārah), the self-reproaching soul (lawwāmah), and the soul at rest (muṭmaʾinnah) — and the eight responsibilities of vv. 22–34 are the road from the first to the last.

**The Question Every Reader Must Ask at This Verse**

The verse is diagnostic, and its diagnostic power is undimmed after fourteen centuries. The honest reader does not ask "how did the Quraysh respond?" but "what does my own inner weather report say?" The hallmarks of halūʿ are easily self-administered as a test: restlessness when possessions are threatened; inability to enjoy a present blessing without scanning for the next; stinginess that arrives automatically with abundance; panic that arrives automatically with loss; the felt certainty, in every difficulty, that this time there will be no way through. The Qur'an's remedy begins not with a technique but with a relationship — the Lord of the ascending ways (v. 3) is a Lord who *rations* — and the entire passage that follows is His prescription, filled out item by item. The believer who prays the five prayers is, among other things, being given five daily appointments at which to surrender the day's accumulated anxiety; the one who pays the acknowledged due is being given a weekly and yearly practice of opening the closed hand; the one who fears his Lord (v. 27) is having his fears *re-objectified* — transferred from the stockpile to the One who owns all stockpiles and loves the giver. Anxiety is not eliminated in this scheme; it is *converted* — the Arabic tradition observed that the believer still fears, but he fears the One who is also merciful, and that single redirection changes the entire economy of the inner life.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:20

> **when evil befalls him, fretful;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jazūʿ: When the Rope of Patience Snaps**

Verse 20 supplies the first half of halūʿ's definition by experiment: *idhā massahu l-sharru jazūʿā* — "when evil touches him, fretful." The verb *massa* ("touches") is the Qur'an's understated verb for affliction — as though adversity needed only to *graze* the human being to trigger the collapse that follows; and *al-sharr* is adversity in its full range — illness, poverty, loss, fear — with al-Rāzī specifying illness and poverty as the central cases. The payoff word is *jazūʿ*, from the root j-z-ʿ whose concrete sense in classical Arabic is *to cut off* — jazʿ al-ḥabl is to cut a rope. The image inside the morphology is unforgettable: the afflicted man is one whose rope of endurance has been severed. He was holding on; adversity snipped the line; he plunges into wailing, blame, desperation. Al-Rāzī states the structural relationship explicitly: jazūʿ is the exact opposite of the patience commanded in verse 5 — the sūrah's first imperative (*fa-ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā*) and its central diagnosis (*jazūʿ*) are the two poles between which every human life is conducted.

**The Qur'an's Parallel Passages: The Same Experiment Repeated**

The Qur'an runs this same experiment on man several times, and the results are always consistent. The closest twin is in Sūrat Hūd:

> *"And if We give man a taste of mercy from Us, and then withdraw it from him — he is despairing, ungrateful. And if We make him taste favor after harm has touched him, he says: 'Ills have departed from me!' — and he is exultant, boastful — save those who are patient and do righteous deeds; for them is forgiveness and a great reward."* (Qur'an 11:9–11)

And in Fuṣṣilat, the longer study the source cites as this passage's twin:

> *"Man wearies not of praying for good; and if evil befalls him, he is despondent, despairing. And if We make him taste mercy from Us after hardship has befallen him, he will surely say: 'This is mine; I think not the Hour will come...'"* (Qur'an 41:49–50, in the longer passage 41:49–51)

Read the results carefully and a subtle asymmetry appears: in loss, man's reaction is *emotional* (panic, despair); in gain, it is *proprietarial* ("this is mine"). Verse 21 will name the second reaction; but the two are one system: the man who panics in loss is precisely the man who clutches in gain, because both reactions flow from the same underlying creed — that nothing lost will be replaced and nothing gained is a trust. The Qur'an's cure for the panic half is stated in its own vocabulary elsewhere:

> *"And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and fruits — but give glad tidings to the patient, who say, when affliction strikes them: 'We belong to God, and to Him we return.' Upon them are blessings and mercy from their Lord; and they are the rightly guided."* (Qur'an 2:155–157)

The formula of the patient is an ownership statement — *we belong to God* — and that is why it works: you cannot lose what was never yours; you can only return what was lent.

**Grief Is Not the Sin: What Jazūʿ Actually Condemns**

The commentary tradition is careful — and the modern reader must be equally careful — not to read verse 20 as a prohibition of sorrow. Jacob, the patriarch of beautiful patience, wept until his eyes whitened: *"and his eyes whitened from sorrow, and he was repressed"* (12:84), and God blamed him for neither the grief nor the tears; the Prophet ﷺ wept at the grave of Ibrāhīm, saying:

> *"The eye weeps, and the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord. We are grieved by your parting, O Ibrāhīm."* (al-Bukhārī 1303, from Anas — grief articulated inside perfect submission)

What jazūʿ condemns is not the tear but the *severance* — the snapping of the rope that tied the mourner to trust: the indictment that God has wronged him, the certainty that the blow is final, the public thrashing of despair, the abandonment of every duty that still stands. Patience, in the tradition's definition, is the *restraint of the tongue from complaint and the heart from panic and the limbs from disorder* — not the numbness of the face. Al-Rāzī's counsel, which the source preserves, remains the classical standard treatment: the circumstances that trigger fretting are *from God*; contentment with what comes from God is both the command and the cure; and the believer should expend his health and wealth in this world on the Hereafter's account, where neither panic nor clutching has any footing, since both are bets on a life that ends.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:21

> **and when good befalls him, begrudging,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Manūʿ: The Closed Hand in the Season of Openness**

Verse 21 completes the experiment and the diagnosis: *wa-idhā massahu l-khayru manūʿā* — "and when good touches him, begrudging (withholding)." Again the verb *massa* — the blessing, like the affliction, need only *touch* him; again the faʿūl-pattern noun of habitual character: *manūʿ*, from m-n-ʿ, to prevent, to withhold, to refuse — the man whose defining reflex, when good arrives, is to *stop it from flowing onward*. Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn specify the mechanism: when he is given wealth, he does not acknowledge the right that God and God's creatures hold in it, and so he expends none of it in charity. Al-Rāzī's caseload — health and wealth — completes the symmetry with verse 20: the same soul, touched by poverty, panics; touched by plenty, clamps shut. Verse 20 showed man a bad *loser*; verse 21 shows him a worse *winner*: ease does not soften him into generosity; it hardens him into custody.

**The Deep Structure: Why Anxiety and Stinginess Are One Vice**

The three verses 19–21 are not a list of three flaws but one flaw filmed from three angles — and the sūrah's ordering shows the causal chain. The root is halūʿ (v. 19): a baseline anxiety about not-enough. When the fear *materializes* (loss touches him), the anxiety detonates into jazūʿ (v. 20). When the fear *recedes* (good touches him), the anxiety does not leave — it merely changes tactics, and becomes the guard at the door of the storehouse: manūʿ (v. 21). The hoarder of v. 18 — *jamaʿa fa-awʿā* — turns out to be not a different sinner but the same halūʿ soul with money; and the Fire that called him (v. 17) calls, in effect, for the cure of vv. 22–35 refused. This unified picture is confirmed by the Qur'an's parallel in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt, where the withholding man is shown calculating to keep his treasure beyond death: *"And what you give in usury, that it may increase in the wealth of the people, increases not with God; but what you give in zakāh, seeking the Face of God — it is they whose recompense is multiplied"* (30:39). The giving cure is prescribed because the disease is withholding; the two are made for each other, and the exception that begins in the next verse — *illā l-muṣallīn*, "save those who pray" — is the hinge on which the entire sūrah turns from diagnosis to treatment.

**Begrudging God's Credit and Man's Due**

The classical commentators heard a second register in *manūʿ*, recorded in the tafsīr of Ibn Kathīr and al-Jalālayn: not only withholding the poor man's due, but withholding *God's* due — the acknowledgment that the blessing came from Him at all. The parallel passage in Fuṣṣilat exposes the inner monologue: *"he will surely say: 'This is mine'"* (41:50) — the two-word deed of theft by which man transfers title from the Giver to himself. Against this the Qur'an legislates gratitude as an *act*, not a mood: *"Work, O family of David, in gratitude — and few of My servants are grateful"* (34:13); and the Prophet ﷺ taught gratitude's most practical form:

> *"Look to those below you, and not to those above you; it is more likely that you will not belittle God's blessing upon you."* (al-Bukhārī 6490; Muslim 2963, from Abū Hurayrah — the anti-hedonic-treadmill instruction, given fourteen centuries before the phrase)

And when blessing flows, the tradition's counsel is not merely to feel thankful but to *open the channel*: the Prophet ﷺ said, *"There is no day upon which the servant rises but two angels descend: one of them says, 'O God, give increase to him who spends in charity'; and the other says, 'O God, give ruin to him who withholds'"* (al-Bukhārī 1442; Muslim 1010). The economic theology could not be plainer: circulation is blessed at the root, and retention is cursed at the root — for the withholding hand is cutting off the very circulation it depends on. The believer's whole training — the zakāh's fixed percentage, the ṣadaqah's open-endedness, the festival alms that end the fast, the prohibition of hoarding measured in the jurists' rule that zakāh is due on *growing* stored wealth precisely to punish the container — exists because man was created halūʿ, and only practiced generosity retrains the reflex. What therapy is to a seized muscle, giving is to the closed hand.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:22

> **save those who perform prayer,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Illā: The Word That Splits the Human Race in Two**

With a single word — *illā*, "save" — the sūrah splits its diagnosis down the middle and opens its treatment plan. Verses 19–21 had pronounced man halūʿ: anxious by creation, fretful in loss, withholding in gain. Verse 22 carves out the exception, and the carving is the sermon: the description is universal, but the destiny is optional. Al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī read the grammar exactly this way — if only disbelievers were meant, there would be no need of an exception; the *illā* proves that prayerful souls are humans who have been *cured*, not different creatures by nature. This is Islam's anthropology in miniature: the baseline is frail, the remedy is prescribed, and the remedy is *practicable* — otherwise the exception would be an empty word. And the first mark of the excepted is the one act that defines Islam's daily shape: *illā l-muṣallīn* — "save those who pray."

**Why Prayer Is the First Prescription for Anxiety**

The order of the treatment plan is itself doctrine. The disease was described as a broken supply-line of trust: the anxious soul believes it is unprovisioned, unguarded, and alone, and therefore panics in loss and clutches in gain. The first prescription is not an argument but a *practice*: five appointments a day at the throne of the Provider. Ṣalāh, at this structural level, is trust rendered in body-language — the standing, bowing, prostrating vocabulary of a servant who reports for duty, listens, and petitions; and it is scheduled precisely at the hours when supply-anxiety re-forms: at dawn, when the whole ungoverned day looms; at midday, when the day's competition is raging; in the afternoon, when fatigue erodes resolve; at dusk, when the day's ledger is tallied; at night, when the unquiet mind rehearses its fears. The commentators' rule of thumb is al-Rāzī's: the circumstances that produce fretting are from God; contentment is commanded; and the prayer is where contentment is *learned by repetition* — drilled into the nervous system, not merely believed in the abstract.

**Ṣalāh: The Word That Means Both Supplication and Following**

The lexicographers, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī among them, preserve a beautiful ambiguity in the root ṣ-l-w. Its oldest sense is *invocation* — the Qur'an uses it so even for God's action toward the believers: *"God and His angels pray upon (yuṣallūna ʿalā) the Prophet"* (33:56) — and the Arabs also called the foal that runs close behind its mother *muṣallā*, so that the word carries both *calling upon God* and *following close behind*. Both senses meet in the believer's prayer: he calls upon his Lord and he keeps pace behind Him. The Qur'an's other great marking of prayer's rank is the story of its institution: it is the only command of the Sharīʿah, the scholars note, that was ordained *above the seven heavens* — on the night journey and ascension, whose connection to this sūrah's Lord of the ascending ways (v. 3) has already been observed — when the five prayers were gifted to the Prophet ﷺ in the direct presence of his Lord:

> *"...then the prayers were enjoined upon me: fifty prayers in a day and a night... I returned until I passed by Moses... he said: Go back to your Lord and ask reduction... until He said: 'Muhammad, they are five in number and fifty in reward, for the word is not changed with Me.'"* (al-Bukhārī 349; Muslim 162, from the long account of the Night Journey transmitted by Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿah — mercy folded into command: five to perform, fifty on the account)

**The First Question of Hell's Inmates**

The seriousness of being among the *muṣallīn* — of praying at all — is fixed by the Qur'an's own resurrection scene in which the people of Saqar are asked what brought them there. Their first answer is not murder, theft, or idolatry; it is this:

> *"They will say: 'We were not of those who prayed, and we did not feed the poor, and we waded with those who wade, and we denied the Day of Judgment, until certainty came to us.'"* (Qur'an 74:43–47)

Notice how closely Saqar's self-indictment follows the structure of our sūrah's exceptions: prayer first, feeding the poor second, idle discourse, and denial of the Day — almost a table of contents of vv. 22–34. The reversal works in both directions and should be read both ways: the man who abandons the prayer should hear vv. 22–35 as a roll call of everything he is currently forfeiting; and the believer who struggles with his prayers — tired, distracted, late — should hear that the sūrah's word for the excepted is simply *muṣallīn*, "those who pray": present tense, habitual participle, no minimum score of excellence stated at the door. The door's question is whether one prays; the excellence is what a lifetime inside the door slowly builds.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:23

> **who are constant in their prayers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dāʾimūn: The Adverb God Chose Instead of "Perfect"**

Verse 23 does not say the excepted are those who pray *beautifully*; it says they are *"ʿalā ṣalātihim dāʾimūn"* — "upon their prayers, constant." The preposition ʿalā paints the prayer as ground beneath the feet — a station held, not an event attended; and *dāʾimūn* means continual, persistent, kept-up. Al-Ṭabarī glosses: those who maintain its times and its rites, not neglecting any of it; some, he reports, took the stringent sense — that they are constant even in the hours between prayers, never leaving the prayer's state of heart; Ibn ʿAjībah preserves this as the prayer of the heart maintained at all times, the outward prayer becoming the ignition of an inward continuity. Al-Ṭabarī also records the simple reading: those who perform all five daily prayers at their appointed times. The spectrum is the lesson: from the entry level (all five, on time) to the summit (a heart that never fully stands up from the last prostration), the quality that God names and rewards is not splendor but *constancy* — the unglamorous, resume-proof, decade-after-decade showing up.

**Constancy Over Intensity: A Divine Pedagogy**

The Qur'an's choice of constancy as the praised quality is a deliberate piece of spiritual pedagogy, and the Prophet ﷺ made it explicit:

> *"The deeds most beloved to God are the most constant, even if they are few."* (al-Bukhārī 6464, from ʿĀʾishah; Muslim 783 — the divine preference for the sustainable over the spectacular)

The wisdom is psychological as much as spiritual. Intensity is a mood; constancy is a structure. The believer who prays passionately for a week and then vanishes for a month has treated the prayer as an experience; the one who prays adequately every day for fifty years has treated it as a *relationship* — and relationships, not experiences, are what re-form the soul. Modern habit science would recognize the pattern: identity follows repeated behavior; the five daily prayers, fixed to the movement of the sun rather than to the movement of enthusiasm, build the believer's identity at the exact rhythm at which the nervous system consolidates learning. The Qur'an states the scheduling principle as law: *"the prayer is enjoined upon the believers at stated times"* (4:103); and it frames the whole life of prayer as preservation: *"Be watchful over the prayers, and the middle prayer, and stand up for God in devotion"* (2:238).

**The Frame of the Sūrah: Beginning and Ending at the Prayer**

The structural note that the classical commentators relished: this long catalogue of the excepted ones' marks (vv. 22–35) *begins* with prayer (vv. 22–23) and *ends* with prayer (v. 34, "mindful of their prayers"). Whatever stands between — the acknowledged due, the affirmation of the Day, the fear, the chastity, the trusts, the testimony — is bookended by ṣalāh, as a wall is bookended by its pillars. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī read the design as a statement of causality: the prayer is not merely first in the list but the *generator* of the list. The one who stands before God five times a day is being continuously re-informed of the Day of Recompense (v. 26), re-frightened and re-comforted concerning his Lord (vv. 27–28), re-reminded that his wealth has claimants (vv. 24–25), and re-armed against the lusts and betrayals that dismantle character (vv. 29–33). The ṣadaqah flows from the prayer's accounting; the modesty flows from the prayer's audience; the trustworthiness flows from the prayer's Book. This is why the Prophet ﷺ fixed the prayer's rank in the famous five upon which Islam is built — *"the testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is the Messenger of God, and the performance of the prayer, and the giving of the zakāh, and the pilgrimage, and the fast of Ramaḍān"* (al-Bukhārī 8; Muslim 16, from Ibn ʿUmar) — and it is why the believer's private rule of thumb has always been the one the Companions handed down: *guard the prayer, and everything else in the life begins to guard itself*.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:24

> **and in whose wealth is an acknowledged due**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Right Embedded in the Wealth Itself**

Verse 24 states the second mark of the excepted with the precision of a legal instrument: *wa-alladhīna fī amwālihim ḥaqqun maʿlūm* — "and those in whose wealth is an acknowledged due." The wording deserves the closest reading, for the whole Islamic economics of giving rests on it. Not: those who *give* from their wealth — as a lord dispenses favors — but those *in whose wealth there is* a due. The due pre-exists the giving; it is constitutive of the wealth, not subsequent to it. The Arabic *ḥaqq* means a right, a claim, an entitlement — the same word the Qur'an uses for God's own claims upon man; and *maʿlūm*, "known," means the right is *defined*: identified, fixed, not left to the spontaneous mood of the holder. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī all record the juristic reading: the acknowledged due is first the zakāh, whose rate the Law fixes precisely so that the poor man's share is *known* — an actual percentage owned by its recipients, not a discretionary gratuity; and beyond it, some of the scholars add, the supererogatory ṣadaqah, known to God, by which a man keeps his surplus perpetually leaking toward need. The verse's syntax makes the theological statement: the wealth is God's trust and the poor are co-beneficiaries named in the deed:

> *"And spend of that whereof He has made you trustees."* (Qur'an 57:7)

> *"Take from their wealth a charity to purify them and cleanse them thereby; and pray upon them; truly thy prayer is a tranquility for them."* (Qur'an 9:103)

**From Verse 18 to Verse 24: The Container Opened**

The verse is the exact mirror image of the Fire's clientele. Verse 18 damned *jamaʿa fa-awʿā* — the one who amassed and contained; verse 24 praises the one in whose wealth there is, by design, an open valve. Al-Qurṭubī reads the two verses as cause and cure: the hoarder is the man who denied that any such right existed; the excepted one is the man who has written the right into the deed of every holding. And the Qur'an's parallel passage in Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt — which the source notes repeats these words almost verbatim — places the due inside its larger portrait of the saved:

> *"Indeed, the pious will be among Gardens and springs, taking what their Lord has given them; for they were before that doers of good: they used to sleep but little of the night, and in the pre-dawn they would ask forgiveness, and in their wealth was a right for the beggar and the deprived."* (Qur'an 51:15–19)

Note what the passage joins: worship in the night, dawn repentance, and the wealth-with-a-right. The tradition read this clustering as deliberate: the prayer at night produces the open ledger by day; a man who converses with the Owner of wealth cannot long pretend he is its sole owner by morning.

**The Dignity of a Right Versus the Condescension of a Favor**

There is a civilizational difference between alms-as-favor and alms-as-right, and the Qur'an deliberately chose the second. When the poor hold a *right*, their claim is legal and their dignity is intact: the zakāh-collector in the Prophet's Medina was not a beggar's visitor but an officer of a public due; the one who withholds is not merely ungenerous but *delinquent*, and in the first Caliph's famous campaign the withholding of zakāh was treated as rebellion against the law itself. The Prophet ﷺ personalizes the transaction in the most tender divine voice:

> *"The Exalted God says: O son of Adam, spend, and I shall spend upon you."* (Muslim 993, from Abū Hurayrah — the infinite solvency of the Creditor who commands the loan)

> *"Every joint of the body owes a charity each day: helping a man mount his beast or hoist his baggage is a charity; a good word is a charity; every step toward the mosque for prayer is a charity; and removing harm from the road is a charity."* (al-Bukhārī 2707, from Buraydah — the same 'right' logic extended from wealth to the whole body: limbs are trusts, and their use is audited)

For the modern reader, verse 24 stands quietly against both the cynicism that says the poor deserve nothing and the paternalism that says they deserve only what escapes the rich man's whim. It legislates a third thing: a floor of guaranteed claim beneath every surplus — the skeleton of a welfare principle stated in one Arabic clause, fourteen centuries before any social contract theorist framed it, and grounded not in the state's monopoly of force but in the ownership of God.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:25

> **for the beggar and the deprived,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Categories, So That No Need Is Missed**

Verse 25 names the right-holders of verse 24, and its two terms are constructed to leave no channel of need unaddressed: *lil-sāʾili wa-l-maḥrūm* — "for the one who asks, and the one who is deprived." Al-Rāzī and the commentators align the pair against the two ways poverty presents itself. The *sāʾil* is need that makes itself audible: the beggar, the petitioner, the one at the door or on the road with a request. The *maḥrūm* — from the root ḥ-r-m, to be denied, to be barred — is need that is *silent*: the one deprived of his share, whether by circumstance, by bashfulness, or by dignity; the man who does not ask, and whom the world therefore does not notice. The design of the verse is a safety net with two meshes: one catches those who fall loudly; the other must catch those who fall without a sound. The Qur'an draws the silent poor in the verses that every jurist cites beside this one:

> *"(Charity is) for the poor who are restrained in the way of God, unable to travel in the land; the ignorant suppose them wealthy because of their abstinence; you will know them by their mark: they do not importune the people. Whatever you spend of good — God knows it."* (Qur'an 2:273)

**The Miskīn Who Is Not the Beggar**

The Prophet ﷺ was asked to define the poor, and his answer — preserved in the Ṣaḥīḥ — draws precisely the sāʾil/maḥrūm distinction of our verse:

> *"'The poor (miskīn) is not the one who goes about the people, satisfied with a morsel or two, or a date or two.' Rather, the poor is the one who does not find enough to suffice him, and so is not noticed as being in need, and does not stand up and beg the people."* (al-Bukhārī 4539, from Abū Hurayrah — the silent poor named as the truer case)

The moral instruction buried in the definition is aimed at the giver: do not wait for the request; investigate the silence. Islamic law operationalized this in the category of zakāh recipients who receive *without asking* — the collector may seek them out — and Islamic ethics operationalized it in the praise of giving quietly, by which the maḥrūm is reached without exposing him. The same hadith literature that dignifies the silent poor also disciplines the professional asker, lest the sāʾil's channel be clogged by those who do not need:

> *"Whoever begs from people while possessing what suffices him, his begging will come on the Day of Resurrection as scratches upon his face... Whoever begs while possessing what suffices him, he is only gathering for himself Hell-fire... So let whoever wishes gather little, and whoever wishes gather much."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Zakāh, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar — the discipline on needless asking, delivered with the Prophet's characteristic closing: the choice is left to the hearer's own responsibility)

The equilibrium the tradition reached: the beggar is owed a response, for his asking is the system working; the deprived are owed a search, for their silence is the system's blind spot; and the self-sufficient are owed the truth that asking without need is a self-harm the Law does not honor.

**Asking and Denial in the Divine Economy**

For the believer, the pair sāʾil/maḥrūm turns back upon himself as a lesson in how to be a creature. Man the halūʿ of verse 19 is a compulsive asker when evil touches him and a denier of others when good touches him. The excepted one inverts both faults: he is restrained in begging from creatures precisely because he begs everything from the Creator — *"And your Lord has said: Call upon Me; I will answer you"* (40:60) — and he is generous in giving to creatures precisely because he received everything as a trust, not a possession. In this inversion, the two categories of verse 25 become a mirror: *be the maḥrūm before God* (poor in yourself, dignified before men, asking Him alone), *and be the provision of the maḥrūm before men* (searching out the silent need your own silence once resembled). The Qur'an's promise to such a one closes the loop of the whole passage: *"Whatever thing you spend, He replaces it; and He is the best of providers"* (34:39).

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:26

> **those who affirm the Day of Judgment,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yuṣaddiqūn: Belief as Verification**

Verse 26 names the third mark of the excepted: *wa-alladhīna yuṣaddiqūna bi-yawmi l-dīn* — "and those who affirm as true the Day of Recompense." The verb is instructive: *yuṣaddiqūn* is the second-form intensive of the root ṣ-d-q — the root of truth (ṣidq) and sincerity. First form, it would mean simply "to believe"; second form, it means to *confirm, verify, credit as true* — the verb of a man who has examined a report and countersigned it. Islamic belief in the Last Day is not, in this verse's grammar, a vague hope that things end well; it is a *verdict the soul has rendered* on the most consequential claim in revelation: that history has an audit. The term *yawm al-dīn* — the Day of the dīn — is the Fātiḥah's own name for the Day: *"Master of the Day of Recompense"* (1:4): *dīn* meaning at once religion, judgment, and repayment of debt — the Day on which every loan of life is called in and every religion's truth is settled by results. The Qur'an gives the Day this name and then, as if knowing the term needs a definition no mortal can supply, answers with a doubled question:

> *"And what shall convey unto thee what the Day of Recompense is? Again — what shall convey unto thee what the Day of Recompense is? The Day when no soul shall have power over any soul whatever; and the command that Day belongs to God."* (Qur'an 82:17–19)

**The Logical Spine of the List: Why the Day Stands Third**

The placement of this verse inside the list is not decorative; it is the list's engine. Prayer (vv. 22–23) establishes the line of command; the acknowledged due (vv. 24–25) establishes the economy; the affirmed Day (v. 26) supplies the *motive power* for both — for who prays five times a day for decades, and pays a fixed percentage of every surplus forever, unless he is settled in the conviction that the account is real? The Qur'an's own great definitional verse builds righteousness on exactly this three-part foundation:

> *"Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west; but righteousness is he who believes in God, and the Last Day, and the angels, and the Book, and the prophets; and gives wealth, out of love for Him, to kin, and orphans, and the poor, and the traveler, and the beggar, and for freeing necks..."* (Qur'an 2:177)

Belief in the Last Day and the spending of wealth are conjoined in the Qur'an with the regularity of cause and effect — because, as verse 21 diagnosed, the withholding hand is governed by its eschatology: *"thinking that his wealth makes him immortal"* (104:3). The man who believes death is the end of accounting builds containers (v. 18); the man who believes death is the beginning of accounting builds channels. Al-Rāzī renders the psychology plainly: to affirm the Day is to live focused upon the Hereafter rather than this world — and focus is destiny, since a life aimed at an audit becomes a life of preparation, exactly as a life aimed at appetite becomes a life of consumption.

**Behavior Under Audit: The Verse's Modern Verifiability**

The verse's claim about human nature can be tested by every reader without leaving the desk: people behave differently when they believe they are observed and their actions will be reviewed. Accounting changes behavior in offices, laboratories, traffic, and sports; the mere presence of a camera reroutes a day's small decisions. The Qur'an's teaching is that the moral geometry of the universe is *already* under a perfect camera — *"And every small or great thing is inscribed"* (54:53); *"No word does he utter but that with him is a watcher, ready"* (50:18) — and the believer's answer to the question "would you do this if it were on the record?" is always: *it is*. This internalized audit, classical ethics observed, is more efficient than any external one: the policeman inside the heart never sleeps, never takes bribes, and cannot be voted out. And it is more humane: it punishes no one, restrains everyone, and motivates not only avoidance of wrong but pursuit of right, since the same audit registers every kindness: *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7). The affirmation of the Day is thus not escapism — the frequent modern slander — but the opposite: it is the refusal to let any act, word, or hidden intention be lost, asserted by a Book that stakes its entire authority on the claim that nothing is.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:27

> **and who are wary of the punishment of their Lord—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mutawajjisūn: The Trembling That Keeps the Soul Awake**

Verse 27 names the fourth mark of the excepted — or, as the source notes, the deepening of the third: *wa-alladhīna hum min ʿadhābi rabbihim mutawajjisūn* — "and those who are wary of the punishment of their Lord." The word is rare in the Qur'an — appearing only here and in Āl ʿImrān's portrait of the praying believers — and its root, w-j-s, denotes a dread that moves: inner agitation, unease, the tremor of apprehension in the chest. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr gloss it as fear combined with watchfulness — not the terror of one who expects to be destroyed, but the keyed-up vigilance of one who knows the stakes are real. It is the emotional opposite of the mockers of vv. 6–7, who saw the punishment "far off"; the mutawajjis sees it near, because — as verse 4 taught — God sees it near, and the believer has aligned his sight with God's. And the object of the fear is specified with tenderness within the severity: the punishment of *their Lord* — *rabbihim*, the same Name of nurturing lordship under which the sūrah's cure is administered. Islamic theology never lets fear float free of relationship: the child fears the father's discipline precisely because the child knows the father's love; the fear of punishment and the trust in the Punisher's mercy are held in one breast, and the tradition's classics — Ibn al-Qayyim among them, in his stations of the heart — describe fear (khawf) and hope (rajāʾ) as the two wings by which the soul flies, and warn that either wing alone grounds the bird.

**The Fear That Is Promised Reward**

The Qur'an systematically attaches reward to this fear — which is the proof that the fear itself is an act of faith, not a failure of it:

> *"Truly for those who fear their Lord unseen, for them is forgiveness and a great reward."* (Qur'an 67:12; cf. 13:21; 16:50; 21:49; 39:23; 98:8)

Read the logic of 67:12 carefully: the fear is exercised *unseen* — *bi-l-ghayb* — in the hours and places where no human enforcement exists. That is exactly where its moral content shows: the one who refrains from wrongdoing only when watched is governed by creatures; the one who refrains when unwatched is governed by his Lord. The Prophet ﷺ gave the inner register of this state its most haunting formulation:

> *"I see what you see not, and I hear what you hear not. The heaven creaked, and it had cause to creak: there is no spot the breadth of four fingers but an angel has prostrated his forehead upon it. By God, if you knew what I know, you would weep much and laugh little."* (transmitted by Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah from Abū Dharr, graded ṣaḥīḥ — the Prophet's disclosure of the unseen density of the divine presence that the mutawajjis lives before)

**Fear of the Lord Versus Fear of Men**

The practical yield of this verse is a re-ordering of the fears by which everyone lives. The unhealed soul fears creatures — the boss, the market, the opinion of neighbors — and the Qur'an diagnoses that misplaced fear as the root of cowardice before truth: *"It is only Satan who frightens you of his partisans; so fear them not, and fear Me, if you are believers"* (3:175). The healed soul fears the One whose displeasure cannot be managed, and discovers a paradox the tradition has always taught: the one who fears God alone ceases to fear anything else. The early Muslims acted on this openly — when the Quraysh threatened ʿUmar's conversion? The better-attested instance is the Prophet ﷺ saying to the dying Abū Ṭālib what he said to the chiefs of Quraysh: *"By God, O my uncle, if they placed the sun in my right hand and the moon in my left to abandon this affair, I would not abandon it until God makes it prevail or I perish"* — the fear of God dissolving every negotiable fear at once. The believer's daily examination therefore asks not "am I afraid?" — everyone is afraid of something — but *"of whom?"* — and the answer is the whole difference between the cramped life and the free one.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:28

> **truly there is no security from the punishment of their Lord,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghayr Maʾmūn: The Doctrine Stated as a Negative**

Verse 28 states the grounds of the wariness: *inna ʿadhāba rabbihim ghayru maʾmūn* — "truly the punishment of their Lord is not a thing to feel secure against." The word *maʾmūn* is built on the root a-m-n — safety, security, amnesty — the same root as *īmān* (faith) and *amān* (a pledge of safety). The verse's negative grammar is doing theology: it does not say the punishment is *imminent* or *inevitable* for believers — God forbid; it says the punishment may never be filed under *impossible*. The believer holds this position as a matter of principle: since my Lord is just and I am a fallible servant under audit, no arithmetic of my own deeds can ever generate a certificate of immunity. The early generations weaponized this verse against the first doctrinal laxity that threatened the community — the Murjiʾah, who held that once faith is professed, sin cannot harm and fear is unnecessary; Ibn Taymiyyah, in his Kitāb al-Īmān, marshals this class of verses to establish that reverent fear is not an ornament added to faith but a component of it: the believer is precisely the one who *cannot* feel safe. The sūrah's own mockers had felt the opposite security — they saw the Day as far (v. 6) — and the Qur'an names that counterfeit peace elsewhere as the signature of ruin:

> *"Do they feel secure from God's plan? None feels secure from God's plan but the people who lose."* (Qur'an 7:99; cf. 7:97–98)

**Two Kinds of Security, Only One of Them True**

The apparent tension — does the believer feel safe or not? — resolves the moment the object of security is specified. The Qur'an simultaneously forbids security *from punishment* (our verse) and promises security *in God*: *"Truly the friends of God — no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve"* (10:62). The forbidden security is the *self-issued* kind: the man who audits himself, grades himself leniently, and files the case closed — against which the Qur'an rules: *"Nay — whoever earns evil, and his sin encompasses him — those are the companions of the Fire, abiding therein"* (2:81); *"It is not your desires, nor the desires of the People of the Book: whoever works evil will be recompensed for it, and he will find for himself, besides God, no protector nor helper"* (4:123). The promised security is *God-issued*: given at the end of the race, to those who ran it fearful — *"Enter it in peace; that is the Day of Abiding Life"* (50:34). The tradition put it in a sentence: fear on the road, safety at the door. The Prophet ﷺ exemplified the ratio — no one taught mercy more tenderly, and no one wept in prayer more heavily — and his community inherited the balance as method: the believer repents *as if* his account were dire, and hopes *as if* his Lord were everything the Most-Merciful's names promise; and the two never meet in the middle, but hold the soul taut between them like the strings of an instrument.

**The Safety That Kills Vigilance**

The verse also encodes a law of human performance that every field of skill confirms: competence decays exactly as felt security rises. The driver who has gone ten years without checking his blind spot no longer sees it; the surgeon who has never had a complication stops running the checklist; the organization that has never been breached stops patching. Safety is not a state that protects; it is a state that disarms. Taqwā — the God-wariness this sūrah's whole cure-rest depends on — is the spiritual discipline of never letting felt security disband the guard. The Qur'an's formula for keeping the watch alive is remembrance of the audit, and it plants the reminder in the very liturgy of the Garden-seekers:

> *"And those who say: 'Our Lord, avert from us the punishment of Hell; truly its punishment is an ever-clinging grief — truly it is an evil abode and resting-place.'"* (Qur'an 25:65–66; cf. 25:63–77, the portrait of the servants of the Merciful — praying against a punishment they already fled, and thereby never ceasing to flee it)

The mutawajjis of verse 27, then, is not a neurotic but a realist: he knows what the heedless forget — that the certificate of immunity does not exist this side of the grave — and his very insecurity is what keeps him inside the eight marks of the excepted, from the prayer of v. 22 to the mindfulness of v. 34, all the way into the Gardens, honored.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:29

> **those who guard their private parts,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Furūj: The Openings That Must Be Guarded**

Verse 29 names the fifth mark of the excepted: *wa-alladhīna hum li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn* — "and those who guard their private parts." The Arabic rewards inspection. A *farj* (plural *furūj*) is literally an *opening*, a gap, a breach in a wall; the word was applied to the private parts because they are the body's openings in that region — but the choice of this word over any anatomical term makes the moral point before the moral point is made: these are *breaches*, and breaches are exactly what a wall's owner must guard. And the verb is *ḥāfiẓūn* — from ḥ-f-ẓ, to preserve, to keep, to stand guard — the very verb the Qur'an uses for its own protection of revelation: *"We have sent down the Reminder, and We of a surety are its Guardians"* (15:9), and the verb it will use of the excepted ones' prayer at v. 34. The resonance is deliberate: the believer stands over his own openings with the same seriousness God stands over His Book. Nothing unguarded gets through; nothing that gets through is unrecorded.

**Guarding Starts at the Eye, Not at the Body**

The source rightly points the reader to the Qur'an's fullest treatment of this duty, the paired verses of Sūrat al-Nūr, where the guard duty is assigned to the eyes first:

> *"Say to the believing men to lower their gaze and guard their private parts; that is purer for them. Truly God is aware of what they fashion. And say to the believing women to lower their gaze and guard their private parts..."* (Qur'an 24:30–31)

The order of operations is the wisdom: the body's fall begins at the retina; the glance admitted becomes the thought, the thought the approach, the approach the act — which is why the Qur'an's command about adultery is framed not as "do not commit" but as "do not *approach*":

> *"And approach not zina; truly it is indecency and an evil path."* (Qur'an 17:32)

The fence stands at a distance from the cliff. The Prophet ﷺ taught the eye's discipline to his cousin and son-in-law in words the tradition memorized:

> *"O ʿAlī, do not follow one glance with another; you may have the first, but not the second."* (Abū Dāwūd 2149; al-Tirmidhī 2777, from ʿAlī — the glance's traffic-light: green once, then red)

And he ﷺ bound the whole duty — tongue and loins — into the single guarantee that became one of the most repeated sayings of his Sunnah:

> *"Whoever guarantees me what is between his two jaw-bones and what is between his two legs, I guarantee him the Garden."* (al-Bukhārī 6807, from Sahl ibn Saʿd — two gates, one promise)

**Why the Qur'an Treats Sexual Integrity as a Load-Bearing Wall**

The sūrah's list is a architecture: prayer (the God-axis), wealth (the neighbor-axis), the affirmed Day (the time-axis), fear (the motive) — and now chastity, the *trust-axis* of society. A moment's reflection shows why the wall matters socially: sexual anarchy quietly destroys every covenant it touches — marriages, lineages, children's paternity, the trust between households, the safety of the vulnerable. The Qur'an prices it accordingly, in the passage where the servants of the Merciful are listed and three sins stand together at the top of the list:

> *"And those who do not call upon another god with God, and do not kill the soul that God has sanctified, save by right, and do not commit zina — for whoever does that shall meet the penalty: doubled for him is the punishment on the Day of Resurrection, and he abides humiliated therein — save whoever repents and believes and works righteous deeds..."* (Qur'an 25:68–70)

The modern reader lives inside the experiment the verse legislates against: an attention economy that monetizes the farj's appetite, screens that deliver unlimited provocation into every pocket, and a culture that has largely removed the fence of 17:32 to see what happens. The results — broken homes, loneliness epidemics, collapsed trust between the sexes, children raised in fractured homes — are reported daily by the very secular sciences that dismantled the fence. The Qur'an's discipline is not, as it is often caricatured, hostility to desire; it is the *containment* of a fire that cooks in the hearth and burns down the house in the open — a point verse 30 will now make explicit.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:30

> **save from their spouses or those whom their right hands possess, for then they are not blameworthy;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Reveals the Qur'an's Theory of Desire**

Verse 30 draws the boundary and immediately opens its lawful gate: *illā ʿalā azwājihim aw mā malakat aymānuhum* — "save with their spouses, or those whom their right hands possess; for then they are not blameworthy." Three teachings ride in one line. First: the exception proves the rule's *intent* — the Qur'an is not against sexual desire but against its anarchy; the existence of a lawful channel shows that the channel is the point. Second: the channel is *marriage* — *azwāj*, spouses, the covenant bond the Qur'an defines with its twin pillars of affection and mercy (30:21). Third: the clause closes with an absolution — *fa-innahum ghayru malūmīn*, "they are not blameworthy" — which quietly rebukes every religion of guilt that has ever hovered over the marriage bed: within God's boundary, the act itself is *not* merely tolerated but blessed, and the spouses owe no apology for it. The Prophet ﷺ taught that even the lawful satisfaction carries reward:

> *"And in the private part of one of you there is a charity."* — he was asked: "O Messenger of God, would one of us satisfy his desire and be rewarded?" He said: *"Do you not see that if he placed it in the unlawful he would bear its burden? So if he placed it in the lawful he would have its reward."* (Muslim 1006, from Abū Dharr — the astonishing economy of grace: appetite, channeled, becomes devotion)

**"What Their Right Hands Possess": Reading a Historical Clause Honestly**

The second category — *mā malakat aymānuhum*, "what their right hands possess" — refers, as the source notes (directing to 4:3 and 4:25), to slave women with whom a master had a conjugal relationship. The verse — repeated verbatim from 23:5–7 — regulated the ancient universal institution of slavery as it existed in seventh-century Arabia and the whole surrounding world: prisoners of war were then, across every civilization, taken into servitude, and the Qur'an addressed that reality while simultaneously erecting the institution's dismantling: manumission became one of the most praised acts of piety — *"and the freeing of a neck"* is listed by God among the steep paths of gratitude (90:12–13); freeing slaves became the required expiation for several sins; the law of *mukātabah* obliged masters to fund the slave's self-purchase contract (24:33); and the Qur'an's summation of righteousness includes *"the giving of wealth... for the freeing of necks"* (2:177). The classical commentators read 70:29–31 as both a boundary and a bridge: a boundary against all intimacy outside covenant and ownership-law as then known, and a bridge in a world moving — by legislation, incentive, and moral suasion — toward the abolition of the entire category. The modern reader should read the clause as history honestly handled: revelation descending into a world whose institutions it regulated in order, over time, to transform; the final state of the sharīʿah's own trajectory is the one the Prophet ﷺ personally modeled — his household freed its bondsmen, and his standing counsel was the full and kind provision of those in one's care:

> *"They are your brothers, whom God has placed under your hands. So whoever has his brother under his hand, let him feed him of what he eats, and clothe him of what he wears. Do not charge them with what overcomes them; and if you charge them, then help them."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Īmān, from Abū Dharr)

**The Household the Verse Protects**

Lest the exception read as mere permission, it should be read beside the Qur'an's portrait of what marriage *is*: *"They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them"* (2:187) — mutual covering, dignity, and rest; and beside the Prophet's ﷺ household ethics: *"The best of you is the best to his family, and I am the best of you to my family."* (al-Tirmidhī 3895, from ʿĀʾishah, graded ṣaḥīḥ) The verse's real subject is therefore not liberty but *fidelity*: the excepted one is the one whose desire has exactly one address — his covenant — which is why the following verse's verdict on everything else is a single, unhesitating word: transgressors.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:31

> **but whosoever seeks beyond that, they are the transgressors;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Warāʾa Dhālika: The Line Drawn One Step Past the Lawful**

Verse 31 completes the fifth mark with the verdict that gives the boundary its edge: *fa-mani btaghā warāʾa dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu l-ʿādūn* — "but whoever seeks beyond that — those, they are the transgressors." Every word is measured. *Ibtaghā* — "seeks, pursues" — from the root of desire and pursuit (b-gh-w): the verb includes the *search*, not only the finding; the browsing, not only the purchase. *Warāʾa dhālika* — "beyond that" — points to the territory immediately past the boundary just drawn: one step beyond spouse and covenant lies the entire forbidden world, undifferentiated in the verse's verdict, whether the step is taken with the body, the eye, or the imagination. And the verdict is a noun of identity, not an incidental act: *al-ʿādūn* — "the transgressors," from ʿ-d-w, to cross over, to overrun a limit: the same root that names an enemy's incursion across a border. The Qur'an thus defines sexual transgression in spatial terms: God drew a border; the border is real; the one who crosses it has not "explored his sexuality" — he has invaded territory whose Owner forbade the crossing.

**The Verse's Genius: One Step, No Gray Zones**

What is quietly radical about the verse is its refusal to negotiate the gray zone. Human ethical systems tend to drift toward the boundary and argue from the border's edge: it is only a look; only a message; only this once; no one is harmed; we are adults. The verse's structure allows no gradient: on one side, "not blameworthy" (v. 30); on the other side, "transgressors" (v. 31); and the frontier between them is a single line one step wide. This is the same legislative style as 17:32 — "approach not" — and the Prophet ﷺ embodied it when a young man asked him for permission to commit the sin itself. He did not shame him; he walked him through the reversal:

> *"He said: O Messenger of God, grant me permission for zina. The people set upon him rebuking him, but the Prophet drew near and said: Come here. 'Would you like it for your mother?' He said: No, by God — may I be your ransom. 'Nor for your daughter, nor your sister, nor your aunt?' Each time: No, by God. He said: 'So likewise, people do not like it for their own.' Then he placed his hand upon him and prayed: 'O God, forgive his sin, purify his heart, and guard his private parts.'"* (transmitted by Aḥmad from Abū Umāmah, graded ḥasan — the boundary taught not as repression but as the golden rule universalized: the desire you would refuse for your mother's honor, refuse for another's daughter)

**Transgression's Escalation and the Mercy of the Wall**

The boundary ethic of vv. 29–31 also encodes a psychology the modern world has now documented from the other side: appetite is not a fixed quantity but an escalating appetite — each transgression raises the threshold for the next (what researchers call tolerance), and the frontier keeps moving inward: the forbidden becomes routine, the routine becomes boring, and the search — *ibtaghā* — moves to new edges. The Qur'an's wall interrupts the escalation at the first step, which is why its placement at the eye (24:30) and the approach (17:32) is not prudery but prophylaxis. And for the one already past the line, the sūrah's larger context keeps the door of return in view: the verse says *transgressors*, not the damned — and the Qur'an's fuller sentence on this sin's people, quoted above from 25:70, ends: *"save whoever repents, and believes, and works righteousness — those, God will change their evil deeds into good deeds; and God is Ever-Forgiving, Merciful."* The boundary is hard; the gate back through it is always unlocked. The believer recites vv. 29–31 in the context of the whole sūrah — between the prayer of vv. 22–23 and the trusts of vv. 32–33 — because the tradition learned what the arrangement teaches: chastity is not an isolated feat of willpower but a wall maintained by the whole structure of a life oriented to the Day.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:32

> **those who abide by their trusts and their pact,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rāʿūn: Shepherds of What Was Handed Over**

Verse 32 names the sixth mark: *wa-alladhīna hum li-amānātihim wa-ʿahdihim rāʿūn* — "and those who are shepherds over their trusts and their covenant." The verb is pastoral: r-ʿ-y is the root of *rāʿī*, a shepherd — one who pastures, tends, keeps watch over a flock that belongs to another. The excepted soul is here defined as a *herdsman of obligations*: everything handed to him — wealth, secrets, office, duty, the faith itself — is a flock he grazes and guards, never a prey he feeds on. The Qur'an's parallel in the believers' portrait of Sūrat al-Muʾminūn is nearly verbatim (23:8), and its legal formulation is a pillar of Islamic public law: *"God commands you to render trusts to those entitled to them, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice"* (4:58); *"O you who believe, betray not God and the Messenger, nor betray your trusts knowingly"* (8:27). The Prophet ﷺ fixed the scope of the shepherd's commission for every rank of the community:

> *"Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock: the leader is a shepherd... the man is a shepherd over his household... the woman is a shepherdess over her husband's house and children... the servant is a shepherd over his master's wealth. Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829, from Ibn ʿUmar — trusts assigned by role, audit assigned to all)

**The Trust No Mountain Could Carry**

The verse's two nouns — *amānāt* (trusts) and *ʿahd* (covenant) — stand for the two axes of fidelity: the horizontal, to whatever creatures place in our hands; and the vertical, to the pact God holds with every soul. Al-Jalālayn note that the covenant runs in both religion and worldly affairs; and the Qur'an anchors the vertical pact in an event before time, the day of *alast*:

> *"And when thy Lord drew forth from the children of Adam — from their loins — their descendants, and made them testify of themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yes (bala) — we testify.' Lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection: Of this we were unaware."* (Qur'an 7:172)

And the Qur'an gives the trust its cosmic frame in a verse the commentators never tire of quoting beside our own:

> *"We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to bear it and were afraid of it — but man bore it; truly he was unjust to himself, and foolish."* (Qur'an 33:72 — the trust the mountains feared, man picked up; vv. 22–35 of our sūrah are simply the curriculum of carrying it without dropping it)

**The Hypocrite's Résumé**

Because trustworthiness is invisible until tested, the Prophet ﷺ made it one of the three signatures of hypocrisy — and Islamic spirituality has used the triad as a self-examination ever since:

> *"The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays the trust."* (al-Bukhārī 33; Muslim 59, from Abū Hurayrah — in another wording: even if he prays and fasts and claims to be a Muslim)

The hadith's severity is diagnostic, not forensic: it does not excommunicate the offender; it locates betrayal on the same anatomical map as lying and promise-breaking, as symptoms of a divided heart. And the tradition read the triad forward into social theory with striking clarity: societies are nothing but networks of trust, and the Qur'an's insistence on amānah is the constitution of those networks. The modern reader can verify the principle from the ruins around him: the Ponzi scheme, the leaked database, the falsified audit, the broken NDA, the official who sells his office — each is an amānah abandoned; and the civilization that cannot generate shepherds must replace them with surveillance, which is both costlier and weaker. The believer's promise-keeping, by contrast, is cheap enforcement: the Shepherd above the shepherds is always watching. Hence the Prophet's ﷺ coupling of belief and credit:

> *"There is no faith for one who has no trust, and no religion for one who keeps no covenant."* (transmitted in the Musnad of Aḥmad from Anas ibn Mālik — the two negations doing the work of a definition: faith *is* fidelity)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:33

> **who uphold their testimony,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qāʾimūn: Standing Up to Speak**

Verse 33 names the seventh mark: *wa-alladhīna hum bi-shahādātihim qāʾimūn* — "and those who stand by their testimonies." The Arabic is built on a posture: *qāʾimūn*, standing — the witness is the one who *stands up* and speaks, and keeps standing by what he said. The word *shahādah* is from sh-h-d, to be present, to witness; it names at once the courtroom deposition, the public attestation, and — in the tradition's own naming — the profession of faith itself, the Testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is His Messenger. The commentators divide accordingly, as the source records: al-Qurṭubī reads the verse of the supreme Testimony — the excepted are those who affirm God's Oneness and the articles of faith and live inside that affirmation; al-Rāzī adds the rendering of those who testify to God's edicts and hold fast to them; and the general reading covers every human court and contract: the truthful witness in all matters. The Qur'an legislates that general case with famous severity:

> *"O you who believe, stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to God, even against yourselves, or your parents, or your kin; whether the person be rich or poor, God is closer to them both... And if you distort, or decline, then God is aware of what you do."* (Qur'an 4:135; cf. 6:152: *"And when you speak, be just, even if it be against a kinsman; and fulfill the covenant of God"*)

**Concealment: The Sin of the Unspoken Testimony**

The Qur'an treats the *suppressed* testimony as a crime of the heart before it is a crime of the court:

> *"...And do not conceal testimony; for whoever conceals it — his heart is sinful. And God knows what you do."* (Qur'an 2:283 — the closing of the Qur'an's longest verse, the charter of written contracts, where testimony is the instrument that keeps memory honest after the parties forget)

The physiology of the phrasing is exact: the concealment happens in the mouth, but the sin is assigned to the *heart* — a darkening of the core organ, not merely a lapse of speech. The classical jurists built an entire evidentiary ethics on this foundation: testimony is a public duty the witness owes, not a favor he may withhold; and the witness who perjures himself has, in the tradition's language, purchased the world's advantage at the price of the Hereafter's record. The Prophet ﷺ honored the opposite act — volunteering the truth before being asked:

> *"Shall I not tell you of the best of witnesses? He is the one who brings his testimony before he is asked for it."* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1717, from Zayd ibn Khālid al-Juhanī — or in the narrations of the dream-interpreters: the man in white garments, of whom the Prophet said he was ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām; the excellence lies in truth offered unprompted)

**The Prophet's Court: Even Against One's Own**

That testimony must be untouchable by rank and blood was demonstrated, not merely legislated, in the most famous judicial incident of the Prophet's life — the case of the woman of the Makhzūm clan, a noble family, who had stolen. Her kinsmen sought intercession, and the Prophet ﷺ delivered instead the sentence that has disciplined every Muslim court since:

> *"O people — those before you were destroyed because when the noble among them stole, they let him go, and when the weak among them stole, they enforced the penalty. By God, if Fāṭimah, the daughter of Muhammad, had stolen, I would cut off her hand."* (al-Bukhārī 6788; Muslim 1688, from ʿĀʾishah — the Hadith of Intercession: blood, rank, and mercy's plea all refused at the door of testimony and judgment)

The verse's placement in the sūrah is itself instruction: testimony (v. 33) stands immediately after trusts and covenant (v. 32), because they are the same integrity in two modes — the trust is the truth *stored* in us, and the testimony is the truth *spoken* by us; and both stand immediately before the prayer's final mention (v. 34), because the One before whom the prostration is made is the same One before whom no truthful word can be swallowed. A community of such people — deposit-refusing no one, testimony fearing no clan — carries in its daily commerce the moral infrastructure that no external regulation can fully replace: honest scales, honest accounts, honest words. The sūrah's sequencing thus sketches a complete civics in miniature: prayer, dues, conviction, fear, chastity, fidelity, testimony — the seven load-bearing columns of the household of the excepted, now to be capped, in verse 34, by the column with which the whole edifice began.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:34

> **and who are mindful of their prayers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Frame Closes: Yuḥāfiẓūn**

Verse 34 completes the list of the excepted where it began: with prayer. *Wa-alladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim yuḥāfiẓūn* — "and those who are mindful of their prayers." The list that opened with *the muṣallīn* (v. 22) and their constancy (v. 23) now closes with their guardianship — and the verb is a third appearance, inside this same pericope, of the root ḥ-f-ẓ that has been quietly threading the sūrah's ethics together: the excepted *guard* their private parts (v. 29), *guard* their trusts (v. 32), and *guard* their prayers (v. 34) — the same discipline of the shepherd applied to the body, to the deposits of others, and to the daily appointment with God; and it is the very verb God uses for His own Book: *"We have sent down the Reminder, and We of a surety are its Guardians"* (15:9). The believer's guarding of the prayer is thus written in the same grammatical ink as God's guarding of the Qur'an — as though the daily prayer were the believer's personal copy of the Protected Book, whose preservation is a covenant mirrored on both sides.

**Dāʾimūn and Yuḥāfiẓūn: The Two Halves of Prayer Excellence**

The classical commentators, al-Rāzī in his gloss on v. 23 (as the source records), heard two distinct notes in the two prayer verses. *Dāʾimūn* (v. 23) names persistence in the appointed times — the showing up, five times a day, for a lifetime. *Yuḥāfiẓūn* (v. 34) names a wider custody: guarding every aspect of the prayer — its correct time, its direction, its purification, its place, its requirements and its commendable parts — and guarding the inner conditions above all: presence of heart, freedom from distraction, and a life outside the prayer that does not contradict the prayer inside it. Ibn Kathīr glosses exactly so: careful to pray at the right time and to maintain all the necessary and recommended aspects, being mindful and present while praying. The two together give the believer a complete curriculum: *dāʾimūn* is the skeleton, *yuḥāfiẓūn* the flesh. And the Qur'an legislates the custody as a command with the same verb at the heart of its liturgy chapter: *"Be watchful over the prayers, and the middle prayer, and stand up for God in utter devotion"* (2:238); *"and those who believe in the Hereafter believe in it, and they are mindful of their prayers"* (6:92).

**Why the Accounting Begins Here**

That prayer is the list's frame is mirrored by the accounting's frame in the ḥadīth the tradition recites beside every verse on guarding prayer:

> *"The first thing for which the servant will be judged on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer: if it is found sound, he has prospered and succeeded; and if it is found deficient, he has lost and failed. If anything of the obligatory prayers is lacking, the Lord — blessed and exalted — says: See if My servant has any voluntary prayers, that they may complete what is lacking of the obligatory. Then the rest of his deeds are weighed likewise."* (Abū Dāwūd 864; al-Tirmidhī 413, from Abū Hurayrah — graded ṣaḥīḥ — the audit's first question is the list's first and last mark)

And the Prophet ﷺ left no doubt that this first-audited deed was also his own deepest delight, in the words the tradition treasures:

> *"The coolness of my eyes is in prayer."* (Aḥmad and al-Nasāʾī 3939, from Anas ibn Mālik — graded ṣaḥīḥ — the one appointed to command it loved it beyond rest itself)

> *"O Bilāl, give us rest by it."* (Abū Dāwūd 4985, from Anas — when the prayer time came, the Prophet called not for the day's burdens but for its relief: adhān as the announcement of rest, not of task)

The modern believer, trained by a culture that treats prayer as a chore to optimize, should let those two reports recalibrate the whole relationship: the Prophet approached the prayer the way the exhausted traveler approaches shade — the five daily stations as the counter-rhythm of a society that measures worth by productivity. Guarding the prayer (*yuḥāfiẓūn*) therefore includes guarding it from the subtlest modern theft: the reduction of it to a box checked at speed. The tradition's warning about hollow performance is blunt:

> *"A man may pray and have nothing written for him of it but a tenth — a ninth, an eighth, a seventh, a sixth, a fifth, a fourth, a third, a half."* (Abū Dāwūd 796, from ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir — graded ḥasan — presence is not decoration; it is the prayer's yield)

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:35

> **those shall be in Gardens, honored.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reward Named as Hospitality**

Verse 35 pays the list its wages in six words: *ulāʾika fī jannātin mukramūn* — "those — in Gardens, honored." The word *jannāt* is the plural of *jannah*, a garden — from the root j-n-n, to cover and conceal, as the garden's trees cover the ground — and it is the Qur'an's standing name for the final home: not a vague heaven of clouds but an environment of shade, water, fruit, green, and enclosure. But the verse's final word is the surprise: *mukramūn* — a passive participle from karāmah, honor and generous hospitality: the excepted are not merely *placed* in the Gardens; they are *honored* there — received the way a great host receives a long-awaited guest. The Qur'an's only other use of the honored-guest idiom is telling, for it belongs to the most famous garden-scene in the Book's sacred history:

> *"Has there come to you the story of the honored guests (ḍayf Ibrāhīma l-mukramīn) of Abraham?"* (Qur'an 51:24 — the visitors seated in Abraham's garden shade, served a fattened calf, greeted with the good news of a son)

The resonance across the two sūrahs is almost an invitation: the God who fed Abraham's guests will Himself host His servants; the believer's eternity is framed not as an award ceremony but as a *dīwān* of hospitality at which he is the guest of honor and God the Host. The Prophet ﷺ said that God says: *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor occurred to the heart of man"* (al-Bukhārī 7498, from Abū Hurayrah, quoting the divine report) — the menu of the Host exceeds the guest's imagination.

**The Reversal the Sūrah Has Been Building**

The word *mukramūn* detonates most powerfully in the sūrah's own context, for the verses immediately following (36–44) will show us the Quraysh nobles sneering at the poor believers clustered around the Prophet — *"If these enter Paradise, we shall enter before them"* — and God's answer: the honored ones are precisely those. The sūrah thus stages the Qur'an's constant inversion of status: those the world seated at the door will be seated at the head of the table; and those who owned the banquet-halls of this world will stand outside the feast, eyes humbled, abasement covering them (v. 44). The Prophet ﷺ saw the ledger's bottom line in his two authenticated visions at the gate of the Garden:

> *"I stood at the gate of Paradise, and behold, most of those entering it were the poor; and the rich were held back..."* (al-Bukhārī 6547, from Usāmah ibn Zayd — the audit sorting the guests by the only rank that survived)

And the parallel pericope's ending in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn — this sūrah's structural twin — crowns the same list with an inheritance: *"Those are the heirs, who shall inherit Paradise, abiding therein"* (23:10–11). The excepted of vv. 22–34, in other words, are the heirs of the Garden: the estate was always theirs; the eight responsibilities were the terms of the will being honored.

**Honor Sourced From the Only Supplier**

For the believer, the verse settles a question every society answers wrongly somewhere: where does honor (ʿizzah) actually come from? The Qur'an's answer is a single sentence that un-does every aristocracy: *"Whoever desires honor — all honor belongs to God"* (35:10). The Quraysh sought it in lineage and refused to pray beside Bilāl; the modern world seeks it in brands, followers, and titles; and the Qur'an locates it in being hosted by God — *mukramūn* — after a lifetime of the quiet disciplines no market ever prices: prayers kept at dawn, dues paid from wealth, chastity kept when no one would have known, trusts returned when embezzlement was easy, testimony spoken against one's own interest. The eight responsibilities of vv. 22–34 are, read from verse 35 backward, simply the *etiquette of the future guest* — the training, in this lower garden of time, of souls being prepared for the Gardens where they will be honored forever. And the sūrah, its first movement done, now turns without transition to those who found all of this ridiculous.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:36

> **So how is it that those who disbelieve scramble toward thee**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Divine Interrogative: Astonishment as Rebuke**

Verse 36 opens the sūrah's final movement with a rhetorical structure Arabic reserves for moral shock: *fa-mā li-lladhīna kafarū* — "so what is it *with* those who disbelieve?" The particle *mā li-* ("what is the matter with...") is the grammar of astonishment: a father's tone at a son's inexplicable conduct. After the majestic close of v. 35 — the excepted honored in Gardens — the verse pivots to the other crowd with the divine equivalent of a double-take: *you have seen the honored; now behold these, scrambling.* The verb that answers the question is *yahtiʿūna* — a rare word, from ḥ-t-ʿ, which the lexicographers gloss as hastening with eagerness and vehemence, mouths agape, jostling toward a spectacle. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī, as the source records, set the historical scene: these were the disbelievers who gathered around the Prophet to listen to him — pressing close, crowding him, deriving from it no benefit whatsoever; instead they denied him and mocked. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same theater of mockery with painful precision:

> *"And when those who disbelieve see thee, they take thee only as a laughingstock: 'Is this the one who makes mention of your gods?' And at the mention of the Merciful alone, they are disbelievers."* (Qur'an 21:36)

> *"And when they see thee, they only take thee in mockery: 'Is this the one God has sent as a messenger? He would well-nigh have led us away from our gods, had we not stood firm over them!'"* (Qur'an 25:41–42)

**Eager Bodies, Absent Hearts: The Anatomy of Useless Attendance**

The verse draws, almost in passing, one of the Qur'an's sharpest psychological distinctions: proximity is not reception. The mockers performed everything the modern conference-goer performs — they attended, they crowded, they leaned in (*yahtiʿūna* is nothing if not eager body language) — and they walked away exactly as they came. The Qur'an's diagnosis of such audiences is sobering for every generation whose religion has become attendance: *"And among them are those who listen to thee; but We have placed covers upon their hearts, so they understand it not, and in their ears a deafness; and if they see any sign, they do not believe in it, so that when they come to dispute with thee, those who disbelieve say: 'This is nothing but the fables of the ancients'"* (6:25). The same ear, in a different chest, became ʿUmar's Islam; the crowd at Badr contained men who had heard the Qur'an more often than some of the Anṣār who died believers. The lesson the sūrah is engineering: what scrambles toward the truth bodily must also cross the threshold of the heart — the eight responsibilities of vv. 22–34 name exactly what the crossing looks like. Attendance, curiosity, entertainment, even fascination, are not faith; the mockers prove that a man can be close enough to touch the Prophet and still be far — as the next verse's absurd geometry will now show.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:37

> **from the right and from the left in droves?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿIzīn: The Man Who Became a Crowd**

Verse 37 completes the picture: *ʿani l-yamīni wa-ʿani l-shimāli ʿizīn* — "from the right and from the left, in droves." The rare noun *ʿizīn* (plural of *ʿizzah*) means companies, bands, factions — people clustering in packs; al-Ṭabarī paints the scene: separate groups converging from every side, surrounding the Messenger from right and left like besiegers closing a ring. The visual the two verses create together is unforgettable: one man seated with God's word, and around him a closing wheel of jeering cliques — this group from the east side of the courtyard, that one from the west, each drawing courage from the others' laughter. The Qur'an's sociology in the single word *ʿizīn* is exact: mockery is rarely a solo act; it is a *pack* behavior, in which each member's unbelief is subsidized by the others'. The psychology is as old as the species: a man alone before the Qur'an is a soul under examination; the same man inside a band of mockers has outsourced his conscience to the band's consensus. The Qur'an exposes the mechanism in the deniers' own confession of borrowed certainty:

> *"And they denied them, though their souls were certain of them, out of wrongdoing and arrogance."* (Qur'an 27:14 — certainty in the chest, denial on the lips, the crowd's approval standing between the two)

**The Geometry of Refusal**

The commentators also pondered the mention of *right and left* specifically — not "before him and behind him" but side to side. Al-Ṭabarī's mainstream reading is simply "from every direction," the closing of all approaches; other readings, recorded in the tradition, hear an additional barb: the mockers positioned themselves at the Prophet's *flanks* — the undefended angles — like men pressing in at the shoulders of a speaker, whispering, jostling, intercepting. Whichever geometry one draws, the theological point is the same and it is the sūrah's quiet irony: they closed every human angle of the Prophet's position, and missed the one direction that mattered — above. The Lord of the ascending ways (v. 3) was the direction they left open; the descent they dared to demand (v. 1) was the descent none of their droves could obstruct; and within two decades of this scene, the droves were gone — Badr, Uḥud's aftermath, the Conquest, and the emptied courtyards of a Makkah that now circled the Kaʿbah in worship. The Qur'an had already given the principle by which crowds evaporate: *"Indeed, those who oppose God and His Messenger — they are among the most abased. God has decreed: 'I and My messengers shall surely prevail'"* (58:20–21).

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:38

> **Does every man among them desire to be admitted into a Garden of bliss?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Interrogative of Incredulity: All of Them?**

Verse 38 delivers the question that stops the mockers' party cold: *a-yaṭmaʿu kullu imriʾin minhum an yudkhala jannata naʿīm* — "Does each and every man of them covet that he be made to enter a Garden of bliss?" The grammar is loaded. The interrogative *a-* of disbelief is affixed to the verb *yaṭmaʿu* — to covet, to aspire greedily, a word the Qur'an otherwise uses of hopes pitched well above desert: *"And do not covet that by which God has preferred some of you over others"* (4:32). The subject is not "they" but *kullu imriʾin minhum* — "every single man of them": not one or two ambitious souls, but the whole band, each member privately assuming the same destiny. The audacity is the point. The Qur'an reserves its incredulity for exactly this presumption, and asks it again, at length, of the believers themselves — lest they too slide into entitlement without cost:

> *"Or did you reckon that you would enter the Garden, while there had not yet come upon you the like of what came upon those who passed away before you? Affliction and adversity befell them, and they were shaken, until the Messenger and those who believed with him said: 'When comes God's help? Surely God's help is near.'"* (Qur'an 2:214)

**The Caste Theory of Paradise and Its Refusal**

The reports preserved by al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī, which the source quotes, put the mockers' inner logic into words. Gesturing at the poor believers clustered near the Prophet — Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Ṣuhayb, Ibn Masʿūd and their like — the Quraysh nobles sneered:

> *"If these were to enter Paradise, we would certainly enter it before them; and we would have therein more wealth than they ever would."* (reported from the circumstances of revelation in the commentaries of al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī)

Here is a theology of Paradise as class inheritance: heaven as the upstairs of the same social order, with the same doormen, the same seating chart, the poor kept below. The sūrah's answer, developed across the remaining verses, dismantles the theory at every joint: vv. 22–34 have just listed the *only* currency of admission — prayer, acknowledged dues, conviction, fear, chastity, trusts, testimony — none of which is minted by lineage; v. 39 will recall the *material* from which every one of them, noble and pauper alike, was manufactured; and vv. 40–41 will swear that their replacements are ready. Meanwhile the Qur'an had already delivered its standing counter-manifesto on the poor believers the nobles despised:

> *"And restrain thyself with those who call upon their Lord, morning and evening, seeking His Face; and let not thine eyes pass beyond them, desiring the adornment of the life of this world; and obey not him whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desire, and whose affair is excess."* (Qur'an 18:28)

The Prophet ﷺ was commanded never to trade his congregation of the poor for the approval of the chiefs — and the chiefs read the refusal correctly, and hated it. Their mockery at Makkah was, at bottom, social panic: the revelation had re-priced admission to eternity, and their entire net worth — pedigree, alliances, wealth — had just been declared non-convertible currency. Verse 38's question — *all of you, then?* — is still the question every entitled religion must face: the Garden of bliss is *jannat naʿīm*, a garden of *delight* — and the God who built it gates it by none of the world's turnstiles.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:39

> **Nay! Truly We created them from that which they know.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā Again: The Second Door Slammed**

Verse 39 opens with the sūrah's second *kallā* — the same gavel-fall that annulled the ransom dreams of v. 15. This time what is annulled is the expectation of v. 38: the assumption, held by every man in the band, that Paradise was somehow owed to him. *Kallā* — not so; and then, in place of an argument, the verse reaches for a fact so familiar that no Makkan could contest it: *innā khalaqnāhum mimmā yaʿlamūn* — "truly We created them from what they know." The pronoun shifts pointedly — the mockers had spoken of *those* people (the poor believers); God answers about *them*, the mockers, and about the substance they know all too well. Every one of them began as what the Qur'an, in its twin sūrah, calls a base fluid:

> *"Did We not create you from a base fluid, deposited in a firm repository...?"* (Qur'an 77:20–21; cf. 86:5–7: *"So let man consider from what he was created: created from a gushing fluid, emerging from between the loins and the ribs"*)

**The Full Genealogy the Qur'an Never Tires of Reciting**

The Qur'an, as the source notes, piles up the physical genealogy of man in passage after passage — dust, then the drop, then the stages of the womb — and it does so for a reason that is never merely biological. The fullest recital is the one the tradition reads at every Muslim burial:

> *"And indeed We created man from an extraction of clay; then We made him a drop in a firm lodging; then We made the drop a clot, and the clot a lump of flesh, and the lump bones, and We clothed the bones in flesh; then We produced him as another creation. So blessed be God, the Best of creators!"* (Qur'an 23:12–14; cf. 35:11: *"And God created you from dust, then from a drop; He made you pairs..."*)

The point of the recital — made explicitly in Sūrat Yā-Sīn — is the resurrection argument the mockers of our sūrah had scoffed at: the God of the first creation cannot be doubted on the second:

> *"Does not man see that We created him from a drop? And then, behold — he is an open adversary! And he coins for Us a likeness, and forgets his own creation; he says: 'Who will give life to the bones, when they are decayed?' Say: He who produced them the first time will give them life; and He is knowing of all creation."* (Qur'an 36:77–79)

**The Egalitarian Drop: No Aristocracy of Origin**

But in this sūrah's context the humbling has a second edge, aimed at the caste theory of Paradise exposed in v. 38. The Quraysh noble who sneered at Bilāl and Ṣuhayb needed to be reminded that he, too, began as *mimmā yaʿlamūn* — "what they know," with all its delicacy of phrasing: the origin too obvious to need naming and too base to bear bragging about. There is no noble drop and no pauper drop; the entire genealogy of aristocracy fits inside a single cell invisible to the naked eye. The Qur'an then legislates the conclusion for all human ranking:

> *"O mankind — We created you from a male and a female, and made you peoples and tribes that you might know one another; the noblest of you with God is the most godwary of you."* (Qur'an 49:13)

The only aristocracy Islam recognizes is acquired, not inherited — taqwā, the very quality the mockers lacked; and the only net worth that converts to the currency of the Garden is the eight-item portfolio of vv. 22–34. Al-Jalālayn, as the source records, draw precisely this moral: mere existence — which is in itself nothing without God — does not merit the Garden; reverence and the responsibilities do. The verse thus completes the humbling arc that began at v. 36: the droves that scrambled so confidently (vv. 36–37) are reminded of the day each of them was a single, immobile cell, and of the One who raised him from it — and the logic is left to do its work: the Power that did that once can do anything He has promised.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:40

> **So I swear by the Lord of the easts and the wests that We are able**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Lā Uqsimu: The Oath That Cancels Their Certainty**

Verse 40 opens with a solemn divine oath — the Qur'an's strongest instrument of emphasis: *fa-lā uqsimu bi-rabbi l-mashāriqi wa-l-maghāribi* — "So I swear by the Lord of the easts and the wests." The little *lā* in *fa-lā uqsimu* is what Arab grammarians call the *lā* of emphasis: an intensifier Arabic inserts into oaths to sharpen their edge ("I do swear..."). God, who needs no oath, here performs the condescension of swearing — as a judge rises from the bench to speak face to face — because the mockers' presumption (v. 38) had hardened into a settled disbelief in His power to do what He promised. The object of the oath will be v. 41's terrible certainties: that God is able to replace them utterly. But the *predicate* of the oath — what God swears *by* — is chosen with surgical care: His lordship over the easts and the wests, the single most reliable spectacle every human being has ever witnessed: the machinery of the heavens, rising and setting, without a missed appointment, for as long as anyone has kept records.

**The Plurals That Measured the Sky**

The plurals — *mashāriq* (easts) and *maghārib* (wests) — set the commentators' astronomy in motion, as the source records. Al-Ṭabarī sets out the readings: the easts are the places from which the sun rises, and the wests the places in which it sets — for the rising point migrates along the horizon across the year between the two solstices, so that the sun has, over twelve months, *many* easts and *many* wests; add the moon and the planets, each with its own risings and settings, and the plural becomes a catalogue of the sky's traffic. The Sūrah of the Merciful uses the dual for the same dominion: *"Lord of the two easts and Lord of the two wests"* (55:17), which the commentators read as the two solstitial extremes; Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt has *"Lord of the easts"* (37:5); and our verse completes the symmetry with both plurals — the whole horizon, every rising and setting point there ever was or will be, claimed as personal estate. For the first hearers — merchants who navigated by these lights and farmers who planted by them — the oath invoked the most precisely ordered system they knew: whoever commands every sunrise owns every schedule, including the schedule of the Day they doubted (v. 4) and the replacement of the people who doubted it (v. 41).

**The Oath's Logic: Faith Anchored in the Observable**

The rhetorical genius of swearing by the easts and wests is that it grounds the invisible promise in the visible order. The mockers demanded empirical proof (v. 1); God answers by pointing at the most massive empirical dataset available to a seventh-century observer: the repeated, unfailing, precisely calibrated succession of day and night — *"a sign for those who reflect"*: *"In the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of night and day, are signs for people of understanding"* (3:190). The argument runs: you already trust, with your life and your commerce, the Regularity that stands behind every dawn; that same Regularity has spoken — through this Prophet — of a Day and a replacement; and the Owner of both is One. He who has never once been late for a sunrise is not going to be late for the Hour. The Prophet ﷺ made the same move, from the same sky, in his famous counsel to the bedouin:

> *"Fear God wherever you are, and follow an evil deed with a good one to erase it, and treat people with beautiful character."* — and in the night-journey reports the sky itself became the witness: *"Then he was taken up... until he saw the greatest signs of his Lord"* (53:18) — the observable sky, from the horizon of the easts and wests to the lote-tree of the utmost boundary, testifying to the One who governs it.

For the modern reader the verse's invitation stands: the same ordered regularity that the seventh century read as sunrise is now measured to the nanosecond and the light-year — atomic clocks synchronized to the turning earth, celestial mechanics charted centuries ahead — and the question the oath poses has not changed: to whom does the machinery belong, and what follows from His word? Verse 41 will now state, under the oath's sanction, exactly what follows — and it is not a comfort to the mockers.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:41

> **to replace [them] with what is better than them; and none outstrips Us.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nubaddila: The Power the Oath Certified**

Verse 41 states the object of verse 40's mighty oath: *ʿalā an nubaddila khayran minhum* — "to replace them with what is better than them; and none outstrips Us." Two readings, both recorded by the source, divide the classical commentators, and each carries its own weight of meaning. Ibn Kathīr reads the replacement eschatologically: God is able to give them new bodies in the Hereafter — the re-creation the mockers doubted (cf. v. 39), certified now by oath. Al-Ṭabarī reads it historically: God is able to replace them *in this world* with another people — and the Qur'an had already made the principle explicit as a standing divine law:

> *"Here you are — these are people who are invited to spend in the way of God, and some of you withhold. And whoever withholds withholds only from himself; and God is the Rich, and you are the poor. And if you turn away, He will replace you with a people other than you, and they will not be the likes of you."* (Qur'an 47:38)

> *"...If He wills, He can remove you and cause whom He wills to succeed you after you, as He raised you from the seed of another people."* (Qur'an 6:133; cf. 14:19–20: *"If God wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation; and that for God is not great"*; and 5:54)

The fulfillment, for the first audience, was not long deferred. Within two decades the droves of vv. 36–37 — al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, the casualty of Badr; Abū Jahl, killed in the same battle; the chiefs who crowded the Prophet from right and left — were gone, and their places in the leadership of mankind were taken by men and women the chiefs had despised: Bilāl the freed Abyssinian, Ṣuhayb the Roman-hearted Greek of the Arabs, Salmān the Persian, ʿAmmār the son of a slave, and the two ʿUmars and ʿAlīs of the second generation. The Qur'an's promise proved literal: replacements *better* than them — better in faith, better in character, and, as history's ledger ran, better in consequence.

**Masbūqūn: The Scramblers Who Cannot Outrun**

The verse's closing clause seals the oath: *wa-mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn* — "and We are not to be outstripped." The root s-b-q means to race, to precede, to get ahead of; *masbūq* is the one who has been beaten to the finish. Against whom is the race run? Against the mockers of vv. 36–37, whose verb *yahtiʿūna* — scrambling, hastening, jostling — is the perfect foil: they race to crowd the Prophet, race to demand the punishment, race to their entertainments — and God announces, under oath, that no one outruns His plan. The doctrine is the Qur'an's standing reply to every conspiracy theory of history: tyrants, mobs, and trend-setters believe they are steering; the Book teaches that they are running a course whose finish-line, gates, and stewards were fixed before they laced their shoes: *"They plan, and God plans; and God is the best of planners"* (8:30); *"I respite them — truly My scheme is firm"* (7:183). For the believer, the verse lands as the most calming sentence in the sūrah: the future of the faith does not depend on the pace of the faithful, the zeal of the mockers, or the odds of any decade. The truth has a schedule; the schedule has an Owner; and the Owner cannot be overtaken.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:42

> **So leave them to indulge in idle talk and play until they meet the Day that they are promised,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dharhum: The Command to Disengage**

Verse 42 draws the practical conclusion from the whole sūrah's verdict: *fa-dharhum yakhūḍū wa-yalʿabū* — "So leave them, that they may wade and play." The command *dharhum* — leave them, let them be — is the Qur'an's standing instruction to the Prophet in the face of bad-faith audiences, and the source notes its twin, verbatim, in Sūrat al-Zukhruf: *"So leave them to wade and play, until they meet the Day of theirs that they are promised"* (43:83; cf. 6:91: *"Say: 'God.' Then leave them to play at their vain discourse"*). The two verbs paint the mockers' inner world without a single adjective of contempt: *yakhūḍū* — to wade, the verb for trudging through water or mire, which the Qur'an uses precisely for the intellectual life of the heedless: *"Woe that Day to the deniers, who wade in vain discourse"* (52:11–12); and the Qur'an's later self-portrait of the damned: *"We used to wade with the waders"* (74:45); and *yalʿabū* — to play, the verb of children and sport, applied to those who made religion itself an entertainment: *"And leave those who take their religion as play and amusement, and whom the life of this world has deluded"* (6:70). The mockers, in other words, are not refuted here; they are *diagnosed* and *released* — left inside the amusement park they built, with its ticket valid *"until they meet the Day that they are promised."*

**Why "Leave Them" Is Policy, Not Surrender**

The command to disengage puzzled some later jurists, and the source records the discussion: some, as al-Qurṭubī transmits, held the instruction abrogated by the later command to fight (9:5) — but most commentators maintained it un-abrogated, and the event proved them right: even after military struggle was licensed in defense of the community, the Prophet's method with *mockery* remained the method of this verse. The reason lies in the Qur'an's own theory of persuasion: *"Wilt thou then compel mankind to become believers?"* (10:99); *"There is no compulsion in religion; the right way has been made distinct from error"* (2:256). Truth is propagated by argument, example, and prayer — never by forcing the scoffer's lips into agreement. Bad-faith mockery is not a position to be debated; it is a *game* (the verse's own word), and the only way to lose a game is to play it. The Prophet ﷺ gave the community its standing rule for the tongue that both guards the truth and declines the game:

> *"Let whoever believes in God and the Last Day speak good, or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47, from Abū Hurayrah — the tongue's two lawful modes, with no third for the mocker's arena)

And the Qur'an explains why the respite is granted — why God does not silence the playground at once:

> *"...And let not the disbelievers reckon that the respite We grant them is good for them; We grant them respite only that they may increase in sin; and theirs is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

The interval is not acquittal; it is accumulation — of deeds, and of the evidence that no excuse will survive: *"And We shall turn to what they have done of deeds, and make it scattered dust"* (25:23)? The Qur'an's sentence on the waders: *"the Day their excuses will not profit the wrongdoers, and theirs is the curse, and theirs is the evil abode"* (40:52).

**The Discipline of Delegated Timing**

For the believer, verse 42 is the sūrah's final medicine against the anxiety it diagnosed at v. 19 — the halūʿ soul that cannot bear to lose an argument, see a mocker walk away, or watch evil prosper. The command *dharhum* assigns the outcomes to God and the clock: leave them *until* — the appointment of v. 1 is kept, the Day of v. 4 arrives, the Fire of v. 15 calls. What remains for the servant is the portfolio of vv. 22–34, lived in front of an audience or none: the prayer kept, the due paid, the trust guarded, the testimony spoken. The modern reader will recognize the verse's sanity instantly, because its opposite is now an industry: the feeds that monetize outrage train their users to answer every mocker, all day, forever — the precise behavior this verse forbids fourteen centuries in advance. The Prophet ﷺ left the droves to their droves, and spent his evenings with Bilāl, ʿAmmār, and the Qur'an; and the ending of that allocation is the sūrah's final image, to which we now turn.

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:43

> **a day when they come forth from their graves, hastening as if racing to a goal,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ajdāth: The Ground Opens Its Archive**

Verse 43 gives the sūrah's last picture before its last word: *yawma yakhrujūna mina l-ajdāthi sirāʿan* — "the day they come forth from the graves, swift." The word *ajdāth* is the plural of *jadath*, a grave, a dug place — the earth's filing system, where every corpse was entered as a record and sealed. The verb *yakhrujūna* — they come out — is the archive opening at once: not the selective resurrection of the famous, but the total retrieval of the species. The Qur'an renders the scene with the imagery of the earth itself emptied:

> *"When the earth is stretched, and casts forth what is within it, and empties itself... "* (Qur'an 84:3–4; cf. 99:2: *"the earth throws up its burdens"*)

The dead are not raised politely, one at a time; they *stream* — *sirāʿan*, swiftly, at speed — and the simile the verse chooses for their motion has puzzled and fascinated the commentators: *ka-annahum ilā nuṣubin yūfiḍūn* — "as though they were streaming toward a *nuṣab*." A nuṣab — from the root n-ṣ-b, to erect, to plant upright — is a planted object: a post, a pillar, a planted banner, a raised goal. Al-Ṭabarī reads the image as a race to a fixed marker — the finish-line post of a contest — toward which runners pour with heads down and bodies straining (*yūfiḍūn*, from w-f-ḍ, to pour forth, to rush in a body). The picture is of a marathon's last hundred meters: a dense mass, all aimed at one planted standard, no individual able to stop or slow.

**The Irony: The Refusers to Hasten Are Made to Hasten**

The simile is loaded with the sūrah's own irony, and the commentators savored it. In this world, these people would not hasten to prayer, would not hasten to the truth when it was offered freely, would not *"compete"* for the Gardens as the Qur'an commanded elsewhere: *"Race toward forgiveness from your Lord, and a Garden whose breadth is as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the godwary"* (3:133). In this world they hastened only to the spectacle (*yahtiʿūna*, v. 36 — the scrambling of the crowd around the Prophet) and to the accumulation (v. 18). On that Day, the will they used to refuse is simply bypassed: the summons carries them, and they run — not toward a Garden, but toward a nuṣab: a planted post where their account is fixed. The Qur'an gives the twin image of the mass in motion twice more, each time with a swarm-creature, and each time with the same detail of the humbled gaze:

> *"...their eyes humbled, coming forth from the graves as if they were scattered locusts."* (Qur'an 54:7)

> *"...and man that Day will be as scattered moths."* (Qur'an 101:4)

The swarm imagery is precise psychology: in the droves of this world (*ʿizīn*, v. 37) each mocker retained the illusion of individuality while surrendering it to the crowd; in the swarm of that Day the illusion is gone — a cloud of creatures, all velocity and no will, exactly the shape their souls had been practicing all their lives.

**Gathered Barefoot: The Prophet's Own Commentary**

The Prophet ﷺ was questioned about the manner of this emergence, and his answer — preserved in the Ṣaḥīḥ — added the details the imagination begs for, and removed the one detail people worried about:

> *"The people will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection barefoot, naked, uncircumcised."* ʿĀʾishah said: "O Messenger of God — men and women together, looking at one another?" He said: *"The matter is too great for them to care about that."* (al-Bukhārī 6527; Muslim 2860, from ʿĀʾishah — the stripping of every costume: no rank-dress, no uniform, no brand, nothing between the creature and his Creator but the record he carried)

It is the final undoing of vv. 36–38: the droves who mocked in their finery, who ranked men by garment and lineage, stand in the crowd they always were — undifferentiated, undressed, streaming past the standard — while the excepted ones, in their Gardens, are *mukramūn* (v. 35): clothed and honored by the Host Himself, *"their Lord gives them to drink a pure drink"* (76:21).

## Sūrat al-Maʿārij 70:44

> **their eyes humbled, abasement shall overcome them. That is the Day they have been promised.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Khāshiʿatan Abṣārahum: The Organs of Arrogance Lowered**

The sūrah's final picture is a face — millions of faces — with one expression: *khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum* — "their eyes humbled." The verb kh-sh-ʿ denotes subjection, being brought low; it is the word the Qur'an uses for the voices, the faces, and above all the *eyes* of that Day: *"Faces that Day will be humbled"* (88:2); *"and all voices will be hushed before the Merciful"* (20:108). The eye is specified because the eye was the instrument of the old order: the arrogant glance, the sizing-up stare, the look that ranked other men — *"And when Our signs are recited to him, he turns away in arrogance, as if he heard them not, as if there were in his ears a deafness; so give him tidings of a painful punishment"* (31:7). The eye that refused to lower itself at God's revelation (24:30) is lowered now by the fact it refused to see. And *tarhaquhum dhillah* — abasement *overtakes* them: the verb r-h-q means to catch up with, to overtake, to cover — the very verb the Qur'an uses of the punishment no one can outrun. The mockers spent their lives chasing (vv. 36–37: scrambling, hastening, racing); the only thing that finally caught them was the abasement, which overtook them at the nuṣub.

**Dhillah: The Wages of Sought-After Honor**

The word *dhillah* — abasement, humiliation — is the precise opposite of the *karāmah* (honor) given to the excepted in v. 35, and the Qur'an has legislated the exchange-rate between the two from one end of the Book to the other: honor belongs to whoever God honors, and humiliation is the terminus of whoever seeks it from any other source:

> *"Whoever desires honor — all honor belongs to God."* (Qur'an 35:10)

> *"Truly those who oppose God and His Messenger — they are among the most abased."* (Qur'an 58:20; cf. 58:5, and 10:27: *"...and abasement covers them, and they have no protector from God, as if their faces were covered with strips of darkest night"*)

And the Qur'an, with its habit of repeating a verdict until it is beyond misreading, gives verse 44 an almost exact twin in the Sūrah of the Pen — against the same audience of deniers: *"the Day they are dragged upon their faces into the Fire... their eyes humbled, abasement overcoming them"* (68:42–43). The sūrah of al-Maʿārij thus closes on the note struck at its opening, with the order of that world now fully inverted: the mockers who asked for the punishment (v. 1), who saw it far and were seen by it near (vv. 6–7), who scrambled for entertainment around the man they scorned (vv. 36–37), and who expected to be first into the Garden of bliss (v. 38) — stand at the appointed post, eyes down, overtaken by the one thing their scramble could not outrun.

**"That Is the Day They Have Been Promised": The Sūrah's Ring Closes**

The final clause is a sentence of accounting: *dhālika l-yawmu lladhī kānū yūʿadūn* — "That is the Day they were promised." The demonstrative *dhālika* points, with courtroom finality, back across the whole sūrah: the *ʿadhāb wāqiʿ* of v. 1 — the impending punishment that a questioner demanded and none could avert (v. 2) — has arrived at v. 44 as a *promised Day*, kept. The promise was made by the One whose word the sūrah has certified at every step: the Lord of the ascending ways (v. 3), whose administration runs on days of fifty thousand years (v. 4); the One who saw it near (v. 7) while they saw it far (v. 6); the One who swore by the easts and the wests (v. 40) that none outstrips Him (v. 41). The Qur'an's own comment on such kept promises is the standing warning and the standing comfort in one verse:

> *"O mankind — the promise of God is true; so let not the life of this world deceive you, and let not the Deceiver deceive you concerning God."* (Qur'an 35:5)

And the sūrah's structure now stands complete as a ring: it opened with a *question* and closes with a *kept promise*; between them lie the two possible answers to the question — the Fire for those who spent the interval scrambling, mocking, amassing, and presuming; and the Gardens, honored, for the excepted ones who spent it praying, giving, believing, fearing, guarding, and testifying. Every reader finishes the sūrah standing at one of the two ends of that ring, with the interval — this life — still in hand. The early Muslims used to say, when they recited al-Maʿārij, that its forty-four verses were a mirror: read vv. 1–18 and 36–44 and see whether your face is anywhere in the mockers' crowd; read vv. 22–35 and see whether your name is among the excepted. The mirror's frame is the promise. The questioner of verse 1 asked *when*; the sūrah's whole answer was *who*: the One who promised — *"We are able"* (v. 40) — has kept, keeps, and will keep every word.
