# Sūrah al-Balad (Chapter 90) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The City, the Human Being, and the Hard Road**

Sūrah al-Balad, "The City" or "The Land," takes its name from the opening oath, *lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-balad* — "I swear by this land." The twenty verses that follow form one of the most tightly composed moral arguments in the Qur'an: an oath, a portrait of the human condition, a rebuke of self-deception, an inventory of human faculties, and finally a definition of the one steep path that leads to salvation. In roughly one page of Arabic text, the sūrah compresses a complete anthropology — what the human being is, what he has been given, what he falsely imagines, and what alone can save him. Classical commentators, as the source commentary notes, are agreed that the sūrah belongs to the Makkan period of revelation, with the majority placing it in the early or middle Makkan years; al-Suyūṭī and others held that it followed Sūrah Qāf (50) directly, while a minority opinion claimed it was Madinan, or that only its closing verses belong to the Madinan period. Whatever the precise dating, its themes are unmistakably Makkan in their intensity: the Hereafter, divine power, the emptiness of wealth without faith, and the moral structure of the life to come.

**An Oath Spoken Over the Sacred City**

The sūrah opens with an emphatic divine oath by a land — the city of Makkah, in the majority reading — and then immediately deepens into a question about human striving, human sight, and human spending. This is a characteristic movement of the Makkan revelation: the created world is summoned as witness, and the human being is placed in the center of the frame to be examined. The rhetorical engine of the sūrah is a sequence of pointed questions — *Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him? Does he suppose that no one sees him?* — each question dismantling a layer of human self-delusion, until the sūrah turns to positive instruction: the freeing of a slave, the feeding of the hungry, faith, patience, and mercy. The structure is not accidental. Before a person can act rightly, he must see truly; the sūrah first cures the eye, then commands the hand.

**The Central Image: The Steep Pass**

At the heart of the sūrah stands its most famous image — the *ʿaqabah*, the steep, rugged mountain pass. The word occurs three times in three verses (11–12, and implicitly through the answer that follows), and the commentators universally read it as a metaphor for the difficult good deed: the act that costs something, that strains the soul, that is undertaken against the pull of comfort and greed. The sūrah's argument is that the human being was created in *kabad* — hardship and struggle — and that the only fitting response to a life of struggle is to choose, voluntarily, the greater struggle: the ascent of the moral pass, whose toll is paid in freeing captives, feeding the hungry, and clothing faith with patience and mercy. What follows is a verse-by-verse expanded commentary, in which each of the twenty verses is treated as a complete and independent discourse, drawing on the Arabic language, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Baghawī, al-Tustarī, al-Ālūsī, and others), authenticated Prophetic reports with their collections identified, the reasoning that binds the verses together, and the practical bearing of the sūrah upon modern life.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:1

> **Nay, I swear by this land,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Begins With a Denial**

The sūrah opens with the particle *lā* followed by the verb *uqsimu* — "I swear." The literal sequence is arresting: the verse begins with a word that negates before it affirms. The classical commentators, as the source notes, were divided over this opening *lā*. One school of readers treated it as a merely emphatic particle, a stylistic prelude whose force is to intensify the oath that follows — as though God were saying, "Indeed, truly, I swear by this land." On this reading the particle is like a hand raised before speech, drawing every ear to attention before the solemn vow. Other readers — among them al-Qurṭubī, who transmits the discussion at length — took the *lā* as a genuine negation, and then asked what exactly is being denied. Two answers dominated. The first: the denial rebukes those whose conduct is condemned later in the sūrah (vv. 5–11) — the person who boasts of squandered wealth and imagines himself beyond all power and all sight. Before indicting them, the sūrah denies their silent assumption: *Nay* — it is not as you suppose; *I swear by this land* — that you will be called to account. The second answer, attributed in the commentaries to Ibn Kathīr and others, read the denial as aimed at the Quraysh of Makkah, who claimed the sacred city as their own secure possession and yet rejected the Messenger sent within it. To them the oath declares: your security in this land is a divine grant, not a human achievement, and it will not shield you from the reckoning.

**What Makes an Oath Necessary**

The very presence of the oath is itself a lesson in divine pedagogy. Human beings swear oaths when they wish to be believed on a matter of great importance — when the claim is weighty and the listener is skeptical. When God swears by His creation, as He does throughout the Makkan sūrahs — *"By the sun and its morning brightness"* (91:1), *"By the night when it covers"* (92:1), *"By the fig and the olive"* (95:1) — the effect is twofold. It honors the created thing by making it a witness, and it signals that the statement to follow is of such moment that the Almighty Himself, whose word needs no confirmation, chooses to confirm it. Al-Zamakhsharī and the rhetorical commentators noted that when the Qur'an swears by a thing, it is generally to draw attention to the marvels within that thing — to make the reader look again at what he has looked at a thousand times without seeing. The oath by the land is thus an invitation to contemplative sight: look at this city, its sanctuary, its history, its people — and then hear what follows about the human being who lives within it.

**Which Land Is This?**

The word *balad* in Arabic denotes a tract of land, a country, or a city, and the commentators explored every shade. The majority — al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Ṭūsī among them — read it as Makkah itself, the sacred city, and this is strengthened by the very next verse, which addresses the Prophet as one who is *ḥill* — free, or dwelling — *bi-hādhā al-balad*, "in this land." A minority suggested Madinah, and some read the word generally as the earth or as the Arabian Peninsula. But the context decides for the majority: the sūrah that swears by the land and then speaks of the Prophet's freedom within it, and of the begetter and the begotten linked to that land, is surely speaking of the city of the Sanctuary — the city toward which every Muslim turns in prayer, and in which the Prophet ﷺ was born, persecuted, and finally honored. God swears by it, and the believer's heart is meant to quicken at the mention of it: the city that was the cradle of revelation is dignified by being made the very ground of a divine oath.

**Why God Swears by a City**

There is profound logic in choosing this particular witness. Makkah was, in the seventh century, a place that seemed to embody human security: it possessed the ancient Sanctuary, the water of Zamzam, the prestige of the pilgrimage, and the commercial networks of the Quraysh. Its inhabitants — and the Arabs generally — placed their trust in its inviolability, believing that the sacred territory and its guardianship made them safe in this world and, as some imagined, in the next. The sūrah's opening therefore performs a quiet reversal: the very thing in which the Quraysh trusted — the sacred land — is summoned not to guarantee their safety but to witness against their complacency. The same rhetorical maneuver recurs in the Qur'an: *"And they say, 'If we follow the guidance with you, we will be driven from our land.' Have We not established for them a secure sanctuary to which fruits of every kind are brought, as provision from Us? But most of them know not"* (28:57). The secure land is a gift that carries obligations; the oath over Makkah is a reminder that every gift is also a test. For the modern reader the verse works the same way toward every beloved place — homeland, city, sanctuary of memory: love of place is legitimate, but no place on earth is beyond the reach of divine questioning, and no geography grants moral immunity.

**The City in the Believer's Heart**

Finally, the oath plants a seed of spiritual geography. When the believer recites *lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-balad*, he is reminded that God Himself has attached honor to a physical place — that matter can be made sacred, that a street and a valley can carry the weight of revelation. This is the logic behind the Qur'an's insistence on the Kaʿbah as the qiblah, and behind the entire institution of the ḥajj: Islam is not a religion of pure inwardness floating free of the world. It sanctifies the body, the direction, the place, the day. And by swearing by Makkah, God teaches that the created world is not a veil that hides Him so much as a language that speaks of Him — for those who have ears. The land is honored because of what occurred in it and because of the one who walked in it; and the highest honor of any land, the sūrah implies in its second verse, is to be the ground beneath the feet of a truthful prophet.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:2

> **while thou art free in this land;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word Ḥill and Its Double Meaning**

The second verse is among the most discussed single phrases in the sūrah, and the pivot of the discussion is the Arabic word *ḥill*, here rendered "free." The root *ḥ-l-l* carries the sense of loosening, unbinding, and settling: a *ḥill* (or *ḥalāl*) stands in contrast to what is tied, forbidden, or taboo (*ḥarām*). In the vocabulary of the sacred territory, the word had a precise technical meaning: Makkah and its surroundings were a *ḥaram*, a zone of inviolability in which hunting was forbidden, fighting was forbidden, and even the cutting of trees was restricted. One who was *ḥill* was outside that zone — or, in another sense, one who was permitted and free within it. The commentators offered several interpretations built on this richness, and the source commentary summarizes the field: the Prophet is free to act as he deems best in Makkah; the verse foretells or recalls the conquest of Makkah; the verse means that the Prophet is an honored resident of the land; or — in the reading preferred by many — the verse is an oath by the Prophet's very presence in the land, which has been honored by God through him.

**The Conquest of Makkah in the Background**

The most historically charged interpretation links this verse to the conquest of Makkah in the year 8/630. For centuries the Arabs had treated Makkah as sacrosanct: bloodshed within the sanctuary was regarded as a catastrophe, and the pre-Islamic Arabs honored the sacred months and the sacred zone with a punctiliousness that often surpassed their other virtues. When the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah with his army in the eighth year after the Hijrah, a brief skirmish at the edge of the city cost a handful of lives, and some of the Quraysh — and later some Muslims — felt the gravity of bloodshed in the inviolable land. The verse, on this reading, answers that anxiety: *while thou art free in this land* — O Muhammad, for you alone this land has been made free; the restriction that binds others is lifted for you by God's decree. The commentators connect this to the famous Prophetic report, transmitted by al-Bukhārī (no. 1833) and Muslim from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"God made Makkah sacred on the day He created the heavens and the earth, so it is sacred by the sanctity of God until the Day of Resurrection. Fighting in it was not lawful for anyone before me, and it was made lawful for me only for one hour of one day; it is not lawful for anyone after me..."*

The report continues with the Prophet's declaration that the sanctuary's game is not to be startled, its thorns not to be cut, and its lost property not to be picked up except by one who announces it — and it closes with the famous exchange in which al-ʿAbbās pleaded that the *idhkhir* grass (used by smiths and for roofs) be excepted, and the Prophet ﷺ granted the exception. The verse is thus read by Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭūsī as a divine ratification of an exceptional permission: the sanctity of Makkah remained intact, but its strictest prohibition was suspended for the Messenger for that single hour — the hour in which the city was purified of its idols. If the sūrah is Makkan, as the majority hold, the verse is then a prophecy of that hour, spoken years before the armies marched; if parts of the sūrah are later, it is a ratification after the event. Either way, the verse announces a principle that governs all of Islamic law: sanctities are real, but the Messenger's commission could, by divine will, suspend them — an exception that proves the rule of the sacred rather than abolishing it.

**Free Passage, or Honored Presence?**

A second interpretation, preferred by al-Ṭūsī and others, reads the verse not as permission to shed blood but as a guarantee of free passage: God gave the Prophet ﷺ freedom of movement within Makkah even when the Quraysh denied it to him. The point is subtle and sharp. The Quraysh were the custodians of the Kaʿbah, proud of their role as the servants of the Sanctuary; yet in their enmity toward the Prophet they forbade entry to a man who was not only a tribesman but a Messenger of God. Their custodianship became, in effect, a lock on the House of God — a disgrace, as the commentators note, for any keeper of the Kaʿbah to bar the door against one of God's own messengers. The verse then carries a tone of divine irony: *thou art free in this land* — whatever the Quraysh decree, God has decreed your freedom here. Al-Rāzī records a third nuance, reading *ḥill* as "present" or "dwelling": the verse alludes to the fact that the land has been ennobled by the presence of the Prophet within it — the way a house is ennobled by the guest it shelters, and the way the whole earth was ennobled by the dust that received the Prophet's footsteps. On this reading, the oath of verse 1 and the clause of verse 2 are welded into a single thought: *I swear by this land — and what a land, for you are in it.*

**The Sanctity of Persons Over Places**

Read morally, the verse yields a principle that runs beneath the surface of the entire episode: places are made sacred by what happens in them, but persons are sacred by what they are. The Kaʿbah is honored because it is the House of God; the Prophet is honored because he is the Messenger of God — and the honor of the House cannot override the honor of the Messenger, any more than a locked gate can override the right of the owner. The Quraysh had inverted the hierarchy of sanctities: they guarded the stones while persecuting the man whom the stones themselves, and the God of the stones, honored. Modern life is full of the same inversion — institutions, flags, buildings, and traditions defended with fervor while the human beings they exist to serve are neglected or harmed. The verse is a standing correction: every sacred thing — a mosque, a scripture, a homeland, a law — exists ultimately for the sake of the divine purpose, and no sanctity of place may be used to oppress the sanctity of persons. When the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah and declared general amnesty to the very people who had tortured and exiled him, he enacted the deepest meaning of being *free in the land*: freedom is not the power to punish but the power to forgive.

**A Land Honored by a Presence**

There is also a gentler, contemplative reading that the believer may carry through the day. God swears by the land, and the honor of the land is the presence within it of the truthful one. Every place where the truth is spoken and lived is thereby dignified. This is why the Qur'an speaks of mosques as houses God has permitted to be raised and His Name remembered in them (24:36), and why the earth itself is described in the ḥadīth as a place of prostration for the community of Muhammad ﷺ. The verse whispers to every believer: you too carry a presence with you; the ground you walk upon, the room you inhabit, the work you do — all are touched by what you bring into them. A believer who understands the *ḥill* of this verse walks through the world aware that his freedom is a divine grant held in trust, and that the most honorable thing he can do with any place is to make it, by his truthfulness and mercy, a place where God is remembered.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:3

> **and [by] the begetter, and that which he begat,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Witness Joins the Oath**

The oath that began with the land now expands to include the human being — or rather, the entire chain of human beings. The verse swears *wa-wālidin wa-mā walad* — "by the begetter, and that which he begat." The Arabic is deliberately spare: *wālid* is the active participle of *w-l-d*, to give birth or beget, and *mā walad* is literally "what he begot." The commentators were quick to notice that the phrase does not say "the father and the son" in the ordinary sense, but casts the net wider: any begetter and everything begotten. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭūsī transmit the majority reading — Adam and his progeny, or every begetter and every begotten, embracing humanity from its first ancestor to its last descendant. The oath thereby sweeps across the whole of human history in four words: the land, then the man, then every child of man. Taken together with verse 4, the sequence yields a complete scene: the sacred city, the human race, and the verdict upon the human condition — *truly We created man in travail*.

**Adam and His Children, or Abraham and Ishmael?**

Within this general frame, the commentators proposed several particular identifications, each with its own resonance. Some read *al-wālid* as Adam — the father of all — and *mā walad* as his entire progeny, since every human being descends from him; the oath then covers the whole race that is addressed in the verses that follow, and the mention of Adam glances at the story of the first trial in the Garden, of which every human trial is an echo. Others, following reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and favored by al-Rāzī and al-Ṭūsī, read the pair as Abraham and Ishmael. The link to the context is strong: the sūrah has just sworn by Makkah, and Abraham and Ishmael are the founders of Makkah's sacred history — the father who, at God's command, settled his wife and infant son in the barren valley, and the son who helped him raise the foundations of the Kaʿbah. The Qur'an records their prayer at the building of the House: *"Our Lord, accept from us; indeed You are the Hearing, the Knowing"* (2:127). On this reading, verse 3 completes a trilogy of sanctities: the sacred land, the sacred lineage that built its Sanctuary, and — in the following verse — the human being who must struggle within it. Al-Qurṭubī also transmits the reading that identifies *al-wālid* with Ishmael specifically — regarded by the Arabs as their ancestral father — and *mā walad* with his progeny, the Arab tribes; this reading ties the oath directly to the audience of the Makkan revelation, who traced their descent to Ishmael through ʿAdnān. Some of the spiritual commentators added a further turn: the begetter is the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and the begotten is his community, since the Prophet called his followers his children in the order of faith — and indeed the Prophet ﷺ said of the relation between the believers and himself:

> *"The likeness of me and my community is that of a man who kindles a fire; the moths and grasshoppers begin to fall into it, and I seize your belts [to pull you back] from the Fire, while you rush past me."* (al-Bukhārī 6483; Muslim 2285)

Each reading is a different angle on the same truth: the oath binds the generations together — the fathers and the children, the prophets and their peoples, the founders and those who inherit their trust.

**Why Swear by Begetting?**

There is a deep reason why begetting — of all human activities — is made a witness in this sūrah. Procreation is the one act in which a human being most closely resembles the creative power of God, and yet it is also the act in which he most clearly does not: the parent brings forth a life he did not originate, a soul he cannot fashion, a destiny he cannot control. Every birth is therefore a living proof of divine creativity enacted through human agency — a daily miracle that habit renders invisible. The Qur'an repeatedly points to this: *"O mankind, worship your Lord, who created you and those before you"* (2:21), and again, *"He created you from a single soul and made from it its mate"* (39:6). The oath by the begetter and the begotten summons the reader to look at the child in the mother's arms and see not merely biology but the signature of the Creator: the same God who made the land made the man, and the same God who made the first man is making every man through the chain of begetting. There is, in addition, a legal and moral weight to the phrase: begetting entails responsibility. The one who brings a child into the world is answerable for that child's nurture — and, in the moral order of the Qur'an, the generations are linked in accountability, each transmitting either guidance or neglect to the next. The oath thus quietly establishes the theme of inheritance that runs through the sūrah: what did the fathers leave the sons, and what will the sons do with it?

**The Chain That Binds Every Reader**

The verse has a way of collapsing time. When the commentators read *al-wālid wa-mā walad* as every begetter and every begotten, the oath is addressed, in effect, to the reader himself: you are both begotten and a potential begetter; you stand in the middle of the chain, receiving from those before you and transmitting to those after. This is the human condition in its social dimension — no one is a self-made island; every person is a link in a chain of care that stretches back to Adam and forward to the last human being. The Qur'an returns to this truth whenever it commands kindness to parents: *"And your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and [show] kindness to parents"* (17:23). The command is not sentimental; it is structural. Since every human being exists only through the begetting and rearing of others, gratitude toward parents is the first school of gratitude toward God, and the person who dishonors the chain of begetting has broken faith with the very mechanism of his own existence. In an age that increasingly treats birth as a mere biological event and the family as an optional arrangement, the verse restores the awe that belongs to generation: the begetter and the begotten are sacred enough to be summoned as witnesses in a divine oath — and whatever is sacred is to be handled with reverence, not with carelessness.

**Generations in the Grip of One Verdict**

Finally, the placement of this verse matters. The oath swears by the begetter and the begotten immediately before announcing the travail of man — as though to say: every generation of this long chain, from Adam to the last child, is bound by the same condition, created for the same struggle, subject to the same reckoning. No era is exempt; no ancestor's merit and no descendant's cleverness cancels the personal trial. This is why the verse is sometimes read as a quiet rebuke to those who trusted in lineage — a vice as common among the Quraysh as among any aristocracy, who imagined that noble birth would save them. The Qur'an meets that assumption elsewhere head-on: *"Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most reverent of you"* (49:13). The oath by begetting honors the chain of descent — and then immediately turns to the one thing that neither descent nor wealth can provide: the personal ascent of the steep pass, which no father can climb for his son.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:4

> **truly We created man in travail.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict After the Oaths**

After the triple oath — by the land, by the begetter, by the begotten — the sūrah pronounces its thesis: *laqad khalaqnā al-insāna fī kabad*. The particle *laqad* is an emphatic affirmation — "truly, indeed" — and the sentence has the ring of a judicial finding: after the witnesses have been sworn in, the verdict is delivered. *Al-insān*, "man," here is generic: the human being as such, every member of the chain just sworn by. And *fī kabad* is the disputed phrase. The root *k-b-d* yields the noun *kabd* (or *kibid*), the liver — the heavy, dark organ seated at the center of the body — and from the same root come meanings of weight, toil, difficulty, and intensity. To be created *fī kabad* is to be created within hardship, fashioned for struggle, placed in a life whose native climate is effort. The commentators explored several registers of this single word, and together they compose a complete picture of the human predicament.

**The Hardships of Life and the Severities of the Hereafter**

The interpretation most widely transmitted is that attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110/728), the great sage of Baṣrah, who glossed the verse as: "Enduring the hardships of the world in life and the severities of the Hereafter." On this reading, *kabad* names the entire curriculum of human difficulty — birth pangs, weaning, the struggle to grow, the labor of earning, the aches of age, the finality of death. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī unfold the thought across a lifespan: the child emerges from the womb into struggle, spends his youth in learning and labor, his maturity in providing, his old age in weakness, and his end in death — *kabad* from the first cry to the last breath. The commentators saw the same truth reflected in the Qur'an's description of motherhood — *"His mother carried him in travail and bore him in travail"* (46:15) — where the very entry of the human being into the world is attended by hardship, so that the first sensation of life outside the womb is the beginning of the struggle that never fully leaves him. Al-Rāzī adds that this is not an accident or a punishment but the very texture of the test: a life without resistance would be a life without the possibility of moral growth, just as a muscle that never meets weight never strengthens. Some commentators extended the meaning to the trials of the grave and the Day of Judgment, reading *kabad* as the standing of the creature before its Creator — the difficulty of the reckoning itself. The verse thus announces the two-sided truth of the human condition: the world is hard, and the Hereafter is serious; the person who understands this will not live as though this life were a playground and the next an illusion.

**The Strange Reading: Created Upright**

Alongside the dominant reading, al-Ṭabarī records a striking minority interpretation based on a different sense of *kabad*. The same root gives the adjective *akbad*, and some of the early readers — on the authority of certain Companions and their students — took the phrase to mean that man was created "upright" or "erect," the only creature that walks upon two legs with his head raised to the heavens. On this reading, the verse points to the distinctive posture of the human being: unlike the beasts that graze with lowered heads, man stands vertical, his face turned toward the sky — a bodily emblem of his spiritual orientation. The two readings are not as distant as they appear: both say something essential about the human being. Man is the creature who stands upright — and therefore the creature who must bear weight. The beasts carry their burdens on their backs involuntarily; man carries his burden on his shoulders voluntarily, upright, conscious, answerable. His erectness is the physical signature of his moral responsibility: the animal is excused because it is bent toward the earth; the human being is accountable precisely because he stands tall enough to see beyond it.

**Why the Human Being Was Made for Struggle**

The verse invites the question: why would a wise and merciful Creator fashion His most honored creature for travail? The answer unfolds logically across the sūrah and across the Qur'an. First, struggle is the only environment in which free moral choice can operate. A being who had no hardship to overcome could display no patience, no generosity, no courage — the virtues would be empty because nothing would test them. The Qur'an states the principle plainly: *"He it is who created the heavens and the earth in six days — and His Throne was upon the water — that He might test you, which of you is best in deed"* (11:7). The world is not a failure of design; it is a testing ground by design. Second, struggle is the mechanism of growth: the pearl forms around the grain of sand, and the muscle forms around the resistance. Third — and this is the insight the sūrah will spend its remaining verses developing — the travail of life is meant to point beyond itself. The creature who finds this world a place of toil is a creature who should suspect that his true home is elsewhere. The discomfort is a compass: it tells the soul it was not made for this alone. The believer who grasps this stops asking "Why is life hard?" and begins asking the only useful question: "What is this hardship for, and how shall I meet it?" The sūrah answers: it is for the ascent of the steep pass — and the steep pass is climbed by faith, by freeing captives, by feeding the hungry, by patience and mercy.

**The Fairness of the Test**

There is a further note of justice in the verse that the classical commentators did not miss. If man is created *in kabad* — if struggle is woven into his very constitution — then the commands of religion are not arbitrary impositions from outside. The command to be patient is proportionate to the creature who was made to need patience; the command to strive is proportionate to the creature who was made for striving; the promise of reward is proportionate to the effort expended. God does not ask the angels to climb the steep pass, because the angels were not created in kabad; He asks the human being, because the human being was created precisely for this — and the very difficulty of the task is the measure of the honor. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have said, weeping at this verse: "What a creation! A creation fashioned in hardship, toiling for a life of hardship — and then the Hereafter, with its Garden and its Fire." The believer's response to the verdict of verse 4 is therefore neither despair nor denial but preparation: since struggle is certain, the only freedom lies in choosing *which* struggle — the involuntary toil of the heedless, who exhaust themselves for a world that will not last, or the voluntary toil of the aware, who spend their strength climbing the pass that leads to God. The rest of the sūrah is an extended answer to the question this verse raises in the soul: given that I was created in travail, how shall I travail well?

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:5

> **Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Question That Unmasks the Delusion**

The sūrah now turns from the divine oath to a human question, and the change of speaker is significant: the One who swore by the land and by the generations now interrogates the human being directly. The verse is a rhetorical question in the most powerful sense — it is not asked for information, because the answer is already known, but asked to make the listener hear the absurdity of his own assumption. *A-yaḥsabu an lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad* — literally, "Does he suppose that never will have power over him anyone?" The indefinite *aḥad*, "anyone," leaves the question deliberately open, and the commentators universally complete the thought: the one the deluded man forgets is God. He imagines that no power exists that can seize him, call him, humble him, or require him — that his wealth, his tribe, his position place him beyond reach. The verse does not argue with him; it simply holds up his silent assumption to the light, the way a single sentence can expose an entire philosophy: the person who lives as though he will never be called to account has, in effect, concluded that no one has power over him — and that conclusion is the root of every other error in his life.

**The Particular Delusion of the Wealthy**

The context identifies the deluded one precisely: he is the man of verse 6, who boasts of squandered wealth. The Qur'an repeatedly observes that wealth produces a characteristic blindness — the illusion of permanence, self-sufficiency, and immunity. The possessor of riches begins to feel that his provision comes from his own cleverness, that his security rests on his own resources, and that his importance is measured by his balance. The Qur'an dissolves the illusion by pointing to the hand that actually distributes: *"God outspreads provision for whomsoever He will and straitens it"* (13:26; and cf. 17:30; 28:82; 29:62; 30:37; 34:36, 39). The source commentary notes that the verse implies two distinct accountabilities: the wealthy will be asked how they acquired their wealth and how they spent it — and no amount of philanthropy launders wealth that was wrongly obtained, just as no charity cleanses earnings that were seized unjustly. The question *Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?* is thus a question about the true owner of wealth. The man who forgets that God outspreads and straitens — that the same hand that filled his account can empty it overnight through a market crash, an illness, a thief, or a natural disaster — has built his confidence on the most volatile foundation in the world.

**Every Age Has Its Own Version of This Supposition**

The delusion wears different clothes in every century. In the Prophet's Makkah it wore the robes of the merchant-aristocrat who could say, as verse 6 records, "I have squandered vast wealth" — spending that was really a boast, an announcement of magnitude rather than of generosity. In the modern age the same supposition wears the clothes of the financier who imagines his portfolio makes him immune to destiny; of the technologist who speaks as though the latest innovation has abolished the ancient limits of human life; of the influencer who mistakes visibility for significance; of the person in any station who simply never lets the thought of the reckoning cross his mind. The verse's genius is that it does not name a class of people but a structure of thought — and that structure can inhabit a billionaire or a beggar. Whenever a human being organizes his life on the working assumption that there will be no final accounting — that nothing transcends his own projects, pleasures, and plans — he is the person addressed by this verse, whatever his bank balance.

**The Psychology of Forgetting the Reckoning**

There is a subtle psychology in the Qur'an's choice of the verb *yaḥsabu*, "to suppose, to reckon, to imagine." The word belongs to the family of *ḥisāb*, "accounting." The deluded man is, in a sense, a bad accountant: he is confident that no one will audit him, and so he keeps no books, or keeps false books, spending as though the audit will never come. The believer, by contrast, is described in the Qur'an as one who *yaḥsabu* in the opposite direction — who takes account of himself before he is accounted for, in the famous saying attributed to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh yourselves before you are weighed, and adorn yourselves for the Great Exposure."*

The difference between the two men is not intelligence but awareness. Both will die; both will be asked; the only question is whether the asking finds them prepared or surprised. The verse invites the reader to perform a simple experiment in honesty: observe your own daily behavior and ask what it reveals about your working beliefs. The person who truly believed a reckoning was coming would not live exactly as he lives now — and the gap between his profession and his behavior measures the depth of the unexamined supposition that no one has power over him. To recite *Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?* with self-knowledge is to ask: is there any corner of my life where I am living as if this verse were false?

**The Answer the Question Implies**

A rhetorical question of this kind carries its answer in its form. The sūrah will soon close with the picture of the Fire enclosed upon those who disbelieve in the signs — the final demonstration that there is indeed One who has power. But even within the question, the believer hears the reassurance that the deluded man is wrong in the most comforting sense as well as the most terrifying: the One who has power over the human being is not a blind fate or a hostile universe but the same God who swore by the land and by the begetter and the begotten — the God who created man in travail and who, as the coming verses will show, equipped him with eyes, tongue, and lips, and pointed him toward the two highways. The power that will call the human being to account is the power of a just and merciful Lord. The question is not whether the power exists; the question is whether the human being will live today as though it does.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:6

> **He says, "I have squandered vast wealth!"**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Boast That Sounds Like a Complaint**

Verse 6 puts words into the mouth of the deluded man, and the words are chosen with extraordinary precision. *Ahlaktu mālan lubadā* — literally, "I have destroyed wealth abundant." The verb *ahlaka* means to destroy, to ruin, to cause to perish; the adjective *lubadā* (from *l-b-d*, to cling, to heap, to accumulate) describes wealth that is piled up, heaped layer upon layer — great masses of it. The sentence can be read in two tones at once, and the commentators heard both. In one tone it is a boast: the man announces, before the world, how much he has spent — he has destroyed, in the sense of lavished, heaped-up fortunes, and he wants everyone to know the scale of it. In another tone it is a complaint: the man protests that his wealth has been consumed — spent and gone — in atonements and payments, as though he had been ruined by his own compliance. The source commentary records the traditional identification of this man with al-Ḥārith ibn ʿĀmir, who — when the Prophet ﷺ advised him to pay money for the atonement of an offense — objected: "My money has gone to atonements and payments since the day I entered the religion of Muhammad," implying that he neither understood the spiritual value of what he had spent nor believed any good could come of it.

**What Kind of "Destruction" Is This?**

The verb *ahlaktu* — "I have destroyed" — is the key to the verse's irony. The man thinks he is describing the *effect* of his spending: his wealth has been destroyed, finished, thrown away. But the Qur'anic usage of *halāk* consistently points elsewhere — to the spiritual ruin that spending without faith brings upon the spender. The Qur'an says of those who spend to be seen: their spending is *"like a rock on which is dust; a heavy rain falls upon it and leaves it bare. They have no power over anything of what they have earned"* (2:264). The wealth is not destroyed by being given; it is destroyed by being given wrongly — or withheld wrongly. The verse's irony is that the speaker announces his own loss while believing he is announcing his own greatness: he says *I have destroyed wealth*, and the listener who hears with the ears of faith understands that the wealth was never the issue — the issue is the soul that could spend vast sums and still stand empty before God. The Arabic permits a further nuance: *mālan lubadā* can describe wealth that was itself wrongly heaped — gathered without right, piled without gratitude — in which case its "destruction" in futile spending is the natural end of wealth that was never blessed. Money obtained without right and spent without purpose destroys itself; the man's complaint is really an audit report on his own methods.

**Spending That Counts and Spending That Vanishes**

The verse raises a practical question that every earner must face: what distinguishes spending that is a virtue from spending that is merely a claim to virtue? The answer the sūrah will unfold in verses 11–17 is that the only spending that counts is the spending that climbs the steep pass — the freeing of captives, the feeding of the orphan and the indigent, done on the basis of faith, patience, and mercy. The man of verse 6 spent vastly and yet is rebuked, because his spending was not of that kind: it was either a boast (look how much I can afford to lose) or a grudge (look what religion has cost me). Both attitudes cancel the value of the deed, because both proceed from the assumption that the wealth was *his* to begin with — his to boast of losing, his to resent losing. The Qur'an states the criterion elsewhere with total clarity: *"Never will you attain righteousness until you spend from that which you love; and whatever you spend of anything, indeed God knows it"* (3:92). Spending that is merely abundant but never costly to the self — never touching what the spender loves, never given with a willing heart — is the "destruction" of verse 6: wealth that perishes and leaves no trace in the ledger of the Hereafter.

**The Psychology of the Reluctant Giver**

There is a recognizable human type in this verse, and the Qur'an dissects it with one quoted sentence. The reluctant giver gives, and then resents having given; he performs the good deed and then holds it against the religion that asked for it. His complaint — "my money has gone to atonements and payments" — reveals that he experienced every religious call on his wealth as a loss rather than an investment, a tax rather than a purification. Such a man may be outwardly generous by the arithmetic of donations while inwardly destitute by the arithmetic of the heart. The Qur'an describes his inner state precisely in the verse the commentators link to this one: *"And naught prevented their spending from being accepted from them, save that they disbelieved in God and in His Messenger, and only come to the prayer lazily, and only spend reluctantly"* (9:54). The acceptance of a deed, the Qur'an teaches, depends not on its size but on its source: the spending that is given grudgingly, or given to be seen, or given while doubting the One who asked for it — such spending is not worship at all but a transaction the giver never wanted to make.

**When Wealth Speaks Louder Than Faith**

The verse also exposes the deeper pathology beneath the boast: the man measures his life by his wealth. When he wants to make a statement about himself — to establish his importance, to answer a charge, to console his ego — the only language he has is the language of money: *I have squandered vast wealth.* His identity is fused with his expenditures. The Qur'an repeatedly warns against this fusion: *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion and adornment and boasting among you and competition in increase of wealth and children"* (57:20). The man of verse 6 is the walking embodiment of that warning: strip away his wealth and he has no answer to give — no faith, no patience, no mercy, no deed that will speak for him when the wealth is gone. The believer, by contrast, is taught to build an identity that wealth cannot touch and death cannot take: *"The day when neither wealth nor children will avail, save him who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). The verse asks every reader to listen to his own speech: when you describe your life, what do you reach for first — your losses, your possessions, your achievements in the world's currency, or the state of your heart before God? The answer reveals which self you believe will stand the reckoning.

**The Quiet Correction of the Surrounding Verses**

Finally, the man's boast is placed between two questions — verse 5, *Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?*, and verse 7, *Does he suppose that no one sees him?* — and the sandwiching is the commentary. The man announces his squandered wealth, and on either side stand the two facts he has forgotten: that power exists over him, and that sight is upon him. His boast is thus framed by his own delusions. Everything he says is true in a sense he does not intend: he *has* destroyed wealth — and more than wealth; he has destroyed the very deeds that might have saved him by performing them without faith; and the heap he boasts of (*lubadā*, piled layer on layer) will be the very evidence against him, since every layer was spent without the one ingredient that gives spending eternal value. The believer reads the verse as a warning about the grammar of his own life: let your giving be said in the language of faith — quietly, thankfully, to God — and not in the language of the ledger, loudly, boastfully, to the world.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:7

> **Does he suppose that no one sees him?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Illusion of Power to the Illusion of Privacy**

The second question advances the indictment from the external to the internal. The first delusion was about *power* — the man imagines nothing can seize him. The second delusion is about *sight* — the man imagines no one sees him. The two together constitute the complete psychology of the heedless: he believes he is beyond reach and beyond observation, that his deeds have no witness and therefore no record, that the private corners of his life — his intentions, his secret transactions, the real motives behind his public gestures — are his alone. The verse asks: *a-yaḥsabu an lam yarahu aḥad* — "Does he suppose that no one has seen him?" The question reaches past the man's public boast in verse 6 to the man's private reality: behind the announcement of squandered wealth stands a hidden history — how the wealth was obtained, how it was spent, what was said in secret, what was intended in the heart. The commentators, as the source notes, transmitted the view that the verse refers to a specific individual who claimed to have spent money for Islam though he had spent nothing — a public pretense of generosity covering a private reality of stinginess. The verse exposes the premise of all such pretense: the pretender assumes the audience that matters is the human one.

**The Witness That Cannot Be Evaded**

The Qur'an returns relentlessly to the doctrine of divine sight, and each return adds a dimension. God sees what the eyes see and what the eyes cannot: *"He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal"* (40:19). The claim of verse 7 is not merely that God *can* see but that He *does* see — that the human being is never outside the field of vision: *"And We are closer to him than his jugular vein"* (50:16); *"He is with you wherever you are; and God sees whatsoever you do"* (57:4). The classical commentators — al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī among them, as the source notes — understood the verse to mean that God will ask how wealth was acquired and how it was spent; the sight of verse 7 is not passive observation but the gaze of an auditor who records every entry. Nothing is lost: *"And the Book will be set in place, and you will see the guilty fearful of what is in it, and they will say, 'Woe to us! What is this book that leaves nothing small and nothing great unnumbered?'"* (18:49). The modern mind, which has grown accustomed to surveillance cameras, data trails, and the slow disappearance of privacy, may find this verse newly vivid — but there is a decisive difference: the human surveillance that troubles us is the gaze of those with no right to our secrets, while the divine sight of the Qur'an is the gaze of the One to whom every secret already belongs, who watches not to exploit but to judge with perfect justice and to call His creatures back from the ruin their hidden sins are building.

**The Two Faces of Being Seen**

There is a profound psychological truth embedded in the verse: human beings are constituted by the awareness of being seen. Children behave differently when a parent's eye is upon them; adults perform for audiences real and imagined; the entire social order runs on the mutual visibility of reputation, shame, and honor. The Qur'anic doctrine of divine sight harnesses this deep structure of the human soul and turns it toward its proper object. The person who believes God sees him carries within himself a permanent, incorruptible witness — he does not need an audience to do right, and he cannot be safe doing wrong in secret, because the one Audience that matters is always present. This is the meaning of the Qur'anic phrase *yuqātūna fī sabīli Llāhi... wa-yakhshawnahu* and of the Prophet's ﷺ definition of excellence (*iḥsān*) in the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel:

> *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him, for even if you do not see Him, truly He sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

The verse from the sūrah is the negative form of the same truth: the heedless man acts as though no one sees him — and thereby loses the most powerful moral support available to the human soul. The one who knows he is seen by God possesses a dignity and a restraint that no external police can produce, because the witness is internal and constant.

**The Hidden History That the Boast Conceals**

The connection between verse 6 and verse 7 deserves attention: the man boasts of what he *spent*, and the question that follows asks about what *no one saw*. The juxtaposition suggests that public spending is frequently a screen for private failures — the donation announced to cover the transaction unannounced; the charitable reputation purchased to obscure an uncharitable method. The source commentary notes the view that the verse targets one who claimed expenditure he never made — and the type is universal: the philanthropist whose fortune was built on exploitation, the celebrity whose public kindness is managed by publicists, the religious person whose visible devotion conceals invisible cruelty. Verse 7 is the scalpel that cuts through all of it: *Does he suppose that no one sees him?* The question does not deny that human beings can be deceived — it affirms that the deceiver deceives only himself, because the sight that matters has already registered the truth. There is liberation in this for the sincere: the believer need not perform, because his real audience of One already knows the whole truth and accepts the sincere heart; and there is terror in it for the insincere, because no performance can finally succeed.

**Living in the Light of the Unseen Witness**

The verse ends the first movement of the sūrah — the indictment — on its deepest note. Power and sight belong to God; the human being is neither beyond reach nor beyond observation. But the sūrah does not leave the reader in dread; it immediately pivots in verses 8–10 to the catalogue of divine gifts — the eyes, the tongue, the lips, the two highways — as though to say: the One who sees you is the One who equipped you; the gaze that will judge you is the gaze that fashioned you. The transition is the whole message of the sūrah in miniature: the awareness of being seen by God is not meant to paralyze but to awaken. When the believer internalizes verse 7, he begins to live with a new coherence — his public and private selves converge, his giving becomes honest, his secret life becomes fit for inspection, because he has accepted the liberating discipline of living coram Deo, before the face of God. And the One before whose face he lives is not a tyrant waiting to pounce but the Lord who swore by the land, by the begetter, and by the begotten that He created man in travail — and then gave him eyes to see the path, a tongue to speak the truth, and the two highways to choose between.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:8

> **Did We not make for him two eyes,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Indictment to the Inventory of Gifts**

With verse 8 the sūrah pivots. The questions of indictment — *Does he suppose...?* — give way to a question of a different kind: *a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn* — "Did We not make for him two eyes?" The rhetorical form is the same, but the direction has reversed. The earlier questions exposed what the human being has forgotten; this question reminds him of what he has been given. The sūrah now conducts an inventory of the body itself, and the inventory is an argument: the God who fashioned these instruments is the God who sees, and the instruments themselves are the proof that the human being was made for sight of a higher kind. The commentators noted the logical structure immediately — the source commentary summarizes it: one who is able to make these subtle instruments, from which human beings derive such great benefit, would certainly be able to know every deed and hold human beings accountable for what they have done with them. The eye that the human being turns upon the world is itself the product of a sight that comprehends the eye's owner. The Maker of the instrument cannot be less perceptive than the instrument He made.

**The Anatomy of the Argument From Design**

The verse is one of the Qur'an's most compressed arguments from design, and it is worth unpacking its logic slowly, because the logic is as fresh in the age of the ophthalmologist as it was in the age of the desert. The human eye is an instrument of staggering refinement: a lens that focuses, an aperture that adjusts to light, a retina that transduces photons into signals, a neural pathway that reconstructs a three-dimensional world of color, depth, and motion from those signals — and all of it packed into two small organs that work in synchrony, correcting each other's blind spots, judging distance by parallax, and doing all of this continuously from the first waking moment to the last. The Qur'an invites the reader to consider: did this come to be by chance, or was it made? And if it was made, what does the making imply about the Maker? The Qur'an's own answer is that the eye is a sign: *"And in yourselves — do you not see?"* (51:21); and again, *"Say: He it is who brought you into being and made for you hearing and sight and hearts; little do you give thanks"* (67:23). The argument from design is not a modern invention; it is woven into the Qur'an's most characteristic mode of address — the invitation to look, to consider, to infer the Maker from the made.

**The Instrument That Testifies Against Its Owner**

But the verse's purpose is not merely to prove God's existence; it is to establish accountability. The eye is not only a gift; it is also a record. The same eye that gazes at the world has gazed at particular things — at what was lawful and what was forbidden, at the needs of the poor and the temptations of the market, at the signs of God and the distractions of vanity. The Qur'an speaks of the bodily members as witnesses on the Day of Judgment: *"On the day when their tongues and their hands and their feet bear witness against them as to what they used to do"* (24:24); and again, *"Until when they come to it, their hearing and their sight and their skins bear witness against them as to what they used to do"* (41:20). The eye, the Qur'an teaches, was not given merely to enjoy; it was given to see — and seeing carries responsibility. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The eyes commit fornication, and their fornication is the gaze."* (al-Bukhārī 6243; Muslim 2657, from Abū Hurayrah)

The verse's placement after the boast of squandered wealth is precise: the man who boasted of his spending is reminded of the eyes through which his wealth was acquired and spent — the eyes that watched for opportunity, sized up rivals, coveted what others possessed, or averted themselves from the needy. Every faculty is a trust, and the first question of the reckoning will be how the trust was used.

**Gratitude as the Missing Response**

The inventory of gifts is, at the same time, a lesson in gratitude — and gratitude is exactly what the deluded man lacks. The sequence of the sūrah is deliberate: the man says, *I have squandered vast wealth* — he measures his life by what he has *lost*; and God answers, in effect: let us count what you have been *given*. Two eyes — instruments no wealth could purchase, no surgeon could fully replicate, no technology could replace. The man who can see the sun rise and the face of his child and the pages of the Book, and who yet complains of his expenditures, has inverted the ledger of his life: he counts the coins he gave away and forgets the sight by which he earned them. The Qur'an repeatedly names this inversion as the essence of ingratitude: *"And if you count the blessings of God, you could never number them; indeed the human being is greatly wrongdoing, greatly ungrateful"* (14:34). The remedy the sūrah prescribes is not complicated: look at your eyes, and thank the One who made them; look at the world your eyes reveal, and recognize the Hand that revealed it; and then use your sight for the purpose for which sight was given — to see the steep pass and climb it.

**Two Eyes and the Depth They Give**

The verse specifies *two* eyes, and the specification is not accidental. Depth perception — the ability to see the world in three dimensions, to judge distance and danger, to move through space with confidence — depends on the pairing of the eyes, each transmitting a slightly different image that the brain fuses into depth. The Qur'an's mention of two eyes quietly points to the gift of dimensionality: with two eyes the human being can see not only that the steep pass exists but how far it is, how steep it is, where the footholds are. The spiritual application writes itself: the believer is given two eyes — one for the world and one for the Hereafter, one for the blessings of God and one for the signs of God — and the fusion of the two images in the heart produces the depth of vision that the one-eyed man lacks. The man who sees only the world sees a flat landscape of pleasures and losses; the man who sees with both eyes sees the same world in depth — its origin in God, its purpose as a test, its end in the reckoning — and walks through it with the confidence of one who can judge distances correctly. The verse asks: did We not give you two eyes? Then see with both.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:9

> **a tongue, and two lips,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Instrument of the Inventory**

The inventory continues: after the two eyes, the tongue and the two lips. *Wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn* — "and a tongue and two lips." The pairing is exact and anatomical: the tongue is the organ of taste and speech, and the lips are the two muscular gates that shape the breath into articulate language — the labial sounds, the closure and release, the fine motor control that turns a stream of air into words, promises, prayers, and oaths. The commentators observed that the verse moves from the organ of *seeing* to the organs of *speaking*, and that together they cover the two great channels by which the human being relates to the world: he takes in through the eyes, and he puts out through the tongue. The eyes are the gate of perception; the tongue is the gate of expression. Between the two lies the entire moral life of a human being — what he allows himself to see and what he permits himself to say.

**The Tongue as the Mirror of the Heart**

The Qur'an's attention to the tongue is relentless, and the reason is simple: speech is the most direct available evidence of the inner life. The Prophet ﷺ made the tongue the criterion of faith itself:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent."* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

And in his instruction to Muʿādh ibn Jabal, the Prophet ﷺ pointed to the tongue as the member that most often carries people to the Fire:

> *"And is anything that casts people into the Fire upon their faces — or upon their noses — other than the harvests of their tongues?"* (al-Tirmidhī 2616, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; Ibn Mājah 3973, from Muʿādh ibn Jabal)

The tongue can testify to truth or bear false witness; it can praise God or curse His creatures; it can reconcile or inflame; it can feed the hungry with a kind word or wound the needy with a sneer. The Qur'an sets the standard at the highest pitch: *"And speak to people kindly"* (2:83); *"And tell My servants to say that which is best"* (17:53). The verse of al-Balad asks the deluded man to consider the tongue he boasts with — the tongue that said *I have squandered vast wealth* — and to ask himself what his tongue has actually produced: has it spoken gratitude or complaint, truth or exaggeration, mercy or mockery? The tongue that announced the squandered wealth was itself the evidence of the heart's squalor; a tongue trained on gratitude would have said, *I have been given so much.*

**Why Two Lips Are Mentioned**

The specification of two lips carries its own subtle lesson. The lips are the seal of the mouth — the gates that close upon speech — and their mention reminds the human being that speech has boundaries. Not everything that rises to the tongue must be released; the lips exist to hold words back as well as to shape them. The Qur'anic virtue of restraint — *iman* guarding the tongue — is built into the very anatomy the verse inventories: the tongue is enclosed, fenced by teeth and lips, as though to teach that the most powerful organ of the body is also the most confined. The person who understands why he was given two lips understands the discipline of silence: the pause before the angry reply, the withheld gossip, the unspoken boast, the swallowed complaint. The man of verse 6 had no such discipline — his boast burst out of him — and the verse's mention of the lips that should have contained it is a quiet rebuke. Conversely, the lips that are opened in the right direction — in prayer, in truth, in kindness, in the testimony of faith — are among the greatest instruments of salvation, for the Qur'an teaches: *"And whoever does a mote's weight of good shall see it"* (99:7), and the tongue's good is among the most available of all goods.

**The Gifts as Witnesses for the Reckoning**

The deeper logic of verses 8–9 is the one the source commentary states plainly: the God who made these subtle instruments certainly knows every deed and will hold the human being accountable for what he did with them. The gifts are the witnesses. The eye that saw and the tongue that spoke are not neutral equipment; they are the very members that will testify on the Day of Judgment — the Qur'an says of the disbelievers that on that Day, *"their tongues and their hands and their feet will bear witness against them as to what they used to do"* (24:24). This is the doctrine of the members as witnesses, and it is one of the most distinctive features of the Qur'anic picture of the reckoning: the human being's own body becomes the archive of his life. The eye remembers what it was shown; the tongue remembers what it uttered; the lips remember what they sealed or released. The man who imagines that no one sees him (v. 7) will find on that Day that his own members see — and speak. The gifts that should have led him to gratitude will instead lead him to judgment, because gifts are always double-edged: they are either used for the purpose for which they were given, or they become evidence against the one who misused them.

**The Modern Multiplication of the Tongue**

The verse acquires fresh urgency in an age in which the human tongue has been amplified beyond anything the first audience could imagine. The tongue that in the seventh century could reach only the ears of a few listeners now reaches millions through broadcast, publication, and the platforms of social media. Every user of these instruments is, in effect, a broadcaster with a global tongue — and the Qur'anic question presses with new force: what is your tongue producing? The verse's inventory asks the modern reader to audit his speech the way he audits his spending: how much of what you say is true, kind, necessary, and beneficial — and how much is boast, complaint, mockery, rumor, or harm? The two lips that close are as important as the tongue that speaks: the discipline of not posting, not forwarding, not repeating, not escalating — these are the modern forms of the ancient virtue of guarding the tongue. And the reckoning that verse 9 implies has not been cancelled by the ephemerality of digital speech; rather, the Qur'an's ancient claim is newly demonstrable — every utterance leaves a trace, and the One who made the tongue and the lips has a perfect record of every word they formed. The believer's task is to make that record one he will be glad to read.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:10

> **and guide him upon the two highways?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inventory Reaches Its Climax**

The catalogue of gifts — eyes, tongue, lips — culminates in the greatest gift of all: guidance. *Wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayn* — "and We guided him upon the two highways." The word *najd* in Arabic denotes an elevated, clearly visible road — a highland track, a way that rises above the surrounding terrain and is therefore unmistakable to the traveler. The dual form, *al-najdayn*, gives two such ways. The commentators unanimously identify them as the way of good and the way of evil, the path of righteousness and the path of ruin. The source commentary cites the well-known report transmitted in the commentaries of al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and al-Ṭūsī:

> *"O people! There are only two highways: the highway of good and the highway of evil. Make not the highway of evil more beloved to you than the highway of good."*

And the Qur'an itself states the same truth in parallel: *"Truly We guided him upon the way, be he grateful or ungrateful"* (76:3). The verse is the pivot of the sūrah: everything before it established that the human being is seen, equipped, and accountable; this verse establishes that he is not left without a map. The God who created man in travail (v. 4) did not abandon him in the travail; He pointed the way out.

**What Guidance Means — and What It Does Not Mean**

The verb *hadaynāhu* requires careful attention. Guidance here is not compulsion. The two highways are both *shown* — the word *hadā* in this context means to point out, to make clear, to guide toward — but the human being is left to walk. The Qur'an is emphatic that divine guidance in this sense is universal and that the human being retains the power of choice: *"And [by] the soul and Him who proportioned it, and inspired it with its wickedness and its reverence; he who purifies it has succeeded, and he who buries it has failed"* (91:7–10). The two highways are the two possibilities planted in the human constitution itself — the capacity for wickedness (*fujūr*) and the capacity for reverence (*taqwā*) — and the guidance of verse 10 is the showing of both, so that the choice is real. A choice that is not informed is not a choice; God's justice requires that no one be destroyed for a path he was never shown. The verse thus answers a question that troubles every thoughtful conscience: how can a person be held accountable for his destiny if God controls everything? The Qur'an's answer is that accountability rests precisely on guidance: the roads were shown, the faculties were given, the messengers were sent — *"so that mankind might have no argument against God after the messengers"* (4:165). The two highways are open, visible, and equally accessible; the walking is the human being's own.

**The Clarity of the Roads**

The image of the *najd* — the high, elevated way — carries a lesson about the visibility of moral choice. A road that runs along high ground can be seen from a distance; the traveler knows where it leads before he commits to it. The Qur'an's moral universe is built on this clarity: the Qur'an does not present right and wrong as a murky fog in which honest seekers cannot find their way. The two ways are *distinct*: the way of faith, patience, mercy, and the freeing of captives (the steep pass of vv. 11–17), and the way of denial, boastfulness, and heedlessness (vv. 5–7). No one is asked to choose between two equally attractive goods or two equally defensible moral systems; the choice is between a road that is hard but leads to salvation and a road that is easy but leads to the Fire enclosed. The difficulty of the moral life is never presented in the Qur'an as a difficulty of *knowledge* — it is a difficulty of *will*. The human being knows the good; the struggle is in doing it. This is why verse 10 precedes the verses on the steep pass: first the roads are shown, then the harder road is described — and the whole structure assumes that the reader, having seen both highways clearly, is now in a position to choose.

**Guidance Built Into Creation**

The commentators also noted that the guidance of verse 10 is not limited to the revelation of scriptures; it is woven into the very structure of the human being. The instincts themselves point: the conscience distinguishes the noble from the base, the healthy soul inclines toward truth and recoils from falsehood, and the Prophet ﷺ described this innate moral compass in a famous report:

> *"Righteousness is good character, and sin is that which wavers in your soul and which you dislike that people should know about."* (Muslim 2553, from al-Nawwās ibn Samʿān)

Added to the innate guidance is the guidance of the senses and reason — the eyes of verse 8 that perceive consequences, the tongue of verse 9 that can ask and learn — and over all of it the guidance of revelation, the messengers, and the Book. The two highways are thus lit by every lamp God has given: creation, conscience, reason, scripture, prophecy. The person who claims he cannot see the way is like the man who complains of darkness at noon with his two eyes open; the guidance was given, the highways were shown, and the only remaining question is which one he will walk.

**The Freedom That Makes Us Human**

Verse 10 is, in the deepest sense, a verse about the dignity of the human being. To be guided upon the two highways is to be treated as a responsible agent — not a puppet, not an animal driven by instinct, but a creature capable of seeing a moral landscape and choosing a direction within it. The angels, in the Qur'anic account, asked God why He would place a vicegerent on earth who would cause corruption (2:30) — and the divine answer is implicit in the very structure of this sūrah: the human being is the creature who was made in kabad, given eyes and tongue and lips, shown the two highways, and then left — not abandoned, but honored — to climb the steep pass by his own free choice. A being that could not choose wrong could not choose right in any meaningful sense; a being that could not fall could not rise. The two highways are the condition of the possibility of moral greatness. The believer reads verse 10, therefore, not as a burden but as a distinction: God did not create me a machine; He created me a chooser, and He showed me the roads so that my choice could be real, my effort meaningful, and my reward genuine.

**Choosing the Highway Every Day**

The verse closes the second movement of the sūrah and opens the third. With the eyes, the tongue, the lips, and the two highways in view, the sūrah now turns to the question that matters: *which* highway is the human being actually walking? The man of verses 5–7 spoke of squandered wealth; the coming verses will show that his spending, whatever its magnitude, never touched the road that counts. The two highways stand before every reader at every moment — not merely at the great crossroads of life, but in the small daily decisions: how to earn, how to spend, how to speak, how to treat the orphan and the indigent, whether to bear the difficulty of the good deed or take the easy road of the boast. Verse 10 is the divine declaration of the human being's moral situation: the roads are open, the light is clear, the choice is yours — and the verses that follow will describe, with unforgettable concreteness, what the harder road actually looks like: not a vague spiritual aspiration, but the freeing of a slave, the feeding of a hungry orphan, faith, patience, and mercy. The two highways have names, and the sūrah is about to show where each one leads.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:11

> **Yet he has not assailed the steep pass.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict After the Inventory**

With verse 11 the sūrah delivers its judgment: *fa-lā iqtaḥama al-ʿaqabah* — "Yet he has not assailed the steep pass." The particle *fa-lā* carries the force of a conclusion drawn from everything that has preceded: after the creation in travail, after the two eyes, the tongue, the lips, after the two highways clearly shown — *yet* he has not done the one thing that matters. The verb *iqtaḥama* is chosen with great care. Its root *q-ḥ-m* conveys the idea of breaking into something difficult — plunging in, forcing one's way, taking on a task that intimidates. The commentators glossed it variously: al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī define it as doing something with great intensity and difficulty; al-Ālūsī and al-Qurṭubī as throwing oneself into a matter without flinching from its consequences. The word is used of a horseman who spurs his mount straight into the mouth of a battle, or of a man who wades into a raging torrent. The *ʿaqabah* is the steep, rugged mountain path — the pass that rises sharply and must be climbed with effort, the road that the traveler would rather avoid. The verse's image is thus vivid and physical: the steep pass is before him, the way over the mountain — and he has not thrown himself into it. He has looked at it, perhaps praised it, perhaps pointed others to it; but he has not *assailed* it. He has not paid the price of the climb.

**Who Is the "He" of the Verse?**

The referent of *he* is deliberately general, and the commentators traced it through the context. In the immediate frame, it is the man of verses 5–7 — the one who boasted of squandered wealth, who supposed that no power could seize him and no eye could see him. The verse tells him: all your spending, whatever its magnitude, never touched the one expenditure that matters — you lavished wealth and called it greatness, but you never assailed the steep pass. The source commentary adds a second reading: the *he* includes those who contribute money for religious purposes and then regret it, muttering as the man of verse 6 muttered, *I have squandered vast wealth* — such givers have given, but they have not given in the way of the pass; their giving was weighed down by reluctance and undone by regret, and so they never achieved the full measure of sincerity. And beyond these particular figures stands every human being: the verse is a universal audit. *He has not assailed the steep pass* is the verdict that awaits anyone — whatever his wealth, whatever his reputation, whatever his religious claims — whose life has not been marked by the costly, difficult, sincere deeds that the coming verses will enumerate. The verse does not ask whether the reader has been busy, successful, or even generous by the world's measure; it asks a single question: have you climbed?

**The Steep Pass as the Costly Good Deed**

What, then, is the *ʿaqabah*? The commentators gave the image several dimensions, and they are complementary rather than competing. The primary reading, which the sūrah itself establishes by the verses that follow (13–17), is that the steep pass is a metaphor for the difficult, meritorious deed — the act of worship and goodness that costs the soul something: the freeing of a slave, the feeding of the hungry at a time of famine, faith, patience, mercy. Al-Zamakhsharī glossed the verse with a striking parallel from the Qur'an itself, comparing the spending of those who give without sincerity to:

> *"The parable of what they spend in this life of the world is that of a frigid wind that strikes the tillage of a people who have wronged themselves, destroying it. God wrongs them not, but themselves do they wrong."* (Qur'an 3:117)

The image of the frigid wind destroying the crop is the exact opposite of the steep pass: the wind costs the farmer nothing and destroys everything, while the pass costs the climber everything and builds what cannot be destroyed. The commentators — al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī among them — also read the steep pass as *mujāhadah*, the striving against the self for God, and they transmitted the famous saying of al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī:

> *"The steep pass of God is severe; it is the human being's striving against his soul, his caprice, and his enemy, Satan."*

Why must the good deed be imagined as a *climb*? Because the deeds that form the soul are precisely the ones that do not come naturally to the unformed soul. Feeding the hungry is easy when the pantry is full and the praise is public; it is a steep pass when the need is great, the stores are few, and no one is watching. Freeing a captive is easy to praise and hard to pay for. Patience is easy to admire and hard to practice. The steep pass is the Qur'an's way of saying that moral growth follows the same law as physical growth: no resistance, no strength; no ascent, no view. The person who spends his life on level ground — doing only what is easy, comfortable, and applauded — may travel far by the world's odometer and yet never rise an inch.

**The Pass as the Obstacle of the Hereafter**

A second line of interpretation, transmitted by al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ṭabarī, reads the *ʿaqabah* as one or more of the obstacles the disbelievers will encounter on the Day of Judgment — the bridge over the Fire, the reckoning, the scales, the requital itself. On this reading, verse 11 says: he has not prepared for the obstacles of the Hereafter; he has not crossed, in this life, the pass that would have carried him over the pass of the next life. The two readings converge beautifully: the steep pass of this world — the costly good deed — is the training ground for the steep pass of the next world. The believer who climbs the *ʿaqabah* of charity and patience in this life is, without knowing it, practicing the very movements he will need when the real mountain looms: the same surrender of self, the same trust in God, the same refusal to turn back. The one who never climbs here will find himself utterly unprepared there — facing, for the first time in his existence, a difficulty he never trained for. Every athlete understands the logic; the Qur'an applies it to the soul.

**The Grace Hidden in the Rebuke**

There is a hidden mercy in the verse's very structure. *He has not assailed the steep pass* is a verdict, but it is pronounced in a sūrah that is busy explaining, in the verses immediately following, exactly what the steep pass is and how it is climbed. The verse does not say, *he never will assail it*; it says, *he has not yet assailed it* — and the door of *yet* is the door of hope. The sūrah's rebukes are always diagnostic, never fatalistic: the Qur'an exposes the disease in order to prescribe the cure, and the prescription follows within three verses. The man who reads verse 11 and recognizes himself — the spender who never gave what cost him, the religious person whose religion never reached his wallet or his temper, the complacent soul that has circled the mountain of the pass for years without ever beginning the climb — such a reader is precisely the one the sūrah is addressing, and the address is an invitation disguised as a verdict. The steep pass is steep, but it is not unclimbable; it has footholds, and the sūrah is about to name them one by one: free the captive, feed the hungry, believe, enjoin patience, enjoin mercy. The climb begins with the next verse's answer.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:12

> **And what will apprise thee of the steep pass?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Demands Explanation**

Verse 12 is a rhetorical device of the Qur'an that appears whenever a term of great weight is introduced: *wa-mā adrāka mā al-ʿaqabah* — "And what will apprise thee of the steep pass?" The formula occurs a dozen times in the Qur'an, always at the introduction of a reality so tremendous that ordinary language cannot convey it: *"And what will apprise thee of the Night of Decree?"* (97:2); *"And what will apprise thee of the Crusher?"* (104:5); *"And what will apprise thee of the Day of Judgment?"* (82:17). The question is not asked because the speaker does not know; it is asked to stop the listener short — to create in his mind a space of expectation, a suspension in which the inadequacy of his present understanding is felt. The effect is like a guide pausing at the foot of a mountain the traveler has never seen, turning and asking: *Do you have any idea what climbing that will involve?* The pause itself is part of the lesson: the steep pass is not a metaphor the reader already possesses; it must be revealed, and the revelation will overturn his assumptions about what is difficult, what is valuable, and what is real.

**A Question That Both Humbles and Honours**

The formula carries two movements at once. It humbles: it confesses, on behalf of the human listener, that he does not know the true magnitude of the things of God — that his imagination, left to itself, will always underestimate the steepness of the steep pass and the weight of the reckoning. Al-Qurṭubī and others noted that this is precisely the pedagogic function of the question: the Qur'an could have simply defined the *ʿaqabah* directly, but it chose instead to announce the inadequacy of the listener's frame before filling it — because a person who thinks he already knows what goodness costs will not be ready to hear the actual price. And the formula also honors: the fact that the Qur'an *explains* the steep pass at all — that God condescends to apprise His servant of the way of salvation — is itself a mercy beyond what the servant deserves. The question *what will apprise thee?* is, in effect, the divine announcement: I am about to apprise you. The One who asks the question is the One who answers it — and the answer occupies the next five verses, the very heart of the sūrah.

**Reading Verse 12 as an Implied Condition**

The source commentary notes that many commentators — al-Qurṭubī and others — read the verse in a particular way in light of what follows: *"What will apprise thee of assailing the steep pass?"* — that is, the question points not merely to the identity of the pass but to the *manner of assailing* it. On this reading, the verse means: you do not know what it truly takes to assail the pass — so listen while I tell you. The implied answer then unfolds in verses 13–17 as a single, connected sentence: the assailing of the steep pass is the freeing of a slave, or the feeding of an orphan near of kin or an indigent clinging to the dust, while being one of those who believe and exhort one another to patience and compassion. The five verses form one grammatical unit, and the whole unit is the answer to the question of verse 12. This is why the question is posed in the first place: it announces that what follows is not a list of optional charities but the definition of the hardest and most important act in human life — an act so demanding that the Qur'an must slow the reader down, make him pause, and force him to attend to the answer the way a student attends to the solution of a problem he has failed to solve.

**The Steep Pass and Spiritual Knowledge**

The spiritual commentators added a further depth. Al-Tustarī (d. 283/896), the early Sufi master whose commentary the source cites, likened assailing the steep pass to attaining spiritual knowledge (*maʿrifah*) — the knowledge of God that can only be reached through the power and strength of God, by which one frees the soul from the grip of desire. On this reading, the *ʿaqabah* is the interior ascent: the long climb of the soul from the lowlands of appetite and self-worship toward the heights of gnosis and divine proximity. The climb is steep because the obstacles are internal — the soul's own habits, its love of ease, its attachment to what it lusts for — and it can be climbed only by a strength that is not the climber's own. Al-Tustarī's reading does not displace the social reading of verses 13–17; it deepens it. The believer who frees the slave without freeing his own soul from desire has climbed only the first foothills; the believer who feeds the orphan but remains enslaved to his own appetites has not fully assailed the pass. The complete climb is outward and inward at once: the hand that gives and the heart that is given, the body that serves and the soul that is served, the creature freed and the self freed — for, as the coming commentary on verse 13 will note, the one who frees another's neck is himself freeing his own.

**Why the Question Still Matters Today**

Modern readers may be tempted to hurry past the rhetorical question to the answer — but the question was placed in the text to do work that no direct statement could do. Direct statement informs; the rhetorical question *transforms*. When the Qur'an says, *what will apprise thee of the steep pass?*, it is asking the reader to inventory his own ignorance before receiving the knowledge — and the person who has felt his ignorance is the person who will value the knowledge and act upon it. The question also performs a lifelong function: even after the answer of verses 13–17 has been recited a thousand times, the question remains open in the sense that the *full* magnitude of the pass — what it truly costs, what it truly yields — exceeds the comprehension of anyone still on this side of the grave. The believer climbs the pass for decades, and only at the summit does he understand what the guide's question meant at the foot of the mountain. *What will apprise thee of the steep pass?* — the answer begins in the next verse, unfolds through the next five, and will not be complete until the Day when the climbers are divided into the companions of the right and the companions of the left.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:13

> **[It is] the freeing of a slave,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Foothold of the Climb**

The answer to the question of verse 12 begins with a single, stunning phrase: *fakku raqabah* — "the freeing of a neck." The verse does not say "charity," or "generosity," or "kindness"; it names the most expensive, most difficult, most transformative good deed available in its world: the redemption of a captive. The word *fakk* means to release, to undo, to break open — the same root used for unfastening a knot or unsealing a bond — and *raqabah* literally means "neck," the metonymy by which Arabic refers to the whole person in bondage: the slave, whose neck is, so to speak, held in the grasp of another's ownership. The phrase is concrete in a way that modern ears must work to hear: the Qur'an is not speaking of a vague benevolence but of a specific, costly, world-altering act — taking wealth that could have built one's own security and using it to purchase the freedom of another human being. The source commentary records the Prophet's ﷺ own weighting of this deed:

> *"Whoever releases a believing slave (raqabah), he will be his ransom from the Fire."* (transmitted by al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī)

The image is mutual redemption: the believer pays to free the captive's neck in this world, and the act becomes the freeing of his own neck from the Fire in the next. The two freedoms are bound together in one transaction.

**Why Freedom Is the First Rung**

The sūrah's placement of manumission at the head of the list is itself a commentary on the priorities of Islam. The steep pass — the ascent to God — begins, not with a ritual or a formula, but with the restoration of a human being's freedom. This ordering is deliberate and revolutionary. In the world of the sūrah's first audience, slavery was a universal and unremarkable institution, woven into the economy of every empire and tribe; the Qur'an did not abolish it in a single decree, but it did something arguably more effective over time: it turned the freeing of slaves into the very definition of righteousness, the first deed named in the answer to the question of how a human being climbs to God. The Qur'an commands manumission as the expiation for major sins — *"And whoever kills a believer by mistake — the freeing of a believing slave"* (4:92); *"And whoever does not find [the means] — then the fasting of two months"* (4:92); and again for the expiation of a broken oath, *"the feeding of ten needy people... or the freeing of a neck"* (5:89). The institution of slavery was thereby placed under a constant, systematic pressure toward its own dissolution: every conscience was taught to see the captive's neck and to recognize in its release the highest worship. The scholars are unanimous that the manumission of a believing slave is among the greatest of good deeds — and some, as the source notes, argued from the very placement of this verse that freeing captives is superior even to general charity (ṣadaqah), since it appears here before the verses on feeding the hungry.

**The Double Freedom in Every Manumission**

There is a profound spiritual reading of the verse that the commentators developed — al-Rāzī among them — and it turns on the word *raqabah* itself. The verse can be read as commanding the believer to free, first of all, *his own* neck: the neck of his soul, bound by sin, habit, and heedlessness. The most pressing captivity is the inner one, and the greatest manumission is the release of the self from the bondage of its own appetites and errors through repentance and worship. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭūsī transmitted this reading: *fakku raqabah* — freeing one's own neck through repentance, or through the acts of worship by which one attains Paradise, which al-Rāzī calls the greatest freedom of all. The two readings are not rivals but mirrors: the believer who frees an enslaved body is simultaneously practicing the release of his own soul — every act of costly compassion loosens the soul's own bonds of selfishness — and the believer who first frees his own soul from the bondage of sin will find his hand naturally opening toward the captives of the world. The outer manumission is the sacrament of the inner one; the inner is the root of the outer. The steep pass cannot be climbed with a bound heart any more than with a closed hand.

**The Economics of Redemption**

The verse also carries a quiet but complete economic philosophy. Notice what the steep pass does *not* include: it does not include accumulating more, securing one's position, or enlarging one's estate. The first deed named in the climb to God is a deed of pure financial loss by the world's accounting — the purchase of another's freedom, from which the purchaser receives no commodity, no service, no return except the release itself. This is the Qur'an's deliberate inversion of the economic logic that rules the man of verse 6, who spoke of wealth in terms of what he had *spent* — as though spending were a loss — while the Qur'an here declares that the only spending that is not a loss is the spending that buys *freedom*, whether for a captive's body or for one's own soul. The verse challenges the modern reader to translate the principle into the equivalents of his own world: wherever a human being is held — by debt, by exploitation, by trafficking, by unjust imprisonment, by the modern forms of bondage that persist in every economy — the freeing of the captive remains the first rung of the pass. And wherever the believer encounters a soul bound by sin and ignorance, the *fakk* of counsel, education, and compassionate correction is the same deed in its spiritual form. The rung has not been removed from the pass; it has only been renamed.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Own Example**

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ embodied this verse as thoroughly as any verse of the Qur'an. His biography is punctuated by manumissions: he freed slaves he had purchased, slaves given to him, and captives of war — most famously Zayd ibn Ḥārithah, whom he adopted as a son and freed, and Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ, the Abyssinian slave whom he raised to the station of the first muezzin of Islam, whose freedom was purchased by Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq from the torment of Umayyah ibn Khalaf. The Prophet ﷺ declared the freeing of slaves among the deeds that most surely carry a believer to Paradise, and he instructed:

> *"Feed the hungry, visit the sick, and free the captive."* (al-Bukhārī 3046, transmitted by Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī)

The man who said this was not speaking of an abstraction; he was describing the daily texture of the community he built. And his own example set the direction of the entire civilization that followed: from the first generation, the freeing of captives became a standing institution — zakāh funds were channeled toward manumission, expiations were built around it, and the jurists developed elaborate provisions by which slaves could contract their own freedom. The verse of al-Balad stands at the head of that long, world-changing current: the steep pass of God begins where a human being's freedom is restored.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:14

> **or giving food at a time of famine**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Foothold: Food at the Moment of Need**

The second rung of the steep pass is feeding: *aw iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghabah* — "or the giving of food on a day of hunger." The word *masghabah* (from *s-gh-b*) denotes severe hunger, the kind that gnaws; the phrase "a day of hunger" means a time of famine, scarcity, or general want — precisely the moment when food is scarce, prices are high, and the temptation to hoard is strongest. The verse does not commend feeding the hungry in the abstract or at one's convenience; it commends feeding *on the day of hunger* — the day when giving food is hardest and most needed. This is the Qur'an's characteristic insistence that the moral value of an act is measured not by its publicity but by its cost: the same loaf of bread is a small deed in a season of abundance and a great deed in a season of famine; the same coin is trifling to the rich man's surplus and decisive to the poor man's survival. The verse aligns moral weight with economic difficulty, and in doing so it teaches a principle that runs through all of Islamic ethics: the deed that costs nothing is worth nothing in the ledger of the soul. As the source commentary notes, al-Rāzī glossed this verse with two Qur'anic parallels:

> *"True righteousness is... giving wealth, despite loving it, to kinsfolk, orphans, the needy, the traveler, beggars, and for the freeing of necks."* (Qur'an 2:177)

And:

> *"And they give food, despite loving it, to the indigent, the orphan, and the captive, saying, 'We feed you only for the face of God; we desire no recompense from you, nor thanks.'"* (Qur'an 76:8–9)

In both parallels the key phrase is "despite loving it" — the giving is done *while* the giver still loves what he gives, at the moment when parting with it is genuinely costly. That is the giving of the steep pass.

**The Psychology of Giving in Scarcity**

The verse understands human psychology with precision. In a time of famine, the natural mind shifts into survival mode: scarcity triggers hoarding, suspicion, and the tightening of the hand; the person who gives food when there is little food performs an act that contradicts his own survival instinct. Why would the Qur'an place such a demanding act at the center of the climb to God? Because the giving that contradicts the survival instinct is the giving that proves the reality of faith. When the believer parts with food on a hungry day, he is declaring, in the most convincing language available to the body, that his true provision comes from God and not from his storehouse — that the God who fed him yesterday will feed him tomorrow, even if the granary is lighter today. The Qur'an states this logic in the most famous of its promises: *"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, that He may multiply it for him many times over?"* (2:245). The loan metaphor is exact: lending implies trust in the borrower's return, and the believer who feeds the hungry on the day of hunger is lending to the Owner of all granaries, whose repayment cannot fail. The hoarder trusts his stores; the believer trusts his Lord. The verse asks which trust is better placed — and history, in every famine and every crisis, has answered: the stores of the hoarders rot or are seized, while the believer who gave in the day of hunger finds himself fed in the day of his own need, often by hands he never expected.

**From Famine to the Modern Forms of Hunger**

The verse's principle translates directly into the conditions of modern life, where famine takes forms the first audience would recognize and forms they would not. There is still literal famine — the acute hunger of war zones, drought-stricken regions, and refugee camps — and the verse's command applies to it without translation: the believer feeds the hungry when feeding is difficult, and the organized giving of zakāh and ṣadaqah to famine relief is the modern *iṭʿām* on the day of *masghabah*. But hunger also wears modern clothing: the hunger of the working poor who must choose between food and medicine; the hunger of children who eat only at school; the hunger of the elderly whose pensions have been devoured by inflation; the hunger of the unemployed whose skills have been made obsolete; the hunger of dignity itself — the person who is not starving but is ashamed, isolated, and unseen. The Qur'an's insistence on *feeding* rather than merely *advising* is a standing rebuke to every form of piety that stops at words: the steep pass is climbed with bread, with rice, with the actual transfer of actual food to an actual stomach. The believer who has never felt the specific discomfort of giving when his own resources felt thin has not yet fully felt this rung of the pass beneath his feet.

**Why Food Is the Universal Language of Care**

There is a reason the Qur'an returns to food again and again as the currency of compassion — it is named in this sūrah, in 2:177, in 76:8, in the expiation laws of 5:89 and 58:4, in the description of the righteous in 70:24–25 and of the inhabitants of the Garden in 52:22. Food is the most basic, most universal, most non-negotiable of human needs: every human being must eat, the rich and the poor alike, and the giving of food is therefore the one form of care that can never be accused of irrelevance. It is also the form of care that most directly imitates the divine care: God is *al-Razzāq*, the Provider, and the believer who feeds the hungry performs, in miniature, the work of the One who *"spread the earth and placed upon it firm mountains and rivers, and of every fruit He made in it two kinds"* (13:3). The act of feeding is thus a form of worship that requires no scholarship, no ritual purity, no special station — only a hand that opens and food to give. This is why it stands on the steep pass: it is the most democratic of the great deeds, available to the poorest believer in a form proportionate to his means, and yet great enough — when done on the day of hunger, for the face of God — to ransom the giver from the Fire. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"O people! Spread peace, feed food, join the ties of kinship, and pray by night while the people sleep — and you will enter Paradise in peace."* (Ibn Mājah 1334; al-Tirmidhī 2485, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām)

The verse of al-Balad is the same teaching carved into the definition of the steep pass: the way up the mountain runs through the kitchen of the hungry.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:15

> **to an orphan near of kin,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Foothold Names the Recipients**

Having commanded the giving of food on the day of hunger, the sūrah now specifies *whom* the believer should feed, and the specification is a lesson in the priorities of compassion. The first named recipient is *yatīman dhā maqrabah* — "an orphan near of kin." The verse thereby joins two meritorious acts into one: the care of orphans and the maintenance of kinship ties. The commentators — al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī among them, as the source notes — observed that the deed is doubled: the giver who feeds a related orphan is simultaneously performing the duty of kindness toward the orphan and the duty of kinship toward the relative, and the reward is compounded accordingly. The word *yatīm* in Arabic usage denotes, as the source commentary notes, a child whose father has died — and some scholars extended the definition to one who has lost both parents — the most vulnerable member of the pre-modern social order: a child without a protector, without an inheritance until maturity, without a voice. And *dhā maqrabah* — "possessor of kinship" — adds the dimension of proximity: this is not a distant abstraction but the orphan who is *yours*, the child of your brother or sister, of your uncle or cousin, the child whose face you see at family gatherings, whose father was your father's brother, whose loss is your loss.

**Why the Orphan of the Family First**

The order is deliberate, and its logic is social as well as moral. Charity, the Qur'an teaches, begins at the point of one's own responsibility: the relative who is needy has a claim upon the believer that the stranger does not, and to leave one's own kin in hunger while feeding strangers is a moral inversion that the Qur'an condemns: *"And give the kinsman his due, and the needy, and the traveler"* (17:26; 30:38). The orphan near of kin is the person whose need is most visible to the believer and whose care is most specifically his duty — the child whom the believer's own tribe, family, and faith are obliged to shelter. In the social world of the sūrah's first audience, the orphan of the clan was the clan's own test: the pre-Islamic Arabs, for all their boasts of generosity, were notorious for devouring the inheritances of orphans and for treating the fatherless as fair game. The Qur'an's relentless defense of the orphan — *"As for the orphan, do not oppress him"* (93:9); *"They ask you about orphans; say: to set right their affairs is best"* (2:220); *"Truly those who devour the wealth of orphans unjustly only devour fire into their bellies"* (4:10) — was aimed at a society in which the orphan was the classic victim. To name the orphan *near of kin* as the first recipient of the food of the steep pass is to say: your first and highest charity is not the grand donation to the distant cause but the daily, unglamorous, unphotographed care of the child your family has been entrusted with. The believer who would climb to God begins by looking at the children closest to him who have no father — and asking what they need.

**The Psychological Weight of Kinship Care**

The verse also understands the particular moral psychology of kinship. Feeding a distant stranger can be done at arm's length, with the heart only lightly engaged; feeding an orphan who is one's own kin engages everything — the family memory, the shared name, the face of the dead brother in the features of the living child. This is why kinship charity is weightier: it cannot be performed as a performance, because the audience is the family itself, and the giver's sincerity or stinginess will be written into the family's story for generations. The related orphan is also the recipient most likely to be *resented* — the child whose existence reminds the family of obligations it would rather forget, whose inheritance is a temptation to the very relatives who should protect it. The Qur'an's command to feed the orphan near of kin is therefore a command to overcome the two great enemies of kinship charity: forgetfulness (the orphan quietly ceases to be a priority) and covetousness (the orphan's share is quietly absorbed into the family's own uses). The verse forces the believer to look at the child and see not a burden or an opportunity but a rung of the steep pass placed in his own household by God.

**The Widening Circles of the Pass**

The sūrah's sequence of recipients is worth reading as a single motion: first the orphan of one's own kin — the closest need; then, in the next verse, the indigent stranger clinging to the dust — the furthest need. Between the two, the entire field of human need is spanned: the believer's compassion must reach both the child whose father he knew and the beggar whose face he has never seen, both the need that is his specific duty and the need that is his general responsibility. This is the Qur'an's consistent geometry of care: the circles of obligation radiate outward from the self — the self, the family, the kin, the neighbor, the needy stranger, the captive, the traveler — and the believer is required to keep every circle in view, never sacrificing the near duty for the distant spectacle, never using the overwhelming scale of the world's need as an excuse to neglect the need at his own door. The orphan near of kin is the near circle; the indigent of the next verse is the far circle; the steep pass crosses both.

**The Prophet ﷺ and the Orphan**

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was himself an orphan — his father ʿAbd Allāh died before his birth, and his mother Āminah died when he was six — and the Qur'an's commands concerning orphans are addressed, throughout, to a man who knew the orphan's specific loneliness from within. The later Makkan sūrah, al-Ḍuḥā (93), consoles him with the divine remembrance: *"Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?"* (93:6) — and the same sūrah that commands the believer not to oppress the orphan (93:9) is the sūrah of the orphan-prophet. In Sūrah al-Balad, the command to feed the orphan near of kin is placed in the mouth of a revelation given to a man who was, in his own childhood, precisely such an orphan — protected first by his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and then by his uncle Abū Ṭālib. The Prophet ﷺ carried the orphan's cause into his teaching with a tenderness his Companions recorded:

> *"I and the one who cares for an orphan will be like this in Paradise"* — and he gestured with his two fingers, the forefinger and the middle finger, held together. (al-Bukhārī 6005; Muslim 2983, from Sahl ibn Saʿd)

The believer who reads verse 15 is standing, in effect, before that gesture: the care of the orphan — especially the orphan of one's own kin — is not a marginal charity in Islam but one of the surest roads to the company of the Prophet ﷺ in the Garden. The steep pass is steep, but at its summit waits the Prophet, holding two fingers together.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:16

> **or an indigent, clinging to the dust,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Recipient: The One Who Clings to the Ground**

The specification of recipients continues, and the second figure is painted with a single unforgettable phrase: *aw miskīnan dhā matrabah* — "or an indigent one, possessor of the dust." The word *miskīn* denotes the destitute, the one crushed by poverty, the person whose need has reduced him to the most abject condition. And *dhā matrabah* is the Qur'an's own definition of that abjectness: the root *t-r-b* is the word for dust, earth, soil — and the phrase describes the one whom poverty has pressed down until he clings to the dust, who has no cushion but the ground, no roof but the sky, no bed but the earth. The classical commentators explained the image directly: as the source commentary notes, al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī described the *matrabah* as one who has no home other than the dust — the person so utterly dispossessed that the earth itself is his only possession. Some of the early readers of the Qur'an — among them Ibn ʿAbbās, as transmitted in the commentaries — took the word even more concretely: the indigent so reduced that he is compelled to sit in the dust by the walls and roadsides, his cheek almost against the ground, his hand outstretched at the level of the traveler's shoes. The verse paints the lowest point of human destitution — and then commands the believer to feed precisely that person.

**The Dust as the Equalizer**

The image of the dust carries meanings that deepen with reflection. The dust is where all human pretension ends: every boastful man of verse 6 — every spender of heaped-up wealth — will himself return to the dust, as the Qur'an never tires of reminding: *"From it We created you, and to it We return you"* (20:55). The indigent clinging to the dust is, therefore, a living memento mori — a walking reminder to the wealthy that the distance between the palace and the dust is one breath, one market crash, one illness, one generation. The man who turns away from the miskīn of verse 16 is turning away from a mirror. And the dust is also the great equalizer of human dignity in another sense: the Qur'an states that the human being was *created from dust* (3:59; 22:5; 35:11) — so that the indigent who clings to the dust shares his origin with the richest man who ever lived. Both were shaped from the same earth; the only difference between them is the distribution of provision, which God outspreads and straitens *"that it not become a commodity circulating among the rich of you"* (59:7). To feed the one who clings to the dust is to honor the shared origin — to acknowledge, with bread, that the dust of the poor and the dust of the rich are the same dust, and that the One who raised both from it will ask both what they did with the interval between.

**The Verse's Rebuke to the Averted Eye**

There is a specific moral failure that verse 16 exposes, and it is the failure of the averted gaze. The indigent clinging to the dust is, by definition, hard to avoid seeing and easy to avoid *acknowledging*: he is at the roadside, at the gate, at the door of the mosque — precisely where the comfortable must step past him to go about their day. The Qur'an describes the comfortable man's interior monologue in a parallel passage: *"And as for him who is miserly and deems himself self-sufficient, and denies the best — We shall ease his way toward hardship"* (92:8–10). The stingy man's self-sufficiency is the psychological engine of his stinginess: he does not need the poor; he does not see himself in the poor; his wealth has built a wall between his eyes and the dust. Verse 16 is the command to breach the wall: the believer is ordered not merely to give when asked, but to *see* — to look at the indigent with the full attention that his humanity requires, and to recognize in the figure clinging to the dust a person with a name, a history, a dignity, and a claim. The Prophet ﷺ made the failure to see the poor a matter of the soundness of faith itself:

> *"He is not a believer who eats his fill while his neighbor goes hungry beside him."* (al-Ḥākim in al-Mustadrak, who said its chain is sound; from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The verse of the miskīn is the same principle applied at the scale of the whole community: the indigent is not an interruption of the believer's day; he is an appointment in it.

**The Modern Indigent Who Clings to the Dust**

Every age produces its own figures who cling to the dust, and the believer who would climb the steep pass must learn to recognize them in their contemporary dress. The literal figure remains: the street-dweller whose address is a pavement, the refugee who has lost country and home, the child of the slum whose playground is the garbage heap, the millions for whom the earth is still literally the only bed. But the *matrabah* of the modern world also wears less visible forms: the "new poor" who have fallen from the middle class and hide their destitution behind a maintained appearance; the working poor whose wages never quite reach the level of survival; the elderly whose dignity has been crushed by inflation and isolation; the mentally ill whose illness has cost them every human tie. The Qur'an's phrase *dhā matrabah* — "possessor of the dust" — is a diagnostic instrument: it asks the believer to look past the surface of respectability and ask who, in his city, has been pressed down to the ground — who has no cushion but the dust of social abandonment — and then to direct his food, his wealth, and his advocacy precisely there. The steep pass does not run through the charity gala; it runs through the places where the indigent cling to the dust.

**The Dignity of the Recipient in the Grammar of the Verse**

Finally, note the Qur'an's grammatical courtesy. The verse does not say "the filthy beggar" or "the useless pauper"; it says *miskīnan dhā matrabah* — an indigent *who possesses* the dust. The word *dhā* (possessor of) is the same construction used throughout the Qur'an to honor: *dhū al-faḍl* — the possessor of bounty; *dhū al-ʿarsh* — the Possessor of the Throne. Applied to the indigent, it declares that even the person reduced to the dust *possesses* something — his own humanity, his own claim, his own honor in the sight of God — and that his poverty has not stripped him of the status of a possessor. The believer is commanded to see, in the one who owns nothing, a dignity that wealth cannot confer and poverty cannot remove. This is why the feeding of the steep pass is not alms-giving from the heights — a lordly toss of coins to a creature below — but the recognition of a fellow human being: the one who feeds and the one who is fed are both, in the end, indigent before God, both clinging to the dust from which they were created, both fed by a Provider whose stores never fail. The verse that begins by describing the lowest human condition ends by disclosing the universal human condition — and the believer who feeds the indigent for the face of God has understood that he, too, is fed.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:17

> **while being one of those who believe and exhort one another to patience, and exhort one another to compassion.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition That Makes the Deeds Valid**

The definition of the steep pass reaches its climax in verse 17, and the verse performs a crucial logical function: it attaches a condition to everything that precedes it. The freeing of the slave, the feeding of the orphan, the feeding of the indigent — none of these deeds climbs the steep pass *by itself*. The verse declares: all of this avails only *thumma kāna min alladhīna āmanū* — "then he is among those who believe" — and who, in addition, exhort one another to patience and compassion. The particle *thumma* ("then," "and moreover") marks the sequence: first the costly deed, then the faith that gives it meaning — or rather, the deed and the faith must be fused. The source commentary notes that the commentators read this verse as the statement of the requisite condition for divine acceptance, citing the Qur'an's own verdict on unbelief-laden giving:

> *"And naught prevented their spending from being accepted from them, save that they disbelieved in God and in His Messenger, and only come to the prayer lazily, and only spend reluctantly."* (Qur'an 9:54)

The logic is airtight: deeds are judged not by their outward magnitude but by their inner source. A deed performed without faith is like a body without a soul — it may have the shape of goodness, but it lacks the life that makes goodness pleasing to God. The Qur'an states the principle in the most sweeping terms: *"And whoever does a righteous deed, male or female, while being a believer — those will enter the Garden"* (4:124; 16:97). The formula is constant: the deed *plus* faith. The steep pass is climbed by believers; the same actions performed by disbelievers may benefit the world — the slave is freed, the hungry are fed — but they do not carry the performer up the mountain, because the ascent is a journey *toward God*, and only the one who believes in God and seeks His face is traveling toward Him. Everyone else, however busy his hands, is walking on level ground.

**Patience: The Vertical Virtue**

The verse then names the two virtues that the believers exhort one another to — and the first is *ṣabr*, patience. The commentators explained that the exhortation to patience (*tawāṣaw bi-al-ṣabr*) pertains to perseverance in religious practice in the face of difficulties and opposition: the patience to keep praying when prayer is burdensome, to keep giving when giving is costly, to keep the commands of God when the world mocks them, to endure the long climb of the steep pass without abandoning it halfway. Patience is the virtue that makes all other virtues durable: charity that cannot endure becomes a one-time gesture; faith that cannot endure becomes a phase; love that cannot endure becomes a sentiment. The Qur'an places patience at the very center of the moral life — *"O you who believe, seek help through patience and prayer"* (2:153); *"And surely We shall test you with something of fear and hunger and loss of wealth and lives and fruits; and give glad tidings to the patient"* (2:155) — and the sūrah of al-Balad places it on the steep pass because the pass is long and the climb is hard: the believer who cannot be patient will turn back at the first switchback. The verse says the believers do not merely *possess* patience; they *exhort one another* to it — *tawāṣaw* — the mutual encouragement of fellow climbers, each steadying the other when the ascent seems endless.

**Compassion: The Horizontal Virtue**

The second virtue is *marḥamah*, compassion — rendered in the source translation as "compassion" and closely related to the divine name al-Raḥmān, the Compassionate. If patience is the vertical virtue (the believer's steadfastness toward God through time and trial), compassion is the horizontal virtue (the believer's mercy toward God's creatures). The commentators — al-Rāzī among them, as the source notes — read the exhortation to compassion as the encouragement of mutual mercy among human beings: the believers urge one another to be gentle with the weak, forgiving with the erring, generous with the needy, tender with the orphan and the indigent whose feeding was commanded in the previous verses. The two exhortations together complete the definition of the steep pass: the climb requires *both* the strength to endure (patience) and the softness to care (compassion) — the spine and the heart, the firmness that does not break and the gentleness that does not bruise. The Prophet ﷺ bound the two together in a single image of the community:

> *"The believers, in their mutual love, compassion, and sympathy, are like a single body: if one of its members complains, the rest of the body responds with sleeplessness and fever."* (al-Bukhārī 6011; Muslim 2586, from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr)

And the Qur'an's most famous statement of the divine response to human compassion is recorded by the commentators in connection with this verse — the report the source cites:

> *"The merciful are those unto whom the Merciful is merciful. Be merciful unto those on earth, and He who is in Heaven will be merciful unto you."* (al-Tirmidhī 1924, who graded it ṣaḥīḥ; Abū Dāwūd 4941, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr)

The logic of the steep pass is now complete: the believer is patient with God's decree and compassionate with God's creatures, and in both directions his virtue mirrors the divine reality — for God is both the Patient (al-Ṣabūr is among His names in the tradition) and the Compassionate (al-Raḥmān), and the human being who is patient and compassionate is, in the words of the ḥadīth, being merciful to those on earth so that the Merciful in Heaven is merciful to him.

**Why Exhortation Matters**

The verse's emphasis on *exhorting one another* — rather than merely possessing these virtues privately — is itself a complete social philosophy. The Qur'an could have said "who are patient and compassionate"; it says instead "who exhort one another to patience and exhort one another to compassion," and the difference is the difference between individual morality and collective moral culture. The believer is commanded not only to be good but to *build* goodness — to speak it, commend it, model it, and gently require it of his fellows. This is the same structure as the famous triple command of Sūrah al-ʿAṣr (103:3), which al-Balad's verse echoes: *"By time! Truly man is in loss, save those who believe and perform righteous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience."* A community of solitary saints is a contradiction in terms: virtue must be contagious or it will die, and the contagion is spread by the tongue — by the parent who tells the child why patience matters, the friend who reminds the friend why the hungry must be fed, the scholar who keeps the ummah's conscience awake. The modern believer who reads this verse is being asked a pointed question: who are you exhorting? Your own patience is a private treasure; the patience you *encourage* in others is a public good — and the verse places the public good on the steep pass.

**The Complete Definition of the Climb**

Reading verses 13–17 as the single answer to verse 12, the Qur'an's definition of the steep pass is now fully visible, and its completeness is its genius. The pass is: freeing the captive (justice to the enslaved), feeding the orphan of one's kin (duty to the family's vulnerable), feeding the indigent of the dust (mercy to the stranger), all upon faith (the vertical relation to God), sustained by mutual exhortation to patience (endurance through time) and compassion (tenderness toward creation). Every dimension of the moral life is spanned: the hand, the heart, the family, the stranger, the body, the soul, the world, and God. The man of verses 5–7 had one dimension only — wealth, spent and boasted of — and the sūrah's verdict was that he had not assailed the pass. The believer who would assail it must be a whole human being: one who gives what costs him, believes with his heart, endures with his patience, and loves with his mercy. This is the mountain; these are its footholds; and the remaining verses of the sūrah will show where the two roads of those who climb and those who refuse finally lead.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:18

> **Those are the companions of the right.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict of the Two Highways**

With verse 18 the sūrah delivers its verdict, and the verdict is pronounced in the Qur'an's own geography of salvation: *ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-maymanah* — "Those are the companions of the right." The demonstrative *those* gathers up everyone described in verses 13–17 — the believer who freed the captive, fed the orphan and the indigent, exhorted to patience and compassion — and assigns them their place in the order of the Hereafter: the right hand, the side of honor, blessing, and the Garden. The Qur'an's moral map is drawn with two sides throughout: the companions of the right (*aṣḥāb al-yamīn* or *al-maymanah*) and the companions of the left (*aṣḥāb al-mashʾamah* or *al-shimāl*). The source commentary directs the reader to the fullest portraits of the two companies in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah: the companions of the right are described at 56:27–40, and the companions of the left at 56:41–46. For the symbolic distinction between right and left throughout the Qur'an, the source also points to 17:71; 18:49; 56:8–9; 69:19–28; and 84:7 — the passages in which the books of deeds are given into the right hand or the left, and the two roads of the Hereafter are opened before the two companies.

**The Right Hand in the Qur'an's Moral Vocabulary**

The image of the right hand runs deep in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary, and each occurrence adds a shade of meaning. On the Day of Judgment, the blessed receive their records in the right hand and exclaim, with relief and joy: *"Take and read my book! Indeed I knew that I would meet my reckoning"* (69:19–20). The companions of the right in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah are described as *"the foremost"* in the Gardens of Delight, reclining on couches, served by immortal youths, amidst lote trees and acacia — the reward of *"those who are brought near"* and of *"the companions of the right"* (56:11–40). The right is also the side of the divine favor in the Qur'an's account of the very first divine-human encounter: the people of the Garden are gathered to the right (and the people of the Fire to the left) at 56:41, and the angels descend upon the believers with the greeting of peace. In the culture of the Qur'an's first audience — and of human beings generally — the right hand is the hand of strength, honor, and blessing: the hand that gives, the hand that is extended in peace, the hand by which oaths are taken and covenants are sealed. The Qur'an takes this universal human symbolism and consecrates it: the *aṣḥāb al-maymanah* are those who have lived in the manner of the right hand — giving rather than grasping, blessing rather than cursing, building rather than destroying — and whose final state is the perfect consummation of the pattern of their lives.

**Why the Companions of the Right Are Named "Companions"**

The word *aṣḥāb* — companions, fellows, associates — is worth pausing over. The Qur'an does not say "the winners" or "the successful" but "the companions," and the word is the same one used for the companions of the Prophet ﷺ and for the companions of the Fire. The choice of word implies a lasting fellowship: the blessed are not merely individuals who happened to be saved; they are a company, a society, a people bound together by what they shared in this world — the faith, the patience, the compassion, the mutual exhortation of verse 17 — and united forever in the next. The Islamic picture of the Garden is never of isolated souls enjoying private bliss but of a community of the righteous: *"And We shall remove whatever rancor is in their breasts; as brothers, upon couches, face to face"* (15:47). The companions of the right are companions *of one another* because they were companions in the steep pass: they climbed together, exhorted one another to patience (v. 17), fed the hungry together, and now they inherit together. The verse is a quiet rebuke to the solitary religiosity of the modern age — the person who believes he can climb alone, give alone, believe alone, without a community of mutual exhortation. The steep pass produces companions; the Garden receives companions.

**The Perfect Justice of the Placement**

The verdict of verse 18 is also a verdict about divine justice, and its logic deserves to be made explicit. The companions of the right are not assigned their station arbitrarily; they are assigned it because of what they *did* with what they were *given* — the eyes, the tongue, the lips, the two highways of verses 8–10. They saw the orphan and fed him; they saw the indigent in the dust and did not avert their eyes; they spent their wealth where it counted; they were patient when patience was hard and merciful when mercy was costly. The sūrah's entire argument has been that the human being was created in travail, equipped with faculties, shown the two highways, and left free to choose — and the companions of the right are the proof that the choice was real and the effort was not in vain. Their reward is not a bribe for belief but the natural fruition of belief: the soul that practiced mercy becomes fit for the company of the Merciful; the soul that practiced patience becomes fit for an eternity that demands no further patience; the soul that fed the hungry enters the Garden where no one hungers. The Qur'an says of them: *"Eat and drink in satisfaction for what you sent ahead in the days gone by"* (69:24) — the deeds of the world become the provisions of the Hereafter, and the companions of the right are those who provisioned themselves well.

**Hope for the Reader Standing at the Foot of the Pass**

Finally, the verse functions as the sūrah's great encouragement. The whole of the sūrah has been a demand — the steep pass, the costly deeds, the faith, the patience, the compassion — and verse 18 is the divine assurance that the demand is not a trap. The climb is hard, but the summit is real: *those* — the ones who freed the captives, who fed the orphans and the indigent, who believed and exhorted one another — *are the companions of the right.* The verse does not say that only the perfect climb the pass, or that only the wealthy can free slaves and feed the hungry; it describes deeds that are open, in some proportionate form, to every believer who has a hand to open and a heart to move. The believer who reads the sūrah and feels the weight of verses 11–17 pressing on his conscience is meant to feel, immediately afterward, the lift of verse 18: the pass is before you, the footholds have been named, the summit has been described — and the One who describes it is the One who created you in travail, gave you eyes and tongue and lips, and showed you the two highways. The companions of the right were not a different species; they were human beings who chose, day by day, deed by deed, to climb. The verse's final gift to the reader is the certainty that the same choice remains open — and that the right hand of God is extended to receive whoever assails the pass.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:19

> **And those who disbelieve in Our signs, they are the companions of the left.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Company and Its Defining Crime**

The sūrah's verdict now turns to the other side of the divide: *wa-alladhīna kafarū bi-āyātinā hum aṣḥābu al-mashʾamah* — "And those who disbelieve in Our signs, they are the companions of the left." The verse names the defining act of the second company with precision: they *disbelieved in Our signs* — the *āyāt* of God. The word *āyāt* (signs) is deliberately comprehensive: it covers the signs of God in creation — the heavens, the earth, the eye, the tongue, the lips, the two highways of the preceding verses — and the signs of God in revelation — the verses of the Book, the miracles of the messengers, the warnings and glad tidings. The companions of the left are those who were shown the signs and rejected them: who saw the design of the eye and denied the Designer; who heard the guidance of the two highways and chose neither to be grateful nor to climb; who were asked, *Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?* and answered with their lives: yes, he supposes. Their disbelief is not merely the absence of belief; it is an active refusal — a *kufr* that covers the truth as night covers the day, that buries the signs under the dust of heedlessness and arrogance. The source commentary directs the reader to the portrait of the companions of the left in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah (56:41–46), where they are described amid scorching wind and boiling water, in the shade of black smoke, "for they had been, before that, indulgent in luxury and had persisted in the great sin."

**The Left Hand and Its Symbolism**

The left, in the Qur'an's moral geography, is the side of loss. The people of the left receive their books in the left hand and cry out: *"Oh, would that I had not been given my book, and had not known my reckoning"* (69:25–26). The same hand that grasped and withheld in this world — the hand of the miser, the hoarder, the one who said *I have squandered vast wealth* without ever freeing a captive or feeding an orphan — receives, in the next world, the record of what it did, and the record is its ruin. The symbolism is consistent and deep: the right hand gives and therefore receives honor; the left hand grasps and therefore receives the book of grasping. In the culture of the Qur'an, the left hand was the hand of uncleanliness and contempt — the hand not extended in greeting, the hand not used for eating — and the Qur'an consecrates this universal symbolism into the geography of the Hereafter: the companions of the left are those who lived in the manner of the closed fist, and their final state is the consummation of their lives, just as the Garden consummates the lives of the companions of the right.

**The Justice of Matching the Crime to the Company**

The verse's logic is a logic of perfect correspondence, and the correspondence is worth drawing out. The companions of the left disbelieved in the *signs* — and the signs they rejected are precisely the instruments by which they might have been saved: the eyes that would have shown them the orphan, the tongue that would have exhorted to patience, the lips that would have spoken mercy, the two highways that would have led them to the steep pass. To disbelieve in the signs is to refuse the entire apparatus of salvation — to look at the eye and deny the Giver, to hear the guidance and deny the Guide, to stand at the foot of the pass and deny that the mountain exists. And because they denied the signs, they never performed the deeds of the signs: the freeing of the slave, the feeding of the orphan, the compassion of verse 17 — all of it presupposes faith in the One who commanded it, and the disbeliever, having rejected the Commander, never undertakes the command. The verse thus completes the sūrah's twofold portrait with perfect symmetry: the companions of the right are those who believed and therefore climbed; the companions of the left are those who disbelieved and therefore never began. The two companies are the two highways of verse 10, walked to their ends.

**The Absence of the Boastful Man's Wealth**

The reader who has followed the sūrah from its beginning will notice what is missing from the description of the companions of the left: there is no mention of their wealth, their lineage, their power, or their achievements. The man of verse 6 — the one who boasted *I have squandered vast wealth* — is gathered into this company, but the sūrah does not dignify his boast with a repetition. In the reckoning of the Hereafter, the heaped-up wealth (*māl lubadā*) that he thought made him great is not even mentioned; what is mentioned is his disbelief in the signs. The Qur'an states the same truth in the most famous of its warnings to the wealthy: *"The day when neither wealth nor children will avail, save him who comes to God with a sound heart"* (26:88–89). The companions of the left may include the richest men of history and the most powerful; they may include those whom the world applauded and those whom the world never noticed; the one thing they share is the refusal of the signs — and that refusal cancels every other achievement, because it cuts the soul off from the only source of lasting achievement. The verse is a sobering reminder to every reader who measures his life by the world's ledger: the world's ledger is not the ledger that decides which company he will join.

**The Warning That Is Also a Mercy**

Like every verdict in the Qur'an, the verdict of verse 19 is pronounced in a sūrah that is still being recited, still being heard, still being obeyed. The verse does not say that the companions of the left *were always* the companions of the left; it describes a company defined by an act — disbelief in the signs — and acts can be renounced, reversed, and replaced while life remains. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a report whose wording varies but whose meaning is constant:

> *"Every child of Adam errs, and the best of those who err are those who repent."* (al-Tirmidhī 2499, who graded it ḥasan; Ibn Mājah 4251)

The sūrah that condemns the companions of the left is the same sūrah that spent its first seventeen verses explaining, patiently and concretely, exactly how a human being assails the steep pass — because the steep pass is open to everyone who breathes. The believer who recites verse 19 is meant to hear in it both the terror of the destination and the mercy of the map: the left company is real, and no one is compelled to join it; the signs are still shining, the two highways are still open, the pass is still climbable — and the very recitation of the warning is itself one of the signs, calling the listener while there is still time to turn from the left to the right.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:20

> **Upon them is a Fire enclosed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Verse and the Final Image**

The sūrah ends as it began — with an oath answered, a verdict sealed, and an image of enclosure: *ʿalayhim nārun muʾṣadah* — "Upon them is a Fire enclosed." The word *muʾṣadah* comes from the root *-ṣ-d* (with the hamzah carrying the sense of locking, closing firmly), and the commentators explained it precisely: it is the Fire whose doors have been locked upon its inhabitants. The source commentary notes the parallel with Sūrah al-Humazah: *"Indeed it will be closed down upon them, in outstretched columns"* (104:8–9) — the same image of the Fire that is *muʾṣadah*, sealed over its people. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭūsī, as the source records, unfolded the meaning of the enclosure: the doors of Hell have been locked upon the disbelievers so that they cannot leave; none of their distress will be lifted from them, and no spirit — no relief, no respite, no breath of ease — will enter upon them. The verse is the sūrah's last word, and it is a word of total finality: the Fire is not merely beneath them or around them; it is *upon them* — pressed down, closed over, inescapable.

**The Grammar of Enclosure**

Every word of the final clause carries weight. *ʿalayhim* — "upon them" — pictures the Fire as a covering, a lid, a weight pressing from above, inverting the normal image of flames rising from below: for the companions of the left, even the geometry of fire is reversed into a seal. *Nārun* — "a Fire" — is indefinite in Arabic, and the indefiniteness is the indefiniteness of horror: a Fire whose magnitude no description can bound, whose quality no comparison can convey. And *muʾṣadah* — "enclosed" — is the participle of locking: the passive form suggests a door that has been shut by an agency other than the inmates — shut by justice, shut forever. The Qur'an elsewhere varies the image of the Fire's confinement — *"a fire whose fuel is men and stones"* (2:24); *"every time their skins are roasted through, We shall replace them with other skins, that they may taste the punishment"* (4:56) — but the image of the *enclosure* is among its most terrible, because it removes the last consolation of the damned mind: the hope of exit. The condemned can endure almost any pain if the end is visible; the muʾṣadah Fire removes the end.

**Why the Qur'an Ends With This Image**

The placement of this verse at the close of the sūrah is not incidental; it is the rhetorical keystone of the entire structure. The sūrah opened with an oath by the land — the beautiful, sacred, beloved city of Makkah. It moved through the creation of man in travail, the gifts of eyes and tongue and lips, the two highways, the steep pass, the freeing of captives, the feeding of orphans and the indigent, faith, patience, and compassion. And it closes with the two destinations — the companionship of the right and the companionship of the left — and with the Fire enclosed upon those who disbelieved in the signs. The Qur'an's method is to place the beauty and the terror side by side so that the choice is never abstract: the same sūrah that promises the Garden to those who feed the orphan warns of the sealed Fire for those who deny the signs. The final verse is the sūrah's way of saying that the steep pass is not optional — the mountain must be climbed or the valley must be endured; the two highways do not loop back into each other; the choice made in the days of the eyes and the tongue is sealed in the eternity of the Fire or opened into the eternity of the Garden.

**The Justice of the Seal**

A final reflection on the *justice* of the enclosure resolves the question that every thoughtful reader brings to this verse: how can a just and merciful God lock human beings in an eternal Fire? The sūrah's own argument supplies the answer, and the answer has three parts. First, the inmates of the Fire are not those who erred in ignorance but those who *disbelieved in the signs* — who were shown the eyes, the tongue, the highways, the prophets, and the Book, and who refused. The Qur'an is emphatic that no one is punished for a truth he was never shown: *"We would never punish until We had sent a messenger"* (17:15). Second, the enclosure is the mirror of the life lived: the companions of the left enclosed themselves in this world — in the enclosure of their own wealth, their own assumptions of immunity (*Does he suppose that none will ever have power over him?*), their own refusal to see the orphan and the indigent. The Fire that is muʾṣadah is the exteriorization of an interior state: the soul that sealed itself against the signs of God is, in the end, sealed — the enclosure of the Hereafter is the soul's own choice made absolute and external. And third, the Qur'an insists that the punishment is *precisely measured*: *"God wrongs not anyone a mote's weight"* (4:40); *"This is for what your hands sent ahead, and God wrongs not His servants"* (8:51). The sealed Fire is terrible because the denial it answers was terrible — the denial of the God who created the seer's eye and then was refused the sight of the seer's gratitude.

**The Sūrah's Last Gift to the Reader**

The final verse is also, paradoxically, a gift to the living reader, and the gift is urgency. The Fire is *enclosed* — but the reader is not yet inside it; the doors of the sūrah are still open, the pass is still before him, the signs are still shining, and the verdict of verse 18 is still available: *Those are the companions of the right.* The Qur'an does not reveal the terrors of the Hereafter to frighten the believer into paralysis but to awaken him into action — to send him, with open eyes and open hand, toward the orphan and the indigent, toward patience and compassion, while the breath remains. The Prophet ﷺ was asked about the majority of the people of the Garden and said: *"The reverent fear of God (taqwā) and good character"* — and about the majority of the people of the Fire, and he said: *"The two hollow ones: the mouth and the private parts"* (al-Tirmidhī 2004, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). The sūrah of al-Balad has said the same thing in its own idiom: the steep pass is climbed by the tongue that exhorted to patience and mercy and by the hand that fed the hungry — and the Fire is enclosed upon those who used their mouths for boasts and their wealth for themselves. The believer closes the sūrah and stands, as it were, at the foot of the mountain with the guide's question still echoing: *And what will apprise thee of the steep pass?* — now he knows. The climb begins with the next breath, the next glance at the orphan, the next opening of the hand. The oath by the land, by the begetter, and by the begotten has been fulfilled; the two highways have been shown; the verdicts have been pronounced. What remains is the walking.
