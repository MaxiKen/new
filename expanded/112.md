# Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ (Chapter 112) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Named by Its Very Meaning**

Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ is one of the shortest chapters of the Qur'an — four verses, eighteen or nineteen words by the most common count — yet no sūrah after al-Fātiḥah has exercised a deeper influence on the daily devotional life of Muslims. Its name is not taken from an event, a people, or an object mentioned within it, which distinguishes it from nearly every other sūrah. Al-Baqarah is named for the cow of the Children of Israel, al-Fīl for the army of the elephant, Quraysh for the tribe addressed in it; but nothing inside al-Ikhlāṣ mentions "sincerity." The title instead describes what the sūrah *does* to the person who recites it with understanding. *Ikhlāṣ* is the verbal noun of *akhlaṣa*, "to make pure, to purify, to devote exclusively," built on the root *kh-l-ṣ*, which also gives *khalāṣ*, "deliverance" or "escape." The two senses meet in the classical explanations of the title: the sūrah purifies a person's knowledge of God from every admixture of false conception, and it thereby delivers him from ruin. One who recites it with a present heart, the early commentators said, is absolved of the practice of idolatry, because the four verses sweep every form of ascribing partners or likeness to God out of the mind. Purity of doctrine and deliverance from doom are, in this sūrah, the same event seen from two sides.

**The Question That Called It Down: "Tell Us the Lineage of Your Lord"**

The reports on the occasion of revelation agree that this sūrah descended as an answer to a question about God's ancestry — a question that sounds strange to modern ears until one remembers that the pagan Arabs thought of their gods in thoroughly tribal and genealogical terms. Every respectable thing in their world had a lineage (*nasab*), and a god without lineage seemed to them to have no standing. The Companion Ubayy ibn Kaʿb related that the idolaters of Makkah came to the Prophet and said, "O Muhammad, tell us the lineage of your Lord," and God revealed the sūrah. Other accounts name the questioners as a group of Jews who demanded to know "the lineage of His Lord," and still others as Christians posing a similar challenge. The variety of the reports is itself instructive: the Qur'an's answer is addressed to every community that has ever tried to insert God into the machinery of birth, descent, and inheritance by which creatures perpetuate themselves. Whatever the identity of the questioners, the answer given is not a genealogy but a declaration that ends all genealogies: God is One, He is the eternally self-sufficient Master to Whom all creation turns, He neither gives birth nor was born, and nothing is like Him. A being that cannot beget and cannot be begotten is a being outside the entire system of ancestry; the question "of whom is He born?" is not answered but abolished.

**A Ḥadīth Qudsī That Echoes the Sūrah's Own Words**

The Prophet himself reported that God took offense at the very style of thinking that prompted such questions. In a ḥadīth qudsī transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Bukhārī, the Prophet said that God declared:

> "The son of Adam tells a lie against Me, though he has not the right to do so, and he abuses Me, though he has not the right to do so. As for his telling a lie against Me, it is his saying that I will not re-create him as I created him the first time — yet the first creation was no easier for Me than re-creating him. As for his abusing Me, it is his saying that I have taken a son — while I am the One, the Eternally Sufficient unto Himself; I beget not, nor was I begotten, and none is like unto Me." (al-Bukhārī, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

The ḥadīth is remarkable for its direct quotation of the language of verses 1–4 inside divine speech: God Himself summarizes His own self-description using the very words of this sūrah. The claim that God has offspring is here classified not merely as an intellectual error but as an abuse of God — a slander against His perfection, comparable in the divine reckoning to the denial of resurrection. The two errors are linked: both shrink God to the scale of creatures. To deny that God can re-create is to treat His first act of creation as if it exhausted His power; to claim that He begets is to treat Him as though He were subject to the laws of generation that govern animals and men. Both are ways of measuring the Infinite by the yardstick of the finite. The sūrah is thus the revealed cure for both diseases of the mind at once.

**Equivalent to One-Third of the Qur'an: What the Phrase Means**

The most famous testimony to the sūrah's rank is the Prophet's statement that it is equivalent to one-third of the Qur'an. The most widely transmitted version comes from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī. A man heard another man reciting *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* repeatedly and complained to the Prophet, who said:

> "By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, it is equivalent to one-third of the Qur'an." (al-Bukhārī 5013; Muslim 811)

In another report, also in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet gathered his Companions and said, "Are any of you unable to recite one-third of the Qur'an in a night?" When they found the idea burdensome, he explained that *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* is equivalent to one-third of the Qur'an. In Muslim's version from Abū al-Dardāʾ, the Prophet recited the sūrah to his Companions and said, "By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, it is equivalent to one-third of the Qur'an." The scholars offered several explanations of the equivalence. Some noted that the Qur'an's subject matter falls into three great divisions — stories and historical accounts, legal commands and prohibitions, and doctrines concerning God Himself — and that this sūrah gathers the whole of the third division into itself, so that in theme it stands for a third of the Book even though in length it is a fraction of a page. Others observed a subtlety: a person who recites the whole Qur'an is rewarded for its recitation, and a person who recites this sūrah is granted the reward of a third of the whole, as a gift and honor, not as an arithmetic exchange. What is beyond dispute is the pedagogical genius of the statement. Islam came to a society drowning in gods; before teaching law, history, or ritual, the Qur'an had to repair the human conception of God Himself, and this sūrah performs that repair in the fewest possible words. Ibn Taymiyyah noted that the Qur'an is fundamentally the Book of tawḥīd — the declaration of God's unity — and that every other theme is subordinate to it; hence the sūrah that states tawḥīd in its purest and most concentrated form can fairly stand for the Book's very heart. Al-Rāzī compressed the whole matter into an image the tradition has never forgotten:

> "The entire Qur'an is like an oyster, and its pearl is His statement: Say, 'He, God, is One.'" (cited by al-Rāzī in his commentary on this sūrah)

**More Than Twenty Names: The Epithets of the Sūrah**

The reverence of the community is written in the extraordinary number of names it gave this four-verse chapter — over twenty are recorded in the commentary tradition. It is called *al-Barāʾah*, "The Absolver," because sincere recitation absolves the believer of idolatry; *al-Munaffirah*, "The One That Puts to Flight," because it drives Satan away from the one who recites it; *al-Najāh*, "Deliverance," because it delivers from likening anything to God; *al-Maʿrifah*, "Recognition," because authentic knowledge of God is obtained by affirming what it contains; *al-Mudhakkirah*, "The Reminder," because it brings Divine Unity to mind in a pure and simple form; and *Nūr al-Qurʾān*, "The Light of the Qur'an," after the report that "everything has a light, and the light of the Qur'an is Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ." Each name is a different facet of one conviction: this sūrah is the distilled essence of what the Qur'an knows about God. A text that accumulates twenty epithets over fourteen centuries is a text that millions of people have not merely read but leaned upon — in prayer, in sickness, in fear, in the hour of death, and in the quiet repetition of the tongue when the mind seeks a fixed point in a turning world.

**The Love That Leads to Paradise**

One report in particular shows how early Muslims themselves felt about the sūrah. Anas ibn Mālik related that a man of the Anṣār led his people in prayer at the mosque of Qubāʾ and, following the custom of opening with a sūrah after the Fātiḥah, opened every time with *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad*, finishing it before adding another sūrah. His congregation complained to the Prophet, who asked the man why he did this. The man answered, "Because it describes the Compassionate, and I love to recite it." The Prophet replied:

> "Tell him that God loves him." (al-Bukhārī, from Anas ibn Mālik; similar reports in al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī)

The psychology of that man is the psychology of the whole sūrah. He did not recite it because it was short or easy; he recited it because its description of God gave him delight, and repetition was his way of remaining in the presence of what he loved. The Prophet's answer — that God loves the one who loves this description of Him — teaches that affection for the true knowledge of God is itself a sign of divine regard. In another version of the same story, the man said he loved the sūrah because it describes *al-Raḥmān*, the Compassionate; the point is the same. The sūrah is not an abstract creed to be memorized but a portrait of the Beloved to be contemplated, and contemplation naturally becomes recitation, and recitation becomes love, and love becomes the companion that leads its owner home.

**A Shield Against the Night: The Three Quls**

The daily practice of the Prophet gives the sūrah its most intimate function. ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet's wife, reported:

> "Whenever the Prophet went to bed at night, he would cup his hands together, blow into them, and recite into them Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad and the two sūrahs of refuge (al-Muʿawwidhatān, i.e., al-Falaq and al-Nās). Then he would wipe with his hands over whatever parts of his body he could reach, beginning with his head and face and the front of his body, doing this three times." (al-Bukhārī 5017; and see Muslim 2192 for the version concerning illness)

The three sūrahs — al-Ikhlāṣ together with al-Falaq and al-Nās — are thus the believer's nightly armor. Al-Ikhlāṣ protects the innermost citadel, the creed itself: it fortifies the heart against the whisper that anything other than God deserves fear, love, or trust. Al-Falaq and al-Nās then guard the outer walls, seeking refuge from the evil of created things and of the whisperer. The order is logical: unless the center is secured — unless one knows precisely Who God is — the defensive prayers at the perimeter lack their foundation. When illness came upon the Prophet, the same practice became a healing rite: he would recite the three and wipe his hands over his body, and ʿĀʾishah described how, when his final illness grew severe, she took his hand and performed the wiping for him with his own blessed hand, seeking the blessing of his touch.

**In the Ritual Prayer and the Witr**

The sūrah's place in the canonical prayer is fixed by the Prophet's own practice. Reports from several Companions describe him reciting al-Kāfirūn and al-Ikhlāṣ in the two supererogatory units before the dawn prayer, and the tradition of the Witr prayer — the odd-numbered closing prayer of the night — consistently pairs the sūrah with two others:

> The Prophet used to recite in the Witr prayer: "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" (al-Aʿlā, 87), "Say: O disbelievers" (al-Kāfirūn, 109), and "Say: He, God, is One" (al-Ikhlāṣ, 112). (al-Nasāʾī, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Tirmidhī, from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb and others)

The choice is meaningful. Al-Aʿlā proclaims God's transcendence, al-Kāfirūn severs the believer from every object of false worship, and al-Ikhlāṣ states positively Who the true Object of worship is. The three together, recited at the conclusion of the night's prayers, summarize the entire movement of Islam: glorification, separation from falsehood, and pure affirmation of the One. Because of its brevity and its centrality, al-Ikhlāṣ is customarily the first sūrah a Muslim child memorizes after al-Fātiḥah, and it is often the last thing whispered to a dying person or recited at a graveside. It accompanies the believer, in short, from the first lesson to the last breath — a summary of everything he needs to know about God at the two moments when everything else has fallen away.

**The Twin of al-Kāfirūn: Two Halves of One Declaration**

The classical commentators noted a deep pairing between this sūrah and Sūrah 109, al-Kāfirūn, which precedes it in the muṣḥaf. Al-Kāfirūn opens with *Say: O disbelievers, I worship not what you worship*, and closes with *To you your religion, and to me my religion* — a complete severance from every false object of worship. Al-Ikhlāṣ opens with *Say: He, God, is One* and proceeds to a complete description of the true Object of worship. One sūrah is the negative half of the testimony of faith — the *lā ilāha* ("there is no god"); the other is its positive half — the *illā Allāh* ("except God"). Together they are known as *al-Muqashqishatān*, "The Two That Cure," from the root *qashqasha*, used of a medicine that drives out disease from the skin. The Prophet is reported to have called them by this name and to have said that whoever recites them has, in effect, recited the pure essence of the declaration of God's unity. The two sūrahs were recited together by the Prophet in the two units before dawn and after sunset, so that the believer begins and ends the day by completing the testimony of faith in both its halves: cutting off every rival, then affirming the One.

**Why Four Verses Are Enough**

Before the verse-by-verse commentary begins, it is worth asking why the Qur'an compresses the knowledge of God into four short verses here when it spreads the same knowledge over hundreds of verses elsewhere. The answer lies in the sūrah's function as a summation — a text meant to be repeated, memorized, and carried. A chapter recited daily in prayer and nightly as protection must be short enough to be always available; a doctrine that must withstand every distortion of every age must be complete in its essentials. The four verses are arranged with perfect economy. The first verse affirms the reality and unity of God's being. The second affirms His self-sufficiency — that He is the Master to Whom all turn and from Whom nothing is hidden. The third strips away the two great false additions that human imagination makes to God: that He has offspring and that He has parents, i.e., that He is subject to time and generation. The fourth closes the circle by denying that anything whatsoever resembles Him, which protects the first three affirmations from being corrupted by analogy. Affirmation, then protection, then negation of false kinship, then the sealing negation of likeness: a complete theology of God in four movements. What follows is a verse-by-verse expansion of each movement, treating each verse as an independent discourse, as the verse-by-verse method requires.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:1

> **Say, "He, God, is One."**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Verse Begins with a Command to Speak**

The sūrah opens with the imperative *qul*, "Say." This single word, repeated at the head of dozens of Qur'anic passages, marks the entire content that follows as revealed speech being transmitted through the Prophet. Grammatically, the command is addressed to Muhammad, but its force extends to every believer: the knowledge of God contained in these verses is not a private insight or a philosophical discovery that each person must originate for himself; it is a message that has been given, and the one who receives it is commanded to utter it. There is a subtle mercy in the command. If the verse had begun, "God is One," the reader might treat the statement as information to be filed away. Beginning with "Say" turns the verse into an act: the tongue must move, the words must leave the lips, the creed must be confessed aloud. Reciting the sūrah in prayer is itself obedience to this command; every Muslim who prays is, in effect, commanded by God to state His unity dozens of times each day. Some early readers, as the commentators record, wondered why the Prophet — who knew God's Oneness with a certainty no instruction could increase — was told to say it at all. The question misses the point: the command is not for the Prophet's information but for the world's reformation. He is commanded to say it so that others may hear it and say it after him. Al-Rāzī and others noted that *qul* also fixes the authority of the statement: what follows is not the Prophet's speculation about God but God's own self-description delivered on His command. A person who recites these words is not offering an opinion about the Divine; he is repeating the very definition of God that God gave of Himself. There is no higher epistemic ground available to a human being than to say of God exactly what God has said of Himself.

**The Pronoun "He": God Speaking About God**

The verse then proceeds: *Huwa Allāhu aḥad* — literally "He is God, One," or as most translations render it, "He, God, is One." The opening pronoun *huwa* ("He") is deceptively simple and carries a weight the commentators have explored for centuries. Arabic uses pronouns where the identity of the referent is already known or is about to be named; here the "He" is followed immediately by the Name *Allāh*, so no ambiguity exists. The commentators asked why the pronoun precedes the Name rather than the Name standing alone. Several explanations were offered. Some said that *huwa* here is what grammarians call the pronoun of separation (*ḍamīr al-faṣl*), which in Arabic sets apart the subject from the predicate — "He, [namely] God, is One" — a reading that makes the sentence a firm, unambiguous equation. Others, particularly the spiritual commentators such as al-Kāshānī and, in places, al-Rāzī, read the pronoun as pointing to the Divine Essence in its pure selfhood: God in and of Himself, before and beyond the multiplicity of His acts and attributes as they relate to creation. On this reading, *huwa* gestures toward the innermost divine reality — the "He-ness" of God that no creature can comprehend — and the Name *Allāh* then names that reality for us. Some of the gnostically inclined scholars even considered *Huwa* to be among the greatest divine designations, and devotional formulas built upon it circulated among the Sufis, such as the invocation *Yā Huwa*, "O He." Whether one follows the grammatical reading or the contemplative one, the practical effect is the same: the verse first turns the heart toward the sheer existence of God — that there is a Reality behind all realities — and only then names and qualifies that Reality. The mind is not permitted to dissolve God into an abstract principle or a projection of human ideals; the pronoun insists that God is a real, conscious, personal Reality — a "He" — before the verse tells us what kind of Reality He is.

**The Name Allāh: The Sole Owner of Divinity**

The Name *Allāh* is the subject of the sentence and the pivot of the whole sūrah. Philologists have debated whether it is a proper name with no derivation or a contraction of *al-Ilāh*, "the Deity," but all agree on its referent: the one true God, the Creator and Sustainer of all. The Qur'an itself testifies that the pagan Arabs acknowledged this Name and used it for the supreme Creator even while they worshipped idols beside Him:

> "And if you asked them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they would surely say, 'God (Allāh).'" (Qur'an 31:25; cf. 29:61, 63; 39:38; 43:87)

The pagans of Makkah, in other words, already believed in a supreme deity they called Allāh. Their error was not atheism but misassociation: they granted Allāh the work of creation while directing their worship, their sacrifices, and their trust to lesser beings they imagined as intercessors. This is precisely why the verse must say *Allāhu aḥad* — "God is One" — rather than merely "there is a god." The affirmation of God's existence was never the issue in Makkah; the affirmation of His exclusive right to divinity was. The pagan creed was, in effect, "God is one *and* these idols are His deputies." The Qur'anic creed is "God is One, *and therefore* nothing else shares His divinity in any degree, in any function, under any title." The Name *Allāh*, as the theologians noted, is the "Gathering Name": every other divine attribute — the Merciful, the King, the Holy, the Creator — is a facet of it, and it alone can never be applied to any creature even figuratively. One may call a king "the merciful" in a qualified sense, but no being other than God has ever been called *Allāh*. The Name is thus linguistically unique as its bearer is metaphysically unique, and the verse locks the two together: *Huwa Allāh* — the Reality pointed to by the pronoun is the sole owner of the Name of Divinity.

**One in a Way That No Other Is: Aḥad and Wāḥid**

The verse ends with *aḥad*, "One." Arabic has two intensive adjectives for oneness — *wāḥid* and *aḥad* — and the choice of *aḥad* here is deliberate. *Wāḥid* is the ordinary word for the number one: one book, one man, one day; it opens a series and invites the possibility of a second. *Aḥad* is reserved for a oneness that excludes all comparison and all composition: the word is used in Arabic for "anyone" in negated sentences, as in *mā raʾaytu aḥadan*, "I saw not a single one," where it negates the very possibility of an instance. The classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī — explained the distinction in various ways, but they converged on the essential point: *wāḥid* is one to which a second may be added, while *aḥad* is a unity that admits no second, no division, no parts, and no comparison. When the Qur'an wants to speak of God as one among several possible lords it sometimes uses *wāḥid*, as in the verse:

> "And your God is one God (ilāhun wāḥid); there is no god but He, the Compassionate, the Merciful." (Qur'an 2:163)

But when it wants to assert the absolute, incomparable, indivisible uniqueness of the Divine Being — as here — it uses *aḥad*. Al-Rāzī drew the full theological picture: the Name *Allāh* gathers the positive attributes of God — He is Creator, Knower, Powerful, Living — while the word *aḥad* gathers His negative attributes — He has no body, no substance, no accident, no equal, no peer, no parts. Together the two words deliver the complete doctrine: *Allāhu aḥad* means that the One to Whom every perfection belongs is the One to Whom no imperfection, limitation, or likeness attaches. The word *aḥad* is also grammatically notable: it occurs in the Qur'an most often inside negations, as in "none is like unto Him" (112:4) and "no god but He," and its use as a positive predicate here is almost unique — God is not merely "not many"; He is positively, affirmatively One.

**The First Half of the Testimony: What Tawḥīd Actually Claims**

Theologians divided the unity of God into three levels, and this verse touches the first two directly. The first is the unity of God's being (*tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah*): there is one Creator, Sustainer, Sovereign, and disposer of all affairs. The Qur'an argues this unity from the order of the cosmos itself:

> "Had there been within the heavens and the earth gods besides God, they would both have been ruined. So glorified is God, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe." (Qur'an 21:22)

> "God has not taken any son, nor has there ever been with Him any deity. Had there been, each deity would have taken away what it created, and some of them would have overcome others. Glorified be God above what they describe!" (Qur'an 23:91)

The logic is both metaphysical and empirical. If two or more independent creators existed, their wills would either agree or conflict. If they necessarily agree, the second is superfluous; if they can conflict, the universe would be torn between rival commands, and the stable order we observe — the same sun rising on the obedient and the rebellious alike, the same laws of nature binding everywhere — would be impossible. The Qur'an repeatedly points to this observed stability as the proof of single sovereignty. The second level is the unity of worship (*tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah*): because there is only one God, only He deserves worship, prayer, fear, hope, and trust. The third level — the unity of God's names and attributes — is the theme toward which the rest of the sūrah moves. The verse's placement at the head of the sūrah makes it the root from which everything else grows: He is One, therefore He alone is the Ṣamad to Whom all turn; He is One, therefore He does not beget (begetting would require a consort and would introduce multiplicity); He is One, therefore nothing resembles Him (likeness would compromise His uniqueness).

**Answering the Lineage Question: The God Who Has No Ancestry**

Returning to the occasion of revelation makes the verse's polemical edge visible. The idolaters asked, "What is the lineage of your Lord?" — a question that assumes God stands at the end of some family tree, begotten by a greater or older being and capable of begetting successors, exactly as their tribal gods were imagined to do. The answer *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* meets the assumption head-on. A being that is *aḥad* — absolutely one, admitting no second and no parts — cannot stand in any genealogical relation, because genealogy is precisely the mechanism by which beings split into related but distinct individuals. A father and a son are two individuals sharing a connection; *aḥad* excludes the very arithmetic in which fatherhood and sonship make sense. The verse does not give God a genealogy; it declares that God is the kind of Being to whom the question of genealogy is meaningless, the way the question "what color is the number seven?" is meaningless. The command to "Say" this in public, to the very people who asked, was itself an act of intellectual courage: the Muslims were few and surrounded by a society whose entire religious imagination was genealogical, and God instructed them to answer the taunt not with accommodation but with a definition that made the taunt look foolish.

**What the Verse Does to the Heart and Mind of the Reciter**

The psychological effect of this verse, repeated constantly in prayer, deserves attention. Modern minds are pulled in two directions at once: toward the fragmentation of attention, in which a person's ultimate loyalty is split among career, family, nation, ideology, pleasure, and self-image; and toward the manufacture of many "gods" in the practical sense — things that command a person's highest fear, hope, and trust. The verse *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* is the standing correction of both tendencies. To recite it with understanding is to perform a daily act of consolidation: all that deserves ultimate devotion is gathered into one Object, and everything else is demoted to the rank of creature. The famous scholar Ibn al-Qayyim observed that the human heart was made to love and trust one thing ultimately, and that when that single devotion is not directed to God, it fastens itself onto substitutes that can never satisfy it — which is why polytheism in all its forms, ancient idolatry and modern idolatries alike, leaves the soul restless and divided. This verse reassembles the scattered self around its true center. And because the verse states the unity of God as a fact rather than as an exhortation, it also steadies the mind against the terror of a universe without a sovereign: the One Who is *aḥad* is also the One Who is — existence itself is anchored in a single, undivided, self-consistent Reality, and the believer who recites this verse is, in effect, declaring that reality has a meaning and a master.

**From Creed to Conduct: The Oneness That Must Become a Life**

Finally, the verse cannot be exhausted by theology. The Prophet's companions understood that the affirmation of God's oneness on the tongue is only the beginning; the affirmation must migrate into conduct. The Qur'an itself binds the two together:

> "And they were not commanded except to worship God, devoting religion entirely to Him, as people of pure faith (ḥunafāʾ)..." (Qur'an 98:5)

*Ikhlāṣ* — the very name of this sūrah — is the creed's translation into action: to do deeds purely for God, with no eye to applause, advantage, or approval. A person who says "God is One" but then lives for the approval of the crowd has, in the practical sense the sūrah's title implies, set up rivals he denies with his tongue. The early Muslims therefore called sincerity (*ikhlāṣ*) the soul of every deed, and they took this sūrah as its banner: the One who is unique in His being must be unique in His worship, and the worship that is uniquely His is the worship performed solely for Him. Every time a believer recites this verse and then performs an act of charity secretly, or works honestly when no one is watching, or refuses to trim his principles for an audience, he is completing the sentence the verse began: God is One — and therefore my deeds are done for One alone.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:2

> **God, the Eternally Sufficient unto Himself.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Occurs Only Once in the Entire Qur'an**

The second verse consists of a single Divine Name: *Allāhu al-Ṣamad* — "God, the Eternally Sufficient unto Himself." The word *al-Ṣamad* appears in the whole of the Qur'an only here, and it is applied to God only here. This uniqueness is itself a form of commentary. The Qur'an names God by hundreds of descriptions, but it reserves this one for a single, concentrated disclosure, as though the word were minted for this one occasion and never needed again because this one occasion says everything it has to say. *Ṣamad* derives from the root *ṣ-m-d*, whose core sense is to direct oneself toward something, to betake oneself to it, or to be solid, compact, and impenetrable. The classical lexicographers explained that the Arabs used the word for a solid mass of rock or metal with no hollow interior — something you could strike and find completely dense, nothing empty inside. From this came the sense of a chief or master to whom people turn and on whom they lean in times of need, and also the sense of one who is not hollow, that is, one who has no need of anything outside himself. All these senses flow into the theological meaning, and the commentators, from Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī down to al-Rāzī, devoted some of their finest pages to unpacking them.

**The Master to Whom Every Need Is Brought**

The most widespread explanation of *al-Ṣamad* among the earliest authorities is "the Master" (*al-sayyid*) in the absolute sense. Ibn ʿAbbās, the cousin of the Prophet and the greatest Qur'anic scholar of the first generation, is reported to have said that *al-Ṣamad* is:

> "The Master Who has reached the utmost degree of mastery, the Noble Who has reached the utmost degree of nobility, the Great Who has reached the utmost degree of greatness." (recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr in their commentaries on this verse, on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās)

A master in human society is someone to whom others turn for provision, protection, judgment, and direction. But every human master is himself dependent — on his health, his wealth, his allies, his information, even on the obedience of his own servants. *Al-Ṣamad* names the Master in Whom mastery has reached its absolute limit: the One to Whom all creatures direct their needs and from Whom nothing is withheld, while He Himself is in need of nothing and of no one. Al-Zamakhsharī expressed this with precision when he said that *al-Ṣamad* is:

> "The One to Whom one betakes oneself when seeking Him — the Master Who is sought in every need. The meaning is: He is God, of Whom you are aware and Whom you affirm as Creator of the heavens and the earth and as your Creator; He is the One unified in divinity, in which none shares; and He is the One to Whom all that is created betakes itself, none being independent of Him while He is independent of them all." (al-Zamakhsharī, al-Kashshāf)

Notice how the two grammatical directions of the root meet here. *Ṣumida ilayhi* — "one betakes oneself to him" — yields the passive sense: He is the One sought. And *ṣamada* in the sense of being dense and self-contained yields the active sense: He is the One Who has no hollow, no lack, no need. The name therefore holds both sides of the Creator-creature relation in a single word: all of creation leans upon Him, and He leans upon nothing.

**The Ontology of Dependence: Why Everything Except God Is "Hollow"**

The lexical image of solidity gives the verse its deepest philosophical resonance. The Arab's *ṣamad* was a mass of rock with no cavity — nothing inside it that needed filling. The commentators drew the implied contrast: every created thing is, in a sense, hollow, because every created thing has needs built into its very constitution. The body needs food, water, air, and warmth; the mind needs knowledge, company, and purpose; the heart needs love, security, and hope; and no creature, however powerful, can supply its own conditions of existence. Scientists describe this as dependence on external systems — the human being requires an atmosphere, a biosphere, a sun, a planet with a molten core and a magnetic field, and behind all of these, causes that reach back to the very origin of existence. The Qur'an states the same truth in direct address:

> "O mankind, you are the poor ones in need of God, and God — He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy." (Qur'an 35:15)

*Al-Ṣamad* is the name of the One exception to universal dependence. God alone is not "hollow": there is nothing in His reality that needs to be filled, nothing outside Him on which He relies, no cause that brought Him into being and no condition required for His continuance. He is, in the language of the theologians who reflected on this verse, the self-subsistent Being Whose existence is from Himself and through Himself, while the existence of everything else is received, borrowed, and sustained from moment to moment by Him. The famous Throne Verse says the same thing in another image:

> "God — there is no god but He, the Living, the Self-Subsistent (al-Qayyūm) by Whom all things subsist..." (Qur'an 2:255)

Al-Ṣamad and al-Qayyūm are close kin: al-Qayyūm is the One by Whom everything stands, and al-Ṣamad is the One to Whom everything turns and Who stands in need of nothing. Taken together they give the complete picture of the relation between Creator and cosmos: the cosmos is a web of interdependence in which every thread ultimately hangs from a single hook that hangs from nothing.

**The Logic of the Second Verse's Position**

The placement of this verse immediately after the affirmation of Oneness is not accidental; the two affirmations imply one another. If God is *aḥad* — absolutely one — then He must also be *al-Ṣamad*, because a being that needed something outside itself would, to that extent, be dependent on another, and dependence on another compromises absolute unity: the dependent being would require the cooperation of what it depends on, and its perfection would be incomplete without an external cause. Conversely, the fact that all creatures turn to Him for their needs confirms His oneness, for if there were many gods, needs would be divided among them, each god being insufficient alone. A single point of dependence requires a single object of dependence. The two verses thus form a perfect syllogism in miniature: the One Who is One is the One to Whom all turn, and the One to Whom all turn must be One. Al-Ṭabarī records that many of the early commentators took the verse to mean precisely this: *al-Ṣamad* is the One Who has no peer, the Master to Whom the affairs of all creation ascend and from Whom they descend, and Who stands above the whole system of mutual need that governs His creatures.

**God's Independence Is Not His Indifference**

A careless reader might hear "self-sufficient" and imagine a distant, indifferent God — a being so complete that He has no concern for His creatures. The Qur'an pre-empts this misunderstanding by pairing God's self-sufficiency with His responsiveness throughout its pages. The very next thing the believer says after reciting this sūrah in prayer is often a supplication, and the Qur'an declares:

> "And when My servants ask you concerning Me — indeed I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me..." (Qur'an 2:186)

Al-Ṣamad means that God does not need His creatures, not that He is unconcerned with them. The two facts are different in kind. A human employer who "does not need" his workers may still care about them; a human king who needs nothing from his subjects may still love them. In God the combination is perfect: He is absolutely independent, so His care for creation is pure gift rather than self-interest — He loves, provides, and answers not because He must but because He wills. The theologians drew from this a beautiful corollary: every benefit God bestows is an act of pure generosity, since He gains nothing from bestowing it. A creature who helps another is often helping himself — the parent needs the child's survival for his own happiness, the patron needs the client's loyalty for his own power. God alone helps with no benefit returning to Himself, and this makes divine generosity the only completely disinterested generosity there is. The verse thus lays the foundation for trust: the One to Whom we turn is not a needy god who serves us in order to be served, but the Self-Sufficient Who serves us because He is good.

**When Everything Else Fails, One Door Remains**

The practical life of the believer is shaped by this verse in a very concrete way. Every human being eventually discovers that the things he leans on can fail: health collapses, wealth evaporates, friends disappoint, institutions break, and even the most reliable systems of this world come to an end. The Qur'an describes the reaction of people in the grip of calamity — at sea, in the storm, in the darkness:

> "And when adversity touches you at sea, those you call upon besides Him vanish from memory — but when He delivers you to land, you turn away. And ever is man ungrateful." (Qur'an 17:67)

The reason every other refuge vanishes in extremity is that every other refuge is itself a creature — a *ṣamad* only in the borrowed, limited sense, hollow at the core, in need of what it pretends to provide. Only *al-Ṣamad* is what He is at all times and under all conditions. The believer who has understood this verse carries an unshakable sense of final security: he may lose every worldly support, but he cannot lose the Support of supports. The Prophet taught this dependence in its most intimate form when he instructed the Muslim to begin every significant act with reliance upon God, and the sūrah's place in the daily prayers keeps the lesson perpetually fresh: before asking for anything, the believer affirms to Whom he is asking — the One Who is never distracted, never depleted, never weary, and never in need of anything that the asker could offer in payment.

**The Creature's Answer to the Self-Sufficient: Prayer, Gratitude, and Service**

Finally, this verse explains why prayer itself makes sense. If God were a being among beings — one more actor in the cosmos, needing this or that from His creatures — then petitionary prayer would be a transaction between peers, and its efficacy would depend on His mood or capacity. Because God is *al-Ṣamad*, prayer is not a transaction but an alignment: the creature acknowledges his own poverty, directs his need to the only One Who can fill it, and thereby enters into the true relation between creation and Creator. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early exegetes noted that *al-Ṣamad* also carries the sense of the One to Whom one repairs in every calamity — the final court of appeal. And because God is self-sufficient, the believer's service to Him is not performed to meet a divine need but to fulfill a human one: God does not need our prayer, our fasting, or our charity; we need to pray, to fast, and to give, because these acts order our souls toward the only Reality that can satisfy them. The verse, in short, teaches the believer to say in every situation: "I am the needy one; He is the Self-Sufficient; and my need is safe in His sufficiency." This is the inner meaning of the sūrah's name — *ikhlāṣ* — for the worship that is purely for God is the worship that has understood that God needs nothing from it, and that nevertheless He accepts it and rewards it out of His sheer generosity.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:3

> **He begets not; nor was He begotten.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Negations That Upend an Entire Way of Thinking**

The third verse delivers the negative half of the sūrah's doctrine: *lam yalid wa lam yūlad* — "He begets not, nor was He begotten." The grammatical form is precise. The particle *lam* in Arabic negates the past tense and, in Qur'anic usage, carries the force of a firm, categorical denial reaching from the past through the present into the future: God has never begotten, does not beget, and will never beget; He was never begotten at any point in the past. The verse thus closes both ends of the genealogical chain at once. Every being in the world of generation stands between ancestors who produced him and descendants he may produce; he is a middle link in an unbroken chain of begetting. The verse declares that God is not a middle link — there is no link above Him from which He issued, and no link below Him that issues from Him. He stands outside the entire chain of generation, as its origin rather than a member of it. The early readers differed over whether the verse should be counted as one verse or two — some of the Qur'an's seven canonical reading traditions divide it into "He begets not" and "nor was He begotten" as separate verses — but the meaning is unaffected: both halves are negations, and together they deny every imaginable form of divine progeny and divine ancestry.

**Why the Denial Was Needed: Gods With Families**

To understand the force of these negations, one must remember what the first audience believed. The pagan Arabs did not merely believe in a supreme creator; they populated the heavens with the offspring of the gods. The Qur'an records their claims with evident dismay:

> "And they assign to God daughters — glory be to Him — while for themselves is what they desire." (Qur'an 16:57)

> "They have made the angels — who are servants of the Compassionate — females. Did they witness their creation? Their testimony will be recorded, and they will be questioned." (Qur'an 43:19)

The Arabs imagined the angels as daughters of God, and their own preference for sons made the ascription particularly insulting — they gave God the children they did not want for themselves. The Qur'an repeatedly attacks the claim with both moral and logical weapons:

> "So ask them: does your Lord have daughters while they have sons? Or did We create the angels female while they witnessed it? Surely it is of their falsehood that they say, 'God has begotten' — and indeed they are liars. Has He chosen daughters over sons?" (Qur'an 37:149–153)

The people of scripture made parallel claims. The Qur'an records that the Jews of the Prophet's time gave divine sonship to a human being, saying that Ezra was the son of God, and that the Christians said the Messiah was the son of God:

> "The Jews say, 'Ezra is the son of God,' and the Christians say, 'The Messiah is the son of God.' That is their statement from their mouths, imitating the saying of those who disbelieved before them. God fights them! How are they deluded?" (Qur'an 9:30)

And it responds to the Christians with a declaration whose terms are exactly those of this sūrah:

> "O People of the Book! Do not go to extremes in your religion, and do not say of God except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of God and His word which He cast upon Mary and a spirit from Him. So believe in God and His messengers, and do not say, 'Three.' Desist — it is better for you. Indeed, God is but one God. Glory be to Him — that He should have a son! To Him belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is on the earth, and sufficient is God as a Guardian." (Qur'an 4:171)

The sūrah's negations, then, sweep across the whole landscape of seventh-century belief: they deny the Arab's daughters of God, the Christian's divine Son, and every other kinship claimed between the Creator and creatures.

**The Logic of Begetting: What Having a Child Would Require**

The verse does not merely assert that God has no children; the surrounding Qur'an supplies the reasoning that makes the assertion necessary. A child is the product of two parents; to have a son, one must have a consort. The Qur'an draws the inference explicitly in the sūrah of al-Anʿām, in the very passage the commentators place beside this verse:

> "How should He have a child when He has no consort, and He created all things, and He is Knower of all things?" (Qur'an 6:101)

The argument is a *reductio*: the claim "God has a son" entails the claim "God has a consort" — and that entails that God, the Creator of all things, would require a partner from among the very things He created in order to reproduce. But the One Who created all things cannot be completed by any thing; a being that needs a partner for anything is not the Creator of everything. Furthermore, begetting in the biological sense implies division and composition: a child carries a portion of the parent's substance, so a god who begot would be a god who could be divided, a portion of whom would go into the child. But the first verse of this sūrah has already established that God is *aḥad* — the One Who cannot be divided, numbered, or replicated. A being that could beget would be a being of the same kind as its offspring — and the fourth verse will close the door on that possibility by declaring that nothing is like God. The three later verses of the sūrah thus form a single argument with verse one: because God is absolutely One, He cannot have a peer; because He is absolutely self-sufficient, He cannot need a child; because He is absolutely unique, He cannot generate a being of His own kind; and the whole chain is compressed into the two negations of verse three.

**The Heavenly Reaction: Creation Itself Shudders at the Claim**

The Qur'an describes the gravity of the claim of divine offspring in language of cosmic proportion. In Sūrat Maryam, after the declaration that God has taken no son, the heavens themselves are pictured as nearly torn apart:

> "And they say, 'The Compassionate has taken a son.' You have indeed brought forth a monstrous thing. The heavens almost burst apart at it, and the earth splits, and the mountains collapse in ruin — that they attribute to the Compassionate a son. And it is not fitting for the Compassionate to take a son. There is none in the heavens and the earth but that he comes to the Compassionate as a servant." (Qur'an 19:88–93)

The imagery is not meant literally — the heavens do not physically tear — but it conveys the ontological enormity of the claim. To say that the eternal, self-sufficient Creator has a son is not merely to make a theological mistake; it is to invert the order of reality, to drag the uncreated into the machinery of the created, to treat the Source of all existence as one more item within existence needing reproduction to continue itself. The verse from Maryam ends by stating the true relation that replaces the false kinship: every being in the heavens and the earth comes to God *as a servant*. Servanthood, not sonship, is the relation between the creature and the Creator; the most honored of creatures, the prophets and the angels, are precisely the ones who declare their servanthood most completely.

**Jesus, Muhammad, and the Dignity of Servanthood**

This point touches directly on the Qur'an's teaching about Jesus, peace be upon him. The Qur'an never denies Jesus' greatness — he is the Messiah, a messenger of God, His word cast upon Mary, a spirit from Him, one of the five great messengers — but it insists that his greatness consists precisely in his perfect servanthood and prophethood, not in a biological or metaphysical sonship:

> "The Messiah would never disdain to be a servant of God, nor would the angels brought near. And whoever disdains His worship and is arrogant — He will gather them to Himself all together." (Qur'an 4:172)

The claim of divine sonship, in the Qur'an's view, is not an honor to Jesus but a distortion of his rank: it makes him a partner where he was a servant, and it makes the unique God the father of a human household. Similarly, the Qur'an insists that the Prophet Muhammad's highest title is *al-ʿabd*, the servant:

> "Exalted is He Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque..." (Qur'an 17:1)

> "And when the servant of God stood up calling upon Him, they almost swarmed over him." (Qur'an 72:19)

The classical commentators observed that in the very verse where the Qur'an denies that God has a son, the creature addressed as the nearest to God is described as His servant — *al-ʿabd*. Sonship in the pagan imagination was a claim to closeness through birth; the Qur'an replaces it with closeness through worship. A son inherits his father's nature without effort; a servant must choose his loyalty and prove it. The Qur'anic model makes the honor of nearness to God available to every human being who chooses servanthood, rather than reserving it for those born into a divine lineage.

**Why Humans Invent Divine Children: The Psychology of the Claim**

The Qur'an also probes why human beings keep wanting God to have offspring. There is a deep psychological root: human beings reproduce to achieve a kind of continuity — through children, a person extends his existence beyond death, is remembered, and feels that his life was not in vain. The pagan Arabs, the Qur'an says, wanted daughters for their gods and sons for themselves because they conceived of the gods as beings like themselves, only more powerful — beings who, like tribal chiefs, needed heirs to perpetuate their dominion. The ascription of offspring to God is thus a projection of human anxiety onto the divine: the creature, terrified of extinction, imagines its Creator is subject to the same terror and must likewise secure Himself through progeny. The sūrah's answer strikes at the root of the projection: *lam yalid wa lam yūlad*. Because God is eternal and self-sufficient, He has no need of the continuity that children provide; because He does not die, He needs no heir; because He does not grow old, He needs no successor. The desire for immortality through offspring is a creature's remedy for a creature's mortality — and the One Who was not born and does not die has no use for the remedy. The believer who understands this is freed from one of the oldest unconscious distortions of religion: the tendency to make God in the image of human need.

**The Ḥadīth Qudsī Connection: The Claim as an Abuse of God**

The verse is the very speech God quotes against the slanderer in the ḥadīth qudsī cited in the introduction — "He abuses Me... by his saying that God has begotten children, while I am the One, the Eternally Sufficient unto Himself; I beget not, nor was I begotten." The classification of the claim as abuse (*shatm*) deserves reflection. In ordinary speech, abuse is an insult delivered to a person's face, and the Qur'an and ḥadīth apply the category here because the claim about God's offspring attacks His perfection. To say that the eternal God has a son is to attribute to Him a need (the need for a consort), a capacity (the capacity to be divided and reproduced), and a vulnerability (the vulnerability of every parent to the loss, failure, or death of offspring) — all of which are indignities to the Divine. The believer's duty, the ḥadīth implies, is not merely to avoid making such claims but to defend God's honor against them — which is precisely what this sūrah does, and why its epithet *al-Barāʾah*, "The Absolver," is tied to its recitation: the one who recites it with understanding has disowned, on God's behalf and his own, every indignity that human imagination has tried to attach to the Divine.

**What Verse Three Teaches About the Modern World**

The verse's relevance does not end with the refutation of ancient creeds, because the underlying error repeats itself in every age. Whenever human beings absolutize anything created — a leader, a nation, a science, a race, an ideology — they are, in effect, giving it a divine parentage: they treat something born in time as though it were eternal, something dependent as though it were self-sufficient. Modern "gods" are rarely worshipped in temples, but they are served with the same total devotion that the ancients gave their idols, and they are often imagined to confer the same immortality — the leader's cause outlives the follower, the nation continues the citizen, the bloodline carries the ancestor. Verse three stands against every such transfer of the divine attributes to the created: only the One Who was not born can give what no born thing can give; and anything that was begotten — anything that had a beginning in time, anything made by causes — is a creature, however impressive, and cannot be the object of final devotion. The verse thus functions as a permanent vaccine against idolatry in all its forms, ancient and modern: whatever was born and will die belongs to the order of creatures, and the heart that reserves its worship for the One outside that order has understood the meaning of *lam yalid wa lam yūlad*.

## Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ 112:4

> **And none is like unto Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seal on the Whole Sūrah**

The fourth verse closes the sūrah with its most comprehensive negation: *wa lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad* — "And none is like unto Him," or more literally, "And there has never been to Him any equal, not one." The particle *wa* links this verse to what precedes it as the conclusion of the entire argument; *lam yakun* is the categorical past-tense negation, declaring that no equal has existed, does not exist, and will not exist; *lahu* ("to Him") states the relation — the equality is excluded with respect to God Himself; *kufuwan* is the word for an equal, a match, a counterpart; and *aḥad* at the end — the very word with which the sūrah began — completes the circle by negating even a single instance of likeness. The repetition of *aḥad* in the first and last verses is a deliberate frame: the sūrah opens by affirming that God is One and closes by denying that anything is equal to that One. Between the two affirmations stands everything the human mind needs in order not to corrupt them: the affirmation of self-sufficiency and the denial of generation. The four verses thus form a ring — *aḥad* at the start, *aḥad* at the end, and in the middle the two truths that protect the divine uniqueness from the two most common corruptions, the corruption of need and the corruption of kinship. Some of the early readers, as the commentators note, even joined verses three and four in a single breath of recitation, so tightly are the negations bound together: no begetting, no being begotten, no equal.

**The Word Kufūʾ: A Match in Kind and Rank**

The word *kufuwan* deserves attention because of its social resonance in the Arabic of the Qur'an. *Kufūʾ* (the same root, *k-f-ʾ*) is the classical Arabic term for equality of rank and kind between two parties — most famously, the "suitability" or compatibility that Islamic law requires to be considered in marriage, the principle that a woman's guardian should not marry her to a man who is her gross inferior in religion, character, and standing. The Qur'an's use of the word here is thus pointed: it takes a term that Arabs used for assessing whether two people were of the same class and kind, and declares that with respect to God there is and can be no *kufūʾ* whatsoever — no being of His kind, His class, His rank, or His nature. A human *kufūʾ* is a peer who can stand beside you, match you, and deal with you as an equal; God has no peer who can stand beside Him, match Him in any attribute, or enter into any relation of equivalence with Him. The verse is the definitive answer to every form of comparison between God and creation — whether the comparison is made by the idolater who sets a statue beside God, by the philosopher who measures God's power against natural forces, or by the theologian who imagines God's attributes on the model of human attributes.

**The Sister Verse: "Naught Is Like unto Him"**

The Qur'an states this negation in several places, each with its own nuance. The most celebrated parallel is in Sūrat al-Shūrā, where the verse follows an entire passage describing the divine attributes:

> "Creator of the heavens and the earth. He made for you from yourselves mates, and from the cattle mates, multiplying you thereby. There is naught like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing." (Qur'an 42:11)

The commentators drew an important lesson from the pairing of the two halves of this verse. God describes Himself as Hearing and Seeing — attributes that human beings also possess in some measure — and then immediately declares that nothing is like Him. The verse thus steers between two errors at once. On one side is the error of *taʿṭīl*, stripping God of His attributes on the pretext that attributes imply likeness to creatures; on the other side is the error of *tashbīh*, imagining that because God hears and sees, His hearing and seeing are like ours. The Qur'anic position is the affirmation of the attributes without comparison: God hears, but not with an ear like ours; God sees, but not with an eye like ours; God knows, but not by a process of learning like ours. The attributes are real, because God truly is Hearing and Seeing; and they are incomparable, because nothing is like Him. The scholars expressed this with the formula *lā tashbīh wa lā taʿṭīl* — neither likening nor stripping — and they took 42:11 as its scriptural charter. This sūrah's fourth verse provides the same charter in its most absolute form: the *kufūʾ* — the equal, the counterpart, the thing that could be placed beside God and compared — is negated without remainder.

**The Attributes of God: Affirmed Without Comparison**

The relation of verse four to the doctrine of the divine names is subtle and important. Some readers have wondered: if nothing is like God, can we say anything positive about Him at all? If every word we use is drawn from human experience, is not every description of God a kind of comparison? The Qur'an's answer is that the names and attributes of God are revealed — God Himself has told us His names, and when He tells us that He is Living, Knowing, Powerful, Merciful, Hearing, and Seeing, we affirm them as true of Him in the manner that befits His majesty, without asking how. The negation of *kufūʾ* does not empty God of attributes; it purifies the attributes of creaturely limitation. God is Living — but His life has no beginning, no end, no dependence on a body or a soul. God is Knowing — but His knowledge is not acquired through senses, inference, or time; He knows all things eternally. God is Powerful — but His power is not exercised through instruments or effort and is not exhausted by any act. The fourth verse of this sūrah is thus the protective fence around every other verse of the Qur'an that describes God: when the Qur'an says God loves, God is angry, God plans, God smiles upon the believer, all of it is true, and none of it makes God like His creatures. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and the great commentators uniformly cite Mujāhid's explanation of this verse — that it means God has no spouse, no counterpart, no equal, no likeness — while the theologians add the further precision: no likeness in essence, no likeness in attributes, no likeness in acts. God's essence is not like essences, His attributes are not like attributes, and His acts are not like acts, even where the same words are used of creatures.

**Refuting Every Form of Likening**

The verse was understood by the earliest Muslims as the summary refutation of all the specific falsehoods around them. The idolaters had not only given God daughters and partners from among the jinn; they had, in effect, declared the angels and their idols to be of God's kind — beings worthy of the worship due to Him. The Qur'an records the strange claim that they even asserted kinship between God and the jinn:

> "And they have made a kinship between Him and the jinn — yet the jinn know well that they will surely be brought forth." (Qur'an 37:158)

Verse four answers the whole complex of claims at once: nothing — not an idol, not an angel, not a jinn, not a prophet, not a king, not a force of nature, not a celestial body — shares God's kind or rank. The Companion Ibn ʿAbbās and his student Mujāhid both read the verse as a comprehensive denial of any equal, and al-Qurṭubī's commentary gathers the early sayings to the same effect: the verse negates any being that could be compared to God in His divinity, His eternity, or His creative power. The verse's placement at the end of the sūrah means that the sūrah's whole movement — affirmation of oneness, affirmation of self-sufficiency, negation of generation — concludes by protecting itself: having said who God is, the sūrah now says what nothing else is. Every later attempt to corrupt the doctrine — whether by making Jesus divine in the sense that would compromise God's uniqueness, or by elevating saints or leaders to a share in God's governance, or by the crude anthropomorphism that pictures God as a being with a body and lineage — is cut off by this final clause.

**The Transcendent and the Near: Two Sides of One Truth**

A profound discussion in the classical tradition concerns how God can be utterly unlike His creation and yet intimately present to it. The Qur'an affirms both truths in adjacent verses:

> "Wheresoever you turn, there is the Face of God." (Qur'an 2:115)

> "And We are nearer to him than his jugular vein." (Qur'an 50:16)

If nothing is like God, how can God be "near" or "with" His creatures? The commentators resolved the apparent tension by distinguishing between God's essence and His relation to creation. In His essence, God is absolutely transcendent: nothing resembles Him, nothing contains Him, nothing comprehends Him. In His relation to creation, He is absolutely immanent: His knowledge encompasses all, His power sustains all, His mercy reaches all, and His presence with the believer is a reality affirmed throughout the Book. The fourth verse protects the first truth, and the verses of nearness protect the second, and the two together — held without blurring one into the other — are the complete Qur'anic doctrine. The early Muslims expressed the balance by saying that God is near in His knowledge and His care while remaining beyond all comparison in His being; the believer who recites *wa lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad* is not declaring God distant but declaring God incomparable — and it is precisely because He is incomparable that His nearness is worth having. Only a God Who is not one more creature among creatures can be the God Who is with every creature at once.

**Why the Sūrah Is Recited at Weddings and Deathbeds**

The word *kufūʾ* carries a beautiful practical echo in Islamic practice. Because the term is the technical word for compatibility in marriage, the sūrah came to be associated — by a kind of linguistic resonance the scholars delighted in — with the marriage sermon. It is reported that the Prophet and his Companions would include this sūrah in the marriage address, and the commentators explained the wisdom: the marriage contract is precisely the place where the question of *kufūʾ* — the suitability of the two parties — is considered with the greatest care, and the sūrah reminds the gathered community that while human beings must weigh compatibility in marriage, no compatibility, likeness, or equality can ever be weighed between God and any creature. The sūrah thus sanctifies the marriage bond by placing it under the sign of the divine uniqueness: the union of two human beings, who are indeed *kufūʾ* for one another, is lawful and blessed precisely because neither party is divine and no divine *kufūʾ* is being claimed. The same sūrah is recited to the dying and at the graveside, and the connection is equally profound: at the moment when every human support falls away, the believer affirms that God is One, self-sufficient, without offspring, without origin, and without equal — the only Reality that remains when every created reality has been stripped away. The four verses that taught the child his first creed are the same verses that steady the adult at his last breath.

**The Complete Cure: What This Verse Adds to Human Life**

Verse four also completes the sūrah's therapeutic work in the human soul. The commentators observed that the deepest disease of the human heart is the tendency to measure everything — including God — by the self. A person who has not purified his conception of God will inevitably worship a god he has made in his own image: a god who shares his prejudices, sanctions his appetites, and resembles his own character writ large. Such a god is not God at all but an idol built from ego — which is why the Qur'an says of the person who follows his own desire:

> "Have you seen the one who takes as his god his own desire?" (Qur'an 25:43)

The fourth verse is the cure for this subtle idolatry, because it forbids the very operation by which the ego builds its idols: the operation of likeness. Nothing is like God — therefore no image, no concept, no mental picture, no projection of the self can stand in for Him. The believer is thus driven beyond every finite conception, in a perpetual ascent of the mind: every time he forms a thought of God, he must remember that God is greater than the thought — a truth the Prophet expressed when he said:

> "I cannot enumerate the praise due to You; You are as You have praised Yourself." (Muslim 2225, from ʿĀʾishah, on the words of the Prophet in his prostration)

This is the final spiritual function of the sūrah: not to leave the believer with a doctrine but to leave him with an orientation — toward a God Who is always beyond, always greater, always other, and yet always near; a God Whose oneness gathers the scattered heart, Whose self-sufficiency silences every needless fear, Whose freedom from generation lifts the mind above the anxieties of time, and Whose incomparability purges the soul of every false image. Reciting *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* with this understanding is what the early Muslims meant by reciting it with *ikhlāṣ* — and it is why the Prophet could say that this sūrah, recited with sincerity, is equivalent to a third of the Qur'an: in four verses it delivers the knowledge on which every other verse of the Book depends, the knowledge of the God to Whom the whole Book calls.
