# Sūrah al-Burūj (Chapter 85) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter Descended Into the Smoke**

Sūrah al-Burūj is a Makkan sūrah of twenty-two verses. In the traditional ordering of revelation it is placed immediately after Sūrah 91, al-Shams, which fixes its home in the hardest stretch of the Makkan period — the years when the Muslim community was a small, unprotected minority inside a city that had decided to crush it.

That context is not background information to be filed away before the exegesis begins. It is the key to the chapter. Al-Burūj is a sūrah spoken to people who had already been tortured, and its central story is the story of people who had already been burned. When it says *qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd* — "may they perish, the inhabitants of the pit" — the Makkan believers hearing it did not need to be told what a pit filled with fire looked like. Some of them had been held beside one.

The testimony comes from Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt, a blacksmith who was among the earliest Muslims and among the most severely punished for it. Al-Bukhārī records his account in his Ṣaḥīḥ:

> *"We complained to the Messenger of God ﷺ while he was reclining on his own cloak in the shade of the Kaʿbah, and we said: 'Will you not seek help for us? Will you not supplicate for us?' He said: 'Among those who came before you, a man would be seized, a pit would be dug for him in the earth, and he would be set in it; then the saw would be brought and laid upon his head and he would be split into two halves, and iron combs would be drawn through his flesh and sinew down to the bone, and none of that would turn him from his religion. By God, this matter will be completed until a rider travels from Ṣanʿāʾ to Ḥaḍramawt fearing nothing but God — and the wolf for his sheep — but you are a hasty people.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3612, from Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt)

Read that report beside al-Burūj and the sūrah opens like a lock turning. The Prophet ﷺ consoles tortured men by pointing to tortured men before them, buried in pits exactly like the pit of verse 4. The Qur'an does the same in the very next lines: your suffering is not evidence that God has abandoned the pattern of history, because the pattern of history has always been this — and it has always ended the same way.

**The Name: Towers Above the Pit**

The sūrah takes its name from the first verse: *wa al-samāʾi dhāti al-burūj* — "By the sky possessed of constellations." *Burūj* is the plural of *burj*, a word whose primary sense in Arabic is something that rises and is made manifest: a tower, a fortified watch-tower on a wall, a palace, a raised station. Applied to the sky it denotes the great stars and their grouped stations — the twelve mansions of the zodiacal belt through which the sun and moon travel, and in wider usage the luminous markers God set as signs for travellers, seasons, and timekeeping.

The name is quietly devastating when set against the sūrah's own content. The chapter begins with towers in the heavens and ends with a book written on a Tablet above the heavens; between them, in the middle, sits a trench dug in the ground. The tyrants of the story went down into the earth, dug a pit, filled it with fuel, and sat upon it to watch human beings burn. God opens the sūrah by swearing on the vast architecture overhead — the constellations, which are to the sky what watch-towers are to a city, stationed, ordered, permanent, and seen by everyone. The juxtaposition makes the argument before a single word of doctrine is stated: the pit is small, low, temporary, and hidden; the heavens are immense, elevated, permanent, and public. Everything the persecutors tried to bury is being watched from a station they cannot reach.

Early Muslims sometimes referred to this sūrah by its other dominant image, calling it "the sūrah of the ukhdūd" — the pit — and it is also known as *al-Samāʾ dhāt al-Burūj* after its opening oath. Its verse count is agreed at twenty-two.

**Twenty-Two Verses in Six Movements**

The sūrah is short enough that its whole architecture can be taken in at a glance, and once seen it does not come apart.

The first movement (vv. 1–3) is three oaths: by the sky with its constellations, by the Promised Day, by the witness and the witnessed. Oaths in Qur'anic style are not decorations; they are the raising of a hand before a verdict, and they tell you what the verdict will concern. Heaven, the Day of Judgment, and testimony: those are the three pillars the rest of the chapter will stand on.

The second movement (vv. 4–10) is the atrocity and its verdict. The people of the pit are named, the fire is described, the persecutors are shown sitting and watching, their only stated grievance is exposed as faith itself, and the sentence is pronounced: for those who persecute believing men and women and do not repent, there is the punishment of Hell and the punishment of burning.

The third movement (v. 11) is a single verse of counterweight: those who believe and do righteous deeds have Gardens beneath which rivers flow — that is the great triumph. One verse is enough to outweigh seven verses of fire, and the disproportion is the point.

The fourth movement (vv. 12–16) turns from human history to Divine nature: your Lord's seizing is severe; He originates and returns; He is the Forgiving, the Loving; Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious; Doer of whatsoever He wills. This is the theological engine of the sūrah, and it is deliberately built out of paired attributes — severity beside forgiveness, power beside love — so that the reader does not receive a God of mere force or a God of mere tenderness.

The fifth movement (vv. 17–20) supplies historical proof: have you heard the report of the hosts — Pharaoh and Thamūd? The disbelievers remain in denial, and God is behind them, All-Encompassing. Two case studies, one from the biblical past and one from the Arabs' own northern desert, both of them peoples with overwhelming power, both of them erased.

The sixth movement (vv. 21–22) closes on revelation itself: nay, it is a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet. After a story about people who tried to erase a community, the sūrah ends by naming the thing that cannot be erased.

**The Sūrah of Witnesses**

Beneath the six movements runs a single unbroken word-field, and it is what gives the chapter its extraordinary unity: *witnessing*.

The sūrah swears by *al-shāhid wa al-mashhūd*, the witness and the witnessed (v. 3). The persecutors are described as *quʿūd*, sitting (v. 6), and as *shuhūd*, witnesses — present and watching (v. 7). God is described as *ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd*, Witness over all things (v. 9). God is *min warāʾihim muḥīṭ*, behind them and encompassing them (v. 20). And everything is inscribed upon a Tablet that is *maḥfūẓ*, preserved — kept, guarded, and never lost (v. 22).

Five times in twenty-two short verses the chapter insists that nothing happened unseen. The men who lit the fire were watching; God was watching them; the angels of the record were watching; and the whole affair is filed in a document that cannot be altered, burned, redacted, or mislaid. This is the deepest consolation the sūrah offers, and it is a consolation addressed to a very specific human wound.

Consider what persecution actually does to a soul. It does not merely hurt the body; it *isolates*. The victim of an atrocity is made to feel that the universe has no record of him — that he suffers in a corner no one is looking at, that the evidence will be destroyed, that the perpetrators will write the history, that in the end it will be as though he never existed at all. That feeling, more than the pain, is what breaks people. The pit was designed not only to kill but to erase.

Al-Burūj answers exactly that design. It says: they sat and watched — and you were seen; God is Witness over all things; God is behind them, encircling them, unable to be eluded; and there is a Tablet, preserved, in which the whole affair is already written. No atrocity has ever successfully deleted anything. This is why the sūrah, though it names fire and trenches and burning bodies, leaves the listener calmer than it found him.

**In the Noon and Afternoon Prayer of the Prophet**

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ recited this sūrah in the ordinary rhythm of his daily prayer. Muslim records in his Ṣaḥīḥ, from Jābir ibn Samurah:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to recite in the noon and afternoon prayers 'By the heaven and the Ṭāriq,' 'By the heaven possessed of constellations,' and the like of them."* (Muslim, Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Ṣalāh, from Jābir ibn Samurah)

That is a significant detail, and worth pausing on. The Ẓuhr and ʿAṣr prayers are the two silent-ish, mid-day, workaday prayers — the ones offered in the heat of the afternoon when a person is tired, busy, and least inclined to be moved. The Prophet ﷺ chose for them sūrahs of consolation and warning that were revealed to the persecuted. There is a pastoral logic in it: the hours when the believer's spirit is flattest are exactly the hours in which he should hear that tyrants are always defeated and that God is a Witness. Recitation is not only for the moments of high feeling; it is scheduled medicine for the ordinary afternoon.

The commentarial tradition also preserves a report on the merit of the sūrah, quoted in the faḍāʾil literature and transmitted in the introductions to the sūrah:

> *"Whosoever recites it, God will give reward for the good deeds ten times the number of all people gathered in Friday prayer and all people gathered on the Day of ʿArafah."*

The honest scholarly position must be stated: reports of this genre, found in the exegetical introductions and in works on the merits of the sūrahs, are generally transmitted through chains that the ḥadīth critics judge weak, and they are accepted for encouragement of good works rather than for establishing doctrine or legal rulings. What is certain and authenticated is the Prophet's practice of reciting this sūrah in prayer, and the Qur'an's own statement about recitation generally: that whoever recites a single letter of the Book has a good deed, and a good deed is tenfold (al-Tirmidhī, from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, who graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). The believer loses nothing by holding the weak report loosely and the sound one firmly.

**The Question Every Persecuted People Asks**

Every reader who has ever watched injustice triumph — a family destroyed by a lie, a country ground down by a dictator, a whistleblower ruined by the institution he tried to save, a child buried under rubble while the world changes the channel — has asked the question al-Burūj exists to answer. The question has three parts.

First: *does anyone see?* The sūrah answers with the witness-theme and with *wa Allāhu min warāʾihim muḥīṭ* — and God is behind them, All-Encompassing.

Second: *does it matter to God?* The sūrah answers with an oath, a curse upon the perpetrators, and a naming of the victims as *al-muʾminīn* — "the believers" — three times in a single verse, refusing to let them be reduced to casualties or statistics.

Third: *is there any justice?* The sūrah answers with verse 10, verse 11, verse 12, and the historical exhibits of verse 17–18: *inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd* — truly your Lord's assault is severe.

Notice the order in which these answers come. God does not begin by promising that the victims will win in this world; He begins by promising that nothing is unseen and that the reckoning is real. This is important for the psychology of faith, because a promise of earthly victory is falsifiable every day by the news, while the truth of Divine witness and judgment is not dependent on how the week turns out. The sūrah builds the believer's endurance on a foundation that cannot be shaken by events. That is why the Makkan community survived the pit, the boycott, and the saw: it had been taught to locate its certainty somewhere the persecutors could not reach.

**How to Read What Follows**

The commentary that follows treats each of the twenty-two verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order, exactly as the workflow mandates. Verses that the source commentary groups together — 4–5, 8–9, 11–12, 14–15, 17–18, 19–20, and 21–22 — are here separated and given full individual treatment, each one developed through the Arabic language and its roots, the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī and others), the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the reasoning behind the teaching, and its bearing on modern life.

Nothing has been shortened because the chapter is short; the opposite discipline has been applied. Because al-Burūj has only twenty-two verses, each verse carries a proportionately heavier share of the sūrah's total meaning, and each is treated accordingly — with the same depth of scholarship, evidence, and practical reflection that the commentary on Sūrah al-Baqarah required across 286 verses, and the fuller treatment that Sūrah al-Fātiḥah received across seven. From the first oath by the towered sky to the last word upon the Preserved Tablet, the whole chapter is given in full.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:1

> **By the sky possessed of constellations.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Is a Hand Raised Before a Verdict**

The sūrah begins with a *wāw al-qasam* — the Arabic letter *wāw* used not as "and" but as "by." *Wa al-samāʾi dhāti al-burūj*: "By the sky possessed of constellations." Grammatically the noun after this *wāw* is drawn into the genitive case by an understood preposition of swearing, and the sentence is incomplete by design. An oath in Arabic — as in English — is a fragment waiting for its conclusion: you swear *by* something, and then you state what you swear *to*. The scholars of Qur'anic rhetoric call the concluding statement the *jawāb al-qasam*, "the answer of the oath," and they debated where the answer to these three opening oaths actually lies.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both note that the answer is most plausibly the declaration in verse 12:

> *"Truly thy Lord's assault is severe."*

with everything in between — the story of the pit, the condemnation, the promise of the Gardens — functioning as an extended affirmation built inside the oath. Others held that the answer is verse 4 itself, *qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd*, "may they perish, the inhabitants of the pit." And others, following the general Qur'anic pattern of oaths about the Hereafter, held that the answer is deliberately left unstated so that the hearer supplies it: *you will surely be raised, you will surely be judged, you will surely be requited.*

That suppression is itself a rhetorical act. When a speaker swears and then pauses, the listener's mind rushes forward to complete the sentence, and whatever he completes it with is his own conviction speaking. The Qur'an frequently uses this device; here it means that the first three verses hang in the air like a raised hand, and the reader arrives at the story of the pit already braced for a verdict.

**Burj: What Rises and Is Made Visible**

The word *burj* comes from the root *b-r-j*, whose basic sense is to rise up and become apparent. From the same root comes *tabarruj*, the displaying of one's beauty or adornment in public, which the Qur'an forbids to the believing women:

> *"And do not display yourselves as was the display (tabarruj) of the former time of ignorance."* (Qur'an 33:33)

The connection is not accidental but essential: a *burj* is anything that rises above its surroundings and cannot be missed. Hence its concrete uses in Arabic — a tower, a fortified watch-tower built into a city wall, a palace, a raised platform. The Qur'an itself uses the word in that architectural sense when it demolishes the human fantasy of safety:

> *"Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even if you were in towers raised high (burūj mushayyadah)."* (Qur'an 4:78)

Apply that image to the sky and the meaning is immediate. The stars are the sky's towers: raised, stationed, visible, permanent, distributed along its walls like sentries along a rampart. Al-Qurṭubī, gathering the interpretations, records that *burūj* here may mean the stars themselves, or the largest and most brilliant of them, or lofty towers and palaces in the heaven, or the twelve stations of the zodiacal belt.

**The Twelve Mansions: Astronomy the Bedouin Already Knew**

The interpretation most frequently adopted by the classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Rāzī all transmit it — is that the *burūj* are the twelvefold division of the belt of the sky through which the sun, the moon, and the planets travel: Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces.

This is a real astronomical fact, not a poetic conceit. The fixed stars of the celestial sphere are grouped, from our vantage point, into recognizable bands; the sun appears to travel through them across the year, spending roughly a month in each. The Arabs of the seventh century knew this sky with an intimacy modern readers have lost. Their calendar was lunar, their navigation was stellar, their poetry was saturated with the rising and setting of particular star-groups — the *anwāʾ* — because rain, seasons, grazing, and travel all depended on reading them correctly. When the Qur'an swore by the sky's *burūj*, it swore by something every listener could verify by walking outside after sunset.

The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere with equal precision:

> *"Blessed is He who placed constellations (burūj) in the sky, and placed therein a lamp and a luminous moon."* (Qur'an 25:61)

> *"And We have certainly placed constellations (burūj) in the sky and adorned it for those who look."* (Qur'an 15:16)

> *"It is He who made the sun a shining light and the moon a light, and determined for it stations (manāzil), that you may know the number of years and the reckoning."* (Qur'an 10:5)

Note in 15:16 the phrase *li'l-nāẓirīn* — "for those who look." The adornment has an audience. The universe, in Qur'anic presentation, is not merely structured; it is *addressed* — decorated for observers, laid out for readers, built to be seen. That single phrase elevates looking at the sky from a hobby into a religious act, and it explains why Muslim civilization produced astronomers of the first rank: for a thousand years, mapping the heavens was understood as reading a text God had written in light.

**A Sign, Not a Controller: The Line Islam Drew**

Because the sūrah swears by star-stations, a word of theological clarification is required, and the classical commentators were careful to supply it. The *burūj* are signs — markers of time, direction, and the grandeur of their Maker — and they are not agents. They do not govern human destiny, they do not distribute fortune, and reading them for that purpose is what the Qur'an and the Prophet ﷺ explicitly rejected.

> *"And it is He who placed for you the stars that you may be guided by them through the darknesses of the land and the sea."* (Qur'an 6:97)

Guidance here is navigation, not divination. The Prophet ﷺ drew the boundary sharply:

> *"Whoever acquires a branch of astrology (shuʿbah min al-nujūm) has acquired a branch of magic; the more he increases, the more he increases."* (Abū Dāwūd 3905; Ibn Mājah 3726, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

Ibn Kathīr and others repeat the caution in their commentary on precisely these verses: the oath is by the sky as a created sign of God, and to treat the sign as the source of power is to commit the oldest error in religion — worshipping the furniture instead of the Owner of the house. The distinction matters more now than ever, because horoscope columns, birth-chart apps, and "mercury retrograde" explanations of a bad week are the same error in digital clothing: an attempt to outsource responsibility for one's life to objects that have no will.

**Why This Oath, Before This Story?**

The interpretive question is why a sūrah about a burning pit begins by swearing on the constellations. Three answers suggest themselves, and they accumulate.

The first is *scale*. The pit is a hole in the ground. The constellations are the architecture of the heavens. By opening on the heavens, God places the coming atrocity in its true proportion before we are asked to feel anything about it — the crime was real and monstrous, and it was also small, local, brief, and performed under a sky that did not blink. Every person who has ever felt crushed by an injustice needs this correction of scale: the thing that is destroying you is not as large as it feels, because you are measuring it from inside.

The second is *permanence*. The stars are the most durable visible objects human beings know. Civilizations rise and fall; the constellations overhead are the same ones that Abraham looked at, the same ones the people of the pit saw from inside their trench. Swearing by them is swearing by the fixed frame within which temporary events occur — a frame that will still be in place when the verdict is delivered.

The third is *witness*. A tower's function is to see. A city's watch-towers exist so that nothing approaches unobserved. If the sky's *burūj* are its towers, then the sky is a city under observation, and the oath quietly introduces the sūrah's governing theme: the tyrants thought they were working in a hidden place, in a hole in the earth, at night. They were in fact in the open, under a sky full of stationed lights, before a Witness. As the following verses will make explicit, they sat and watched — and they were watched.

**The Sky Over Every Grave**

There is a last, more human reason this oath lands as it does. Everyone who has stood at a grave has looked up. It seems to be a reflex of the species: when the earth takes someone, the eyes go to the sky. Perhaps it is because the sky is the only visible thing that has not changed. The city where a person died is rearranged within a decade; the house is sold; the name on the mailbox changes; the constellations do not.

That is what God swears by at the head of a sūrah about mass murder: the permanent, the stationed, the visible-to-all, the never-altered. Before He speaks of the pit, He speaks of the towers above it. Before He mentions what was buried, He mentions what cannot be moved. And before He describes the fire that men lit, He describes the lamps that He placed — *wa jaʿala fīhā sirājan wa qamran munīran*, "and placed therein a lamp and a luminous moon" (25:61). The listener is being prepared, without yet knowing it, to hear that the fire in the trench is not the last word about light.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:2

> **By the Day promised,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Oath Moves From Space to Time**

The first oath was spatial: by the sky and its stationed towers. The second is temporal: *wa al-yawmi al-mawʿūd* — "by the Day promised." Within two short clauses the sūrah has swept the whole of creation, height and horizon and history, into the courtroom. There is the frame of the heavens, fixed and permanent; and there is the appointed hour at which everything done beneath that frame will be reviewed.

The commentators are essentially unanimous that "the Day promised" is the Day of Resurrection. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Suyūṭī all state it plainly, and the Qur'an itself uses the same language repeatedly:

> *"And the Hour is coming — there is no doubt about it — and God will raise up whoever is in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:7)

> *"Truly the Hour is coming; I almost conceal it, so that every soul may be recompensed for what it strives."* (Qur'an 20:15)

> *"Indeed, the Day of Separation is an appointed time (mīqāt)."* (Qur'an 78:17)

The pairing of oaths here deserves attention in its own right. God swears by the heaven and then by the Day: by the greatest of His visible creations and by the greatest of His unseen appointments. Al-Rāzī's method of reading such sequences — asking what each element contributes to the statement being sworn to — yields a clean result. The heaven answers the question *is there an order above us?*; the Day answers the question *will that order ever settle accounts?* The two together answer the exact question a persecuted community is asking: is the universe moral, and will the morality ever be enforced?

**Mawʿūd: The Passive That Names Everyone**

The word *mawʿūd* is a passive participle from *wa-ʿa-da*, "to promise": literally, "that which has been promised." The grammar is doing careful work.

The promiser is not named, because it needs no naming: the Day is promised by God. The promisee is also not named — and this is where the silence becomes eloquent. A passive participle without a stated object covers everyone. The Day was promised to the prophets, who were told their message would be vindicated. It was promised to the martyrs, who were told their blood would not be wasted. It was promised to the tyrants, who were warned of it and did not listen. It was promised to the ordinary sinner, who was told there would be mercy if he repented and ruin if he did not. It was promised to every human being who has ever felt that things are not finished. One word, unqualified, holds the whole of humanity inside it.

The Qur'an insists elsewhere that this particular promise cannot be defaulted upon, and the insistence is repeated with almost legal emphasis:

> *"Our Lord, You will surely gather mankind for a Day about which there is no doubt; indeed, God does not fail His appointment (lā yukhlifu al-mīʿād)."* (Qur'an 3:9)

> *"So do not think God will fail His promise to His messengers; indeed, God is Almighty, Owner of retribution."* (Qur'an 14:47)

> *"[It is] the promise of God. God does not fail His promise, but most people do not know."* (Qur'an 30:6)

Three separate verses, three separate formulations, one point: the Day is not a prediction, a hope, or a probability. It is a *waʿd* — a pledged commitment by the One who alone controls whether it arrives.

**The Difference Between a Prediction and a Promise**

This distinction is worth pressing, because it is the hinge on which a believer's endurance turns.

A prediction is a statement about what will happen; its truth depends on the accuracy of the predictor's model, and it can be falsified by events. A promise is a statement about what the promiser *commits himself to do*; its truth depends on his power and his faithfulness. A forecast about the weather can be wrong without the forecaster being wicked; a promise broken by a father to his child is not an error but a betrayal.

When God says the Day is *mawʿūd*, He has placed Himself, in His own words, under obligation. He has made His own faithfulness the guarantee of your justice. That is why the Qur'an returns to the phrase so often — not to add information but to convert information into trust. The believer facing an unjust court, a stolen inheritance, a slander he cannot disprove, or a government that will never be held accountable does not need to know *when*. He needs to know that Someone has pledged, and that the pledge cannot be revoked.

The logic is identical to the logic of a debt. If you lend money on a signed contract, you do not spend the following years anxiously wondering whether repayment is likely; you know there is an instrument, a signer, and a court. The signed document does not make the money arrive sooner, but it changes the psychological position of the lender completely: he is no longer hoping, he is holding a claim. *Al-yawm al-mawʿūd* is that signed document. The Day is described in the passive because it has already been written down.

**An Appointment With a Fixed Hour**

The Qur'an's other vocabulary for the same reality reinforces this: *mīqāt*, an appointed time; *ajal musammā*, a named term; *sāʿah*, the Hour. Each carries the sense of a scheduled meeting rather than an open-ended possibility.

> *"They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive? Say: knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none will reveal it at its time except Him."* (Qur'an 7:187)

The concealment of the date and the certainty of the appointment are held together deliberately. God withholds the *when* and guarantees the *that*. Both halves serve a single purpose: readiness. If the date were published, human beings would procrastinate with mathematical precision — repent on the eve, sin for a lifetime, and treat the meeting like a dentist's appointment they can reschedule. If the appointment were uncertain, human beings would dismiss it. The concealed-but-certain date produces the only psychological state that moral formation requires: sustained alertness.

The Prophet ﷺ expressed this with a gesture. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record from Sahl ibn Saʿd:

> *"I and the Hour were sent like these two."* And he held up his index and middle fingers. (al-Bukhārī 4936; Muslim 2950)

Two fingers side by side — the mission and the Hour, adjacent, no third between them. Every generation since the Prophet ﷺ has lived in that narrow space, which is why no generation has ever been entitled to feel safe and none has ever been entitled to despair.

**Why the Day Is Sworn By, at This Point in the Sūrah**

The oath's placement directly after the constellations is not arbitrary. The sūrah is about to narrate an atrocity in which the victims died and the killers, in all likelihood, prospered and died in their beds. Any account of such an event that stops at the grave is unbearable — not merely sad, but *unintelligible*, because it leaves the moral ledger permanently unbalanced. A woman holding her infant was pushed into a fire; the men who pushed her went home to dinner.

The second oath exists to prevent that reading. Before the story is told, the hearer is made to swear by the Day. The effect is to install an interpretive frame around the narrative in advance: what you are about to hear is not the whole event. The pit is the middle of a sentence whose beginning is in the sky and whose end is on the Promised Day. The atrocity is real; its finality is not.

There is also a structural echo to notice. Verse 2 swears by *al-yawm al-mawʿūd* — the Day promised. Verse 3 will swear by *al-shāhid wa al-mashhūd* — the witness and the witnessed. A promised day plus a witness equals a court. The two oaths together constitute the entire apparatus of judgment before a single human being appears in the sūrah. This is characteristic of Qur'anic method: establish the tribunal, then present the case.

**Living Toward an Appointment**

Practical reflection on this verse tends toward two errors, and the sūrah avoids both.

The first error is terror: an imagination so fixed on the Day that life becomes paralysis and religion becomes dread. The Qur'an does not present the Promised Day as a threat to the innocent. To the wronged it presents it as relief — the day the debt is collected. The people of the pit are not warned about the Day; their killers are.

The second error is deferral: treating the Day as so distant that it exerts no pressure on conduct. The cure the Qur'an applies is not more information about the date but repeated reminders of its nearness in moral terms — *iqtarabati al-sāʿah*, "the Hour has drawn near" (54:1) — and the Prophet's advice to live in the world as a traveller, taking from the road only what the road requires.

The most useful way to hold the verse is the way an adult holds a fixed deadline. A student with an examination six months away does not think about the examination every minute; he thinks about it once, notes the date, and then organizes his days around it. The date's power lies not in being constantly contemplated but in being certain. *Al-yawm al-mawʿūd* is that date. God has fixed it, He has pledged it, He has concealed its hour, and He has told you it is coming — which means the ordinary Tuesday you are living is not an ordinary Tuesday. It is one of the days on which the evidence is being gathered.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:3

> **and by the witness and the witnessed.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words, Both Indefinite, Both Deliberately Open**

The third oath is the shortest and the most expansive: *wa al-shāhidi wa al-mashhūd* — "by the witness and the witnessed." Both words come from the root *sh-h-d*, the root of testimony, presence, and knowledge acquired by direct observation. *Shāhid* is the active participle — the one who witnesses, who is present, who testifies. *Mashhūd* is the passive participle — the one or thing witnessed, attended, testified to.

A crucial grammatical feature governs everything that follows. Both nouns are preceded by the definite article in the written text, yet the commentators treat the expression as effectively generic, because the oath is not tied to any single specified witness. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī observe that if the Qur'an had intended one particular day or one particular person, it would have named him; instead it names a *category* — witnessing as such, in both of its directions. The result is that the verse has attracted a wide garden of interpretations, none of which excludes the others.

**The Garden of Interpretations**

Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Suyūṭī all gather the readings. The principal ones are as follows.

*Friday and the Day of ʿArafah.* The most widely transmitted interpretation identifies *al-shāhid* with Friday and *al-mashhūd* with the Day of ʿArafah. The basis is a report narrated from Abū Hurayrah:

> *"The witness (al-shāhid) is Friday, and the witnessed (al-mashhūd) is the Day of ʿArafah."* (al-Tirmidhī, who graded it ḥasan gharīb; also transmitted by al-Bayhaqī and others)

The reasoning is elegant: Friday "witnesses" for the deeds performed in it and gathers the community, while ʿArafah is "witnessed" by the assembled pilgrims and by the angels. Both days are authenticated peaks of the Islamic calendar. Muslim records:

> *"The best day on which the sun has risen is Friday: on it Adam was created, on it he was admitted to the Garden, and on it he was expelled from it."* (Muslim 854, from Abū Hurayrah)

> *"There is no day on which God frees more servants from the Fire than the Day of ʿArafah."* (Muslim 1348, from ʿĀʾishah)

*The Prophet ﷺ and mankind.* Others take the witness to be Muhammad ﷺ, on the strength of the Qur'an's own explicit statement:

> *"So that the Messenger may be a witness against you, and you may be witnesses against mankind."* (Qur'an 22:78; cf. 2:143)

> *"O Prophet, indeed We have sent you as a witness and a bringer of good news and a warner."* (Qur'an 33:45)

*The messengers and their communities.* A related view generalizes the previous one: every prophet is the witness over his own community, as in:

> *"And the day We raise from every community a witness against them from among themselves, and We bring you as a witness against these."* (Qur'an 16:89)

> *"...and I was a witness over them as long as I remained among them; but when You took me up, You were the Watcher over them."* (Qur'an 5:117)

*This community and all others.* Some reverse the direction: the Muslim *ummah* is the witness, and all other communities are the witnessed, again from 2:143 — *"Thus We made you a middle community, that you may be witnesses for mankind."*

*Human beings and God.* A subtle reading transmitted in the commentaries holds that the witness is the human being and the witnessed is God — that is, mankind testifies to God through His signs, and God is the One "seen" by the eye of faith in everything that exists. Al-Qurṭubī records it.

*Human beings and the Black Stone.* Another report holds that the witnessed is the Black Stone of the Kaʿbah, which pilgrims touch in renewal of the covenant, and which will testify. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"By God, God will raise it up on the Day of Resurrection with an eye by which it sees and a tongue by which it speaks, and it will testify for whoever touched it in truth."* (al-Tirmidhī 961; Ibn Mājah 2944, from Ibn ʿAbbās)

*God Himself as the Witness.* Finally, some take the witness to be God, since He names Himself so:

> *"God suffices as a Witness between us and you."* (Qur'an 4:79) — *"Say: what thing is greatest in testimony? Say: God is a Witness between me and you."* (Qur'an 6:19)

Al-Ṭabarī's method with such a spread of readings is instructive and should be learned by every student of the Qur'an. When a revealed word admits several meanings, none of which contradicts another established text, he does not force a choice. He affirms that the word may carry all of them, because God's speech is not confined to a single signification as human speech is. The multiplicity here is not confusion; it is density.

**The Anatomy of a Courtroom**

Set the three oaths side by side and a legal architecture appears.

Verse 1 swears by the sky — the *jurisdiction*, the fixed and permanent order within which the case arises. Verse 2 swears by the Promised Day — the *date of hearing*, fixed and unpostponable. Verse 3 swears by the witness and the witnessed — the *evidence*. Jurisdiction, calendar, testimony: nothing is missing for a trial.

The Qur'an describes the same courtroom elsewhere with unnerving specificity:

> *"On a Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet will testify against them for what they used to do."* (Qur'an 24:24)

> *"This is Our Book, speaking against you in truth. Indeed We used to record what you did."* (Qur'an 45:29)

> *"And the earth will shine with the light of its Lord, and the Book will be laid down, and the prophets and the witnesses will be brought, and it will be judged between them in truth, and they will not be wronged."* (Qur'an 39:69)

Note in 39:69 that the word used for those brought forward is *al-shuhadāʾ* — the very plural of the word in this verse. The Day promised is the day the witnesses arrive.

**Why This Oath Belongs to This Sūrah**

The connection to the story that follows is so tight that it reads like a deliberate setup. Within five verses of swearing by *al-shāhid wa al-mashhūd*, the sūrah describes the persecutors sitting by the fire (v. 6) and being *shuhūd* — witnesses, present spectators — of what they did to the believers (v. 7), and then declares that God is *ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd*, Witness over all things (v. 9).

The same word-field, three times, in six verses. This is not accidental vocabulary; it is the sūrah's thesis in the form of a grammar. The men who burned believers did so as *witnesses* — they stayed to watch, they wanted to see, they treated the atrocity as a spectacle. And God answers them by becoming, in the same word, the Witness over them. The voyeur becomes the observed. That is the entire argument of al-Burūj compressed into a root.

**The Obligation to Be a Witness**

Islam does not treat witnessing as a passive condition of having been present. It treats it as a duty with moral weight, and the Qur'an legislates it in the strongest terms:

> *"O you who believe, be upholders of justice, witnesses for God, even if it be against yourselves, or your parents and relatives."* (Qur'an 4:135)

> *"And do not conceal testimony; whoever conceals it — his heart is indeed sinful."* (Qur'an 2:283)

The second verse is remarkable in its psychology. It does not say the concealer has committed a technical infraction; it says *his heart is sinful* — the sin has migrated from the act into the organ. A person who watches an injustice and stays silent does not merely fail to help; he damages himself. Silence in the presence of oppression is self-harm wearing the disguise of self-preservation.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the same teaching a political edge:

> *"The best jihad is a word of truth spoken before a tyrannical ruler."* (Abū Dāwūd 4344; al-Tirmidhī 2174, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

**Witnessing in the Age of the Camera**

No generation in history has possessed the technical capacity to be a witness that this one has. Every pocket holds a camera; every city is instrumented; every atrocity now arrives with footage, timestamps, and metadata. The world has become, in the most literal sense, *mashhūd* — witnessed.

And yet the moral position has not automatically improved, which tells us something important about this verse. Footage without a witness is only data. The modern failure is not a shortage of evidence but a shortage of *shuhadāʾ* — of people who will look at the evidence, name it accurately, and accept the cost of naming it. Millions view; a handful testify. The bystander effect, which social psychology documents in ordinary street emergencies, has been scaled up to a global phenomenon: the more people who see, the more each individual feels that seeing has already been done by someone else.

The Qur'an's answer is that witnessing is not a state but an act, and that the act is owed to God before it is owed to the victim — *shuhadāʾa li-llāh*, "witnesses for God." You do not testify because the audience is sympathetic, the case is winnable, or the victim is on your side; you testify because there is a Witness above the witnesses who has recorded both the crime and your silence. That is the only foundation for testimony strong enough to hold when the crowd turns.

The sūrah swears by it before it tells the story. Whatever else al-Burūj is about, it is about the fact that someone saw — and that the seeing was never neutral.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:4

> **May they perish, the inhabitants of the pit,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Is Both a Curse and a Report**

The three oaths end, and the first word of the case is *qutila* — literally "he was killed," in the passive, applied here to a plural subject: "they were killed." The commentators divide over its grammatical function, and the division is genuine and productive.

The majority, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, read it as an *imprecation* — a divine curse, in the Arabic idiom of *duʿāʾ*: "May they perish; may they be destroyed; cursed are they." The Qur'an uses this idiom elsewhere with unmistakable force:

> *"Perish the conjecturers (qutila al-kharrāṣūn)."* (Qur'an 51:10)

> *"Perish man — how ungrateful he is (qutila al-insānu mā akfarah)."* (Qur'an 80:17)

In Arabic, *qutila* said of a person who is alive is the strongest available form of condemnation, an invocation that the worst possible thing befall him. When God uses it, the classical commentators explain, it is not a wish but a decree: what God curses is already destroyed in the ledger of reality, whether or not it still walks.

A second reading — transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Qurṭubī — takes the word as a plain *statement of fact*: "The inhabitants of the pit were slain," i.e., they were destroyed and perished. This is the reading noted in the source commentary as "Slain were the inhabitants of the pit." On it, the verse is not a curse but an announcement: the thing they did has already been answered, and their end has already been written.

Both readings survive in the tradition because both are true. The perpetrators are cursed, and they are destroyed.

**Who Are the "Inhabitants of the Pit"? A Transmitted Difference**

There is a further question the exegetes took seriously: does *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* mean the people who *dug* the pit, or the people who were *thrown into* it?

The majority view identifies them as the persecutors — the king and his men who dug the trenches, lit the fires, and sat to watch. The reasons are textual: the curse is pronounced upon them; verses 5–7 describe them sitting beside the fire and witnessing what they did to *the believers*, which distinguishes them from the believers; and verse 10 then condemns all who persecute believers, continuing their thread.

Ibn ʿAbbās, in a report preserved by al-Qurṭubī and others, held that *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* are the believers who were cast into it, and that *qutila* is a report of their killing. On that reading the sūrah opens by naming the martyrs — and it opens with a passive verb that places them as the object of an action done *to* them, while the following verses transfer the guilt to those who did it.

The Arabic idiom supports both, because *ṣāḥib* — "companion of, possessor of, belonging to" — attaches a person to whatever defines him. The Qur'an speaks of *aṣḥāb al-nār* and *aṣḥāb al-jannah*, of *aṣḥāb al-kahf*, *aṣḥāb al-shimāl*, *aṣḥāb al-rass*, *aṣḥāb al-aykah*. To be a "companion of" a thing is to be permanently associated with it. And here a detail of immense moral weight emerges from the Qur'an's choice of names.

**The Naming of Victims and the Naming of Killers**

The persecutors are named in the Qur'an by their crime: *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* — the companions of the pit. The pit is their identity, their epithet, their entry in the record of history. Fourteen centuries later, no one remembers the king's personal name with certainty; everyone remembers what he dug.

The victims, by contrast, are never named by what was done to them. The Qur'an does not call them "the burned ones," "the victims of the pit," or "the companions of the fire." In verse 8 they are called *al-muʾminīn* — the believers. In verse 10, *muʾminīn* and *muʾmināt* — believing men and believing women.

That asymmetry is a deliberate act of restoration. Tyranny's chief ambition is definitional: it wants to write the victim's identity as *victim*, to make the injury the summary of the person, to reduce a human being to the thing that was done to him. The Qur'an refuses. It hands the killers their crime as a name and hands the martyrs their faith as a name. One group is remembered for what it destroyed; the other for what it held.

Anyone who has ever been reduced by other people — to a diagnosis, a failure, a scandal, a nationality, a statistic, a case number — should understand what this verse is doing. It is the Divine refusal to let your worst day become your title.

**Ukhdūd: The Word Itself**

*Ukhdūd* (plural *akhādīd*) comes from the root *kh-d-d*, whose basic meaning is a groove, furrow, or channel cut into a surface. A plough makes *akhādīd* in a field; a scar or a deep line makes *akhādīd* in a face; tears make them in cheeks (*khadd* is the cheek). The word therefore carries the sense of a long, narrow incision into something that was whole.

The Qur'an uses *ukhdūd* only here, in this sūrah. It is not a general word for a hole or a grave; it is specifically a *trench* — long, dug into the earth, and here filled with fire. The image is of a canal of flame.

**The Authoritative Account: The Ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb**

The single most important source for identifying these verses is an authentic ḥadīth narrated by Ṣuhayb ibn Sinān al-Rūmī, recorded by Muslim in his Ṣaḥīḥ (no. 3005) in the Book of Asceticism and Softening of Hearts. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī and al-Suyūṭī all cite it as the primary explanation of these verses. It is long, and it must be given, because the Qur'an's seven verses are its summary.

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ said: There was a king among those before you who had a sorcerer. When the sorcerer grew old he said to the king: 'I have become old, so send me a boy whom I may teach sorcery.' So he sent him a boy to teach.*
>
> *Now on the boy's road there was a monk, and the boy would sit with him and listen to his speech, and it amazed him. When he came to the sorcerer he would pass by the monk and sit with him; and when he arrived at the sorcerer's, the sorcerer would beat him. He complained of this to the monk, who said: 'When you fear the sorcerer, say: my family detained me; and when you fear your family, say: the sorcerer detained me.'*
>
> *While he was in that state he came upon a great beast that had blocked the people's road, and he said: 'Today I shall know whether the monk's affair is better or the sorcerer's.' He took a stone and said: 'O God, if the monk's affair is more beloved to You than the sorcerer's, then kill this beast so that the people may pass.' He threw it and killed it, and the people went on.*
>
> *He came and told the monk, and the monk said: 'O my son, today you are better than I. Your affair has reached what I see. You will be tested — and if you are tested, do not inform on me.'*
>
> *The boy used to heal the blind from birth and the leper, and to treat people of all kinds of diseases. A courtier of the king who had become blind heard of him and came to him with many gifts, saying: 'All this is yours if you heal me.' The boy said: 'I heal no one; only God heals. If you believe in God, I will call upon Him and He will heal you.' So he believed in God, and God healed him...*
>
> *The king tortured him until he indicated the boy. The boy was brought and the king said: 'O my son, your sorcery has reached the point where you heal the blind from birth and the leper, and you do such and such.' He said: 'I heal no one; only God heals.' The king tortured him until he indicated the monk.*
>
> *The monk was brought and told: 'Renounce your religion.' He refused. The saw was called for and placed at the parting of his hair, and he was sawn until his two halves fell. Then the king's courtier was brought and told: 'Renounce your religion.' He refused, and the same was done to him. Then the boy was brought and told: 'Renounce your religion.' He refused...*
>
> *The king said to some of his men: 'Take him to such-and-such a mountain, climb it with him, and when you reach its summit, if he renounces his religion let him go; otherwise throw him down.' They went, and the boy said: 'O God, suffice me against them however You will.' The mountain shook with them and they fell, and the boy came walking back to the king. The king said: 'What happened to your companions?' He said: 'God sufficed me against them.'*
>
> *He handed him to others and said: 'Carry him in a small boat, take him to the middle of the sea; if he renounces his religion let him go, otherwise throw him in.' They went, and the boy said: 'O God, suffice me against them however You will.' The boat capsized over them and they drowned, and the boy came walking back to the king. The King said: 'What happened to your companions?' He said: 'God sufficed me against them.'*
>
> *Then the boy said to the king: 'You will not be able to kill me until you do what I command.' He said: 'What is it?' He said: 'Gather the people in one open plain, tie me to a trunk, take an arrow from my quiver, place it in the middle of the bow, and say: "In the Name of God, the Lord of the boy" — then shoot at me. If you do that, you will kill me.'*
>
> *So he gathered the people in one open plain, tied him to a trunk, took an arrow from his quiver, placed it in the middle of the bow, and said: 'In the Name of God, the Lord of the boy.' Then he shot, and the arrow struck his temple. The boy put his hand over the wound and died. And the people said: 'We believe in the Lord of the boy' — three times.*
>
> *The king was informed: 'Do you see what you used to fear? By God, it has come upon you — the people have believed.' So he ordered that trenches (akhādīd) be dug at the mouths of the roads, and fires were kindled in them, and he said: 'Whoever does not renounce his religion, throw him into it' — or: he was told to cast himself in. They did so, until a woman came with her infant son and hesitated to fall into it, and the child said: 'O mother, be patient, for you are upon the truth.'"* (Muslim 3005, from Ṣuhayb)

**What the Story Contributes That the Verses Do Not State**

Several details in the ḥadīth illuminate the verses and should be marked now.

The king did not begin as a persecutor of the masses; he began as a man protecting a monopoly. His sorcerer was the state's instrument of control, and the boy's exposure to a monk — a man with no office, no salary, and no power except the truth of what he said — was the first threat. Persecution almost always starts as the defence of an institution's monopoly on meaning.

The conversion happened by healing, not argument. The blind courtier came with gifts; the boy refused payment and named God as the only healer; the man believed and was healed. Faith here entered through *benefit received and honestly attributed*. The courtier's confession — *"my Lord and your Lord is God"* — cost him his life under saw.

The boy's death was chosen, and its purpose was public. He explicitly instructed the king in how to kill him, requiring that the arrow be loosed in the Name of "the Lord of the boy" — before the whole assembled population. The scholars, including al-Nawawī and Ibn Kathīr, discuss this: the boy did not seek death for its own sake but accepted it as the means by which an entire city would believe, which is exactly what happened. It is a case of the Islamic principle that a life may be risked where a greater religious interest is secured by the risk, and it stands beside the tradition's accounts of those who plunged alone into enemy lines.

And the trenches came *after* the mass conversion. The pit was not the instrument of the persecution; it was the response to the persecution's failure. The king had tried murder and produced a nation of believers. The pit was the act of a man who had lost.

**The Historical Candidates**

Alongside the ḥadīth, the exegetical tradition preserves historical identifications. Ibn Kathīr gives most space to the account of Dhū Nuwās, the last great king of Ḥimyar in Yemen — known in the sources as Yūsuf, having adopted Judaism — who is reported to have attacked the Christians of Najrān in the early sixth century and to have burned many of them in a trench. The event was famous enough in Arabia to have entered Byzantine, Syriac, and South Arabian records, and it triggered the Abyssinian intervention in Yemen, which in turn produced the governor Abraha and his march upon Makkah — the event commemorated in Sūrah 105, al-Fīl. One pit in Najrān, on that chain of reading, stands a few decades behind the elephants at Makkah and within living memory of the Prophet's own generation.

Other identifications are transmitted: some said the persecuted were Magians; some placed the pit in Syria; some in Babylon; and some — as the source commentary notes — held that the story concerns one of those messengers whom the Qur'an did not name, since God says: *"And messengers We have related to you before, and messengers We have not related to you"* (40:78). Reports going back to ʿAlī and to Ibn ʿAbbās mention more than one *ukhdūd* — pits in Yemen, at Najrān, and in the Levant — and al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr gather these without insisting that the Qur'an means any single one.

**Why the Qur'an Refuses to Name Them**

The refusal is the point. The Qur'an does not say "Dhū Nuwās," or "Najrān," or a date, or a dynasty. It says *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* — the companions of the pit.

This is a deliberate act of generalization, and it is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic narrative techniques. A named event belongs to a place and a year; a type belongs to all of history. By leaving the identification open — while the authentic ḥadīth supplies a concrete instance — the Qur'an converts a sixth-century atrocity into a permanent category. Every regime that has dug a trench, every stadium used as a holding pen, every mass grave located with ground-penetrating radar, every family that received a death certificate without a body, belongs to *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd*.

The source commentary states the principle correctly: although the verses pertain to a particular historical incident, they present a universal condemnation of all who persecute believers of any faith. That universality is not a modern liberal reading imposed on the text; it is built into the text's own decision not to name anyone.

And note the direction of the curse. The verse does not begin by consoling the victims — the consoling comes in verse 11 and in the promise of verse 10. It begins by *condemning the perpetrators*. There is pastoral wisdom in that ordering. A person who has been wronged cannot receive comfort until the wrong has first been named as wrong. To be told "be patient" before being told "they are cursed" is to be asked for a virtue the situation has not yet earned. God curses the pit-diggers first, and only then opens the Gardens.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:5

> **the fire well fueled,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Waqūd: The Fuel, Not the Flame**

The verse is three words in Arabic: *al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd* — "the fire, possessed of fuel." The genitive phrase stands in apposition to *al-ukhdūd*, describing what the pit contained: a fire that had fuel in it, and fuel being added to it.

The lexical precision matters. *Waqūd*, with a *fatḥah* on the *wāw*, means the fuel itself — the wood, the kindling, the combustible material. *Wuqūd*, with a *ḍammah*, means the act of blazing. The verse chose the word for the material, not the event, and the commentators drew the consequence: *dhāt al-waqūd* describes a fire that is *fed*. Al-Ṭabarī renders it as a fire whose fuel is abundant and which is continually supplied; al-Qurṭubī notes that the phrase indicates a fire deliberately maintained rather than one that flared up and burned out.

Consider what maintenance requires. A fire that will consume a whole community — men, women, children, brought in sequence, over what must have been hours or days — cannot be lit once. It must be stoked. Someone had to gather the wood. Someone had to carry it. Someone had to stand at the edge and add more, repeatedly, while the fire did its work. There were shifts. There was logistics. There was a supply line.

**Atrocity Is Administrative**

This is the single most modern insight in a fourteen-century-old verse, and it is worth stating without flinching: mass cruelty is never spontaneous. It is organized, budgeted, staffed, and refueled.

The Qur'an is unusually attentive to this fact about evil. It rarely presents persecution as a burst of passion; it presents it as a *plan*. Pharaoh does not merely hate Moses — he convenes, he consults, he issues a decree, he builds, he commands the *malāʾ*, his inner council. The Qur'an's word for this is *makr*, scheming:

> *"And they plotted (makarū), and God plotted; and God is the best of plotters."* (Qur'an 3:54)

> *"And thus We placed in every land the greatest of its criminals, that they might plot therein; and they plot only against themselves, though they do not perceive."* (Qur'an 6:123)

Every twentieth-century atrocity confirmed the pattern with documentary precision. The archives of the great crimes of modern history are archives of *fuel*: railway timetables, requisition orders, chemical procurement lists, quotas, invoices, staff rosters, and correspondence about efficiency. The horror of those records is not that monsters wrote them but that clerks did. A fire that consumes a nation requires an entire department devoted to keeping it alight, and the department is staffed by ordinary men performing ordinary administrative tasks.

*Al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd* names this in three words. The pit did not burn by accident. It was supplied.

**A Fire Prepared in Advance**

The Qur'an elsewhere uses the identical vocabulary of *preparation* when it speaks of Hell, and the echo is not incidental:

> *"And fear the Fire whose fuel is men and stones, prepared (uʿiddat) for the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 2:24)

> *"And fear the Fire prepared (uʿiddat) for the disbelievers."* (Qur'an 3:131)

Read the two together. The tyrants *prepared* a fire whose fuel was believing men, women, and children. God has *prepared* a fire whose fuel is men and stones. The tyrants dug their pit at the mouths of the roads so that no one could avoid it. God's fire is described in verse 10 of this same sūrah as *ʿadhāb al-ḥarīq* — the punishment of the burning.

The structure of the reply is exact retribution in kind, and it is one of the Qur'an's most consistent patterns of justice: the instrument becomes the sentence. Those who made fire their method will find fire their destination. Verse 10 will state it as a legal formula rather than a metaphor, and the commentators — including al-Rāzī — noted the deliberate mirroring: they burned the believers for a limited time; they will be burned without limit.

**The Location: At the Mouths of the Roads**

The ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb supplies a detail the verse implies but does not spell out: the trenches were dug *bi-afwāhi al-sikak* — "at the mouths of the roads." That is, at the entrances to the city's thoroughfares, where traffic converges and where no one entering or leaving could avoid passing.

The choice of location converts the atrocity from an execution into a broadcast. A killing performed in a dungeon is a secret; a killing performed at the city gate is a policy statement. The purpose was not only to destroy believers but to *produce fear in everyone else*: look at this, and decide what you will say when asked.

Every age has its mouths of the roads. The public square where sentences are read. The stadium converted into a holding pen. The checkpoint on the highway out of the city. The gallows erected at a crossroads in the medieval town. And in our own century, the front page, the evening broadcast, the trending feed — venues chosen for exactly the same reason: maximum visibility, maximum compliance. The Qur'an records the tactic and refuses to grant it the dignity of analysis. It simply says where they put the fire, and lets the reader understand why.

**Fire: The Element God Also Uses as a Sign**

There is a further resonance the classical exegetes appreciated. Fire in the Qur'an is not one thing. It is the element of destruction in the hands of tyrants — and it is also the medium of God's most intimate self-disclosure to a prophet.

Moses, lost in the desert with his family, sees it:

> *"When he saw a fire, he said to his family: remain here; I perceive a fire. Perhaps I will bring you news from it, or a brand from it, that you may warm yourselves."* (Qur'an 20:10)

> *"Then when he came to it, he was called: O Moses, indeed I am your Lord."* (Qur'an 20:11–12)

And the fire Abraham was thrown into obeyed its Lord rather than its nature:

> *"We said: O fire, be coolness and peace upon Abraham."* (Qur'an 21:69)

The theological point is foundational to the whole sūrah, and it is the point the martyrs of the pit died holding: fire has no independent power. It is a created thing that behaves as its Creator permits. The men who lit this fire believed they had enlisted the most terrible force in the physical world on their side. In reality they had borrowed a servant of God and used it against His friends — and verse 9 will remind them whose sovereignty covers the heavens and the earth.

Some exegetical reports transmitted in the tafsīr literature add that the believers' souls were taken before the flames touched their bodies, and that the fire was denied its power over them as it had been denied over Abraham. Whether or not one accepts those specific reports, the Qur'an's own testimony is stronger than any of them: those who were thrown in are called, forever after, *al-muʾminīn* — and their killers are called, forever after, the companions of the pit.

**The Fires That Are Still Being Fed**

The verse generalizes further than literal combustion, and it should be allowed to.

There are fires that are fueled with words. A campaign of hatred against a religious minority does not ignite spontaneously; it is stoked by years of editorializing, by caricature, by dehumanizing vocabulary introduced gradually into respectable speech, by rumors repeated until they acquire the authority of fact. Every ethnic massacre of the last century was preceded by a decade of *waqūd* — fuel: pamphlets, radio broadcasts, schoolbooks, jokes. By the time the trenches are dug, the fuel has been stacked for years, and the people doing the stacking never once thought of themselves as arsonists.

There are fires fueled mechanically. An attention economy that rewards outrage is, structurally, a fuel-supply system for social fire: the more combustible the content, the more it is distributed, the more it is produced. Nobody in that chain intends harm; everybody in it is delivering wood.

And there are fires a person fuels in his own chest. A grudge survives only because it is fed — replayed, rehearsed, retold, checked for freshness each morning. Anger that is left alone dies within days. Anger that is maintained for years is being maintained by someone, and that someone is the person carrying it. The practical instruction of *dhāt al-waqūd* in the interior life is therefore almost mechanical: if you want a fire out, stop bringing wood. Forgiveness, in its first and simplest form, is not an emotion but a cessation of supply.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:6

> **when they sat by it,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Words That Fix a Posture Forever**

*Idh hum ʿalayhā quʿūd* — "when they were sitting upon it." The verse is so short that it is easy to pass over, and it is the most chilling line in the sūrah.

The Arabic deserves slow attention. *Idh* is a particle of time, "when." Then comes a nominal sentence — subject *hum*, "they," followed by the prepositional phrase *ʿalayhā*, "upon it," and then the predicate *quʿūd*, a broken plural of *qāʿid* (one who sits), or a verbal noun meaning "sitting."

Two grammatical choices carry enormous weight.

First, it is a *nominal* sentence, not a verbal one. Arabic distinguishes between a verbal sentence (*faʿala* — "he did"), which denotes an action occurring and passing, and a nominal sentence (*huwa fāʿil* — "he is a doer"), which denotes a state that stands and persists. The rhetorical tradition, from ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī through al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī, made this a science: the nominal sentence expresses *thubūt* — stability, continuity, settled existence. By using it, the Qur'an does not say "they sat down." It says *they were sitters*. Sitting was their condition, their posture, their settled state throughout the event.

Second, the prepositional phrase *ʿalayhā* is advanced before the predicate. In Arabic word order, fronting a phrase emphasizes it. The natural order would be *hum quʿūdun ʿalayhā*; instead we get *hum ʿalayhā quʿūd* — "they, *upon it*, were sitting." The emphasis falls on proximity and elevation: right at it, right on it, on its rim, above it.

**Sitting Is the Posture of Leisure**

Standing implies work. Leaning implies fatigue. Kneeling implies submission. Sitting implies *rest* — and, in a crowd, it implies an audience.

The commentators seized on precisely this. Ibn Kathīr states that the verse describes them seated around the fire, watching the believers being tormented in it. Al-Qurṭubī draws the moral consequence: the sitting indicates the completeness of their tyranny and the death of their hearts — they were not standing over a task in exertion, but settled at ease, as men settle to watch something they have arranged for their own satisfaction. Al-Baghawī and al-Thaʿlabī transmit the same sense.

The sūrah thus records the posture and nothing else. These men are given no names, no speeches, no thoughts, no motives, no interiority. The Qur'an does not narrate what they said to each other or what they believed. They are reduced, permanently, to a body position: *quʿūd*. Seated.

That is a kind of judgment in itself. A human being who arranges the burning of other human beings and then sits down to watch has ceased, in the Qur'an's presentation, to be a person with a psychology worth exploring. He is a posture. History will not remember his reasons; it will remember that he sat.

**The Reading That Turns the Verse on the Believers**

Al-Rāzī records an alternative interpretation with real spiritual force: that "they" in this verse are the *believers*, and that they are described as sitting *upon* the fire rather than beside it — because it was the believers who were in it.

The reading has two branches. One holds that the believers who were cast in sat in the fire with composure, as though the fire were a seat, because their hearts had been given a stillness that their bodies' situation could not disturb. Another holds that it refers to those believers who, under unbearable pressure, verbally recanted and were then made to sit and watch the others burn — the most exquisite torture the persecutors could devise, and the state of a person who has saved his body by surrendering something worse.

The majority reading remains the persecutors. But the alternative deserves to be kept, because it records an intuition the tradition found in the text: the verse is about *who is at peace in the presence of fire*. And the answer the sūrah gives — in the ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb, in the woman who hesitated at the trench's edge, in the infant who told her to be patient — is that the composure belonged to the ones inside it.

**The Spectator Is a Participant**

Islamic law and ethics refuse to treat mere presence at evil as neutral, and this verse is the Qur'an's exhibit for that refusal.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."* (Muslim 49, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī)

Note the structure: the minimum requirement — the floor below which faith fails — is that the heart *reject* what the eyes see. A man who sits by a fire and inwardly approves of it has not remained uninvolved; he has taken the last and lowest rung of a ladder that leads into the pit with them.

The same principle appears in the Prophetic prohibition on mere attendance:

> *"Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him not sit at a table at which wine is being circulated."* (al-Tirmidhī 2801; Aḥmad)

Wine is the sin here, but the ruling targets the *chair*. Presence at wrongdoing, willingly undertaken, is a form of endorsement — and endorsement is what a persecutor is trying to manufacture. The pit at the mouths of the roads was designed to produce spectators, because a spectator who does not object becomes, in the arithmetic of terror, a collaborator.

The Qur'an states the same logic in its account of the punishment of passive peoples:

> *"And He will call to them and say: what did you answer the messengers?"* (Qur'an 28:65)

Not "what did you do?" but "what did you answer?" Silence is an answer, and it is recorded as one.

**Every Age Builds Its Own Stands**

The phenomenon this verse immortalizes has not diminished; it has been industrialized.

In the twentieth century, lynchings in several countries were photographed and the photographs were printed as postcards and mailed to relatives — crowds posing beside the corpse, well-dressed, smiling, some seated in front rows as at a wedding. Public executions were ticketed events. Stadiums were converted into detention sites partly because they already had seating.

The twenty-first century has not abolished the seated crowd; it has distributed it. Violence now arrives live, with a comment section beneath it, and the modern equivalent of *quʿūd* is a posture of the thumb: the viewer who watches a human being destroyed on a screen, at ease, in bed, and adds a reaction before scrolling on. Nobody in that crowd considers himself a persecutor. None of them is holding the wood. All of them are *ʿalayhā quʿūd* — sitting at it.

The verse supplies the diagnostic question with brutal economy: where are you sitting, and what are you sitting to watch?

There is also a domestic and professional scale at which the same test applies, and it is where most readers will meet it. A colleague is being dismantled in a meeting, and you say nothing. A relative is being humiliated at a dinner table, and you look at your plate. A neighbour's child is crying every night through the wall, and you turn up the television. A student is being failed by an institution you work for, and you sign the form. In each case the posture is the same: seated, adjacent, able to stand, not standing. The sūrah does not require heroism of everyone; it requires that the heart refuse (Muslim 49). But it will not grant the seated the comfort of innocence.

**The Posture God Answers**

The reply to verse 6 is verse 12: *inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd* — "truly your Lord's assault is severe." The word *baṭsh* means a sudden, overwhelming seizure, and the commentators relate it to 11:102:

> *"Such is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong; indeed, His seizing is painful, severe."* (Qur'an 11:102)

The connection between the two postures is precise. They sat because they believed themselves safe — settled, in control, on the rim of the fire rather than in it, with nothing coming for them. The Divine *baṭsh* is defined by its suddenness: it arrives in the exact condition of seated security. The Prophet ﷺ warned of it explicitly, and al-Bukhārī and Muslim record his words:

> *"Indeed God grants respite to the wrongdoer, until when He seizes him, He does not let him escape."* Then he recited: *"Such is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong."* (al-Bukhārī 4686; Muslim 2583, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī)

So the sūrah sets two postures against one another across six verses: men sitting comfortably beside a fire they lit, and God standing over them with a seizure that has been delayed but not cancelled. The men who sat thought the delay meant absence. Verse 20 will correct them in four words — *wa Allāhu min warāʾihim muḥīṭ*, "and God is behind them, All-Encompassing" — and verse 12 will tell them what the encompassing is for.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:7

> **and were witness to what they did unto the believers,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shuhūd: Present, Watching, and Attesting**

*Wa hum ʿalā mā yafʿalūna bi'l-muʾminīna shuhūd* — "and they were witnesses to what they did to the believers."

*Shuhūd* is the plural of *shāhid*, and it is also a verbal noun of the same root, which gives it a rare grammatical property in Arabic: as a *maṣdar* it can describe one or many without changing form. The commentators note that this ambiguity enriches the word — it means at once "they were present" and "they were testifying."

The two meanings are not alternatives. In Arabic, and in the Qur'an's usage especially, presence *is* testimony. To be a witness is not first to have signed a statement; it is to have been there with your eyes open. What these men did by sitting was to constitute themselves as witnesses — and the verse records that they accepted the role.

**The Imperfect Verb: They Kept Doing It**

A detail of the verb deserves notice. The Qur'an says *ʿalā mā yafʿalūna* — using the imperfective *yafʿalūna*, "they do / they were doing," rather than the perfective *faʿalū*, "they did."

Arabic aspect works differently from English tense, and the choice is meaningful. The perfective presents an action as completed and closed; the imperfective presents it as ongoing, repeated, or habitual. By selecting the imperfective, the verse describes a *process*: they were doing this, continuously, one after another, and they were watching throughout. This was not a single act but a programme — a queue of people, a fire being fed, hours passing, and men remaining in their seats the whole time.

The same aspectual choice colors *ʿalayhā quʿūd* in the previous verse: a settled, sustained sitting. Together the two verses paint a scene not of a moment but of a *duration*. And duration is what converts an act into an institution.

**"What They Did" — Not "Whom"**

The object of the witnessing is *mā*, "what" — *what they did* — not *man*, "whom." The grammar keeps the eye on the deed.

This has a rhetorical effect that is easy to miss. The persecutors believed they were dealing with a category of people — a sect, a faction, a problem population. The Qur'an's phrasing turns their attention back onto their own conduct: what you *did*. On the Day of Judgment the question will not be "who were they," which is the persecutor's favorite question, but "what did you do," which is the only question that survives.

It also prepares verse 8's exposure. Once you are looking at the deed instead of the label, you are forced to ask what justified the deed — and verse 8 answers that nothing did.

**They Will Testify Against Themselves**

The second layer of *shuhūd*, transmitted by al-Rāzī and others and noted in the source commentary, is eschatological: these witnesses will bear witness — against themselves.

The Qur'an describes that scene repeatedly, and the language of the body doing the testifying is among its most arresting imagery:

> *"On a Day when We will seal their mouths, and their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to earn."* (Qur'an 36:65)

> *"And they will say to their skins: why have you testified against us? They will say: God made us speak — God, who makes everything speak."* (Qur'an 41:21)

> *"On the Day when their tongues, their hands, and their feet will testify against them for what they used to do. That Day, God will pay them in full their just due, and they will know that God is the Manifest Truth."* (Qur'an 24:24–25)

> *"Read your record; sufficient is yourself today as a reckoner against yourself."* (Qur'an 17:14)

The last of these is the culmination of the logic. On that Day the prosecution will not be an external adversary. The defendant will read his own file. The eyes that sat and watched will be the evidence; the hands that fed the fire will speak; the skin that felt the warmth of the trench's edge will testify.

There is a severe justice in this arrangement, and it is worth articulating as a principle: *the faculties you use to commit a crime are the faculties that will be called to describe it.* The persecutors wanted to see. They will see, forever, and they will be unable to close the eye.

**The Irony of Assembling Witnesses**

Read verse 7 beside the historical fact of the story's survival and a remarkable irony appears.

The persecutors dug their trenches at the mouths of the roads so that everyone would see. They wanted an audience. They believed that public witnessing would produce terror and thereby extinguish the faith. Instead, the audience became the record. The people who watched told their children; the children told theirs; the story travelled to Arabia; it reached a Companion named Ṣuhayb, who heard it from the Prophet ﷺ; Muslim wrote it down; and it is now preserved in the Qur'an itself — recited five times daily in the prayers of a billion people, in the very verses that curse the men who assembled the crowd.

They wanted witnesses, and they got them. Not one witness but every witness — including, at the head of the list, God: *"and God is Witness over all things"* (v. 9).

This is a permanent law of history that tyrants never learn. Atrocity performed in public is atrocity that enters memory. A crime committed in secret may be forgotten; a crime committed before a crowd is guaranteed a thousand chroniclers, and crowds outlive regimes. The pit at Najrān, or wherever it was, destroyed a generation of believers — and in doing so it made those believers unforgettable, while the king who burned them has no certain name.

**Domination Requires Feedback**

Why do they watch at all? This is the psychological question the verse raises, and it has an answer that explains much of human evil.

Power, in its pathological form, is not satisfied by the outcome; it is satisfied by the *visible confirmation* of the outcome. The tyrant does not merely want the believer dead; he wants to see the believer break, to hear the recantation, to watch the fear register on a face. Domination requires feedback, and the feedback must be observed to count. That is why torture is so often performed before an audience, why confessions are extracted publicly, why humiliations are staged, and why the modern criminal who films his own crime and uploads it is not an anomaly but the naked form of an ancient appetite.

The Qur'an exposes this appetite by recording it without comment. *Wa hum ʿalā mā yafʿalūna bi'l-muʾminīna shuhūd.* No adjective. No rhetoric. Just the fact that they watched — and that the watching was, for them, part of the point.

The believer's answer to that appetite is found in the ḥadīth: the boy refused to break, and the woman refused to step back, and the infant told his mother to be patient. The persecutors assembled an audience to witness a demonstration of power and instead witnessed a demonstration of faith, and the entire city said, three times, *"We believe in the Lord of the boy."* The spectators were converted by what they came to see. That is what happens when the audience God provides is bigger than the audience the tyrant provides.

**You Are Now a Witness Too**

Every reader of this verse occupies one of the two positions in it. Either you are doing, or you are watching what is done; and in the modern condition almost all of us are watching, constantly, from a seat we have chosen.

The verse does not permit the comfortable conclusion that watching is harmless. *Shuhūd* carries obligation with it. In Islamic law a witness who conceals his testimony is sinful in his heart (2:283); a witness who testifies falsely is numbered among the gravest offenders, and the Prophet ﷺ listed false testimony beside idolatry and disobedience to parents among the greatest sins (al-Bukhārī 2654; Muslim 87). The one who saw and said nothing is not acquitted; the one who saw and lied is condemned more severely.

To have witnessed is to have been entrusted. That is the deepest meaning of the word in this sūrah, and it is why the Qur'an swears by it in verse 3 before it accuses anyone in verse 7. Every person who has seen an injustice has been made a trustee of the truth about it, and the trust will be called in.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:8

> **and took vengeance on them for naught but that they believed in God, the Mighty, the Praised,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naqama: The Resentment That Has No Object**

The verb is *naqama* (also vocalized *naqima*), from the root *n-q-m*, meaning to resent intensely, to find fault with, to blame, to punish in requital of a perceived wrong. The construction here is the key: *wa mā naqamū minhum illā an yuʾminū bi-llāh* — "and they resented nothing of them *except* that they believed in God."

The *illā* — "except" — does devastating work. It sets up an accusation and then reveals, in the same breath, that the accusation is the only thing there is. They blamed the believers. For what? For believing in God. Which is to say: the charge and the crime are not two things; the charge *is* the virtue.

This is a Qur'anic formula of exposure, and it appears wherever a prophet or a believing community faces persecution:

> *"Say: O People of the Book, do you resent us for anything except that we believed in God, and in what was sent down to us, and in what was sent down before, and that most of you are transgressors?"* (Qur'an 5:59)

> *"They said: O our Lord, we have wronged ourselves; and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we shall surely be among the losers."* — and before that, Pharaoh's magicians answered Pharaoh: *"And you take vengeance upon us for nothing except that we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us."* (Qur'an 7:126)

> *"They resented nothing except that God and His Messenger had enriched them from His bounty."* (Qur'an 9:74)

Three other verses, three other persecutions, one identical structure. The Qur'an is teaching a diagnostic method: when people are being punished, ask what the charge actually is, and say it out loud. Almost every persecution in history, once its charge is stated plainly, collapses into a sentence of this form — "they resented nothing except that..."

**The Logical Refutation Contained in the Sentence**

The commentators, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, note that the verse functions as an argument, not merely as a description.

Vengeance (*naqma*) presupposes a wrong. To take vengeance is to inflict harm as a *response* to harm or offence received. The verse states the entire content of the offence: belief in God. Now either belief in God is an offence, or it is not.

If it is not an offence — which is the case, since belief in the Creator is the single obligation from which every other obligation descends — then the vengeance has no cause. And harm inflicted without cause is not vengeance; it is *ẓulm*, oppression, which in its classical Arabic definition is "placing a thing in other than its proper place."

Ibn Kathīr's summary is blunt: they had no sin against them except belief in God, the Mighty, the Praised — and that is not a sin at all; it is the reason for their honour.

The logical force of this is why the verse is placed exactly here. Verse 7 established that they watched what they did. Verse 8 asks: what were they doing it *for*? And the answer, once uttered, indicts the doers more thoroughly than any curse could. A curse says "they were evil." This sentence says "they were absurd" — men who burned other men because the men believed that God exists.

**Al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd: The Two Names Placed at the Scene of the Crime**

The verse closes with two Divine Names, and their placement is not decorative.

*Al-ʿAzīz* comes from the root *ʿ-z-z*, which carries two related senses: might that cannot be overcome, and preciousness that cannot be matched (something *ʿazīz* is both powerful and rare, hence dear). As a Name of God, al-ʿAzīz is the Invincible — the One whom nothing defeats, who is not to be overcome by any coalition, and who possesses the power to requite.

*Al-Ḥamīd* comes from *ḥ-m-d*, praise. Al-Ḥamīd is the One who is praised in Himself, deserving of all praise whether or not anyone offers it; the One whose essence, attributes, actions, and decrees are praiseworthy; and — as the commentators add — the One who praises those who deserve praise. The Qur'an uses the same pairing elsewhere:

> *"...that you may believe in God and His Messenger, and honour Him, and revere Him, and glorify Him morning and evening — the path of God, the Mighty, the Praised (ṣirāṭ al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd)."* (Qur'an 42:52–53)

> *"...indeed He is Praised, Glorious (innahu Ḥamīdun Majīd)."* (Qur'an 11:73)

Notice in 11:73 that *Ḥamīd* is paired with *Majīd* — and that al-Burūj will close by calling the Qur'an *majīd* (v. 21) and God *al-Majīd* or the Throne *al-Majīd* (v. 15). The sūrah's own vocabulary is a single web of praiseworthiness and glory.

Now ask why these two Names, at this moment, in this sentence.

*Al-ʿAzīz* answers the imbalance of power. The king had soldiers, saws, boats, trenches, and fuel. The believers had nothing. The Name declares that the real distribution of might is elsewhere: the One they believed in is the Invincible, and the men burning them are burning servants of a Master they cannot touch. It is also a quiet warning: al-ʿAzīz is the One who *can* avenge, and verse 12 will confirm that He does.

*Al-Ḥamīd* answers the imbalance of honour. Persecution's second weapon, after pain, is shame. The persecutor wants to make the victim's belief look contemptible — a superstition, a defect, a source of disgrace. By naming Himself the Praised at the exact point where the believers are being burned for praising Him, God reverses the shame. The believers were not following something base; they were following the One who is the object of all praise. Their torturers, who appeared honourable in robes and entourages, are recorded forever as the companions of a ditch.

**The Verse That Removes False Guilt**

Anyone who has been punished for something good knows the specific damage that persecution does to the conscience: it installs guilt where there is none.

The mechanism is simple and it works on almost everyone. When the whole environment treats you as an offender — the employer, the family, the media, the institution, the peer group — you begin to search your own conduct for the offence. *What did I do? Where did I go wrong? Was I harsh? Was I proud? Did I deserve this?* Victims of persecution spend enormous energy auditing themselves for a crime that does not exist, because the human mind finds an unjustified punishment almost intolerable; it would rather invent a fault than accept that the world is arbitrary.

Verse 8 performs the audit for you and closes the file. *Wa mā naqamū minhum illā an yuʾminū bi-llāh.* They found nothing. Not one defect. The whole charge is that you believed in God, the Mighty, the Praised.

The pastoral application extends across fourteen centuries. The Muslim woman denied a job because of her headscarf. The convert whose family has cut him off. The employee disciplined for declining to sign something his conscience refuses. The student mocked for praying. The prisoner of conscience whose cell number is longer than his sentence. Each of them will be told, in one form or another, that they brought it on themselves. The Qur'an's answer, given once and for all at a fire in an unnamed city, is that they did not.

**Why God Permits the Fire: The Question the Verse Provokes**

The verse cannot be read without the question surfacing: if God is *al-ʿAzīz*, why did He not stop it?

The Qur'an answers elsewhere and repeatedly, and the answer is that this world is constituted as a test in which the tested are genuinely free:

> *"Do people think that they will be left to say 'we believe' and they will not be tested? We tested those before them, and God will surely make evident those who are truthful, and He will surely make evident the liars."* (Qur'an 29:2–3)

> *"Or did you think that you would enter the Garden while God had not yet made evident those of you who strive, and made evident the patient?"* (Qur'an 3:142)

> *"And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger, and a loss of wealth, lives, and fruits — but give good news to the patient."* (Qur'an 2:155)

The freedom of the persecutor is the same freedom that makes the believer's patience meaningful. A faith that costs nothing cannot be tested; a test that cannot cost anything cannot distinguish the truthful from the liar. God does not approve the fire — He curses the men who lit it in the same sūrah — but He permits the world to be a world in which choices are real and consequences follow. And He reserves the settlement: verse 10, verse 12, verse 20.

Ibn Taymiyyah's treatment of Divine wisdom is relevant here in outline: God's permission of an evil is not His approval of it, and the permission is always ordered toward goods that could not otherwise be realized — the manifesting of patience, the exposure of the persecutor's nature, the elevation of the martyr, the instruction of subsequent generations. The pit produced, among other things, this sūrah; and this sūrah has consoled more sufferers than the trench ever consumed.

**Say the Charge Out Loud**

The practical instruction embedded in the verse is a method, and it is one of the most useful tools the Qur'an gives for navigating conflict.

When you are being opposed, blamed, excluded, or punished, do not begin by defending yourself. Begin by *stating the charge*, precisely and completely, in the form this verse uses: "They resent nothing of me except that..." Then look at what you have written.

Very often the sentence will not survive being written. It will read: "...except that I refused to falsify the numbers." "...except that I told the truth about what happened." "...except that I pray." "...except that I will not say the thing everyone else is saying." At that point the emotional weight of the persecution — the fear, the shame, the doubt — loses its footing, because you can see that the accusers have no accusation. You are not in a dispute about your conduct; you are witnessing someone else's discomfort with your integrity.

That is not a promise of safety. The people of the pit stated the charge and were burned anyway. But it is a promise of clarity, and clarity is what allows a person to be patient rather than merely bewildered — which is exactly the difference between the woman who hesitated at the trench's edge and the infant who told her, *"be patient, for you are upon the truth."*

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:9

> **unto Whom belongs sovereignty over the heavens and the earth; and God is Witness over all things.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Property Deed Inserted Into a Court Case**

The verse continues the description of the One named in verse 8: *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥamīd, alladhī lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "the Mighty, the Praised, unto Whom belongs the sovereignty of the heavens and the earth."

The word *mulk* denotes sovereignty in its fullest sense: ownership combined with authority, command, and disposal. It is not mere possession (*milk*) of an object one cannot alter; it is the right to rule what one owns. The Qur'an states the same claim dozens of times, in virtually identical words:

> *"And to God belongs the sovereignty of the heavens and the earth, and God is Powerful over all things."* (Qur'an 3:189)

> *"Say: O God, Owner of sovereignty (mālika al-mulk), You give sovereignty to whom You will and You take sovereignty away from whom You will."* (Qur'an 3:26)

> *"Blessed is He in whose Hand is the sovereignty, and He is over all things Powerful."* (Qur'an 67:1)

Placed here, in this sentence, the claim is aimed at a specific person: the king who dug the pit. He believed he was a sovereign. He exercised what a sovereign exercises — legislation, execution, the power to determine who may live in his country and on what terms, the authority to decide what religion may be professed within his borders. The verse answers him by relocating the title. The sovereignty he wielded was a lease, not a freehold; it was local, temporary, and revocable; and the Owner of the heavens and the earth holds the deed.

**Why the Heavens and the Earth, in That Order**

The sūrah opened by swearing on the sky (v. 1). It now declares God's ownership of the sky first and the earth second.

The ordering answers the structure of power. Every tyrant's reach is terrestrial. He can confiscate land, close roads, control the ports, occupy the cities, and dig trenches at the mouths of the streets — all of which are actions upon *the earth*. He cannot touch the sky. He cannot move a constellation, delay a sunrise, cancel a season, or prevent the constellations from being seen by the people he is oppressing.

By naming the heavens first, the verse places the majority of God's declared sovereignty outside the tyrant's jurisdiction, and then includes the earth as the smaller, nearer portion of the same estate. It is a correction of perspective for anyone who has ever felt enclosed by a regime: the walls you are inside are on a property you do not belong to, held by Someone whose estate is mostly elsewhere.

There is also a doctrinal point about the *ʿAzīz* of verse 8. Might that has no dominion is mere force; dominion that has no might is mere title. The verse joins them: the Invincible One is also the Sovereign, which means His power is not arbitrary but rightful. He does not merely overpower the persecutors; He has jurisdiction over them.

**The Throne Is Coming**

The movement from *mulk* here to *dhū al-ʿarsh* in verse 15 is deliberate, and the classical exegetes read the two together. The Throne is, in Qur'anic language, the seat of sovereignty:

> *"The Exalted in rank, Owner of the Throne, who casts the Spirit of His command upon whom He wills of His servants."* (Qur'an 40:15)

> *"And the angels will be at its edges, and there will bear the Throne of your Lord above them, that Day, eight."* (Qur'an 69:17)

Verse 9 announces the sovereignty; verse 15 will present the throne. Between them come the attributes of forgiveness and love — so that the reader arrives at the Throne having already been told what kind of Sovereign sits on it. This is Qur'anic pedagogy in its purest form: power is disclosed gradually, and mercy is disclosed before majesty.

**The Third Appearance of the Witness**

The verse closes by returning, for the third time in seven verses, to the root *sh-h-d*: *wa Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd* — "and God is Witness over all things."

Count the sequence. Verse 3 swears by the witness and the witnessed. Verse 7 says the persecutors were *shuhūd*, witnesses, of what they did to the believers. Verse 9 says God is *shahīd* over all things. The escalation is exact: men witnessed a deed; God witnesses the men, the deed, the fire, the fuel, the hesitation of the woman, and the words of the infant.

The preposition is *ʿalā* — over, upon — and it carries authority as well as observation. A witness *over* something is a witness with jurisdiction. The same formula recurs throughout the Qur'an; the source commentary lists its occurrences at 4:33, 5:117, 22:17, 33:55, 34:47, 41:53 and 58:6. Two of them are worth quoting in full, because they show the formula in use.

In the words of Jesus on the Day of Judgment:

> *"I was a witness over them as long as I remained among them; but when You took me up, You were the Watcher over them — and You are Witness over all things."* (Qur'an 5:117)

And in the promise that the signs will be shown:

> *"We will show them Our signs in the horizons and in themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth. Is it not sufficient concerning your Lord that He is Witness over all things?"* (Qur'an 41:53)

The second is particularly close to our verse: God's witnessing is offered as *sufficiency* — *kafā* — enough. The believer does not need a second witness, a lawyer, or a sympathetic journalist. The Witness over all things is enough.

**Nothing Escapes: The Precision of Divine Knowledge**

The Qur'an describes the scope of that witnessing with an almost forensic specificity:

> *"And not absent from your Lord is the weight of an atom in the earth or in the heaven, nor anything smaller than that or greater, except that it is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 10:61)

> *"He knows the betrayal of the eyes and what the breasts conceal."* (Qur'an 40:19)

That last phrase — *khāʾinata al-aʿyun*, "the treachery of the eyes" — reads as though it were revealed for verse 7. It is not only the deeds that are witnessed but the *look*: the glance of satisfaction, the flicker of pleasure at another's pain, the eye that lingers on a humiliation. The men who sat by the pit thought their faces were private. The Witness over all things records the direction of a gaze.

**Surveillance and the Watcher Above the Watchers**

The modern reader lives inside a surveillance system that no previous generation could imagine, and it gives this verse an edge it did not have before.

Cameras record public space. Employers monitor keystrokes and messages. Governments hold metadata on entire populations. Corporations build behavioural profiles accurate enough to predict a pregnancy before a family knows it. The technical capacity to be a witness over all things has, in a limited and grim sense, been partially achieved by human institutions — and the effect on ordinary people is a mixture of protection and dread, because everyone knows that whoever holds the record holds the power.

The Qur'an's declaration reorganizes this landscape at a single stroke: above every watcher is a Watcher, and His record is the one that will be read. The state's file on you can be corrupted, leaked, misread, or used against you unjustly. God's file cannot be edited, cannot be lost, cannot be sold, and cannot be produced without you also being present to contest it. That is why the verse is a warning to the powerful and a comfort to the powerless simultaneously — and why, historically, belief in the Divine Witness has been the single most reliable constraint upon tyrants who could otherwise act without any constraint at all.

**Living as One Who Is Witnessed**

The practical consequence is the state the tradition calls *iḥsān*. The Prophet ﷺ defined it in the ḥadīth of Gabriel, recorded by Muslim:

> *"Iḥsān is that you worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then He sees you."* (Muslim 8, from ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb)

This is the interior form of verse 9, and its effects on character are well documented in the tradition. A person who genuinely lives as one witnessed does not need supervision; he is honest when the audit is impossible, kind when nobody is scoring him, careful with other people's reputations when a rumour would benefit him. The awareness does not produce anxiety so much as *consistency*, because it removes the gap between the public self and the private self — the gap in which most human corruption grows.

And for the wronged, the same verse supplies what no court can: the certainty of being believed. The woman who walked to the edge of the trench with her infant had no lawyer, no evidence, no witnesses willing to speak, and no country to appeal to. She had this: *wa Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd*. God is Witness over all things. It is the sentence that has carried more sufferers through more nights than any other in the Book — because it means that the truth of what happened to you does not depend on anyone else agreeing with you.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:10

> **Truly those who persecute believing men and believing women, then do not repent, theirs shall be the punishment of Hell, and theirs shall be the punishment of the burning.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fatana: To Put a Person in the Fire to Test Him**

The verb is *fatanū*, from the root *f-t-n*, and its etymology is one of the most precise word-choices in the Qur'an.

In classical Arabic *fatana* means, first and literally, to assay metal — to put gold or silver into the fire in order to test its purity and separate the true metal from the dross. From that physical act the word extended to every kind of trial: to test, to tempt, to afflict, to seduce, to cause someone to stumble in their faith. The noun *fitnah* carries the whole range — trial, temptation, persecution, sedition, civil strife.

When the Qur'an says *innā alladhīna fatanū al-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināt*, it is therefore not choosing a general word for "harmed." It is choosing the word for *putting people through fire to see what they are made of*. The men at the pit were assayers. They were testing the believers' faith with the literal instrument the root denotes — and the Qur'an uses their own technical vocabulary against them, declaring the assay a crime.

The same root describes the believer's normal condition in this world:

> *"Do people think that they will be left to say 'we believe' and they will not be tested (lā yuftanūn)?"* (Qur'an 29:2)

And it states the moral axiom that governs the whole subject of religious persecution:

> *"And kill them wherever you find them, and expel them from where they expelled you — for persecution (al-fitnah) is more severe than killing."* (Qur'an 2:191)

> *"And fighting in the sacred month, and the sacred precincts. Persecution (al-fitnah) is greater than killing."* (Qur'an 2:217)

Two verses declare a hierarchy of harms: forcing a human being out of his faith is worse than ending his life. This is why the people of the pit were given a curse for eternity and a fire in return — they committed, in the Qur'an's assessment, the gravest of injuries, against the most defenceless of victims, repeatedly, and enjoyed it.

**Men and Women, Named Separately**

*Al-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināt* — "believing men and believing women." The pairing is the Qur'an's habitual way of ensuring that women are not absorbed into a masculine plural by implication. The same pattern runs through the great catalogue of spiritual equality:

> *"Indeed the submitting men and submitting women, the believing men and believing women, the obedient men and obedient women, the truthful men and truthful women, the patient men and patient women..."* (Qur'an 33:35)

In the present context the pairing is not merely formal; it is dramatically earned. The final figure in the ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb is a woman carrying her infant, hesitating at the lip of the trench — and the child speaks to steady her. The last person the story shows us is a mother being persecuted for her faith, and the verse that condemns her persecutors names her explicitly.

Islamic history keeps the same order in its roll of honour. The first person killed in Islam for her faith was a woman: Sumayyah bint Khayyāṭ, the mother of ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, pierced with a spear by Abū Jahl for refusing to recant — and both she and her husband Yāsir were tortured to death in the Makkan sun. Bilāl was dragged over burning sand with a rock on his chest, saying only *Aḥad, Aḥad* — "One, One." Khabbāb was pressed with heated iron. These are not footnotes to the sūrah; they are the audience it was revealed to.

**"Then Do Not Repent": The Door Left Open**

The hinge of the verse is a subordinate clause of five Arabic letters: *thumma lam yatūbū* — "then do not repent."

Its presence changes everything about how the sentence is to be read. The threat is not addressed to a fixed class of damned people; it is addressed to a *condition* that the reader can still exit. Whoever has persecuted believers and repents is not in this verse. The verse describes the ones who did not.

Ibn Kathīr draws out the mercy explicitly: this is among God's grace and generosity, that He has made repentance available even to people who have done this — even to the men at the pit, had they repented before death. Al-Qurṭubī says the same: the verse shows that the sin of persecution, however enormous, does not close the door of return.

The conditions on that door are stated elsewhere with equal clarity. Repentance must precede the arrival of death:

> *"Repentance is only for those who do evil in ignorance and then repent soon after; it is those whom God turns to in mercy... But repentance is not for those who go on doing evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says: 'Now I repent' — nor for those who die while they are disbelievers."* (Qur'an 4:17–18)

> *"And God accepts the repentance of His servant so long as the death rattle has not begun."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537, from Ibn ʿUmar, graded ḥasan)

> *"Faith did not benefit them when they saw Our punishment — the established practice of God concerning those who passed before."* (Qur'an 40:85)

Pharaoh's declaration of belief came as the water closed over him, and it was not accepted. The men who sat by the pit will have their own moment of clarity, and it will be too late. The verse's *thumma lam yatūbū* is therefore also a deadline: repentance is available *now*, while the fire has not yet been lit, and it is the only exit.

**The Rights of People: A Harder Question**

Islamic jurisprudence distinguishes between the rights of God (*ḥuqūq Allāh*) and the rights of human beings (*ḥuqūq al-ʿibād*), and the distinction bears directly on this verse. Repentance discharges the first. The second are not discharged by repentance alone; they require restoration, compensation, or the wronged party's pardon.

The Prophet ﷺ explained the mechanism with an image that is impossible to forget. Muslim records:

> *"Do you know who the bankrupt (al-muflis) is?" They said: the bankrupt among us is the one with no dirham and no property. He said: "The bankrupt of my community is the one who comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, fasting, and zakāh — and comes having insulted this one, slandered that one, consumed the wealth of this one, shed the blood of that one, and beaten this one. So this one is given from his good deeds, and that one from his good deeds. And if his good deeds are exhausted before the account is settled, their sins are taken and cast upon him, and he is thrown into the Fire."* (Muslim 2581, from Abū Hurayrah)

The persecutor's problem is therefore not only that he sinned but that he *owes*. He has incurred debts to specific people, and those debts will be collected from his account on a day when currency is deeds. The one who tortured a hundred believers arrives with a hundred creditors.

Ibn Taymiyyah, in his treatise on the one who insults the Prophet ﷺ (*al-Ṣārim al-Maslūl ʿalā Shātim al-Rasūl*), uses this very verse — 85:10 — in that discussion. His point is precise: the verse makes the punishment of persecution contingent upon not repenting, which shows that the sin is of the kind that repentance addresses before God; but it does not follow that the human rights violated by the persecution are thereby waived, since the injured party's claim stands on its own footing. The theological conclusion is sober: God may forgive, and the victim's claim remains. Only the victim can release it — or God can satisfy the victim from His own bounty, which the tradition holds He does for those He chooses.

**Two Punishments, and the Reason for the Pair**

* Lahum ʿadhābu jahannama wa lahum ʿadhābu al-ḥarīq* — "theirs is the punishment of Hell, and theirs is the punishment of the burning."

Why name two punishments where one would suffice? The commentators offer readings that accumulate rather than compete.

Al-Rāzī observes that Hell (*jahannam*) and burning (*al-ḥarīq*) may denote two distinct forms of punishment; or they may mark the contrast between the *eternal* burning the persecutors will suffer and the *temporary* burning they inflicted on the believers — a finite crime answered by an infinite sentence, because the crime was against an infinite Good and was committed without repentance. Others, as al-Thaʿlabī records, hold that *ʿadhāb jahannam* is for their disbelief and *ʿadhāb al-ḥarīq* specifically for their persecution of others: two charges, two sentences.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase elsewhere, and its use is instructive:

> *"...and We will say: taste the punishment of the burning (ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq)."* (Qur'an 3:181)

There it answers those who mocked God and killed the prophets; here it answers those who burned the believers. The pattern is the Qur'an's principle of exact retribution — *wa jazāʾu sayyiʾatin sayyiʾatun mithluhā*, "the recompense of an evil deed is an evil like it" (42:40). They chose fire as their instrument; fire is returned as their sentence. The instrument becomes the verdict.

**The Oppression That Does Not Need a Trench**

It would be a mistake to file this verse under ancient history, and the commentators guard against precisely that reading by noting that the verse applies generally to all who persecute believers for their belief.

Persecution has a wide range of registers, and the word *fatana* covers all of them, because all of them are forms of pressure applied to a person's faith. There is the trench and the fire. There is also the father who tells his son he will not pay for his education if he keeps praying. There is the husband who forbids his wife the mosque. There is the employer who passes over the woman in the headscarf. There is the state that requires a religious minority to register, that closes their schools, that imprisons their clerics, that makes conversion a crime. There is the crowd online that destroys a person's livelihood for a sentence of sincere conviction. There is the classroom that teaches that belief is a mental illness.

All of these fall under *fatana*, and all of them are answered by this verse. The Qur'an legislates the opposite conduct with equal clarity:

> *"God does not forbid you, concerning those who did not fight you over religion and did not expel you from your homes, that you be righteous toward them and act justly toward them; indeed God loves the just."* (Qur'an 60:8)

And it governs even the hardest domestic case — parents who persecute their child's faith:

> *"And if they strive to make you associate with Me that of which you have no knowledge, do not obey them; yet accompany them in this world with kindness."* (Qur'an 31:15)

Disobey the persecution; do not abandon the person. That is the Islamic standard, and it is a standard the people of the pit failed in the most extreme way imaginable.

**The Warning and the Invitation in One Sentence**

Read as a whole, verse 10 is simultaneously the severest warning in the sūrah and its widest invitation. The warning: for those who persecute believers and do not repent, there are two fires. The invitation: *thumma lam yatūbū* — and if they repent, there are not.

The men sitting at the mouths of the roads had this verse in front of them in principle, had they chosen to hear it. The door was open until their last breath. That they did not walk through it is not a statement about the size of the door; it is a statement about them.

And the Prophet ﷺ warned that the delay of the sentence is not its cancellation:

> *"Beware of oppression, for oppression will be darkness upon darkness on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 2447; Muslim 2578)

Verse 11 will now turn, in a single verse, from the fire to the Gardens — and the suddenness of the turn is itself part of the message.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:11

> **Truly those who believe and perform righteous deeds, theirs shall be Gardens with rivers running below: that is the supreme triumph.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verse Set Against Seven**

Seven verses of fire — the pit, the fuel, the seated watchers, the testimony, the charge, the sovereignty, the double punishment — and then a single verse of Paradise.

The disproportion is the argument. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the Garden at length, over entire sūrahs: the reclining, the shade, the vessels of silver, the fruits within reach, the spouses, the peace, the meeting with God. Here it is compressed to one line, and the compression is exactly what the situation requires. The believer standing at the edge of a trench does not need a tour of Paradise; he needs to know that one verse of reward outweighs seven verses of fire, that the whole of what he is being made to suffer is *outweighed* — and outweighed by so much that the counterweight can be stated in a single sentence and still tip the scale.

The Qur'an states the principle of that disproportion explicitly:

> *"Say: O My servants who have believed, fear your Lord. For those who do good in this world there is good, and the earth of God is spacious. Indeed the patient will be given their reward without reckoning."* (Qur'an 39:10)

*Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — without calculation. The persecutors counted everything: the fuel, the bodies, the hours. The reward will not be counted, because counting has been abandoned as inadequate.

**The Formula That Defines a Muslim**

*Inna alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — "those who believe and perform righteous deeds." This pairing is among the most repeated formulas in the Qur'an, occurring dozens of times, and its two halves are never separated in the reward statements.

The reason is doctrinal and practical at once. Belief without action is inert; it produces no evidence of itself and no benefit to anyone. Action without belief is unstable; it depends on an audience, a mood, or a payoff, and it collapses when the payoff is withdrawn — which is precisely what happened to the persecutors of this sūrah, whose entire conduct was action with a false belief underneath it. The Qur'an's anthropology holds that a human being is complete only when conviction and conduct are welded together: what the heart holds, the limbs perform.

Note also who is described here. Not "those who were burned," not "those who suffered," not "those who were innocent." *Those who believed and did righteous deeds.* The verse restores the victims' identity a second time — verse 8 called them *al-muʾminīn*, and verse 11 adds their conduct. Suffering alone is not what saves a person; suffering *borne in faith and accompanied by righteousness* is. This matters, because it means the promise is available to every believer in every age, not only to martyrs.

**A Garden Defined by Water**

*Jannāt tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "Gardens beneath which rivers flow."

*Jannah* comes from the root *j-n-n*, meaning to cover, conceal, or screen. A *jannah* is not simply a garden but a garden so dense with foliage that the ground beneath is hidden — an enclosed, shaded, private abundance. From the same root come *jinn* (beings concealed from sight), *junnah* (a shield), *janīn* (an embryo, hidden in the womb), and *majnūn* (one whose reason is covered).

The pairing with running water is deliberate and would have been felt acutely by the first audience. In the Arabian peninsula, an enclosed garden with a permanent water source is not a luxury; it is the single most desirable thing that exists on earth — the oasis, the walled date-palm, the shade in a land of exposure. The Qur'an consistently describes Paradise in the vocabulary of what its hearers already recognized as the highest good, and then multiplies it beyond comprehension.

And here the contrast with the sūrah's other canal could not be sharper. The pit was a *trench filled with fire* — a channel of combustion dug at the mouths of roads. Paradise is *trenches filled with flowing water* — channels of life running beneath shade. The Qur'an describes the contents of those channels elsewhere in a verse that reads like a direct answer to verse 5:

> *"The likeness of the Garden promised to the reverent: in it are rivers of water that does not stagnate, and rivers of milk whose taste does not change, and rivers of wine delicious to those who drink, and rivers of purified honey."* (Qur'an 47:15)

Water, milk, wine, honey — against fuel, flame, smoke, and ash. The persecutors dug one kind of channel; the believers inherit four.

**Al-Fawz al-Kabīr: What Triumph Actually Means**

The verse ends by naming what has just been given: *dhālika al-fawz al-kabīr* — "that is the great triumph."

*Fawz* comes from *f-w-z*, whose core sense is to escape with gain — to come out of a danger alive and holding something. A merchant whose caravan crosses the desert and arrives with its cargo *fāza*. A man pursued by enemies who reaches the city before the gates close *fāza*. The word always contains both deliverance and acquisition.

The Qur'an defines the term with total clarity in one of its most important verses on the subject:

> *"Every soul will taste death, and you will be paid in full your wages on the Day of Resurrection. Whoever is drawn away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has triumphed (fa-qad fāza); and the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 3:185)

Two propositions in one sentence. The first defines triumph as escape-plus-entry, exactly as the root requires. The second dismisses the entire competing ledger: *mā al-ḥayātu al-dunyā illā matāʿu al-ghurūr* — the life of this world is nothing but the pleasure of delusion. Once that second clause is accepted, the persecutors' apparent victory evaporates. They won a city for a few decades; the believers won the definition of winning.

The commentators note the parallel with the Qur'an's more frequent superlative, *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* — the tremendous triumph — used at 5:119, 9:89, 44:57, 57:12, 61:12 and 64:9, and universally identified as the Garden. Al-Bayḍāwī and others explain that the triumph is called *great* because it exceeds every triumph attainable in this world in both kind and duration: what is gained here is lost or leaves you, and what is gained there neither perishes nor is taken.

**What the Martyrs of the Pit Received**

The verse does not stand alone; the Qur'an specifies elsewhere what happens to people who die as these believers died.

> *"And never think of those killed in the cause of God as dead; rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision — rejoicing in what God has given them of His bounty, and rejoicing for those who have not yet joined them from behind them, that no fear will come upon them nor will they grieve."* (Qur'an 3:169–170)

The Prophet ﷺ enumerated the martyr's privileges. Al-Tirmidhī records:

> *"The martyr has six things with God: he is forgiven at the first drop of his blood; he is shown his place in the Garden; he is protected from the punishment of the grave; he is secure from the greatest terror; a crown of dignity is placed on his head, one ruby of which is better than the world and all it contains; he is married to seventy-two of the dark-eyed; and he intercedes for seventy of his relatives."* (al-Tirmidhī 1663, from al-Miqdām ibn Maʿdī Karib; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

Read that list beside the ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb. The monk was sawn in half; he received it. The blind courtier who confessed *"my Lord and your Lord is God"* was sawn in half; he received it. The boy was shot at a trunk in a public square; he received it. The woman and her infant went into the trench; they received it. The Qur'an's account of the pit is not the end of their story — it is roughly the tenth of it, and verse 11 is where the rest begins.

**The Scale of the Exchange**

Muslim records a report that calibrates the exchange with almost offensive precision:

> *"The most wretched man in the world, among the people of Paradise, will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and dipped once in Paradise, and he will be asked: O son of Adam, have you ever seen any misery? Has any hardship ever passed over you? He will say: No, by God, O Lord — I have never seen any misery and no hardship ever passed over me."* (Muslim 2807, from Anas ibn Mālik)

One immersion. A lifetime of the worst suffering any human being has ever endured — and a single moment of Paradise erases it from memory so completely that the sufferer swears it never happened.

The same ḥadīth's other half warns in the opposite direction: the most blessed man in the world among the people of the Fire will be dipped once in the Fire and will swear he never saw any good.

Between those two immersions lies the entire content of verse 11, and the entire reason the woman at the trench's edge stepped forward.

Al-Bukhārī records the Prophet ﷺ putting a figure on the smallest portion of the reward:

> *"The space of a bow in Paradise is better than the world and all that is in it."* (al-Bukhārī 2793)

A bow's length. Not a palace, not a garden — a bow's length of Paradise outweighs the whole planet and its contents. That is the arithmetic the sūrah is asking its readers to accept, and it is why seven verses of fire and one verse of Garden produce a net result in favour of the Garden.

**What People Call Triumph**

Every human being organizes his life around some conception of *fawz* — of what it means to have won. The verse challenges all of the modern candidates.

The career conception of triumph: a position, an income, a reputation. It fails the test of *f-w-z* immediately, because it is not an escape from danger — it frequently *is* the danger, purchased with health, family, prayer, and honesty. The revenge conception: making the person who hurt you pay. It fails because it binds you permanently to your persecutor; a man consumed by vindication has handed his enemy the management of his inner life. The survival conception: outlasting them. It is closer, and the Qur'an honours it — *"and be patient; indeed God is with the patient"* (8:46) — but survival alone is not triumph if what survives is a compromised soul.

The Qur'an's definition is narrower and stranger: triumph is to be kept out of the Fire and brought into the Garden. It has no competitive element, no audience, no ranking against other people, and no dependence on worldly outcomes whatsoever. A person can lose every contest in this world — job, health, reputation, family, freedom, life — and still be described by God as the possessor of *al-fawz al-kabīr*.

That is an extraordinary revaluation, and it is why the sūrah can be recited by a prisoner and mean the same thing it meant to a king. The people of the pit lost the entire case: they were outnumbered, outpowered, outmaneuvered, and killed. Verse 11 declares them the winners. Verse 12 will declare what the others are.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:12

> **Truly thy Lord's assault is severe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Baṭsh: The Seizing Hand**

*Inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd* — "Indeed the seizing of your Lord is severe."

*Baṭsh* comes from the root *b-ṭ-sh*, meaning to seize violently, to grasp with overwhelming force, to fall upon someone suddenly and without escape. It is not the word for slow pressure or gradual decline; it is the word for a hand closing. The commentators explain it as God's sudden taking of a people in their security — and al-Rāzī relates it directly to the Qur'an's own gloss on the concept:

> *"Such is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong; indeed, His seizing is painful, severe."* (Qur'an 11:102)

The other occurrence of the word in the Qur'an fixes its historical referent:

> *"The Day We will strike the greatest strike (yawma nabṭishu al-baṭshata al-kubrā) — indeed, We will take vengeance."* (Qur'an 44:16)

The commentators identify that greatest strike with the Day of Badr, when the Quraysh's leadership was broken in a single morning. The word therefore carries both a historical and an eschatological charge: God's *baṭsh* has happened, in dates and places, and it will happen again, finally.

**Why "Your Lord"**

The verse makes a sudden shift in grammatical person. Everything around it is in the third person — the persecutors, the believers, God. Here God turns and addresses a *you*: *rabbika*, "your Lord."

The addressee is the Prophet ﷺ, and through him every believer who hears the verse. The effect of the shift is enormous and consoling: the severe seizing is not merely announced as a fact about the universe; it is announced as *yours*. The One whose hand closes upon the pit-diggers is the Lord of the man being persecuted for believing in Him. Divine severity, in this verse, is protection seen from the other side.

This is the answer to the deepest emotional problem raised by verses 4–10. A victim looking at unpunished evil does not merely ask whether justice exists; he asks whether anyone is *on his side*. Verse 12 answers in the possessive. Not "God's seizing is severe" but "your Lord's seizing is severe."

**The Grammar of Certainty**

The Arabic sentence is doubly emphatic. *Inna* — "indeed, truly" — front-loads the assertion; and the *lām* attached to the predicate (*la-shadīd*) — known as the *lām* of confirmation — closes it. Between the two, the sentence admits no hedging. It is not "God's seizing may be severe" or "will be severe"; it is: *it is severe*, stated with both available instruments of Arabic emphasis engaged at once.

Many commentators hold that this verse is the *jawāb al-qasam* — the answer to the oaths of verses 1–3. On that reading the entire architecture of the sūrah's opening resolves here: *By the sky with its towers, by the Promised Day, by the witness and the witnessed — your Lord's seizing is severe.* Three cosmic oaths sworn for one assertion. That is a measure of how seriously the Qur'an takes the proposition that injustice will be answered.

**Severity Aimed at a Seated People**

The word *baṭsh* — sudden seizure — is chosen with verse 6 in view. *Idh hum ʿalayhā quʿūd*: they were sitting upon it.

Sitting is the posture of people who believe nothing is coming for them. The severe seizing is defined by its timing: it arrives at the moment of maximum felt security. That is exactly what the Prophet ﷺ described in the report cited earlier in this sūrah (al-Bukhārī 4686; Muslim 2583): God grants respite to the wrongdoer, and when He takes him, He does not let him go.

The respite is what deceives people. A tyrant who has burned believers for ten years and is still alive concludes from his survival that he is exempt. The Qur'an answers with a whole theology of delay:

> *"And were God to take people to task for what they have earned, He would not leave upon its back a single creature; but He defers them to a named term. And when their term comes — indeed, God is ever Seeing of His servants."* (Qur'an 35:45)

> *"And let not those who disbelieve think that Our respite to them is good for them; We only respite them so that they may increase in sin — and for them is a humiliating punishment."* (Qur'an 3:178)

Delay is not acquittal. It is an extension of the period in which repentance remains possible — which is why verse 10 placed *thumma lam yatūbū* in the middle of the threat. God's severity is held back by God's patience, and the patience is what the persecutor mistakes for permission.

**Severity Before Forgiveness: The Order of the Verses**

The sequence that follows this verse is a lesson in Divine self-description:

- v. 12: your Lord's seizing is severe.
- v. 13: He originates and returns.
- v. 14: He is the Forgiving, the Loving.
- v. 15: Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious.
- v. 16: Doer of whatsoever He wills.

Severity is stated first, and then it is immediately qualified by forgiveness and love. This ordering is not accidental; it is the Qur'an's habitual method of preventing either error about God. A God described only as severe produces terror and despair; a God described only as loving produces presumption and moral carelessness. The Qur'an holds both, always, and in this sūrah it puts them five words apart.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the same balance in the report al-Bukhārī records about the Book above the Throne:

> *"When God decreed creation, He wrote in His Book, which is with Him above the Throne: 'My mercy prevails over My wrath.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751, from Abū Hurayrah)

The severe seizing is real and it is coming. It is also, in the Divine economy, the subordinate attribute. The verse says *la-shadīd* — severe; it does not say *dāʾim* — perpetual, or *sābiq* — preceding.

**Why a Moral Universe Requires Severity**

The modern temperament has a strong allergy to the idea of Divine severity, and the allergy is worth examining rather than dismissing.

Much of it is a legitimate reaction to bad preaching — to religion used as a club, to preachers who enjoyed the Fire more than they enjoyed God, to childhoods darkened by threats. The Qur'an's own answer to that abuse is the ordering above: severity inside a context of forgiveness, love, glory, and mercy-prevailing-over-wrath.

But some of the allergy is a failure of moral seriousness. Consider the analogy of pain. The human body is equipped with nociceptors whose entire function is to make certain experiences unbearable. There is a rare congenital condition in which a person feels no pain at all, and it is not a gift: those who have it burn themselves, break bones without noticing, chew through their own tongues, and die young. Pain is the body's severity, and its absence is fatal.

Moral reality works the same way. A universe in which burning children has no consequences is not a kinder universe; it is a universe with no nociceptors — a body that cannot feel damage and therefore cannot protect itself. The doctrine of severe Divine seizing is the moral nervous system of the cosmos. It is what makes the statement "this is evil" a statement of fact rather than an expression of taste. Remove it, and injustice is not abolished; it is merely declared unproblematic.

There is also the social function. Every human legal system rests on the same foundation: penalties exist mostly not because they are applied but because they are credible. A law with no sanction is advice. The Qur'an's severity is what converts its ethics from advice into law — and it does so for the protection of the weak, since the strong have never been deterred by the weakness of the vulnerable, only by the strength of someone else.

**The Warning Is Addressed to the Reader**

One final application, and it is the uncomfortable one.

*Inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd* is not only about the king at the pit. The verse is recited by believers, in prayer, five times a day, in a sūrah the Prophet ﷺ chose for the noon and afternoon prayers. Whoever recites it is not only informed about other people's punishment; he is warned about his own capacity for it.

Everyone carries a small version of the pit: a person he has power over and no one is watching him with. An employee, a child, a spouse, a subordinate, a student, a foreign worker whose passport he holds, a stranger online whom he can destroy with a sentence. The temptation at the mouth of that road is real, and verse 12 exists for the person standing there. The hand that closes is severe, and it closes on those who thought they were unseen.

The sūrah's next verses will explain why that severity can be trusted: because the One who holds it is the Originator who returns, the Forgiving, the Loving, the Possessor of the Throne. Severity without those attributes would be tyranny. With them, it is justice — and justice is the only thing that has ever consoled a person who was wronged and could not get redress.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:13

> **Truly it is He Who originates and brings back.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs That Contain All of History**

*Innahu huwa yubdiʾu wa yuʿīd* — "Indeed it is He who originates and brings back."

*Yubdiʾu* is from *b-d-ʾ*: to begin something with no prior instance, to originate, to bring into being what was not. *Yuʿīd* is from *ʿ-w-d*: to return, to repeat, to bring back again. Between the two verbs lies the entire career of the created universe — its coming forth from nothing, and its coming back.

The pronoun *huwa* is placed where grammar does not require it. *Innahu yubdiʾu wa yuʿīd* would be complete; the Qur'an inserts *huwa* as what the grammarians call the pronoun of separation (*ḍamīr al-faṣl*), and its function is exclusion: *it is He* — He, and not anyone else. No partner originates; no partner returns.

That exclusion is aimed, in this sūrah, at a specific human claim. The king at the pit exercised the power of life and death over his subjects: he decided who was sawn in half and who was thrown into the fire. In doing so he was performing, in his own mind, the function of a god. The Qur'an's history contains the same claim in the mouth of a man who argued with Abraham:

> *"Have you not considered him who argued with Abraham concerning his Lord, because God had given him sovereignty? ... My Lord gives life and causes death, said he."* (Qur'an 2:258)

Verse 13 removes the ground from under that claim. Giving life is not the same as *originating* it; killing is not the same as *returning* it. The tyrant can end a life he did not begin and cannot restore. God begins and restores. That is the difference between a criminal and a Creator.

**The Argument From Beginning to Return**

The verse is placed immediately after the severe seizing of verse 12 for a logical reason, and the reason is the Qur'an's most frequently deployed rational argument for the resurrection.

The objection is ancient and it is still heard: how can bodies that have rotted, burned, been scattered in the sea, or — as in this sūrah — consumed in a trench, be brought back and held accountable? The Qur'an answers not with mystery but with an inference:

> *"It is He who begins creation and then brings it back again, and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

> *"And he presents for Us an example and forgets his own creation, saying: who will give life to bones that have decayed? Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time, and He is Knowing of every creation."* (Qur'an 36:78–79)

> *"As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it — a promise binding upon Us. Indeed, We are the doers of it."* (Qur'an 21:104)

Note in 21:104 the phrase *waʿdan ʿalaynā* — "a promise binding upon Us." That is the guarantee behind verse 2 of this sūrah, *al-yawm al-mawʿūd*, "the Day promised." The Day is promised; the promise is binding; and the binding promise is backed by the power described in verse 13. The three verses — 2, 12, 13 — form a single syllogism: there is an appointed day of reckoning; the reckoning requires severe seizing; the seizing requires the ability to bring the dead back; and He who originated is the One who returns.

**Easier for Whom? A Point of Precision**

The phrase *wa huwa ahwanu ʿalayh* in 30:27 — "and that is easier for Him" — has been carefully explained by the commentators, because taken carelessly it would imply that origination is difficult for God, which no Muslim theologian accepts.

Al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr converge on the point: nothing is easier or harder for God, since He creates by command — *"His command, when He wills a thing, is only to say to it 'Be,' and it is"* (36:82). The comparison is made *relative to human understanding and human reckoning*. In your estimation, to make something from nothing is the harder act and to remake it from existing material is the easier; and if you concede the harder, you cannot deny the easier. The argument is addressed to the objector's own scale of difficulty, not to God's.

This is a good example of Qur'anic method generally: the text meets the disbeliever inside his own assumptions and shows that his conclusion does not follow from them. It is an argument, not an assertion.

**The Coccyx and the Body's Record**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the doctrine of bodily return a physical anchor. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record:

> *"There is nothing of a human being that does not decay, except one bone — the coccyx (ʿajb al-dhanab) — and from it he was created, and from it he will be reconstructed."* (al-Bukhārī 4935; Muslim 2955, from Abū Hurayrah)

And he described the manner of the gathering. Muslim records from ʿĀʾishah, and in its version the Prophet ﷺ then recited the verse from Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ:

> *"The people will be gathered on the Day of Resurrection barefoot, naked, uncircumcised." — "As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it."* (Muslim 2859–2860)

The tradition is careful about what resurrection means: not the survival of a disembodied pattern, but the return of the person — this body, this identity, restored. That matters enormously for the people of this sūrah. The monk's two halves will be rejoined. The boy's temple will be whole. The woman and her infant will be restored as they were before the trench. Resurrection in the Qur'an is not merely a legal proceeding; it is *repair*. The word *yuʿīd* carries both meanings at once: to bring back, and to restore.

**The Information Is Not Lost**

The Qur'an anticipates the sharpest form of the objection, and answers it in a way that reads almost like a modern discussion about information:

> *"And those who disbelieve say: shall we guide you to a man who informs you that when you are utterly torn to pieces, you will surely be in a new creation?"* (Qur'an 34:7)

*Torn to pieces* — *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — scattered to the last fragment. The objection is essentially: the pattern is destroyed; the information is gone.

The Qur'an's answer is that the information was never held by the body. It is held by God:

> *"And not absent from your Lord is the weight of an atom in the earth or in the heaven, nor anything smaller than that or greater, except that it is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 10:61)

> *"And We have certainly created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him; and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein."* (Qur'an 50:16)

And this sūrah will end by naming the register: *fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ* — upon a Preserved Tablet (v. 22). The structure of the argument across the whole chapter is now visible. Nothing about the pit was lost — not the fuel, not the sitting, not the glance, not the infant's words — because the One who originates and returns keeps a preserved record of everything He originated.

**Every Night Is a Rehearsal**

The Qur'an does not leave the doctrine of return as a purely intellectual proposition; it points to an experience every human being has twice a day.

> *"And it is He who takes your souls by night and knows what you commit by day; then He restores you in it, that a named term may be fulfilled."* (Qur'an 6:60)

> *"God takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die during their sleep; then He keeps those for which He has decreed death, and He releases the others to a named term."* (Qur'an 39:42)

Sleep is called the brother of death in the tradition's language, and waking is a small resurrection. Every morning you are *returned* to a body that lay unconscious and unmoving for hours, with your identity, memory, and obligations intact. Whoever finds bodily resurrection unintelligible should consider that he has already experienced the mechanism approximately twenty-five thousand times without being able to explain a single one of them.

This is the Qur'an's characteristic method of proof: it does not ask for belief in the unprecedented but for recognition of the repeated. *Fa-anzhur ilā āthāri raḥmati Allāh kayfa yuḥyī al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā* — "look at the traces of God's mercy, how He gives life to the earth after its death" (30:50). The seed buried in the ground in autumn and the body buried in the ground at death are, on this argument, the same kind of case handled by the same power.

**Why This Verse Comforts**

Verse 12 spoke of severity; verse 14 will speak of forgiveness and love; verse 13 stands between them and makes both possible.

Severity requires return: without resurrection, the pit-diggers escape. Forgiveness requires return: without resurrection, the believer's patience is wasted and his loss is final. Love requires return: what God loves, He does not consent to lose.

So the middle verse of this triad is the hinge. He originates — which means your existence is not an accident but an act, willed and known. He returns — which means your existence is not terminated but interrupted, and that the interruption has a fixed length. The person who understands this can stand at the edge of a trench with his child in his arms, as the woman of the ḥadīth did, and take the step forward. Not because he does not fear the fire, but because he knows the fire is not the last institution in the universe that will handle him.

The last two institutions are already named in the verse: the One who began him, and the One who brings him back.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:14

> **And He is the Forgiving, the Loving,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name That Appears Only Twice**

*Wa huwa al-Ghafūru al-Wadūd* — "and He is the Forgiving, the Loving."

Before anything else, a fact about this verse that changes how it is read: the Divine Name *al-Wadūd* occurs exactly twice in the entire Qur'an. The first is here, in al-Burūj. The second is in Sūrah Hūd:

> *"And seek forgiveness of your Lord, then repent to Him; indeed my Lord is Merciful, Loving (Raḥīmun Wadūd)."* (Qur'an 11:90)

Two occurrences in six thousand verses — and one of them is placed in the middle of a sūrah about mass burning, immediately after a curse upon the burners and a declaration of severe Divine seizing. The placement is not incidental. God has chosen to reveal His love in the very context where it would be most doubted.

**Al-Ghafūr: Covering and Protecting**

*Ghafūr* is the intensive form of the root *gh-f-r*, whose primary sense is not "to pardon" but "to cover and shield." A *mighfar* is a helmet — the piece of armour that covers the head. *Ghufrah* is a covering. So when God is called *al-Ghafūr*, the image is twofold at once: He *covers* the sin, so that it is not exposed to the world, to the angels, or to the sinner's own shame; and He *shields* the sinner, so that the sin's consequence does not fall upon him.

Forgiveness in the Qur'anic sense is therefore not merely the cancellation of a debt. It is the withdrawal of a case, the sealing of a file, and the protection of the person who was in it. The commentators distinguish it from related Names: *al-ʿAfūww* effaces the sin, *al-Ghafūr* covers it, *al-Raḥīm* bestows good after it, and *al-Tawwāb* accepts the return of the one who turns. Al-Ghafūr is the Name that handles the exposure — and exposure is what a human being fears most about his own sins.

This is why the Name is placed here, in a sūrah about people whose sins were performed publicly. The persecutors' conduct was witnessed, recorded, and published to fourteen centuries of readers. The verse announces that even so, the covering is available — which is exactly what verse 10's *thumma lam yatūbū* promised.

**Al-Wadūd: The One Who Loves and Is Loved**

*Wadūd* comes from the root *w-d-d*, the root of love in its warmest and most personal register: *wudd*, *widād*, *mawaddah*. The Qur'an uses the noun in the description of marriage:

> *"And among His signs is that He created for you mates from yourselves that you may find rest in them, and He placed between you love (mawaddah) and mercy."* (Qur'an 30:21)

The form *Wadūd* is the intensive *faʿūl* pattern, and the classical exegetes read it in two directions, both of which they affirm.

Ibn ʿAbbās glossed it simply: *al-Wadūd* is *al-Ḥabīb* — the Beloved. Al-Khaṭṭābī, in his foundational work on the Divine Names, explained it as "the One who loves His righteous servants and is loved by them." Al-Ghazālī, in *al-Maqṣad al-Asnā*, refined the theological content: God's love is not passion — passion implies need, lack, and agitation in the one who loves, all of which are impossible for God — but the willing of good to the beloved and the bestowal of that good. Love in God is an act, not an emotion; it is generosity directed at a particular person.

Both directions matter to the believer. That God loves His servants means your faithfulness is not going unnoticed in an indifferent universe. That God is loved by His servants means the relationship is not one-way servitude but reciprocal affection — which is why the tradition treats love of God as the highest station of the spiritual life, and why the Qur'an makes it the test:

> *"Say: if you love God, then follow me, and God will love you and forgive you your sins."* (Qur'an 3:31)

**Why Forgiveness and Love Are Paired**

The combination is theologically loaded, and the commentators draw out its logic.

Forgiveness without love is administrative: a debt cancelled by a creditor who feels nothing. Love without forgiveness is tragic: an affection that cannot survive contact with the beloved's failure. The two Names together describe a relationship that is both real and durable — one in which the lover continues to love the person who has repeatedly failed him, and continues to cover him.

There is also a psychological necessity in the pairing, and it addresses the exact wound this sūrah is treating. The people of the pit, and every persecuted community after them, are liable to a specific spiritual temptation: to conclude from the violence done to them that God is angry with them, or indifferent, or that they have been abandoned. Suffering produces this inference with terrifying reliability. The verse answers it by naming God's love at the very point in the narrative where abandonment would feel most plausible — three verses after the curse, two after the severe seizing.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the same doctrine in the most explicit terms in a ḥadīth qudsī recorded by al-Bukhārī:

> *"Whoever shows enmity to a friend of Mine (waliyyan lī), I have declared war upon him. My servant draws near to Me with nothing more beloved to Me than what I have obligated upon him, and My servant continues to draw near to Me with supererogatory deeds until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees, his hand with which he strikes, and his foot with which he walks. If he asks Me, I will surely give him; and if he seeks refuge in Me, I will surely protect him."* (al-Bukhārī 6502, from Abū Hurayrah)

Read that beside verses 4–10. The men at the pit showed enmity to the friends of God — sawed them, drowned them, shot them, burned them. The ḥadīth announces what they actually did: they declared war on God, and God had already declared it back. And the friends they tortured are described, in the same report, as the objects of a love so total that God becomes their faculties.

Verse 14 is the Qur'an's own summary of that ḥadīth, placed inside the sūrah that the ḥadīth explains.

**The Joy of Forgiveness**

That God is *al-Ghafūr* is not a reluctant concession in the Divine nature; it is a delight. Muslim records:

> *"God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one of you who is in a desolate wilderness, whose riding animal escapes with all his food and drink, so he despairs of it, lies down under a tree waiting for death — and then it appears before him. He takes hold of its reins and says, from the intensity of his joy: 'O God, You are my servant and I am your Lord' — having garbled the words."* (Muslim 2747, from Anas ibn Mālik)

The image is deliberately extreme: a man dying of thirst in a desert whose entire means of survival returns to him, so overcome that his language inverts. That, the Prophet ﷺ says, is a lesser joy than God's at the return of one servant.

And the scale of mercy is stated in a report both al-Bukhārī and Muslim preserve:

> *"God divided mercy into one hundred parts; He kept ninety-nine parts with Himself and sent down one part to the earth. Through that one part the creatures show mercy to one another, until the horse raises its hoof from its foal for fear of striking it."* (al-Bukhārī 6000; Muslim 2752)

Ninety-nine parts remain in reserve — held by the One who is *al-Ghafūr al-Wadūd*, and available at the point of return.

**Two Kinds of People Need This Verse**

Verse 14 is addressed to the persecutor and to the persecuted simultaneously, and each needs a different half of it.

The persecutor needs *al-Ghafūr*. The door of verse 10 — *thumma lam yatūbū* — is real, and it stays open until the death rattle. History is full of men who did terrible things and returned: the Quraysh who tortured Bilāl and later stood in the row behind him in prayer; Wahshī, who killed the Prophet's uncle Ḥamzah and later accepted Islam; whole cities that had persecuted prophets and repented. There is no crime that exceeds the covering, and any preacher who tells a sinner that his sin is too large has misdescribed God.

The persecuted needs *al-Wadūd*. The person who has been beaten for his faith, fired for his convictions, cut off by his family, imprisoned for refusing to lie, or mocked for praying in a workplace — this person does not primarily need to be told that his enemies will be punished. He needs to be told that he is loved. Punishment of the oppressor is justice; love of the oppressed is *relationship*. Verse 11 gave him the Garden; verse 14 gives him something the Garden is only the outward form of.

The Prophet ﷺ illustrated how much this love counts with a small incident al-Bukhārī records. A man used to lead his companions in prayer and always concluded his recitation with Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ, because he loved it. When the matter reached the Prophet ﷺ, he said:

> *"Ask him why he does that." He said: because it is the attribute of the Merciful, and I love to recite it. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Inform him that God loves him."* (al-Bukhārī 7375; Muslim 813)

In another narration the summary is shorter and even more striking: *"Your love for it has admitted you to the Garden."* (al-Bukhārī 774)

A man's affection for a description of God became his admission ticket. If love of a *sūrah* carries that weight, what does the love of God Himself carry — and what does being loved *by* Him carry?

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Architecture**

Look at the sequence again:

- v. 12 — His seizing is severe.
- v. 13 — He originates and returns.
- v. 14 — He is the Forgiving, the Loving.
- v. 15 — Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious.
- v. 16 — Doer of whatsoever He wills.

The five verses are a compact doctrine of God, and their order is instructive. Power is stated (12), then capacity (13), then character (14), then majesty (15), then sovereignty of will (16). Character is placed in the middle, between capacity and majesty — so that the reader meets the power of God only after being told what that power is *for*, and meets His majesty only after being told what His majesty is *made of*.

This is the answer to the accusation that Islam presents a God of terror. The same sūrah that curses the pit-diggers and warns of severe seizing puts, at the centre of its theology, a Name that appears twice in the whole Book and says: *He is the Loving.* Severity is real. It is also surrounded.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:15

> **Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reading That Changes Who Is Called Glorious**

*Dhū al-ʿarshi al-Majīd.* The verse is three words, and one of its vowels is a matter of transmitted recitation.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim — the reading in nearly universal use today — *al-Majīd* carries a *ḍammah*: nominative case, making it an attribute of *Dhū*, and therefore of God. "Possessor of the Throne, the Glorious."

In the reading of Ḥamzah and al-Kisāʾī, and of Khalaf, it carries a *kasrah*: *al-Majīdi*, genitive case, making it an attribute of the Throne. "Possessor of the Glorious Throne."

Both readings are canonical, both were transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ, and the exegetes — al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī — affirm both meanings rather than choosing between them. This is one of the clearest illustrations of a principle students of the Qur'an must grasp early: variant readings in the tenfold transmitted corpus are not rival texts competing for correctness. They are layers of a single meaning, and where the grammar permits two attributions, both are intended. God is *al-Majīd*, and His Throne is *majīd* — because the majesty of the seat derives from the majesty of the One who possesses it.

**Majd: Nobility That Gives**

*Majīd* comes from the root *m-j-d*, and *majd* in Arabic is not simply greatness of size or force. It denotes the fullness of nobility — a nobility that is generous, wide-reaching, and beneficial to others. A *mājid* man is one whose honour is accompanied by open-handedness; the word is closer to "magnanimous" than to "mighty."

Al-Qurṭubī, following the lexicographers, explains that *majīd* indicates the utmost in nobility, magnanimity, and grace. When applied to God it means that His glory is not the glory of a tyrant — which is glory extracted from subjects — but the glory of a giver, which flows toward them.

The Qur'an uses the word of itself as well, and the usage illuminates the Name:

> *"Qāf. By the glorious Qur'an (wa'l-Qurʾān al-majīd)."* (Qur'an 50:1)

> *"Nay, it is a glorious Qur'an (Qurʾānun majīd)."* (Qur'an 85:21)

And it pairs the concept with the Name *Ḥamīd* used in verse 8 of this same sūrah, in the angels' words to Abraham's household:

> *"They said: do you wonder at the command of God? Mercy of God and His blessings be upon you, people of the house; indeed He is Praised, Glorious (Ḥamīdun Majīd)."* (Qur'an 11:73)

The web of vocabulary across al-Burūj is now complete: God is *al-Ḥamīd* (v. 8) and *al-Majīd* (v. 15), and His Book is *majīd* (v. 21). The sūrah begins by swearing on the sky and ends by describing a glorious Book, with a glorious God and a glorious Throne in the middle. Glory is the chapter's second great theme, running beneath the theme of witness.

**The Throne in Qur'anic Language**

The *ʿarsh* is the greatest of created things and the summit of the cosmos as the Qur'an describes it. The word in ordinary Arabic means a roofed structure, a canopy, a throne, a trellis — as in the vine-trellises and the roofed buildings of a ruined town:

> *"And how many a town We destroyed while it was wronging itself, so it is collapsed upon its roofs (ʿurūshuhā)..."* (Qur'an 22:45)

Applied to God, the Throne is presented with consistent features: it is His; it is borne by angels; it is the station from which command descends.

> *"Those who bear the Throne, and those around it, glorify the praise of their Lord and believe in Him, and seek forgiveness for those who believe: 'Our Lord, You encompass all things in mercy and knowledge...'"* (Qur'an 40:7)

> *"And the angels will be at its edges, and there will bear the Throne of your Lord above them, that Day, eight."* (Qur'an 69:17)

> *"The Exalted in ranks, Possessor of the Throne (Dhū al-ʿarsh), who casts the Spirit of His command upon whom He wills of His servants, that he may warn of the Day of Meeting."* (Qur'an 40:15)

Elsewhere God is called Lord of the Throne — *rabb al-ʿarsh* — at 9:129, 21:22, 23:86, 27:26 and 43:82, and in one of these the Throne is itself given an adjective of honour:

> *"So exalted is God, the True King; there is no god but He, Lord of the Noble Throne (rabb al-ʿarsh al-karīm)."* (Qur'an 23:116)

*Noble* there, *glorious* here: the Throne is described in the language of honour in two places, which is why the genitive reading of *al-Majīd* is not a strained possibility but a natural one.

The scale of the Throne is indicated in authenticated reports. Abū Dāwūd records from Jābir ibn ʿAbdullāh:

> *"Permission has been granted me to speak of one of the angels of God among those who bear the Throne: the distance between the lobe of his ear and his shoulder is a journey of seven hundred years."* (Abū Dāwūd 4727)

And in the tradition's most cited statement of proportion, transmitted by Ibn Abī Shaybah and affirmed by Ibn Taymiyyah:

> *"The seven heavens compared to the Footstool (kursī) are like a ring thrown into a desert; and the excellence of the Throne over the Footstool is like the excellence of the desert over the ring."*

These reports are not meant to be measured. They are meant to produce a specific effect in the mind of the reader: the demolition of scale as a category in which human beings can compete with God.

**Believing Without Imagining: The Discipline of Istiwāʾ**

Wherever the Throne is mentioned, the tradition confronts a question that has produced some of Islamic history's fiercest intellectual conflicts: what does it mean that God is related to the Throne?

The Qur'an says:

> *"The Merciful established Himself upon the Throne (al-Raḥmānu ʿalā al-ʿarshi istawā)."* (Qur'an 20:5; also 7:54; 10:3; 13:2; 25:59; 32:4; 57:4)

The response of the earliest generation became the classic formula of Sunni creed. It is attributed to Imām Mālik ibn Anas (d. 179/795), and al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and Ibn Taymiyyah all cite it with approval:

> *"The establishing is known; the how is unknown; believing in it is obligatory; and asking about it is an innovation."*

The formula is a model of intellectual hygiene. It affirms the text without pretending to comprehend it, refuses to reduce God to a body located in space, refuses also to explain the verse away into pure metaphor, and identifies the boundary beyond which questioning becomes unprofitable speculation. Ibn Taymiyyah developed this at length: God's attributes are affirmed *as He described them*, without *tashbīh* (likening Him to creatures), without *taʿṭīl* (stripping the attributes of meaning), without *taḥrīf* (distorting the words), and without *takyīf* (specifying a mode).

Al-Rāzī, coming from the rationalist-theological side, records the alternative readings in his commentary — including the view that the Throne signifies sovereignty and dominion, so that "Possessor of the Throne" means Possessor of absolute kingship — and he weighs them with characteristic thoroughness. The mainstream position has remained the one articulated by Mālik: affirm the words, decline the how, and let the meaning do its work in the heart rather than in the diagram.

**The Vertical Architecture of the Sūrah**

Placed where it is, verse 15 completes a climb that the sūrah has been making since its first line.

Verse 1: the sky and its towers — the heavens.
Verse 4: a trench dug in the earth — the lowest point.
Verse 9: sovereignty over the heavens and the earth — the whole estate.
Verse 15: the Possessor of the Throne — above the estate.

The Qur'an has moved the reader from the pit to the Throne in eleven verses, and the movement is the consolation. The men at the bottom of the picture, sitting on the rim of a hole they dug in the ground, are being judged by a Sovereign whose seat is above the constellations they can see and whose glory exceeds anything their eyes can register.

Notice too the answer to the earthly throne. Every tyrant has a throne; the Qur'an does not deny its existence, it relocates it. *Dhū al-ʿarsh* — Possessor of the Throne, with *dhū* indicating that the Throne is His by ownership, not by achievement. The king of the pit sat on a throne he inherited by violence. God possesses a Throne that no one gave Him.

There is a further connection the exegetes drew between this verse and the end of the sūrah. The ḥadīth recorded by al-Bukhārī states that God wrote in a Book which is with Him *above the Throne*: "My mercy prevails over My wrath" (al-Bukhārī 3194). The sūrah closes with a Book — *fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ*, upon a Preserved Tablet (v. 22) — and some commentators place that Tablet in relation to the Throne. Verse 15 and verse 22 are thus quietly linked: the Throne is the seat of the Sovereign, and the Tablet is the record of the Sovereign. Between them, everything that happens on earth is both governed and documented.

**Living Under a Throne**

The practical effect of this verse is best understood by asking what changes in a person who really believes it.

Two opposite disorders are cured by the same doctrine. The first is the disorder of the powerful: the conviction that one's own authority is ultimate. A man who holds real power over others — a judge, a minister, a CEO, a patriarch, an officer — is in constant danger of forgetting that his throne is a chair. The Throne above all thrones is the permanent correction to megalomania, and it is why the Qur'an so often addresses kings through the story of their destruction.

The second disorder is the disorder of the powerless: the conviction that the human throne above you is final, that the person who controls your visa, your salary, your sentence, or your reputation controls your fate. The woman at the trench's edge did not believe that. She stepped forward because the authority being exercised over her was, in her reckoning, a subordinate authority operating inside a larger jurisdiction whose Sovereign was on her side.

Between those two disorders stands the ordinary day, in which most people live in a vague practical atheism about the Throne — professing it in the creed and ignoring it in the calendar. The discipline the verse proposes is not speculative but directional: when you find yourself either swollen by your own power or crushed by someone else's, look up. There is a Throne. It is possessed, not contested. Its Possessor is *al-Majīd* — noble, magnanimous, generous beyond the measure of any human patron — and He is described in the same breath as *al-Ghafūr al-Wadūd*.

That is the whole of the verse's pastoral force: the highest thing in existence is not the power that can hurt you. It is a glory that gives.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:16

> **Doer of whatsoever He will.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faʿʿāl: The Form That Means He Never Stops**

*Faʿʿālun limā yurīd* — "Doer of whatsoever He wills."

The word *faʿʿāl* is an intensive pattern in Arabic morphology, the *mubālaghah* form, and it carries two meanings at once: abundance and repetition. A *faʿʿāl* is not someone who does a thing once; he is someone who does it much, does it often, and does it thoroughly. The Qur'an uses the pattern for the same reason elsewhere: *ghaffār* (ever-Forgiving), *ʿallām* (All-Knowing of the unseen), *qahhār* (Ever-Dominant), *tawwāb* (ever-Returning in mercy).

Applied here, the form says something more than "God can do what He wants." It says: God is *continually* doing what He wills. His action is not an occasional intervention into an otherwise self-running universe; it is unbroken. Every moment in which anything happens is a moment in which He is doing what He wills.

The same phrase appears in the Qur'an's other occurrence at the close of Sūrah Hūd:

> *"...your Lord does whatsoever He wills (faʿʿālun limā yurīd)."* (Qur'an 11:107)

And the plain form appears repeatedly:

> *"Truly God does whatsoever He wills."* (Qur'an 22:18)

> *"God creates whatsoever He wills."* (Qur'an 24:45)

> *"Unto God belongs the sovereignty of the heavens and the earth and whatsoever is in them, and He is over all things Powerful."* (Qur'an 5:120)

**The Two Wills: A Distinction That Solves the Sūrah's Hardest Problem**

The statement "He does whatsoever He wills" raises immediately the question this entire sūrah has been circling: did He will the pit?

The classical theologians answered with a distinction that is essential to Islamic creed and that Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim articulated with particular clarity. God's will is spoken of in two senses.

The first is the *will of decree* — *al-irādah al-kawniyyah* or *al-qadariyyah* — by which He brings things into existence. Nothing occurs in the cosmos outside it. Under this will, everything that happens happens, including sin, disbelief, persecution, and the pit.

The second is the *will of command and pleasure* — *al-irādah al-dīniyyah* or *al-sharʿiyyah* — by which He commands what He loves and is pleased with what He has commanded. Under this will, He loves faith, justice, mercy, and patience, and He hates disbelief, oppression, and corruption:

> *"And God does not love corruption."* (Qur'an 2:205)

> *"He does not approve disbelief for His servants."* (Qur'an 39:7)

The two wills are not contradictory; they operate on different objects. God *decreed* that the pit would occur — permitting it within a world He created with real causes, real freedom, and real consequences — while He *hated* the pit, cursed its diggers in this very sūrah, and prepared for them the punishment of burning. He permitted it for reasons known to Him and partly disclosed to us: the manifestation of the believers' patience, the exposure of the persecutors' nature, the instruction of every generation that reads the verses.

Without this distinction, the doctrine of Divine omnipotence collapses either into the claim that God approves everything that happens — which is refuted by the curse in verse 4 — or into the claim that things happen against God's will, which refutes *faʿʿālun limā yurīd*. The distinction preserves both the sovereignty and the goodness of God, and it is the intellectual spine of Islamic theodicy.

**Not Questioned, and Not Arbitrary**

The Qur'an states the sovereignty of Divine action in its most uncompromising form in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ:

> *"He is not questioned about what He does, but they will be questioned."* (Qur'an 21:23)

That verse has sometimes been misread as a declaration of arbitrary power, and the misreading is serious enough that the tradition answered it explicitly. Ibn Taymiyyah and the scholars who followed him insist that God's not being questioned does not mean His acts lack reasons. It means He is not a defendant. His actions are wise (*ḥakīm*), purposeful, and just — and the Qur'an says so constantly:

> *"We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them as a plaything."* (Qur'an 44:38)

> *"And your Lord does not wrong anyone."* (Qur'an 18:49)

> *"And God wills justice for you."* (Qur'an 4:26–27, in context)

The correct understanding of *faʿʿālun limā yurīd* is therefore not "God may do anything, however senseless" but "nothing can obstruct what God has wisely willed." The emphasis is on *effectiveness*, not caprice. Read it in the sūrah's own context and the emphasis becomes plain: the men who dug the pit believed their will was the operative force in the region. The verse informs them that the operative force was never theirs.

**Human Will Inside Divine Will**

If God does whatsoever He wills, what remains of human choice? This is the question that produced Islam's earliest theological schisms — the Qadariyyah who denied the decree, the Jabriyyah who denied human agency, and the mainstream position between them.

The Qur'an holds the two together in a single sentence, and it does so without embarrassment:

> *"For whoever among you wills to go straight — yet you do not will except that God wills, Lord of the worlds."* (Qur'an 81:28–29)

> *"Indeed, this is a reminder; so whoever wills may take a path to his Lord — and you do not will except that God wills."* (Qur'an 76:29–30)

You will — genuinely, such that you are praised or blamed for it. And your willing occurs within His willing, such that nothing escapes Him. The mainstream theologians expressed the relationship through the concept of *kasb*, acquisition: God creates the act; the human being acquires it by choosing it, and is therefore accountable for it. The practical test of the doctrine is simple and it works: when you decide, you experience a decision, and when you fail, you experience your own failure as yours. Nobody has ever sincerely blamed God for a lie he told.

The Qur'an uses this balance as an argument, not a puzzle. When a person says "God willed it" to excuse his own sin, the Book answers that his forebears said the same:

> *"Those who associated partners will say: had God willed, we would not have associated partners, nor would our fathers, and we would not have forbidden anything. Thus did those before them deny, until they tasted Our punishment."* (Qur'an 6:148)

Fatalism as an excuse for wickedness is refuted by the outcome: they tasted the punishment anyway. Responsibility survives every metaphysical argument made against it.

**Why This Verse Follows the Throne**

Verse 15 presented the Sovereign; verse 16 presents His effective authority. The pairing answers a worry the previous verse might otherwise raise: what good is a Throne if its Possessor cannot act?

The sequence within the sūrah is now complete and devastating to the persecutors. Verse 9: to Him belongs the sovereignty of the heavens and the earth. Verse 12: His seizing is severe. Verse 13: He originates and returns. Verse 15: He possesses the Throne. Verse 16: He does whatsoever He wills. Five verses, five attributes — dominion, severity, resurrection, majesty, and unobstructed will — assembled into a single legal instrument against every tyrant who ever lived.

The Qur'an makes the political application explicit in the story of Abraham's opponent, who claimed the power of life and death (2:258), and in the story of Pharaoh, who claimed *"I am your lord, most high"* (79:24) and was answered by being seized for the hereafter and the former life (79:25). Every such claim is a claim to *faʿʿālun limā yurīd*. The verse declares the title taken.

**Two Supplications That Say the Same Thing**

The Prophet ﷺ taught this doctrine in the form of ordinary daily speech, which is how it enters a believer's life rather than remaining a proposition.

At the end of every obligatory prayer:

> *"O God, none can withhold what You have given, and none can give what You have withheld, and no possessor of fortune is benefited by his fortune against You."* (al-Bukhārī 844; Muslim 593)

And in the counsel he gave to Ibn ʿAbbās as a boy:

> *"Know that if the whole creation gathered together to benefit you with something, they could not benefit you except with what God has already written for you; and if they gathered together to harm you with something, they could not harm you except with what God has already written against you. The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* (al-Tirmidhī 2516, graded ṣaḥīḥ)

*"The pens have been lifted and the pages have dried."* The image connects forward to the end of this sūrah, where the Qur'an itself is described as written upon a Preserved Tablet (v. 22). What is written there is finished; what is not written there cannot be added by any human being.

**The Freedom Inside the Doctrine**

The practical fruit of *faʿʿālun limā yurīd* is a specific kind of courage, and the pit is its exhibit.

A person who believes that God does whatsoever He wills stops being manageable. He cannot be controlled through threats, because the threats belong to someone whose will is subordinate; he cannot be purchased through promises, because the promised goods are already allocated; he cannot be broken by loss, because nothing he loses was ever his to keep. This is why the doctrine of the decree has historically produced the least servile human beings in Islamic civilization — scholars who refused to sign false opinions under torture, and among them Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, who was flogged for years during the inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an and would not say what the caliph wanted.

He could not be made to say it, because he believed verse 16. The caliph had soldiers, prisons, whips, and a throne; the imām had a sentence: *faʿʿālun limā yurīd*. The sentence outlasted the caliphate, and the report of the flogging is read today in every biography of the schools of law.

That is also the verse's message to the ordinary reader facing a smaller tyrant — a boss, a bureaucracy, an illness, a debt, a person who has power over your family. It does not promise that the tyrant will fail this week. It promises that the tyrant's will is not the last will in the chain, and that whatever he does to you has already passed through a permission you did not have to grant. The one who knows that can stand straight at the edge of a trench, hold his child, and hear the child say: *be patient, for you are upon the truth.*

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:17

> **Hast thou heard tell of the hosts—**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Turns the Reader's Head**

*Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd* — "Has there come to you the report of the hosts?"

The verse is a question with no answer required. Its function in Arabic rhetoric is *tanbīh* — alerting. A speaker who asks "have you heard what happened?" has not requested information; he has changed the listener's posture. Attention rises. The ear leans in.

The Qur'an uses this device repeatedly at the head of its great historical recitals, and the formula becomes almost a genre marker:

> *"Has there come to you the report of the overwhelming calamity?"* (Qur'an 88:1)

> *"Has the report of Moses come to you?"* (Qur'an 20:9; 79:15)

> *"Has there come to you the report of the honoured guests of Abraham?"* (Qur'an 51:24)

In each case the question introduces a story the Prophet ﷺ is about to tell — and that produces a second layer of meaning, which the classical exegetes did not miss. The Prophet ﷺ was unlettered; he had not studied the histories of Egypt or the inscriptions of northern Arabia. When he asks his listeners *"has the report come to you?"*, and then proceeds to supply it with detail, the question is also an implicit claim about the source. The Qur'an makes that claim explicit elsewhere:

> *"That is from the reports of the unseen which We reveal to you; you did not know it, you nor your people, before this."* (Qur'an 11:49)

So the interrogative does three things at once: it seizes attention, it establishes the speaker's authority over the material, and it signals that what follows is *news* rather than reflection.

**Ḥadīth: The Word for Report — and for the Book Itself**

The word used is *ḥadīth*, from the root *ḥ-d-th*, meaning that which is new, that which occurs, and hence a report, a narrative, a thing told. In ordinary Arabic *ḥadīth* is simply news or discourse.

The Qur'an appropriates the word for itself:

> *"God has sent down the most beautiful discourse (aḥsana al-ḥadīth), a Book consistent, repeating..."* (Qur'an 39:23)

> *"Then in what discourse after it will they believe?"* (Qur'an 77:50)

> *"These are the verses of God which We recite to you in truth; then in what discourse (ḥadīth) after God and His verses will they believe?"* (Qur'an 45:6)

There is a subtle power in using that word here. God says, in effect: *let Me tell you the news about the hosts.* The report is being delivered by the only source whose report is guaranteed. The histories of Pharaoh and Thamūd survive in fragments — an inscription here, a ruin there, a biblical paragraph, an Arab oral memory. The Qur'an presents itself as the authoritative file on the matter, and it opens the file with the question.

**Junūd: The Word for Organized Multitude**

*Junūd* is the plural of *jund* — an army, a host, a gathered force organized for a purpose. The word is not used for a crowd; it is used for a *formation*. It carries the sense of structure, command, discipline, and numbers.

The Qur'an uses it of the armies of God as well as of men, and the contrast is deliberate:

> *"And to God belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth."* (Qur'an 48:4, 7)

> *"And none knows the hosts of your Lord except Him."* (Qur'an 74:31)

It also uses it of the confederate armies that besieged Madinah:

> *"O you who believe, remember the favour of God upon you when hosts (junūd) came to you, and We sent upon them a wind and hosts you did not see."* (Qur'an 33:9)

That last verse deserves a moment in the context of this sūrah. Sūrah al-Aḥzāb's *junūd* were the armies of the Confederates at the Battle of the Trench — and the Muslims met them with a *trench of their own*, dug on the advice of Salmān al-Fārisī, a defensive ditch across the vulnerable face of Madinah. The audience of al-Burūj therefore knew both words in this passage experientially: they knew what an *ukhdūd* was, and they knew what *junūd* meant, because they had lived through a siege in which the enemy assembled and the ditch was dug.

The difference in the two trenches is the whole of Islamic ethics: the trench of Madinah was dug to keep an army out and to save a community; the trench of al-Burūj was dug to burn believers and to erase a faith. The Qur'an does not need to draw the contrast for its first hearers, who had stood beside one and were being told about the other.

**The Hosts as a Type, and the Pause at the End of the Verse**

The verse ends without completing its sense. *Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd* — and then a full stop. The answer comes in verse 18: *Firʿawna wa Thamūd*.

Recited aloud, this produces a held breath. The listener hears "the hosts" and waits a beat before learning which hosts. That beat is not filler; it is where the mind runs through its own catalogue of powers — Persia, Byzantium, Quraysh, the king of Yemen, the empire that taxed his grandfather — and every one of them is included in the indefinite plural before two examples are named.

Note the grammar: the definite plural *al-junūd* — "the hosts" — as though they were a single known class, and then two proper names given as specimens. The exegetes, including Ibn Kathīr, read the verse as referring to the forces of the ancient deniers of the prophets who were destroyed: the class is universal, the instances are illustrative. Pharaoh and Thamūd are not the whole list; they are two exhibits filed under a standing charge.

**The Argument the Word Makes**

Why "hosts" rather than "people" or "nations"?

Because *jund* is the word for organized power, and organized power is precisely what a persecuted minority does not have. The believers of the pit had no army, no treasury, no walls, no allies, and no state. The men who burned them had all five. The verse announces that the case is about to be examined from the side of the five.

The Qur'an's answer to numerical and military superiority is consistent and it is stated with a kind of statistical contempt:

> *"There was a sign for you in the two parties that met: one party fighting in the cause of God, and the other disbelieving, who saw them with their own eyes as twice their number. And God supports with His victory whom He wills."* (Qur'an 3:13)

> *"O Prophet, suffice you is God, and those who follow you of the believers. O Prophet, urge the believers to fight. If there are twenty patient among you, they will overcome two hundred."* (Qur'an 8:64–65)

> *"How many a small company has overcome a large company by the permission of God; and God is with the patient."* (Qur'an 2:249)

The verse before us is the historical demonstration of that principle. *Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd* — have you heard what happened to the armies? Every one of them lost.

**The Illusion of the Coalition**

The word *junūd* speaks to a permanent feature of political life: the belief that assembled strength is decisive strength.

Coalitions are built, alliances are signed, deterrence is calculated, and the analysis of every serious power begins with counting. Counting is not irrational — it is often accurate in the short term, which is exactly why it is dangerous. The short term is where the pit was dug and the short term is where the saw was used, and in the short term the king won completely.

The Qur'an's counter is not that numbers never matter but that they are not the last term in the calculation. There is an unseen column on the balance sheet: *wa junūdan lam tarawhā* — "and hosts you did not see" (33:9); *wa mā yaʿlamu junūda rabbika illā huwa* — "and none knows the hosts of your Lord except Him" (74:31). Wind, angels, fear in the enemy's heart, a sudden decision by an advisor, a change of weather, an internal collapse: the history of the destroyed hosts is the history of factors no strategist had counted.

For the individual reader the application is smaller and more common. Everyone at some point faces a *jund* — a coalition of circumstances arrayed against him: a diagnosis plus a debt plus a family crisis plus a hostile workplace, arriving together, so that the total seems to have been organized. The verse's answer is that hosts have been assembled before, by powers far greater than your difficulties, and that the record of their outcomes is public. *Hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd.* Have you heard what happened to them?

Verse 18 supplies two names.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:18

> **Pharaoh and Thamūd?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Names, Two Audiences, No Excuse Left**

*Firʿawna wa Thamūd* — "Pharaoh and Thamūd."

The grammar is apposition (*badal*): the hosts are identified, and the identification is given as two proper names in the genitive/accusative case answering the indefinite plural of verse 17. The choice of *these two* has been explained by the commentators with a precision that reveals the Qur'an's rhetorical design.

Pharaoh is the example from the scriptural past — the great oppressor of Israel, familiar to the Jews of Arabia, to the Christians of Najrān and Yemen, and to anyone who had heard the stories of the People of the Book. He is distant in time (some fourteen centuries before the Prophet ﷺ) and distant in place (Egypt).

Thamūd is the example from the Arabs' own ground. They were a northern Arabian people, the tribe of the Prophet Ṣāliḥ, who carved dwellings into the rock at al-Ḥijr — the site known today as Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ — which sat directly on the caravan route between Makkah and Syria. The Quraysh passed those ruins every year on their summer trade journey. They walked among the doorways, drew water from the wells, and sheltered in the carved chambers.

The pairing therefore closes every avenue of dismissal. Pharaoh: too far away? Then look at Thamūd, whose houses you can touch. Thamūd: too long ago? Then look at Pharaoh, whose story the People of the Book still recite. One exhibit is visible, one is documented, and together they establish that this is not legend but a pattern.

**Pharaoh of the Stakes**

The Qur'an's portrait of Pharaoh is extensive — the confrontations with Moses (7:103–141; 20:24–79; 26:10–68; 28:3–42), the magicians' conversion (7:115–126; 20:65–73), the plagues (7:130–135), the boast *"I am your lord, most high"* (79:24), the drowning (10:90; 26:66; 44:24).

But the detail most relevant to this sūrah is his epithet in Sūrah al-Fajr:

> *"And Pharaoh of the stakes (dhī al-awtād), who transgressed in the lands and multiplied corruption therein — so your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment. Indeed, your Lord is on the watch (inna rabbaka la-bi'l-mirṣād)."* (Qur'an 89:10–14)

*Al-awtād* — the stakes, pegs, or pilings. The commentators explain the phrase in several ways: the stakes by which Pharaoh fastened people for torture, driving pegs into the ground and stretching his victims upon them; or his vast buildings and monuments, standing like stakes in the earth; or his armies and his firm establishment of power. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record the first explanation prominently, and the Qur'an states the practice explicitly elsewhere in the mouth of Pharaoh himself:

> *"Pharaoh said: I will surely cut off your hands and your feet on opposite sides, and I will surely crucify you on the trunks of palm trees, and you will surely know which of us is more severe in punishment and more lasting."* (Qur'an 20:71)

*On the trunks of palm trees* — *fī judhūʿi al-nakhl*. Compare the ḥadīth of Ṣuhayb, in which the king ordered the boy tied to a trunk (*ṣalabahu ʿalā jadhl*) and shot at him. The instrument is the same. Two tyrants, separated by centuries, using the same method: the human being fixed to a post in public view.

Al-Fajr 89:10–14 is thus a parallel passage to al-Burūj in nearly every particular: a tyrant with instruments of torture, transgression multiplied in the lands, a poured-out punishment, and a God who is *bi'l-mirṣād* — on the watch, at the observation post. The two sūrahs, both Makkan, both short, were revealed into the same persecution and make the same argument.

**Thamūd: The Engineers Who Carved the Rock**

The Qur'an's account of Thamūd is detailed and its emphasis is unusual. It is not primarily a story about disbelief in the abstract; it is a story about a civilization's *technical achievement* and its misuse.

> *"And [We destroyed] Thamūd, who carved out the rock in the valley."* (Qur'an 89:9)

> *"And you carve out houses in the mountains with skill."* (Qur'an 26:149)

> *"And remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the land: you take palaces on its plains and carve the mountains into houses."* (Qur'an 7:74)

Their prophet Ṣāliḥ came to them with a she-camel as a sign, and they hamstrung her:

> *"Thamūd denied, through its transgression, when the most wretched of them rose up, and the Messenger of God said to them: 'the she-camel of God, and her drinking.' But they denied him and hamstrung her, and their Lord destroyed them for their sin and levelled them."* (Qur'an 91:11–14)

Note that Sūrah al-Shams — the sūrah immediately preceding al-Burūj in the traditional order of revelation — ends with exactly this story. The audience had just received the account of Thamūd's destruction, and al-Burūj names them again as one of its two exhibits.

Their end came by the cry and the earthquake:

> *"And the earthquake seized them, and they became within their homes motionless corpses."* (Qur'an 7:78)

> *"So the cry seized them, and We made them like the dry twigs of a cattle-pen."* (Qur'an 54:31)

The houses they carved into the rock — engineered to last forever, and they did — became their tombs. The architecture outlived the civilization and survives today as a warning site.

**The Prophetic Instruction at Al-Ḥijr**

The ruins were not a memory for the first Muslims; they were a location, and the Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions how to behave inside it. During the expedition to Tabūk the army passed al-Ḥijr. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the incident:

> *"When the people passed by al-Ḥijr they drew water from its wells and kneaded dough with it. The Messenger of God ﷺ ordered them to pour out the water they had drawn and to feed the dough to the camels; and he ordered them to take water from the well that the she-camel used to drink from."* (al-Bukhārī 3379; Muslim 2980)

And in the companion narration:

> *"Do not enter upon these people who were punished, except while weeping; and if you cannot weep, then make yourselves weep — lest what befell them befall you."* (al-Bukhārī 3378)

Two instructions, both profound. Do not use the infrastructure of a destroyed people as though nothing happened there — take the water, but be conscious of where you are. And do not visit a place of punishment as a tourist: enter grieving, or work to grieve, because the alternative is to become the kind of person whom such places do not move.

The sūrah's question — *has the report of the hosts come to you?* — is asking the reader to be one of those who weep rather than one of those who photograph. The difference between the two is the difference between Thamūd's neighbours who learned and Thamūd's neighbours who did not.

**Why Not ʿĀd, or Noah's People, or Lot's?**

The Qur'an destroys many nations and names them often. The selection here is purposeful.

Pharaoh is the archetype of the *state* that claims divinity — the administrative apparatus, the court, the magicians, the propaganda, the decree, the personal claim to lordship. Thamūd is the archetype of the *civilization* that trusts its engineering — the carved mountains, the plains' palaces, the technical mastery that produced a false sense of permanence.

Between them they cover the two sources of human confidence: political power and material achievement. The pit-diggers had both — a king with authority and men who could dig trenches, saw bodies, and coordinate a public spectacle. The two named hosts are therefore not random samples; they are the same case in two earlier forms.

Sūrah al-Fajr makes the same selection with a third added: *"Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with ʿĀd — Iram of the pillars, the like of which was never created in the lands — and Thamūd, who carved the rock in the valley, and Pharaoh of the stakes?"* (89:6–10). The three together are the Qur'an's standard exhibit list for destroyed greatness.

**The Message to the Men Who Lit the Fire**

Read in its historical moment, verse 18 is a message to the Quraysh, and it is short enough to be counted.

Pharaoh had an empire, a river, an army, a divine claim, and a court of magicians. Thamūd had cities carved into mountains. The Quraysh had a city of mud-brick and leather, a temple, three hundred idols, and a few hundred fighting men. If the first two were destroyed for opposing a prophet, what calculation could the third be making?

The same verse is a message to the believers, and it is equally short: your enemies are smaller than Pharaoh. The people who were torturing them in Makkah — a few chiefs of a merchant town — were being compared, by God, to the two greatest concentrations of power the region had ever known, and both had been swept away for doing precisely what the Quraysh were doing.

That comparison is the source of the community's endurance, and it is available to every subsequent generation of the persecuted. It does not promise timing. It promises a pattern, and the pattern has not broken in fourteen centuries: hosts assemble, trenches are dug, the believers are made to look foolish for a season, and then the wind comes — *wa junūdan lam tarawhā*.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:19

> **Yet those who disbelieve are in denial.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bal: The Word That Turns the Page**

*Bal alladhīna kafarū fī takdhīb* — "Nay, rather: those who disbelieve are in a state of denial."

The verse opens with *bal*, a particle whose function the Arabic grammarians call *iḍrāb* — striking away what preceded and turning to something new. It is the word you use when you set aside the previous topic as insufficient: "no — rather." After the two exhibits of verse 18, the sūrah turns from history back to the present, and *bal* marks the turn with a snap.

The structure of the argument is worth stating plainly, because it is a complete syllogism in three verses:

- v. 17: Have you heard the report of the hosts?
- v. 18: Pharaoh and Thamūd? — *the evidence*
- v. 19: Yet those who disbelieve are in denial. — *the verdict on those who refuse the evidence*

The exhibits were produced. The response to the exhibits is recorded. And the response is not an answer; it is a state.

**Takdhīb: Not Doubt, but Repeated Refusal**

The word is *takdhīb*, the intensive verbal noun of *kadhdhaba*, the second form of the root *k-dh-b* (lying). The second form in Arabic takes a simple meaning and intensifies or makes it transitive: *kadhaba* is "he lied"; *kadhdhaba* is "he gave the lie to," "he declared something false," and in its noun form, sustained and repeated falsification.

*Takdhīb* is therefore not the absence of belief. It is an *activity*. It is what a person does when confronted with something he has decided will not be accepted: he denies it, and he keeps denying it, and the denial becomes his condition.

The Qur'an distinguishes this state carefully from doubt (*shakk*), hesitation (*rayb*), ignorance (*jahl*), and even from plain disbelief (*kufr*). Doubt is a search; it moves; it can end. Takdhīb is a position; it does not move; it entrenches.

And the preposition matters: *fī takdhīb* — "*in* denial." Being *in* a state, in Arabic as in English, means being inside it, surrounded by it, seeing from within it. The same construction is used of doubt (*wa kānū fī raybin yataraddadūn*, "they wandered in doubt," 9:45) and of error. The denier is not a person who holds a denial among other beliefs; he is a person whose belief-system is organized around the denial.

Again the nominal sentence does its characteristic work: *alladhīna kafarū fī takdhīb* states a settled condition, not a passing act. They *are* in denial, continuously, as verse 6's persecutors *were* sitting, continuously. The Qur'an describes the persecutors' whole existence in two nominal sentences: seated, and denying.

**Denial With Certainty Inside**

The Qur'an's most searching statement about the psychology of *takdhīb* is not in this sūrah but in Sūrah al-Naml, where it describes Pharaoh's court receiving Moses' signs:

> *"And they denied them (jaḥadū bihā) unjustly and arrogantly, while their souls were certain of them. So see how was the end of the corrupters."* (Qur'an 27:14)

*While their souls were certain of them.* The denial was not produced by a lack of evidence. It was produced by injustice (*ẓulman*) and arrogance (*ʿuluwwan*) — two moral conditions — operating on a mind that already knew.

The same diagnosis appears in the direct address to the Prophet ﷺ:

> *"Indeed We know that what they say grieves you; but they do not call you a liar — rather, the wrongdoers deny the signs of God."* (Qur'an 6:33)

They did not consider him a liar. Every Meccan called him *al-Ṣādiq al-Amīn* — the truthful, the trustworthy — before the revelation and after it. They deposited their valuables with him; when he migrated, he left Ali behind to return their trusts. The verse states the diagnosis without softening: the problem was never his credibility. The problem was what accepting him would cost them.

And the Qur'an puts that cost into the mouths of the deniers themselves:

> *"And they said: why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from one of the two cities?"* (Qur'an 43:31)

> *"Is the reminder sent down to him from among us?"* (Qur'an 38:8)

> *"And the chiefs of his people who disbelieved said: we see you only as a human being like us, and we see that none follow you except the lowest of us, at first opinion."* (Qur'an 11:27)

Read those verses and the mechanism of *takdhīb* is fully exposed. It is not intellectual. It is a calculation about status: if this is true, then the hierarchy I sit at the top of is false, my ancestors were fools, my income from the pilgrimage is illegitimate, and the slaves and the poor who followed this man are my equals. The cost of the truth exceeded the appetite for it, so the truth was denied — and denied not once but continuously, *fī takdhīb*, as a settled condition.

**Denial Is Expensive**

There is a practical law here, and it applies far beyond theology.

Denial requires maintenance. A person who refuses to believe something that is visibly true must generate a continuous supply of justifications, avoidances, and counter-narratives, and he must recruit other people to help him. He must not look at the evidence. He must change the subject. He must discredit the person who keeps raising it. He must arrange his life so that the fact never appears in front of him.

The sūrah has already supplied the image for this, three verses earlier: *al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd* — the fire with fuel. A denial, like a fire, goes out if it is not fed. That is why denials are so often communal: a group can take turns supplying the wood. That is why a lone dissenter in a family, an organization, or a movement is so threatening — he is not offering a counter-argument, he is refusing to bring fuel, and everyone present knows that if the supply stops, the fire dies.

The ordinary instances are familiar to every adult. The marriage in which both partners have stopped saying the sentence that would end it. The addiction managed by not counting. The business failing while the accounts go unopened. The illness with symptoms that are described as stress for eleven months. In each case there is a truth the person has the capacity to know and a state he is *in*: *fī takdhīb*. And in each case the maintenance cost rises with time, until the day it becomes unpayable and the truth arrives as catastrophe rather than information.

Verse 19's use of the present-state construction is a warning about exactly this: denial is not a moment of decision but a condition of residence, and people who live in it lose the ability to notice the door.

**Why the Qur'an Names Them Here**

The sūrah has just given two exhibits of destroyed hosts. Naming the deniers' state immediately afterwards performs a specific function: it tells the believers that the failure of the exhibits to convince anyone is not evidence against the exhibits.

This is a recurring pain for the person who argues with a hostile audience. You produce the evidence — historical, textual, rational — and the audience is unmoved, and you begin to suspect your evidence. Verse 19 interrupts that inference. It states that the audience is *in denial*, which means the location of the problem has been correctly identified: not in the argument, but in the arguer.

The Qur'an elsewhere consoles the Prophet ﷺ in the same way, and the consolations are almost clinical in their precision:

> *"And if it is hard upon you their turning away — then if you are able to seek a tunnel in the earth or a ladder in the sky to bring them a sign, then do so. Had God willed, He would have gathered them upon guidance; so be not among the ignorant."* (Qur'an 6:35)

> *"Indeed you do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills; and He is most knowing of those who are guided."* (Qur'an 28:56)

The duty of the believer is delivery, not conversion. *Fa-innamā ʿalayka al-balāgh* — "upon you is only the conveyance" (42:48). The result belongs to a will that has not delegated it to you, and a heart in *takdhīb* is not a puzzle to be solved by a better argument.

**The One Exit**

The verse is a diagnosis, and the sūrah has already prescribed the cure, eleven verses earlier: *thumma lam yatūbū* — "then they do not repent" (v. 10). The denial of verse 19 is precisely the state of the person who does not repent.

And the exit is available, which is why the verse is stated as a condition rather than a verdict. History records people who came out of it. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb left his house with a sword intending to kill the Prophet ﷺ and returned a Muslim within the hour. Abū Sufyān, who led the confederate hosts against Madinah, entered Makkah's conquest as a man whose house was a sanctuary. Wahshī, who had killed Ḥamzah, came and testified and was accepted. The whole city of Ṭāʾif, which had stoned the Prophet ﷺ until his sandals filled with blood, sent a delegation within a decade.

*Takdhīb* is a place people live, and people leave places. But the leaving is not achieved by accumulating more evidence for someone who is not evidence-limited. It is achieved when the cost of the truth becomes bearable, or when the cost of the lie finally exceeds it — and both are matters of the heart, which is why the Qur'an's appeal is always to the heart first: *afa lā tatafakkarūn*, *afa lā taʿqilūn*, *afalā tubṣirūn*. Will you not think? Will you not reason? Will you not see?

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:20

> **And God is behind them, All-Encompassing.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Min Warāʾihim: The Direction That Is Every Direction**

*Wa Allāhu min warāʾihim muḥīṭ* — "And God is behind them, All-Encompassing."

Four words in Arabic, and the first phrase is a small masterpiece of ambiguity.

*Warāʾ* means "behind." But the lexicographers and the exegetes — al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī — note a peculiarity of the word: it denotes what is concealed from you, whether it lies behind you or ahead of you, because in both cases it is outside your field of vision. The Qur'an itself uses it in the forward sense, in the story of Moses and the ship:

> *"And behind them (wa kāna warāʾahum) was a king who seized every ship by force."* (Qur'an 18:79)

The king was not following them; he was *ahead*, at their destination, waiting for them to arrive. The word describes him as *warāʾahum* because he was hidden from their sight and lay on their path.

Read with that in mind, verse 20 becomes inescapable. God is behind the deniers — in the record of what they have already done, which they cannot alter. God is ahead of them — at the destination they are walking toward, which they cannot avoid. And the second word removes any remaining gap.

**Muḥīṭ: The Wall Around**

*Muḥīṭ* is the active participle of *aḥāṭa*, from the root *ḥ-w-ṭ* — a wall, an enclosure, that which surrounds a thing on every side. A garden enclosed by a wall is *maḥīṭ*. To be *muḥīṭ* by something is to be unable to find an edge.

The Qur'an uses the word of God repeatedly, and the pattern of usage is consistent:

> *"And God encompasses the disbelievers (wa Allāhu muḥīṭun bi'l-kāfirīn)."* (Qur'an 2:19)

> *"They conceal from the people what they cannot conceal from God... and God encompasses what they do."* (Qur'an 4:108)

> *"And God encompasses what they do."* (Qur'an 8:47)

> *"Unto God belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth... and God encompasses all things (wa kāna Allāhu bi-kulli shayʾin muḥīṭā)."* (Qur'an 4:126)

> *"Behold, He encompasses all things (alā innahu bi-kulli shayʾin muḥīṭ)."* (Qur'an 41:54)

> *"...and that God has encompassed all things in knowledge (wa anna Allāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā)."* (Qur'an 65:12)

The last of these identifies what the encompassing consists of: knowledge. The commentators explain *muḥīṭ* as encompassing by knowledge, by power, and by disposal — not by physical location. God is not spatially around creatures the way a wall is around a garden; He is above His Throne as He has described Himself, and His knowledge and authority reach everything without exception. Ibn Taymiyyah is emphatic on this point, insisting that the verses of encompassing describe attributes of knowledge and power, and that reading them spatially is the error of likening God to creation.

**The Encircler Is Encircled**

The verse is aimed at men who believed they had surrounded someone else.

That is the essence of the pit. The trench was dug at the mouths of the roads so that there was no route out. The victim brought to its edge was enclosed: soldiers on either side, the king's decree in front of him, the fire below him. The whole design was an architecture of *iḥāṭah* — encirclement — erected by men who enjoyed the sensation of being the ones who surround.

Verse 20 inverts the geometry in four words. The ones doing the surrounding are surrounded. The wall they built around the believers is inside a wall built around them, and the outer wall belongs to God.

The Qur'an has already shown this inversion in a cave, in the same Makkan period, and the parallel is exact:

> *"If you do not aid him — God has already aided him, when those who disbelieved drove him out, the second of two, when they were in the cave, and he said to his companion: do not grieve, indeed God is with us. And God sent down His tranquility upon him and supported him with hosts you did not see."* (Qur'an 9:40)

Abū Bakr and the Prophet ﷺ were in a cave with pursuers standing above them, so close that one of them said: "if any of them looks down at his feet, he will see us." They were, by every military calculation, encircled and finished. The verse records what the encircled man said: *lā taḥzan, inna Allāha maʿanā* — do not grieve, God is with us. And it records what the encirclers did not know: hosts they could not see, and a Companion they could not surround.

A believer at the edge of a trench and a believer on the floor of a cave are in the same position, and verse 20 is the description of that position from above.

**No Exit: The Verse as Warning**

For the deniers of verse 19, the verse is a statement about the impossibility of escape, and the Qur'an says as much in words:

> *"And do not think God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do; He only defers them to a Day when eyes will stare in horror."* (Qur'an 14:42)

> *"Do they not see that We have made the Sanctuary secure, while people are snatched away all around them?"* (Qur'an 29:67)

The modern attempts at escape are more sophisticated than the ancient ones, and no more effective. A person can change cities, change names, change careers, delete accounts, start again in a country where nobody knows him. He can construct an identity from scratch, curate it, and present it to strangers. He can, with enough money and enough effort, become almost unrecognizable to his own past.

And verse 20 says: *min warāʾihim muḥīṭ*. The enclosure is not a location, so it cannot be left. You cannot travel outside a knowledge that has no geography. The man who reinvents himself does not escape his record; he merely stops being able to read it — while the One who encompasses it continues to hold every version, including the ones he deleted.

There is a form of this that is commoner and sadder than the criminal's flight: the person who escapes from himself into noise. Continuous entertainment, continuous work, continuous social performance, continuous consumption of other people's lives — all of it organized around not being alone for four minutes with what he knows. The enclosure he is running inside is *muḥīṭ* too, and the running is what keeps him from noticing that the One who surrounds him is described in this very sūrah as *al-Ghafūr al-Wadūd*.

**The Verse as Comfort**

Because the same sentence that traps the tyrant shelters the believer.

*Muḥīṭ* is the attribute of a God who is not absent from any place you are sent. Prison, hospital, exile, the hostile workplace, the room where the diagnosis is delivered, the trench at the mouth of the road — in each of them the believer is inside God's knowledge, which means he is not outside God's care. The Qur'an states the same truth in the language of accompaniment:

> *"He is with you wherever you are, and God is Seeing of what you do."* (Qur'an 57:4)

> *"There is no private conversation of three but that He is the fourth of them, nor of five but that He is the sixth... He is with them wherever they are."* (Qur'an 58:7)

The companionship is not sentimental; it is jurisdictional. He is with you in the sense that nothing is happening to you outside His authority, and His authority is described in verse 16 as *faʿʿālun limā yurīd* — the doing of whatsoever He wills. The pit was permitted; it was not approved; it was witnessed; it was recorded; and it will be answered.

**Why This Verse Comes After the Hosts**

The placement is the argument.

Verse 17–18 produced the exhibits: Pharaoh and Thamūd. Verse 19 recorded the response: denial. Verse 20 supplies the reason denial is futile — not by argument but by position. They cannot escape the conclusion because they cannot escape the One who holds it.

And so the sūrah's middle section closes with the tyrant completely enclosed: enclosed by history behind him (Pharaoh and Thamūd, whose fate is his), enclosed by denial around him (which will not save him), enclosed by God above and before and after him. Every direction he looks, there is a wall.

Verse 12 already told him what the walls are for: *inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd* — the seizing is severe, and it is sudden, and it comes to people who feel safe. Verse 20 tells him why he feels safe and is not: he has mistaken the deferral for the absence of the encirclement. The wind, the hosts he cannot see, the wall he cannot find — all of it is already in place.

The last two verses will now turn away from the enclosed men entirely, and describe the thing they were trying to destroy and could not reach: a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:21

> **Nay, it is a glorious Quran,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Bal: The Denial Is Set Aside**

*Bal huwa Qurʾānun majīd* — "Nay, rather: it is a glorious Qur'an."

This is the second *bal* in the sūrah, and the two work together as a hinge. The first, in verse 19, set aside the historical exhibits and turned to the deniers: *nay, rather — those who disbelieve are in denial.* The second sets aside the deniers themselves and turns to what they are denying: *nay, rather — it is a glorious Qur'an.*

The particle is doing exactly what it always does in Arabic: it strikes away the previous statement as inadequate and substitutes something decisive. But here the striking-away is directed not at an argument but at a *state of mind*. Verse 19 diagnosed the condition — men living inside a refusal. Verse 21 declines to argue with the condition. It simply places the object in front of it, fully named, in its own majesty.

This is a method worth learning. When someone is in *takdhīb*, more evidence rarely helps; the problem is not informational. What sometimes helps is to stop responding to the denial and to start describing the thing denied, accurately and without reference to the denier. The Qur'an models the move: it does not spend verse 21 refuting anyone. It says what the Book is.

**Majīd: The Same Word Used of God and His Throne**

*Majīd* is from *m-j-d*, and verse 15 of this same sūrah has already used it — of God, or of His Throne, depending on the reading: *Dhū al-ʿarshi al-Majīd*.

The repetition is not incidental; it is a statement about the relationship between the Book and its Speaker. Glory is one reality with two locations here: the One who possesses the Throne is glorious, and the speech that comes from Him is glorious. The Qur'an swears by the same attribute at the head of another sūrah:

> *"Qāf. By the glorious Qur'an (wa'l-Qurʾān al-majīd)."* (Qur'an 50:1)

And it pairs the concept with the Name that al-Burūj used eight verses earlier, in the angels' words to the household of Abraham:

> *"...indeed He is Praised, Glorious (Ḥamīdun Majīd)."* (Qur'an 11:73)

*Al-Ḥamīd* appears at 85:8 — the Name attached to the God in whom the people of the pit believed. *Al-Majīd* appears at 85:15 and 85:21. The sūrah's closing verses are woven from the same vocabulary as its middle ones, which is how the Qur'an makes a twenty-two-verse chapter feel like a single sealed object.

Al-Qurṭubī explains that *majīd* indicates the utmost in nobility, magnanimity, and grace — a glory that is generous rather than merely impressive. Applied to a book, it means that the Book's excellence is not only aesthetic or intellectual; it gives. It confers honour on whoever carries it, as the Qur'an says of the community:

> *"And indeed it is a reminder for you and for your people, and you will be questioned."* (Qur'an 43:44)

**The Reading That Makes It a Doctrine**

The source commentary notes that some exegetes read *majīd* here as indicating the *uncreatedness* of the Qur'an, and the reasoning deserves to be stated carefully, because it produced one of the defining crises of Islamic intellectual history.

The argument runs: glory (*majd*) of this absolute degree is not an attribute of created things. Every created entity is contingent, limited, and originated at a point in time; to describe speech as *majīd* in the superlative, and to locate it — as verse 22 immediately does — in a register that exists with God, is to describe something whose mode of existence is God's own, not the creature's. Since speech is an attribute of the speaker, and God's attributes are not created things distinct from Him, the Qur'an is His uncreated speech.

Ibn Taymiyyah articulated the Sunni position in its classic form: the Qur'an is the speech of God, uncreated — from Him it originated and to Him it returns. The letters, the sounds, the paper, and the ink are created; the speech they carry is not. This was the position for which Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal was imprisoned and flogged across three caliphates during the *miḥnah* of the early third/ninth century, when the state attempted to impose the Muʿtazilite doctrine that the Qur'an was created. He refused, on the ground that the doctrine was not transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ or the Companions, and his refusal shaped Sunni creed permanently.

It is a striking fact that the sūrah whose central story is a state torturing people over a statement of belief became, twelve centuries later, the intellectual property of a man being tortured by a state over a statement of belief. The verse had not changed. The pit had.

**Qurʾān: A Name That Describes a Method**

The word *Qurʾān* is a verbal noun from *q-r-ʾ* — to read, to recite, to gather together. Its primary sense is "a recitation," or "that which is gathered."

The name is a declaration of the text's mode of existence. The Qur'an did not first exist as a book on a shelf; it existed as a voice in a cave, repeated aloud, memorized, recited in prayer, taught mouth to ear. Writing followed recitation, and recitation has never stopped. That is why the Book's central act of transmission is not publication but *ḥifẓ* — memorization — and why the Prophet ﷺ attached excellence to it:

> *"The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān)

Verse 21's assertion that this Qur'an is *majīd* is therefore an assertion about something living: a recitation carried in the chests of people, in every generation, in the same language, with an unbroken chain back to the mouth that first received it.

**What Glory Looks Like When Tested**

A claim of glory is easy to make and hard to verify. The verification is historical, and it is available.

Fourteen centuries after its revelation, the Qur'an is recited today in the consonantal text standardized under the third Caliph ʿUthmān, in the language it was revealed in, by people who learned it from teachers who learned it from teachers, in an unbroken chain of mass transmission (*tawātur*) that no scholar of any tradition has seriously broken. Tens of millions of people have the entire text — some 6,236 verses — memorized, including children. Every word is prayed aloud five times daily across the planet. Its authorized variant readings, the *qirāʾāt*, were transmitted with the same care as its base text, so that even its vocalization options are documented rather than lost.

Compare this with the fate of comparable ancient texts, whose manuscripts exist in fragments, whose languages died, whose transmission passed through a handful of copyists, and whose critical editions are built from a dozen partial witnesses. The Qur'an's textual history is not merely better; it is in a different category.

The verse anticipates this and states it as a doctrinal claim:

> *"Falsehood does not approach it from before it or from behind it; a revelation from the Wise, the Praised (tanzīlun min Ḥakīmin Ḥamīd)."* (Qur'an 41:42)

Note two things in that verse. First, the direction-language — *min bayni yadayhi wa lā min khalfih*, from before it and from behind it — which echoes verse 20 of this sūrah (*min warāʾihim muḥīṭ*). The deniers are enclosed from behind and before; the Book is protected from behind and before. Second, the closing Name: *Ḥamīd* — the same Name attached to God in verse 8 of this sūrah. The Book is a revelation from the Praised, and it is glorious because its Source is glorious.

And God's guarantee of that protection is stated without qualification:

> *"Indeed it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are its Preserver."* (Qur'an 15:9)

**The Glory That Broke Through the Denial**

Verse 19 said the disbelievers are in denial. Verse 21 says the Book is glorious. The biography of the first generation shows what happens when those two statements meet, and it is not what a reader might expect.

The sīrah literature preserves a famous incident recorded by Ibn Isḥāq and cited by Ibn Kathīr: three chiefs of Quraysh — Abū Jahl, Abū Sufyān, and al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq — each secretly went at night to listen to the Prophet ﷺ reciting the Qur'an in his prayer, each unaware of the other two, each returning night after night. On the third night they discovered one another and made a pact not to return, because, as they put it, the common people might see them. They did not return; but they had gone, repeatedly, in spite of themselves.

The report is transmitted in the biographical literature rather than in the ḥadīth collections, and it is cited by the exegetes precisely to illustrate verses like this one. Its point is not historical curiosity. It is that *majd* exerts a force that denial cannot fully block: men whose political interest required them to reject the Book could not stay away from hearing it. The glory was in the sound.

The same force is documented in conversions. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb went out with a sword to kill the Prophet ﷺ, was diverted to his sister's house, heard Sūrah Ṭā Hā being recited from a leaf, and said the words that changed the history of Islam: *show me what you have.* Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib recited the opening of Sūrah Maryam to the Negus of Abyssinia, and the Christian king wept until his beard was wet, and the bishops wept over their copies, and he said: "This and what Moses brought come from one lamp." Quraysh's own poet-critics admitted it — they had no category for the text, having tried poetry, soothsaying, and madness, and found it none of them.

That is the empirical content of *majīd*. Not an argument that compels assent — verse 19 records that the assent was refused — but a quality that compels attention, and attention that keeps returning.

**The Answer to the Pit**

Read verse 21 where the sūrah places it and its function is unmistakable.

Verses 4–10 described an attempt at erasure: a community put into a trench and burned so that its faith would end with its bodies. Verses 12–18 described the power that answers such attempts. Verses 19–20 described the deniers as enclosed and unable to escape. And verse 21 turns to the object of the whole affair — the revelation the persecutors were trying to extinguish — and announces that it is *glorious*.

The men in the trench died. Their killers died. The king's name is uncertain. The city's name is disputed. The empire that authorized it is gone. And the chapter that records it is recited in the noon and afternoon prayers of a community that now numbers close to two billion, in the same Arabic, in the same words, by people who have never heard of the king.

That is the verdict of verse 21, and it does not require an adjective to be impressive. But the verse supplies the adjective anyway: *majīd* — noble, generous, glorious, giving honour to whoever carries it. The pit took the believers' bodies and gave them their name for eternity. The Book took their story and made it part of the prayer of the world.

## Sūrah al-Burūj 85:22

> **upon a Preserved Tablet.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lawḥ and Maḥfūẓ: The Board and the Guard**

*Fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ* — "upon a Preserved Tablet."

*Lawḥ* is an ordinary Arabic word: a board, a plank, a flat piece of wood or stone prepared for writing. It is the same word the Qur'an uses for the timbers of Noah's vessel:

> *"And We carried him upon a thing of planks and nails (dhāti alwāḥin wa dusur)."* (Qur'an 54:13)

The choice of an everyday, physical word for a heavenly reality is characteristic. The Qur'an consistently describes the unseen in the vocabulary of the seen, so that the meaning is grasped while the mode is left to God: a *tablet*, a *book*, a *register*, a *pen*, *pages* — the language of a document, applied to a reality beyond documents.

*Maḥfūẓ* is the passive participle of *ḥ-f-ẓ*, the root of guarding, protecting, keeping safe, and preserving against loss or tampering. A *ḥāfiẓ* is one who guards; something *maḥfūẓ* is a thing that has been guarded. The Qur'an uses the same root for its own preservation:

> *"Indeed it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are its Preserver (la-ḥāfiẓūn)."* (Qur'an 15:9)

There is also a transmitted reading — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and discussed by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī — that vocalizes the word in the nominative, *maḥfūẓun*, so that it describes not the Tablet but the Qur'an itself: "a glorious Qur'an, preserved." The Ḥafs reading in general use keeps it in the genitive, describing the Tablet. Both are affirmed, and both are true: the Tablet is guarded, and what is written on it is guarded.

**What Is on the Tablet**

The exegetes gather the Qur'an's statements about this register, and the picture is consistent.

> *"And there is nothing hidden in the heaven or the earth except that it is in a clear register (kitābin mubīn)."* (Qur'an 27:75)

> *"No calamity befalls the earth, nor yourselves, except that it is in a register before We bring it into being; indeed that is easy for God."* (Qur'an 57:22)

> *"And not absent from your Lord is the weight of an atom in the earth or in the heaven, nor anything smaller than that or greater, except that it is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 10:61)

And God names it as the source of the revealed Books:

> *"God effaces what He wills and establishes, and with Him is the Mother of the Book (umm al-kitāb)."* (Qur'an 13:39)

> *"And indeed it is in the Mother of the Book with Us, exalted and wise."* (Qur'an 43:4)

> *"Indeed, it is a noble Qur'an, in a hidden Book (kitābin maknūn) that none touch except the purified."* (Qur'an 56:77–79)

> *"[It is] in honoured pages, exalted and purified, in the hands of scribes — noble and righteous."* (Qur'an 80:13–16)

Al-Rāzī records the range of identifications, as the source commentary notes: some held that the "hidden Book" of 56:78 and the Preserved Tablet are the same reality; others described the Tablet as a book recited by the angels in the presence of God; others related it to the Mother of the Book of 3:7, 13:39 and 43:4, understood as the source from which the Qur'an and all earlier revelations derive.

The transmitted descriptions of the Tablet in the exegetical literature are of a kind that requires scholarly honesty. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record reports describing it as a tablet of white pearl, extending the distance between heaven and earth and the width between east and west, positioned in relation to the Throne. Such reports about the unseen come through chains of varying strength, and the responsible position — the one the sober scholars themselves take — is to affirm what the Qur'an states plainly, namely that there is a preserved written register with God in which His knowledge and decree are established, and to leave its physical description where the revelation left it: undescribed in detail.

**The Pen Wrote First**

The Prophet ﷺ described the writing itself in reports of the highest authenticity. Al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd record from ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit:

> *"The first thing God created was the Pen. He said to it: Write. It said: My Lord, what shall I write? He said: Write the decree of everything until the Hour is established."* (al-Tirmidhī 2155; Abū Dāwūd 4700; graded ṣaḥīḥ)

And Muslim records from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr:

> *"God ordained the measures of His creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth — and His Throne was upon the water."* (Muslim 2653)

These two reports, taken with verse 22, produce the Islamic doctrine of the decree (*al-qadar*) in its essentials: everything that occurs was written before it occurred; the writing is complete; and the pens, as the Prophet ﷺ told Ibn ʿAbbās, *"have been lifted and the pages have dried"* (al-Tirmidhī 2516).

The relation of this to human freedom is the same relation the sūrah already stated in verse 16, and it must not be re-litigated as though the Qur'an were confused about it. The writing does not compel the choice; it records it. God's knowledge of what a person will choose is not the cause of his choosing it, any more than your knowledge that your friend will order the same dish he has ordered for twenty years causes him to order it. The difference is that God's knowledge is complete and prior, and its completeness is what makes the register possible.

**Al-Qushayrī's Reading: The Tablet in the Chest**

The source commentary records a beautiful interpretation from al-Qushayrī, who relates the Preserved Tablet to a verse in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt:

> *"Nay, it is clear signs in the breasts of those who have been given knowledge."* (Qur'an 29:49)

The Qur'an, he holds, is preserved in the hearts of true believers. The Tablet above is the archetype; the chest of the ḥāfiẓ below is its earthly counterpart.

The reading is not allegorical licence; it is a description of a fact. Every generation of Muslims has contained people — hundreds of thousands of them, in every century, in every region — who carry the entire text in memory, letter for letter, with the rules of its recitation, from the age of childhood. The chain is unbroken. A memorizer today recites to a teacher who recited to a teacher who recited to a teacher, back through Baghdad, Cairo, Kufa, Madinah, and Makkah, to the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ.

When the Mongols destroyed Baghdad's libraries in 656/1258, the books burned and the Qur'an did not, because the Qur'an was not in the books. When manuscripts were lost, the text was not lost. The preservation promised in 15:9 was executed through human chests, exactly as al-Qushayrī read verse 22 to mean.

**The Sūrah's Last Word**

It matters what the final word of a chapter is, and this one is *maḥfūẓ* — preserved.

Look at the arc the twenty-two verses have travelled. Verse 1: the sky and its towers. Verse 2: the Promised Day. Verse 3: the witness and the witnessed. Verses 4–9: a trench, a fueled fire, men sitting, men watching, a charge that was nothing but faith, and a God who is Witness over all things. Verse 10: two punishments for those who do not repent. Verse 11: Gardens with rivers flowing beneath, the great triumph. Verses 12–16: severe seizing, origination and return, the Forgiving and the Loving, the Possessor of the glorious Throne, the Doer of whatsoever He wills. Verses 17–20: the hosts, Pharaoh and Thamūd, the deniers, and a God behind them, encompassing. Verses 21–22: a glorious Qur'an, upon a Preserved Tablet.

The chapter ends where it began — in permanence. The first verse swore by the standing towers of the sky; the last verse describes a standing record above the sky. Between the two stands the pit: a hole in the ground, dug by men who believed that putting bodies into it would end a matter.

Everything the pit was for has failed. The faith was not extinguished; it spread to the king's own city. The victims were not erased; they were named *al-muʾminīn* forever. The crime was not concealed; it was witnessed by God, recorded, and read aloud in the prayers of the world. The perpetrators were not vindicated; they were cursed in the first word of the case. And the message they tried to burn is on a Tablet that no fire reaches, in a Book that no hand alters, in chests that have carried it without interruption for fourteen hundred years.

That is why the sūrah is recited at noon and in the afternoon, in the flat hours, by tired people. It is medicine for the specific despair that comes from watching injustice appear to work.

**Two Uses of the Tablet**

The verse leaves every reader with one of two positions, and the choice between them is the practical content of the commentary.

For the person who is being wronged, *fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ* means: it is written down, and it cannot be edited. No statute of limitations runs on it; no powerful party can suppress it; no revision of history can reach it; no settlement can close it without your signature. You do not need to prove your case to anyone who is not willing to hear it. The file exists, it is complete, it is guarded, and the hearing date was fixed before you were born.

For the person who is doing wrong — and everyone is, in some measure — the same verse means: it is written down, and it cannot be edited. The same sentence, the opposite comfort. There is an entry for you as well, and its contents are not flattering, and no amount of subsequent achievement will delete it.

Between those two positions stands the exit the sūrah has already named twice: *thumma lam yatūbū* (v. 10) and *al-Ghafūr al-Wadūd* (v. 14). The Tablet records, and the Forgiving covers. The record is preserved; the covering is available; and the door is open until the death rattle begins.

**A Final Word on What Is Preserved**

The most immediate application of verse 22 is the one al-Qushayrī pointed to, and it is available to every reader today.

There is a Tablet above, and there is the possibility of a tablet in the chest. The tradition has always treated memorization of the Qur'an — even a portion of it — as one of the highest acts of worship, precisely because it places the preserved Book in a preserved place. The Qur'an itself makes the invitation four times over:

> *"And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance; so is there any who will remember?"* (Qur'an 54:17)

The Prophet ﷺ described the person with nothing of the Qur'an in him as a ruined house (al-Tirmidhī 2913, from Ibn ʿAbbās, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ).

Whatever else a reader takes from Sūrah al-Burūj, this is the verse's own invitation: to become one of the places where the glory is kept. The pit has been dug many times since Najrān and will be dug again. The fire needs fuel and men will keep bringing it. And in every generation the answer has been the same — a community that carries the Book in its chest, that recites it at noon and in the afternoon, that remembers the people of the trench, and that believes, without being able to prove it to anyone who refuses to look, that it is written down, and preserved, and will be read aloud on the Day that was promised.

*Bal huwa Qurʾānun majīd. Fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ.*
