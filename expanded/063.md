# Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn (Chapter 63) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter That Names the Double Life**

Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn, "The Hypocrites," is a Madinan chapter of eleven verses. It takes its name from the people it addresses in its first eight verses: those who attested to the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ with their tongues, swore fealty to him, and sat in his mosque, while continuing to ally themselves with his enemies and to withhold their hearts from the faith they publicly claimed. The Arabic *munāfiq* is formed from *nafaq*, the burrow of the desert jerboa — a tunnel with two mouths, so that the animal can declare one exit closed while slipping out the other. The image is not ornamental. It is the whole anatomy of the condition this sūrah diagnoses: a person who maintains two openings to his profession, one facing the believers and one facing their opponents, and who moves through whichever mouth the hour requires.

The sūrah can be read as a deliberate counterpart to the chapter immediately before it. Sūrah al-Jumuʿah addresses the believers, calling them to the Friday prayer and warning them not to leave the remembrance of God for commerce. Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn addresses the hypocrites, exposing their oaths, their speech, their appearance, and their plots, and then turns, in its last three verses, to the believers with a warning of a more interior kind: that property and children, if they divert the heart from God, become a quieter form of the same disease. During the Friday congregational prayer the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have frequently recited these two sūrahs together — al-Jumuʿah in the first cycle as encouragement for the believers, and al-Munāfiqūn in the second as a rebuke to the hypocrites. The pairing is pedagogical. One chapter builds; the other unmasks. One summons the congregation to presence; the other reminds the same congregation that presence of the body is not presence of the heart.

**Why a Whole Chapter for a Hidden Vice**

Hypocrisy is, by definition, hard to prosecute in a courtroom of men. Open disbelief announces itself. Sincere faith, though it may falter, has a direction. Hypocrisy occupies the space between: it wears the uniform of the first community while serving the interests of the second. In Makkah, when Islam cost blood and honor and offered no worldly prize, almost no one pretended to it. In Madinah, after the Hijrah, Islam became the city's ascendant order — and pretense arrived with the prize. A faction, led in the tradition's memory by ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy ibn Salūl, a man who had expected to be crowned king of Yathrib before the Prophet's ﷺ arrival displaced that fortune, entered the religion outwardly while nursing alienation inwardly. They prayed in the rows, swore the oaths, and privately coordinated with the community's opponents.

The Qur'an does not treat this as a local police report. It treats it as a standing human type. Al-Baqarah had already opened its longest chapter with thirteen verses on the hypocrites (2:8–20). Sūrah al-Tawbah would later devote some of the sternest language in the Book to them. This short sūrah concentrates the whole file into eleven verses of unusual density: the lying testimony (v. 1), the oath used as a shield (v. 2), the sealed heart (v. 3), the impressive body and hollow interior (v. 4), the arrogant refusal of forgiveness (vv. 5–6), the economic boycott of the Messenger's companions (v. 7), the claim of tribal might (v. 8), and then the believer's own exposure to a subtler hypocrisy — the hypocrisy of a life so filled with property and children that death arrives with the charity still ungiven and the remembrance still postponed (vv. 9–11).

**The Occasion That Unlocked the Chapter**

Most of verses 1–8 were reportedly revealed concerning ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy during an expedition with the Prophet ﷺ — commonly identified as the expedition against Banū al-Muṣṭaliq. After a quarrel between an Emigrant and a Helper, Ibn Ubayy said, in substance: "Do not spend on those who are with God's Messenger, that they may disperse. And if we return to Madinah, the mightier will surely expel the weaker therefrom." His nephew by alliance, the young Companion Zayd ibn Arqam, heard him and reported the words. Ibn Ubayy swore that he had not said them. For a time the Prophet ﷺ appeared to accept the oath over the youth's report. Then these verses descended, after which the Prophet ﷺ said to Zayd: "O Zayd, God confirmed your statement." The episode is not gossip preserved for its drama. It is the sūrah's own demonstration of its first teaching: God hears what men deny under oath, and a community that cannot distinguish a sworn tongue from a truthful heart will be corrected from above.

**From the Gross Form to the Subtle Form**

The placement of verses 9–11 is the sūrah's second genius. After eight verses that no sincere believer would apply to himself with comfort — "they are the enemy," "God will never forgive them" — the address turns: *O you who believe.* The danger named is no longer the double oath of Ibn Ubayy. It is the double life of a man who believes, prays, and belongs, yet lets wealth and children occupy the inner room that remembrance should occupy. The sūrah thus refuses to let the reader finish the chapter as a spectator of other people's crimes. The hypocrite of Madinah is the coarse type. The distracted believer is the fine type. Both lose, and both lose by the same inner mechanism: the heart's real allegiance is not where the tongue said it was.

What follows treats each of the eleven verses as an independent discourse, in strict verse-by-verse order. Verses that the source commentary groups together (5–6, 10–11) are here separated and given full individual treatment. Nothing has been compressed because the chapter is short. The shortness of the sūrah is not an invitation to brevity in reading it. It is an invitation to density.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:1

> **When the hypocrites come unto thee, they say, “We bear witness that thou art indeed the Messenger of God.” Yet God knows that thou art indeed His Messenger, and God bears witness that the hypocrites are surely liars.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Testimony Spoken to the Wrong Audience**

The sūrah opens not with a definition of hypocrisy but with a scene. *Idhā jāʾaka al-munāfiqūn* — "When the hypocrites come to you." The addressee is the Prophet ﷺ himself. They do not stay away. They come. They enter his presence, occupy the space of discipleship, and produce the most sacred sentence a tongue in that room could produce: *nashhadu innaka la-rasūlu Allāh* — "We bear witness that you are indeed the Messenger of God." The Arabic is not casual. *Nashhadu* is the verb of the shahādah, the same root from which the Muslim's testimony of faith is named. The particle *inna* and the *lām* of emphasis in *la-rasūlu* pile certainty upon the claim. Grammatically the sentence is a perfect confession. Theologically it is the exact formula by which a person enters Islam. And the verse then performs an act no human court can perform: it splits the sentence from the speaker.

Two divine statements follow, set in deliberate parallel. First: *wa Allāhu yaʿlamu innaka la-rasūluh* — "Yet God knows that you are indeed His Messenger." Second: *wa Allāhu yashhadu inna al-munāfiqīna la-kādhibūn* — "And God bears witness that the hypocrites are surely liars." Notice what God does *not* do. He does not deny the content of their testimony. He confirms it. The Messenger is His Messenger; that is knowledge God Himself owns and here restates. What He denies is their *bearing witness*. They are liars not because the proposition is false, but because they do not believe the proposition they are asserting. The lie is located in the gap between the tongue and the heart.

Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on this verse, states the principle with classical compactness:

> *Faith is an affirmation with the heart, not only with the tongue, and true speech comes from the heart.*

The same principle is restated elsewhere in the Book with even harsher legal consequence:

> *“They swear by God that they said nothing, while they had indeed said the word of disbelief, and had disbelieved after their Islam.”* (Qur'an 9:74)

**Why God Repeats What Everyone Already Knows**

A reader may ask why God bothers to say, "God knows that you are indeed His Messenger," in a Book revealed to that Messenger, recited by believers who already accept the fact. The commentators, among them al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr, saw the repetition as a mercy to the Prophet ﷺ and a lesson in the ethics of testimony. The hypocrites' sentence, taken by itself, is true. Hearing it from mouths that do not mean it could, over time, make the true sentence itself feel soiled — as though truth became cheaper by being rented. God therefore intercepts the sentence, lifts it out of their mouths, and re-houses it in His own knowledge: the Messengership is not established by their witness and is not damaged by their insincerity. It stands in God's knowledge whether they speak or are silent.

There is a second function. In Arabic rhetoric, when two clauses are set in this kind of parallel — God knows X, God bears witness Y — the second clause borrows the certainty of the first. If the first is beyond dispute, the second is equally beyond dispute. The lying of the hypocrites is placed on the same epistemic floor as the Messengership of Muhammad ﷺ. A community tempted to give the well-spoken man the benefit of every doubt is hereby told that the doubt has already been settled from above.

**The Historical Door: Zayd ibn Arqam and the Oath**

The verse, along with verses 2, 7, and 8, was reportedly revealed regarding ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy. On an expedition, after a clash of tempers between an Emigrant and a Helper, Ibn Ubayy spoke of starving the Prophet's companions of support and of expelling the "weaker" party from Madinah upon return. Zayd ibn Arqam, then a youth, heard him and reported the speech. Ibn Ubayy came, swore that he had not said it, and was believed for a time over the young man. Zayd was distressed; some even rebuked him for speaking against a notable of the Khazraj. Then this revelation came. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“O Zayd! God confirmed your statement.”* (transmitted in the reports of al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī, and the asbāb literature of al-Wāḥidī)

The episode teaches several things at once, and none of them is tribal score-settling. First: a truthful report from a person of no rank outweighs a sworn denial from a person of rank, once God has spoken. Second: the Prophet ﷺ himself, in the ordinary conduct of affairs, judged by apparent evidence — oaths, demeanor, standing — until revelation disclosed what appearance concealed. That is a consolation to every later judge who cannot open chests. Third: the community is not asked to become a police of interiors. God reserved the interior. What the community is asked to do is to stop treating a fluent shahādah as proof of a fluent heart.

**Shahādah Without Shahādah: The Theology of the Split Witness**

Islamic creed has always distinguished between the utterance of the testimony and the reality it names. The utterance has legal effects in this world: it protects blood, property, and honor; it admits a person to marriage within the community, to inheritance, to the funeral prayer. The reality has effects in the next: it is the key of Paradise, or it is not. This verse is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements that the two can come apart. A man may possess the legal shell of Islam and lack its substance. The Prophet ﷺ described the inner signs of that split in a hadith that the commentators of this sūrah never fail to bring nearby, recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr:

> *“The signs of the hypocrite are four, and whoever possesses one of them possesses a characteristic of hypocrisy until he abandons it: when he is entrusted, he betrays the trust; when he speaks, he lies; when he makes a promise, he breaks it; and when he quarrels, he behaves immorally.”* (al-Bukhārī 34; Muslim 58)

And in the three-sign version from Abū Hurayrah:

> *“The signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays the trust.”* (al-Bukhārī 33; Muslim 59)

Notice that the Prophet ﷺ did not list theological errors. He listed failures of ordinary integrity. The sūrah's first verse and the hadith interpret one another. The hypocrite's opening act in 63:1 is a lie of speech — the most sacred speech put to a false use. The hadith then generalizes: once speech has been detached from the heart, promise, trust, and dispute follow it into the same detachment. Character is downstream of the first split.

**Coming to the Prophet: Proximity Is Not Proof**

The verb *jāʾaka* — "they come to you" — deserves its own pause. Hypocrisy in this sūrah is not the hypocrisy of the absent. It is the hypocrisy of the near. They come to the Messenger. They sit where revelation is heard. They produce the right sentence in the right room. Later Islamic spirituality made a standing caution of this: nearness to sacred company, sacred language, and sacred space can become the most effective camouflage a divided heart ever finds, because the camouflage is made of real light. A man who never comes near the mosque is an easier read than a man who never misses it. The verse therefore trains the eye away from attendance as a metric. The question is not "Did he come?" The question is "What did the coming cost him on the inside?"

This is why al-Baqarah's portrait of the same type begins with almost the same split:

> *“And among mankind are those who say, ‘We believe in God and in the Last Day,’ though they are not believers.”* (Qur'an 2:8)

Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn is that verse expanded into a complete case study, with names, oaths, plots, and a closing warning to the people who would never think the case study included them.

**The Logic of Why the Tongue Is Not Enough**

Why does Islam insist that faith is of the heart, when hearts cannot be subpoenaed? Because a religion that accepted the tongue as sufficient would become, within a generation, a theater. Every ambitious man would learn the lines. Every fearful man would recite them for safety. The legal protections of the utterance — and Islam did protect the utterance, refusing to license inquisitions of the chest — would then be mistaken for the thing they only fence. God protects the fence in this world and audits the house in the next. The verse holds both truths without confusion: do not kill the man who says the shahādah; and do not imagine that God is bound by the shahādah he said.

A useful analogy is the difference between signing a contract and intending the contract. Courts, of necessity, treat the signature. God treats the intention. A society that had no courts of signatures would dissolve into suspicion; a religion that had no audit of intentions would dissolve into performance. Islam built both rooms and forbade either to annex the other.

**What the Verse Asks of a Modern Tongue**

The first application is not the unmasking of others. The first application is the audit of one's own most fluent religious sentences. Every age produces its equivalent of *nashhadu innaka la-rasūlu Allāh*: the correct creed posted, the correct slogan shared, the correct grief expressed, the correct allegiance announced in the room where that allegiance is cheap. The verse does not forbid the sentence. It forbids the rental of the sentence. Before God, a true sentence said without a true heart is not a half-truth. It is a lie — *la-kādhibūn*, "surely liars," the *lām* of emphasis matching the *lām* they had put in their own testimony, as though God returned their grammar upon them.

The second application is patience with God's timing. Zayd was right and was not immediately vindicated. The notable was wrong and was not immediately exposed. Between the report and the revelation there was a stretch of human uncertainty in which a truthful young man sat under a cloud. Communities that demand instant unmasking of every insincere actor are asking for a power God did not give them. The verse's comfort is that the cloud is temporary. God bears witness. The file is not lost because the oath was impressive.

The third application is reverence for the testimony itself. If God confirmed the Messengership in the same breath with which He named the hypocrites liars, then the sentence "Muhammad is the Messenger of God" is not a tribal badge. It is a claim about the world. To say it is to enter a covenant whose other party is God. The sūrah begins by taking that covenant with utmost seriousness — which is why everything that follows, from the shield-oath to the deathbed plea for one more day, is a commentary on what happens when the covenant is signed in ink the heart never mixed.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:2

> **They took their oaths as a shield and thus turned from the way of God. Evil indeed is that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Recycled into Armor**

The second verse names the instrument. *Ittakhadhū aymānahum junnatan* — "They took their oaths as a *junnah*." A *junnah* is a shield, a covering, anything held between the body and a blow. In the law of the new polity, the oath of Islam — and the subsequent oaths of denial, loyalty, and exculpation — protected blood, property, and honor. The hypocrites learned the rule and inverted it. What was given as a sanctuary for the sincere became camouflage for the insincere. They swore they had not said what Zayd reported. They swore they were of the believers. They swore by God, which is to say they pressed the most solemn Name in the language into the service of the most unsacred use of language: the covering of a lie.

The Qur'an had already photographed the same maneuver in Sūrah al-Tawbah:

> *“They swear by God that they are truly of you, but they are not of you. They are a people who are afraid.”* (Qur'an 9:56)

And in Āl ʿImrān the same oaths are priced:

> *“Indeed, those who sell God’s covenant and their oaths for a paltry price — they shall have no share in the Hereafter.”* (Qur'an 3:77)

Al-Qurṭubī and the early commentators note that the "paltry price" is not always coins. Sometimes the price is the continuation of a reputation, the protection of a house, the avoidance of a social cost. The shield is cheap relative to what it covers, and expensive relative to what it costs in the next world. That is the whole of their commerce.

**How a Sacred Thing Becomes a Tool**

An oath in Islam is not a verbal flourish. It is the invocation of God as witness to a human claim. To swear is to say: if I am lying, I have made God a party to the lie. That is why the Prophet ﷺ treated casual oaths as a disorder of speech and false oaths as a destroyer of the soul. He said, in a hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim:

> *“Five things: there is no expiation for them. The first of them is associating partners with God, then killing a soul, then accusing a chaste woman, then fleeing from the advancing army, then the devouring oath by which one seizes the wealth of a Muslim.”* (the grave oath, *al-yamīn al-ghamūs*, is treated across the collections as among the destroyers; al-Bukhārī 6675 discusses the devouring oath)

The *yamīn ghamūs* — the "oath that immerses" its speaker in sin, or immerses him in the Fire — is precisely the shield of this verse: an oath sworn to cover falsehood and consume a right. Ibn ʿAbbās and the jurists after him distinguished the impulsive oath, which has an expiation, from this immersing oath, which has no expiation except repentance, because it is not a slip of the tongue. It is a strategy.

The logic is grim and clear. A community that honors oaths can be gamed by people who do not. The more the sincere treat the Name of God as a boundary they will not cross, the more valuable that Name becomes as cover for those who will. This is why the verse does not say they "broke" their oaths only. It says they *took* them as a shield. The oath is not an accident they failed to keep. It is equipment they chose.

**“And Thus They Turned from the Way of God”**

*Fa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli Allāh.* The verb *ṣaddū* in Arabic faces two directions at once, and the commentators — al-Qurṭubī especially — refuse to collapse them. It can mean "they turned away" from the way of God themselves. It can mean "they turned others away" from the way of God. Both readings are true of the Madinan hypocrites, and both are true of the type. By using the legal protections of Islam they remained inside the community, where they could dissuade the hesitant, leak the secrets, mock the sacrifices, and keep the Jews and the remaining pagans of the oasis from entering a religion whose inner ranks appeared, through them, divided and insincere. A convert considering Islam in Madinah did not only see Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. He also saw Ibn Ubayy. The shield that protected the hypocrite's life became a signboard that confused other lives.

This is why hypocrisy is treated in the Qur'an as more socially toxic than open disbelief. Open disbelief is a position. It can be answered, argued, even respected as a clarity. Hypocrisy is a fog. It makes the straight path look crooked to spectators because some of the people walking on it are not walking. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, in a wording preserved across the admonitory literature:

> *“The thing I fear most for my community is the hypocrite with an eloquent tongue.”*

Whether every chain of that particular wording is of the highest rank, the meaning is the meaning of this verse: eloquence plus a shield-oath is a public hazard. The way of God is not only a private road. It is a road other people are trying to find. Those who stand on it in costume turn seekers around.

**“Evil Indeed Is That Which They Used to Do”**

The verse closes with a moral verdict in the past continuous: *innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "evil indeed is that which they used to do." The tense matters. *Kānū yaʿmalūn* names a practice, a habit, a way of operating, not a single collapse. The evil is not the one false oath after the expedition. The evil is the settled method: live by the community's protections, speak by the community's creed, and steer by a private compass pointed elsewhere. Al-Ṭabarī glosses the "doing" as their combination of inner disbelief with outer profession and with the active harm that combination enabled. Ibn Kathīr reads the closing as God's own appraisal, so that no later reader can call the method clever, pragmatic, or "just politics." It is *sāʾa* — evil. The word is not technical. It is the ordinary human word for what should sicken the conscience.

**Why the Shield Works on Men and Fails with God**

The method makes sense as a human calculation. In a city where saying the shahādah prevents the sword, the rational actor who does not believe will say the shahādah. Game theory predicts the shield. Revelation answers the prediction not by removing the legal shield — Islam continued to judge by the outward, and the Prophet ﷺ refused to unmask every hypocrite by name for killing — but by announcing that the shield does not reach the sky. God is not a magistrate bound by procedure. The oath that stops a human hand does not stop the divine audit. This is the only stable solution to the problem. If the law pierced chests, the community would become an inquisition. If God also accepted chests as closed, the cosmos would become a theater with no critic. Law protects the tongue; God judges the heart. Verse 1 stated the judgment. Verse 2 states the exhibit.

**The Everyday Shield: From Madinah to Any Institution**

Every institution that runs on trust grows a version of this verse. A professional code of ethics becomes a letterhead behind which malpractice hides. A marriage vow becomes a sentence recited at a ceremony and retired by sunset. A workplace value statement becomes the language in which exploitation is renamed. A religious community's shahādah becomes, for some, a passport rather than a covenant. The common structure is identical: a sacred or quasi-sacred utterance, originally meant to bind the speaker, is reused to bind the audience — to make *them* stop asking.

The believer's counter-practice is correspondingly simple and correspondingly hard: do not swear what you do not mean, and do not treat other people's fluent oaths as a substitute for their fruit. The Prophet ﷺ reduced the whole ethics of speech to a rule a child can memorize:

> *“Whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent.”* (al-Bukhārī 6018; Muslim 47)

Silence, in that teaching, is not emptiness. It is the refusal to manufacture shields. And the Qur'an, in a verse that belongs beside this one, forbids the believer from making God Himself cheap:

> *“And do not make God an excuse for your oaths, that you should not be righteous and reverent and make peace among people.”* (Qur'an 2:224)

An oath that prevents goodness is already on its way to becoming a *junnah* in the sense of this verse. The way of God is turned from not only by the spectacular hypocrite of Madinah but by every smaller craftsman who has learned to hold the Name between himself and a consequence he does not wish to meet.

**Evil as Method, Not as Mood**

One last distinction keeps the verse from being misread as a study in villainous emotion. The hypocrites of this sūrah are not necessarily cartoon villains rubbing their hands. Ibn Ubayy had a grievance: he had been near kingship, and a prophet from Makkah had reordered the city. Grievance feels like justice from the inside. The verse does not psychoanalyze the grievance. It names the method the grievance chose: the oath as shield, and the turning of others from the road. Evil here is not a feeling. It is a *doing* — *mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — and doings can be stopped without waiting for the feelings to become noble. That is why the sūrah will later offer the hypocrites a door (come, the Messenger will ask forgiveness for you) and why their refusal of that door, not their original grievance, becomes the seal. The shield can be put down. Verse 2 records that they kept it up.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:3

> **That is because they believed, and then disbelieved; so a seal was set upon their hearts such that they comprehend not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cause Named: A Faith That Was Followed by Its Opposite**

Verse 3 opens with *dhālika bi-anna* — "that is because." The shield-oaths, the turning from the road, the evil practice: the sūrah now traces them to a prior motion of the soul. *Āmanū thumma kafarū* — "they believed, then they disbelieved." Al-Qurṭubī records the two classical readings, and both deserve to be held. The first: some of them actually entered Islam with a degree of assent, then reverted inwardly when the costs appeared — the battles, the levelling of tribal rank, the Prophet's ﷺ growing authority, the arrival of poor Emigrants who had to be fed. The second: they "believed" with the tongue from the first moment and disbelieved with the heart from the same moment, so that the sequence is logical rather than chronological — profession, then the reality under the profession.

The word *thumma* ("then") allows both. What it does not allow is the claim that they were simply never addressed. Something was offered. Something was refused. The verse is a history of a door that was not always sealed.

This sequence is a Qur'anic pattern, not a local accident:

> *“Indeed, those who believed, then disbelieved, then believed, then disbelieved, then increased in disbelief — God is not to forgive them, nor to guide them to a way.”* (Qur'an 4:137)

> *“The hypocrites seek to deceive God, but it is He who deceives them… That is because they believed, then disbelieved; so their hearts were sealed, and they do not understand.”* (Qur'an 4:142, 155 in adjacent argument; cf. 9:77)

Sūrah al-Tawbah describes a related inner contract:

> *“So He punished them with hypocrisy in their hearts until the Day they meet Him, because they broke their promise to God and because they used to lie.”* (Qur'an 9:77)

Belief withdrawn does not return the soul to a neutral blank. It leaves a residue — and the residue, if not repented, hardens.

**The Seal: What It Is and What It Is Not**

*Fa-ṭubiʿa ʿalā qulūbihim* — "so a seal was set upon their hearts." The verb *ṭabaʿa* is the verb of the signet ring pressed into wax: a mark that closes, authenticates, and makes further impression difficult. The same image governs the Qur'an's most famous statement of the condition:

> *“God has sealed their hearts and their hearing. Upon their eyes is a covering, and theirs is a great punishment.”* (Qur'an 2:7)

And it recurs across the Book with a consistency that is itself a teaching: 6:46; 7:100–101; 9:87, 93; 10:74; 16:108; 17:46; 18:57; 30:59; 40:35; 41:5; 42:24; 47:16. No serious reader is permitted to treat the seal as a one-off metaphor.

Two errors must be refused at once. The first error is compulsionism: that God sealed them arbitrarily, then blamed them for not understanding. The second is the denial of God's act: that "seal" is only a figure of speech for their own stubbornness, with no divine role. The Qur'an itself supplies the sequence that holds both divine sovereignty and human responsibility:

> *“And We turn away their hearts and their eyes just as they did not believe in it the first time, and We leave them in their transgression, wandering blindly.”* (Qur'an 6:110)

> *“I shall turn away from My signs those who are arrogant upon the earth without right. Even if they see every sign, they will not believe in it.”* (Qur'an 7:146)

The seal follows the refusal. God confirms a direction the servant has already chosen, the way a physician's "chronic" does not create the illness but names its having become structure. Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, in the tradition that critiques both pure compulsion and pure self-creation of acts, treat such verses as *khatm* after *iʿrāḍ* — sealing after turning away. The will is not stolen. It is indulged until it can no longer easily will otherwise.

**The Heart as the Organ of Understanding**

The result of the seal is *fa-hum lā yafqahūn* — "so they comprehend not." The verb is *faqaha*, the root of *fiqh*: not mere data-collection but the understanding that penetrates to the point of a thing. In Qur'anic anthropology the heart (*qalb*) is not the seat of private emotion as opposed to the brain's thought. It is the organ of comprehension itself:

> *“They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear.”* (Qur'an 7:179)

> *“Have they not traveled in the land, so that they might have hearts by which to understand, or ears by which to hear? For indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts within the breasts.”* (Qur'an 22:46)

A sealed heart is therefore not a sad mood. It is an epistemological event. Arguments still enter the ear. Facts still enter the eye. What fails is uptake: the passage from information to the kind of knowing that reorganizes a life. This is why the hypocrites of this sūrah can be handsome, well-spoken, politically astute, and still *lā yafqahūn*. Their intelligence is intact for every object except the object that would cost them their double life. The seal is selective in that sense. It is not stupidity. It is unavailability.

Al-Rāzī notes that religious understanding has conditions in the receiver the way sight has conditions in the eye. A light can be true and a healthy eye still fail if it has been staring at a glare of its own making. Persistent lying (v. 1), persistent shielding (v. 2), and the inner cancellation of a once-offered faith (v. 3) are such a glare. After enough of them, the heart's surface no longer takes the imprint of a verse. It takes only the imprint of the last self-justification.

**Two Accounts of How a Seal Is Made**

The commentators, following the Qur'an's own dual language, describe the seal in two compatible ways. One is inherent insensitivity: some temperaments, long trained in pride and calculation, meet revelation the way water meets oil. The other is judicial punishment: God, in justice, withholds further opening from those who treated previous openings as threats. Both are present in the cross-references. 10:74 says of earlier nations that, after they denied, God "seals the hearts of the transgressors." 42:24 asks, rhetorically, whether they say the Prophet ﷺ has invented the Qur'an, and answers that if God willed He would seal the heart — meaning, among other things, that even a prophet's heart is under God's lock, and a fortiori the heart that has already refused.

For ʿaqīdah, the verse is therefore a text of both warning and precision. God is *al-Khatim* in the sense that no heart opens except by His leave, and *al-ʿAdl* in the sense that He does not misplace a seal. Human beings remain responsible because the history that precedes the seal is theirs: they believed (or professed), then disbelieved (or disclosed the profession's emptiness). The door of repentance, as later verses of this sūrah and as 4:17–18 make clear, remains procedurally open until death. What the seal describes is not the padlocking of that door from the outside so much as the loss of the wish to knock.

**Psychology: How Understanding Actually Dies**

Ordinary observation confirms the verse's mechanism without needing a laboratory. A person who has publicly committed to a false position will, to protect the self, stop being able to hear the evidence that would force a recantation. The first refusal is effortful. The tenth is reflex. Cognitive dissonance, habit, and identity fuse. The Qur'an named the end-state fourteen centuries before the vocabulary: a seal. The hypocrite cannot "comprehend" not because the creed is obscure — the shahādah of verse 1 is the least obscure sentence in Islam — but because comprehension would require dismantling the shield of verse 2, and the cost of dismantling has been judged, silently, to be too high.

This is why the tradition's spiritual masters always applied the verse reflexively. Reading "they comprehend not" as a description of a seventh-century faction is the one reading that the sealed heart itself would prefer. The useful reading is: which of my refusals is becoming structure? Which reminder do I now meet with impatience rather than with argument, because argument would still imply that I might be wrong? Taqwā, in the analogy the instructions permit, is driving a dangerous road with both hands on the wheel. The sealed heart is the driver who has decided the road is not dangerous and has therefore let go — and who, when the curve comes, cannot understand why the warning signs were there.

**What Remains Possible After a Seal**

The verse is severe, and it is not the last word of the religion. The same Book that speaks of sealed hearts speaks of hearts that are opened:

> *“Whomsoever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast to Islam; and whomsoever He wishes to leave astray, He makes his breast tight and constricted, as though he were climbing into the sky.”* (Qur'an 6:125)

> *“Is one whose breast God has opened to Islam, so that he is upon a light from his Lord…”* (Qur'an 39:22)

The contrast is the believer's work. Expansion is asked for. The Prophet ﷺ used to pray:

> *“O Turner of the hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion.”* (al-Tirmidhī; Ibn Mājah, from Anas — graded authentic)

If the most guided heart in creation still asked not to be turned, then the seal of 63:3 is not a curiosity about villains. It is the named alternative to that daily prayer. Verses 5 and 6 will show the hypocrites refusing even the means of unsealing — the Messenger's prayer of forgiveness. Verse 3 explains why the refusal will look, from the inside, like common sense. They do not comprehend. That is not their alibi. That is their condition.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

> **And when thou seest them, their bodies impress thee, and if they speak, thou listenest to their speech. [Yet] they are like leaning timbers. They suppose that every cry is against them. They are the enemy; so be wary of them. May God curse them! How are they perverted?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Surface That Wins the Eye and the Ear**

The fourth verse turns from the hypocrite's inner history to his public presentation, and it does so by addressing the Prophet ﷺ with a candor that is itself instructive. *Wa idhā raʾaytahum tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum* — "And when you see them, their bodies impress you." *Wa in yaqūlū tasmaʿ li-qawlihim* — "and if they speak, you listen to their speech." God does not scold the Messenger for being impressed. He names a fact of human perception. Some of the leading hypocrites of Madinah — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy and a number of his companions — were, the commentators report, handsome, well-built, and articulate. Al-Qurṭubī records that they had presence. Their frames filled a room. Their sentences had cadence. In a culture that associated dignity of body with dignity of rank, they looked like leaders, and they sounded like men who should be heard.

The verbs are carefully chosen. *Tuʿjibuka* is from *ʿajab*: admiration, the aesthetic catch of the eye. *Tasmaʿ li-qawlihim* is not merely "you hear"; it is "you listen" — the ear grants them attention. Revelation hereby admits that charisma is real. It is a created fact, like height or a fine voice, and it operates even upon a prophet. What revelation then does is refuse to let charisma finish the sentence. The impressive body and the listened-to speech are placed, within a few words, against a simile that undoes them.

**Leaning Timbers: The Simile of the Unsupported Self**

*Ka-annahum khushubun musannadah* — "as if they were leaning timbers." *Khushub* is wood, timber, planks. *Musannadah* is wood propped up, leaned against a wall, not standing by its own grain. The commentators unfolded the image in several complementary directions, all of them sharp. Al-Qurṭubī transmits the readings: they are like shapes without spirits, or bodies without minds; like wood that has been eaten from the inside, so that the exterior seems strong while the interior is hollow; like beams that cannot stand unless something else holds them. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī add that timber leaned against a wall has no root, no fruit, and no independent stance. It occupies space. It does not live.

The simile is exact because it grants the surface. Timber *looks* solid. A row of propped beams can look like a colonnade. Only a tap, or a load, or a termite, discloses that nothing in them is carrying anything. The hypocrite's body is the grain of the wood; his speech is the polish; his faith is the missing root. He borrows stance from the community he leans against — its legal protections, its mosque, its Prophet, its public dignity — and he calls the leaning standing.

A living tree, by contrast, is the Qur'an's own opposing image for the good word and the good person:

> *“A good word is like a good tree: its root is firm, its branches are in the sky. It gives its fruit every season by the leave of its Lord.”* (Qur'an 14:24–25)

The hypocrite is the photographic negative of that tree: impressive timber, no root, no fruit, no season, no Lordward branch. He is furniture. He is not a plant.

**“They Suppose That Every Cry Is Against Them”**

The next clause moves from how they look to how they live inside the look: *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* — "they suppose that every cry is against them." A *ṣayḥah* is a shout, a battle-cry, a sudden loud announcement in a camp or a city. The hypocrites, having used oaths as shields (v. 2) and having, in some cases, maintained secret understandings with the community's enemies, inhabit a permanent startle. Every raised voice might be the voice that names them. Every summons to the mosque might be the summons that exposes them. Every new revelation might be "about us."

Al-Jalālayn and the occasion-commentators explain the fear concretely: they were afraid that their true nature, and in some cases their alliances, had been discovered, in which case their oaths would no longer protect them. Ibn Kathīr notes the general rule: the traitor is a coward in a specific way — not always a coward in battle, but a coward before disclosure. He must monitor every room. He cannot rest in any conversation, because any conversation might be the one that connects his two mouths.

The psychology is law-like. A person with one story can hear a shout and ask, "What happened?" A person with two stories hears a shout and asks, "Was that about me?" Guilt converts the public world into a system of possible indictments. Modern life knows the type without the Madinan costumes: the embezzler who flinches when the office door opens, the double-dealing spouse who reads accusation into a delayed reply, the public moralist whose private contradiction has made every headline feel like a summons. The verse is a diagnostic of that flinch. It is also, indirectly, a portrait of the opposite peace: the sincere have no second story for a shout to discover.

**“They Are the Enemy; So Be Wary of Them”**

The verse then drops the simile and speaks in the plainest nouns in the language: *humu al-ʿaduwwu fa-ḥdharhum* — "they are the enemy, so be wary of them." Not "they have some enmity." Not "treat them as a concern." *The* enemy — the definite article making the identification complete. The command is *ḥdharhum*, from *ḥadhar*: caution, wariness, the attention one gives to a real hazard on a real path.

Why "the enemy" rather than "a problem"? Because an open enemy attacks the gate, and the gate can be manned. The hypocrite attacks the storehouse from inside the house. He hears the war councils. He knows who is short of mounts. He knows which Helper is wavering. Al-Jalālayn notes that they may communicate the believers' secrets to the disbelievers. Al-Qurṭubī notes that their words may deceive. Both harms are interior harms. The Prophet ﷺ was therefore not commanded to stage a theater of distrust — he continued to accept their outward Islam, to pray over some of them until forbidden, to refuse a general inquisition — but he was commanded not to be naive. Wariness is not persecution. Wariness is the refusal to hand the keys to a man because he is handsome and well-spoken.

The ethical balance is the same balance the sūrah has been building since verse 1. Judge the outward for legal purposes; do not confuse the outward with safety. A community that cannot be wary of eloquent interiors will be led by them. A community that cannot stop at wariness and proceeds to chest-opening will become something the Prophet ﷺ refused to found.

**“May God Curse Them! How Are They Perverted?”**

*Qātalahumu Allāh* — literally "May God fight them," conventionally "May God curse them," a Qur'anic idiom of expulsion from mercy. *Annā yuʾfakūn* — "How are they turned away?" *Yuʾfakūn* is from *ifk*: inversion, the spinning of a thing from its face to its back, the same root as the slander called *al-ifk* in Sūrah al-Nūr. Al-Qurṭubī paraphrases the question:

> *“How can they turn from the truth and deviate from guidance, and how can their intellects be blind to the clarity of these proofs?”*

The question is not a request for information. It is a marveling rebuke. Given the proofs, given the Prophet's ﷺ character, given the coherence of the revelation, given even their own startle at every cry — how does a mind this close to the light keep choosing the lean of the timber? The perversion is not lack of data. It is the inversion of the evaluative faculty itself: they treat the shield as wisdom, the hollow as stature, the flinch as prudence, and the enemy-role as survival.

**Charisma Without Character: A Permanent Human Problem**

The verse is among the Qur'an's most current. Every culture trains the eye to equate presence with worth: the taller candidate, the better suit, the more practiced cadence, the face that photographs well. The Qur'an does not deny that such things attract. It denies that they conclude. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who was himself physically imposing, is remembered in the tradition as a man who feared exactly this confusion in himself and in others; the early community's praise-lists rank men by *taqwā* and truthfulness, not by *ajsām*. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“God does not look at your bodies or at your forms, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds.”* (Muslim 2564)

That hadith is the divine counter-metric to this verse's opening clause. What impresses you is not what God looks at. What you listen to may be timber. The practical discipline is correspondingly concrete: do not appoint, marry, follow, or fully trust on the basis of the first two impressions this verse names. Watch what the timber does when a load is placed on it — a financial load, a secrecy load, a load of unpopular truth. The leaning beam announces itself at the first real weight.

**Wariness as a Form of Charity to the Community**

Being wary of the eloquent hypocrite is not only self-protection. It is protection of the weak who will believe him. Ibn Ubayy's speech almost split Emigrants from Helpers; a less restrained moment would have done it. The command *fa-ḥdharhum* is therefore a social command. Communities die by handing microphones to hollow timber because the timber is good on a platform. The verse authorizes a kind of institutional unsentimentality: admire, if the eye insists; listen, if the ear is caught; then check for roots. And leave the curse to God. *Qātalahumu Allāh* is not a license for the believer's tongue to become a weapon of perpetual damnation. It is God's own verdict, spoken by God, so that the believer's share can remain the more modest work of wariness, justice, and the refusal to be recruited by a fine voice into another man's inversion.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:5

> **And when it is said unto them, “Come! The Messenger of God will ask forgiveness for you,” they twist their heads, and thou seest them turning away in arrogance.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation That Should Have Ended the Chapter**

After four verses of exposure, a door is opened. *Wa idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw yastaghfir lakum rasūlu Allāh* — "And when it is said to them, 'Come, the Messenger of God will ask forgiveness for you.'" The speakers, the commentators report, were Muslims who still hoped for the hypocrites' repair. After verses of the kind of 63:1–4 had begun to circulate, some believers went to the named men and said, in effect: the matter is now public; come, apologize, let the Prophet ﷺ pray for you. The invitation is theologically loaded. In Islam, the Messenger's prayer of forgiveness is not a priestly magic, but it is not nothing. God Himself commanded him, in other places, to pray for the believers:

> *“And pray over them. Indeed, your prayer is a comfort for them.”* (Qur'an 9:103)

> *“If they, when they had wronged themselves, had come to you and asked God's forgiveness, and the Messenger had asked forgiveness for them, they would have found God Accepting of repentance, Merciful.”* (Qur'an 4:64)

Verse 5 offers that exact sequence: come, and the Messenger will ask. The medicine is named. The clinic is open. What follows is not a failure of provision. It is a failure of the will to be treated.

**The Twist of the Head: A Gesture That Is a Theology**

*Lawwaw ruʾūsahum* — "they twist their heads." The verb *lawwā* is to twist, wring, turn aside with a torque; it is the body's small theatre of contempt. Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr describe the motion as the motion of a man who has been offered a humiliation he will not swallow: the chin, the glance, the half-turn that says *you have misjudged my rank*. They do not argue. Argument would still concede that the invitation deserves a reply. They *gesture*. The head, which in prayer is the limb that goes to the ground, is here the limb that refuses even to face the one who could pray.

Then the verse names the motion's meaning: *wa raʾaytahum yaṣuddūna wa hum mustakbirūn* — "and you see them turning away, while they are arrogant." Two verbs, one state. *Yaṣuddūna* is the same family as verse 2's *ṣaddū*: turning away, and turning others. *Mustakbirūn* is the active posture of *kibr* — greatness claimed for the self. The Prophet ﷺ defined that word with a precision that belongs over this verse:

> *“No one will enter Paradise in whose heart is an atom's weight of arrogance… Arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people.”* (Muslim 91)

Both halves of the definition are present in the gesture. They reject the truth of their need for forgiveness. They look down on the people who invited them — and, by extension, on the Messenger whose prayer was offered. The twist of the head is *kibr* performed as choreography.

**Why Forgiveness Offended Them**

Forgiveness offends a certain kind of pride more than accusation does. Accusation at least treats a man as a worthy opponent. An offer of *istighfār* treats him as a sinner who must come. Ibn Ubayy's circle had a rank to protect: notables of Madinah, men of lineage and presence (v. 4), men who had almost been kings. To "come" — *taʿālaw*, the verb of approaching, of closing a distance on someone else's terms — was to concede that the center of the city was no longer their assembly but the Prophet's ﷺ mosque. The invitation to be forgiven was, in their hearing, an invitation to be demoted.

This is a law of the soul, not a seventh-century anecdote. The people most in need of a repair are often the people for whom the repair feels like the largest loss of face. A man will sometimes prefer the private continuity of his fault, which he has learned to inhabit, to the public moment of having been wrong. The hypocrites' refusal is extreme, but the mechanism is ordinary. Marriage counselors, teachers, and friends meet the twist of the head whenever a true diagnosis is offered with a true medicine. The sūrah simply shows the mechanism at the highest possible stake: refusing the Messenger's prayer.

**Hardness After the Offer, Not Before It**

The commentators, al-Qurṭubī among them, read in this refusal a proof of the seal already named in verse 3. A heart still soft would have used the invitation even as a political calculation — come, be seen coming, let the prayer be made, keep the shield. Their inability to make even that calculation shows that something in them now preferred the posture of refusal to the benefits of pretense. Hypocrisy has a late stage in which even hypocrisy's own interests are sacrificed to pride. They will not come. Not because coming would cost them the game, but because coming would cost them the self-image that the game was being played to protect.

The Qur'an elsewhere names this late stage as the moment when reminders only increase aversion:

> *“And when their messengers came to them with clear proofs, they rejoiced in the knowledge they had, and they were enveloped by what they used to mock.”* (Qur'an 40:83)

> *“And among them are those who torment the Prophet and say, ‘He is an ear.’ Say: ‘An ear of good for you. He believes in God and believes the believers, and he is a mercy to those of you who believe.’”* (Qur'an 9:61)

They had reduced even the Messenger ﷺ to a manageable object — "an ear," a man who listens too readily. Verse 5 is what happens when such a reduction meets an invitation that would un-reduce him to what he is: the one whose prayer on your behalf matters.

**The Social Meaning of “Come”**

*Taʿālaw* is a plural command. The invitation is to a movement of the feet, not only of the tongue. Islam's repentance is characteristically embodied: one *goes*, one *appears*, one sits where the prayer will be made. The hypocrites' entire method, from verse 1, has been speech without the corresponding motion of the self. Here they are asked for a motion, and the body answers before the mouth: the head twists, the person turns away. The religion of the next two verses will confirm that this bodily refusal is decisive. There are moments when the door is so clearly opened that remaining seated is itself the answer.

For a modern reader the application is uncomfortably near. The "come" of this verse has many later forms: a parent asking a child to repair a wrong, a scholar offering a path back, a friend naming a harm and staying in the room while it is mended, the five daily calls to a prayer that includes *istighfār* as a built-in habit. Arrogance is often not a speech. It is a neck that will not bend. The verse trains the believer to notice his own neck. And it trains the community, having offered the door, not to chase the twisted head down the street. Verse 6 will assign that chase its limit.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:6

> **It is the same for them whether thou askest forgiveness for them or thou askest forgiveness for them not; God will never forgive them. Truly God guides not iniquitous people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Messenger’s Prayer Has a Jurisdiction**

Verse 6 addresses the Prophet ﷺ with a sentence that is at once a ruling and a consolation. *Sawāʾun ʿalayhim astaghfarta lahum am lam tastaghfir lahum* — "It is the same for them whether you ask forgiveness for them or you do not ask forgiveness for them." The invitation of verse 5 was real. The prayer, had they come, would have been real. Their refusal has now moved the file. The Messenger's *istighfār* is a means God honors for those who accept the means. It is not a key that opens a lock the owner has welded shut.

The same ruling is given, with even sharper numerical emphasis, in Sūrah al-Tawbah:

> *“Ask forgiveness for them or do not ask forgiveness for them. If you ask forgiveness for them seventy times, God will not forgive them. That is because they disbelieved in God and His Messenger. And God does not guide the iniquitous people.”* (Qur'an 9:80)

And again:

> *“It is not for the Prophet and those who believe to ask forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they are relatives, after it has become clear to them that they are companions of Hellfire.”* (Qur'an 9:113)

The reports about ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy's funeral belong in this file. When Ibn Ubayy died, his son — a sincere Muslim — asked the Prophet ﷺ to pray over him. The Prophet ﷺ came. ʿUmar took his garment to dissuade him, citing the man's hypocrisy. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the substance of the authentic reports, that he had been given a choice and that he hoped, if he prayed, that more than seventy of his people might be forgiven; he prayed. Then 9:84 was revealed, forbidding prayer over the dead hypocrites. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record the episode. The point for 63:6 is not the biographical detail of one funeral. It is the theological boundary the sūrah states in advance of that funeral: there is a state of iniquity in which even Muhammad's ﷺ prayer does not avail, because prayer is not a second religion beside God's decree. It is a means inside it.

**“God Will Never Forgive Them”: Reading the Severity Without Distortion**

*Lan yaghfira Allāhu lahum* — "God will never forgive them." The particle *lan* in Arabic negates the future with emphasis. The sentence is among the sternest in the Qur'an, and it must be read with the Qur'an's own rules of interpretation, not with a haste that either gloats or despairs.

First, it is a verdict upon a type in a settled state: those who have refused, with arrogance, the offered means of forgiveness, and who persist in *fisq* — iniquity, the stepping-out from the covenant. It is not a verdict upon every person who has ever hypocritically said the shahādah and later wept. The Book elsewhere opens a door so wide that despair itself becomes a sin:

> *“Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful.”* (Qur'an 39:53)

Second, the "never" is tied, in 9:80 and here, to a cause: disbelief after profession, refusal of the Messenger, iniquity as a standing description. If the cause lifts — if a man actually turns — the Qur'an's own habit is to welcome him. Several Madinan figures associated with hypocrisy are reported to have later repaired. God's *lan* describes the path as long as it is walked, not a name tattooed on a soul against its future repentance. What it does close is the fantasy that a man can refuse to come (v. 5) and still be carried in by someone else's prayer.

Third, 4:18 states the temporal edge that verses 10–11 of this sūrah will return to:

> *“Repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death confronts one of them, he says, ‘Now I repent,’ nor for those who die as disbelievers.”* (Qur'an 4:18)

The unforgiveness of 63:6 is of a piece with that edge. Forgiveness in this religion is vast, and it is not a toy. It has a door, a time, and a posture: coming, while coming is possible, without the twist of the head.

**“God Guides Not Iniquitous People”**

The closing clause — *inna Allāha lā yahdī al-qawma al-fāsiqīn* — is a refrain of the Book. *Fisq* is from a root used of a date bursting its skin: the going-out of a thing from its proper covering. In law it names the one who steps outside obedience while remaining, perhaps, inside the community's census. God "does not guide" such a people not because guidance is scarce, but because guidance is not dropped into a clenched fist. The same sentence is said of disbelieving people, wrongdoing people, and people who deny the signs, in various verses (2:264; 5:108; 9:24, 80; 61:5). It is a statement of moral law more than of a sudden divine mood: a community organized around *fisq* is not, while it remains so organized, on the road that *hidāyah* names.

This is a consolation to the Prophet ﷺ of the same kind as 2:6 and 28:56:

> *“You cannot guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills.”* (Qur'an 28:56)

> *“Truly God leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will; so let not your soul be expended in regrets over them.”* (Qur'an 35:8)

The most merciful man ever sent is told to stop spending his soul. Love does not rewrite the rules of opening. The Prophet's ﷺ grief for his uncle Abū Ṭālib, his hope for Ibn Ubayy, his tendency to pray for those who hurt him — all of that is real, and all of it is here given a shore. He may love. He may offer. He may not annex God's guidance.

**Why the Verse Must Exist in a Religion of Mercy**

A religion that spoke only verse 5's invitation, without verse 6's limit, would teach people to treat messengers as spare keys to a house they refuse to enter. A religion that spoke only verse 6, without verse 5's invitation, would teach despair. The sūrah's pairing is the creed in motion: means are offered; means are not weapons against the Giver of means. Intercession in Islam is real and is by God's leave (2:255; 53:26). It is not a second throne.

The logical principle is one every honest parent already knows. You can stand at the door of a child's room and offer reconciliation until your voice is hoarse. If the child will not come, your love has not failed; it has met a freedom you yourself taught the child to have. God's *lan yaghfira* is the cosmic form of that unmet offer. It honors the hypocrites' own choice more than their defenders would like. They twisted their heads. The rest follows.

**Living Under the Verse Without Playing God**

The believer's danger after such a verse is to appoint himself its administrator — to decide which living person God will never forgive, and to withhold ordinary kindness on that basis. The Prophet ﷺ did not do this. Until a revelation named a man and forbade a prayer, he dealt with the outward. The verse is primarily a teaching about God and about the Prophet's ﷺ own heart, not a hunting license. Its daily use is closer to this: do not delay your own coming on the theory that someone else's prayer, parentage, or affiliation will carry you; and do not exhaust yourself to self-destruction over a refusal that has become a personality. Warn as if lives depend on it. Accept, when the head has twisted and stayed twisted, that the file has been moved to a court you do not run.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:7

> **They are the ones who say, “Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of God, that they may disperse.” Yet to God belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, but the hypocrites comprehend not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Boycott Imagined as a Strategy**

The sūrah now quotes the hypocrites' economic plan. *Hum alladhīna yaqūlūna lā tunfiqū ʿalā man ʿinda rasūli Allāh ḥattā yanfaḍḍū* — "They are the ones who say, 'Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of God, until they disperse.'" The addressees of that sentence, the commentators explain, were the Helpers — the Anṣār of Madinah, who had opened their houses, dates, wells, and fields to the Emigrants. In the first years after the Hijrah, many of the Muhājirūn had arrived with nothing. The Anṣār's generosity was not a metaphor. It was food, shelter, clothing, and a share in palm gardens. The brotherhood (*muʾākhāh*) the Prophet ﷺ instituted between specific Emigrants and Helpers made that generosity a covenant, not a mood.

Ibn Ubayy's circle looked at this covenant and saw a lever. If the spending stopped, the hangers-on — as they counted the Emigrants — would scatter. The Prophet ﷺ would be left without his imported base. Madinah would revert, in their hope, to its old notables. The sentence is thus not miserliness in the ordinary sense. It is politics conducted as famine. Starve the community around the Messenger, and the Messenger's community becomes a man.

**Why They Thought the Lever Would Work**

The source commentary notes a further calculation. As raids and expeditions began to bring spoils, some of the Emigrants became less dependent on the Helpers. The balance of power inside Madinah shifted. Men who had measured their rank by their capacity to patronize now watched their clients acquire independent means — and watched, behind those means, the growing prestige of a prophet they had not chosen. The call not to spend was, in that light, an attempt to restore a patronage order: we feed, therefore we lead; if we stop feeding, they must fold.

The calculation is worldly, and on worldly terms it is not stupid. Armies do disperse when pay stops. Clients do leave when tables close. What the calculation cannot see is the one fact the rest of the verse names.

**“To God Belong the Treasuries of the Heavens and the Earth”**

*Wa li-llāhi khazāʾinu al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ.* A *khazīnah* is a treasury, a storehouse, a locked room of provision. The plural *khazāʾin* and the pairing of heavens and earth together deny every provincial account of wealth. Dates in a Madinan garden are not the base of the food chain. They are an item in a storehouse whose Owner is not the Anṣār and not Quraysh and not the hypocrites. God states the same ownership throughout the Book:

> *“To God belongs what is in the heavens and what is in the earth.”* (Qur'an 2:284)

> *“And there is not a thing but that with Us are its treasuries, and We do not send it down except in a known measure.”* (Qur'an 15:21)

> *“Or do they have the treasuries of the mercy of your Lord, the Mighty, the Bestower?”* (Qur'an 38:9)

The hypocrites' plan treats provision as a human monopoly. The verse relocates provision. If the Helpers stopped spending, God would not therefore stop feeding. He fed the Emigrants from Abyssinia to the cave of Thawr to the first months in Madinah, and He could feed them from a line the notables did not control. The later spoils that so annoyed Ibn Ubayy's faction were themselves a demonstration: wealth arrived from a direction the old patrons did not own.

The same misreading of treasure appears in the disbelievers' demand that a prophet should come with a storehouse of his own:

> *“And they say: ‘Why has a treasure not been sent down to him, or why has an angel not come with him?’”* (Qur'an 11:12; cf. 25:8)

They wanted the treasury visible, sitting beside the Messenger, so that patronage would remain the frame. God kept the treasury with Himself, so that faith would remain the frame.

**“But the Hypocrites Comprehend Not”**

The verse ends by returning to verse 3's diagnosis: *wa lākini al-munāfiqīna lā yafqahūn*. They do not *faqah* — they do not reach the point of the thing. The point is not a mystery of metaphysics. It is the elementary creed that the Provider is God. A man can watch rain fall on his enemy's field and still, if the seal is in place, believe that he himself is the weather. The hypocrites' failure of understanding is here specifically economic-theological. They can count dates. They cannot count the Owner of dates.

This failure has a social form still visible wherever religious communities are funded. There is always a temptation for donors to imagine that the religion is a dependent of the donation, and that doctrine, personnel, and even the honor of scholars should therefore track the donor's will. "Do not spend until they disperse" is the naked form of a sentence that, in polite rooms, sounds like "we will have to reconsider our support." The verse does not forbid the rich from giving, and it does not forbid institutions from needing gifts. It forbids the metaphysics in which the gift is the tap of being. God is the tap. The donor is at most a pipe, and pipes are replaceable.

The Prophet ﷺ taught the opposite economics in a hadith the charity-verses of al-Baqarah also live under:

> *“Charity does not decrease wealth.”* (Muslim 2588)

And every day, in a hadith of Abū Hurayrah:

> *“There is no day on which people wake except that two angels descend, and one of them says: ‘O God, give the spender a replacement,’ and the other says: ‘O God, give the withholder destruction.’”* (al-Bukhārī 1442; Muslim 1010)

The hypocrites' boycott, heard against those reports, is not only cruelty. It is a bad investment even in their own chosen currency. They withhold to control. God withholds, from the withholder, the very increase they thought they were protecting.

**The Anṣār’s Honor, and the Test That Generosity Meets**

It is worth saying plainly that the verse is also a monument to the Helpers. The plot only makes sense because their spending was load-bearing. God had praised them already:

> *“And those who, before them, had made their dwelling in the Home and in faith, love those who emigrated to them, and find in their breasts no need for what the Emigrants were given, and prefer them to themselves even if they are in poverty. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul — it is they who are the successful.”* (Qur'an 59:9)

The hypocrites attempted to recruit that same population into stinginess, rebranded as strategy. Some of the weak of heart always hear such a recruitment. The verse answers the recruitment over the Helpers' heads: even if you stopped, He would not. Give, therefore, as pipes, not as owners. And do not let a notable's political famine become your religious diet.

**Comprehension as the Ability to Locate a Treasury**

A last practical word. "They comprehend not" in this verse can be turned into a self-test. When money tightens, where does the mind run first — to God's treasuries, or to the human tap one can threaten to close? When a community's poor need bread, is the first thought "how do we squeeze the donors" or "the Owner has not gone bankrupt"? Taqwā in provision is a way of walking a dangerous financial road with the hands on the wheel and the eyes on the actual source, not on the billboard of the last patron. The hypocrites counted the billboard. The verse counts the heavens and the earth.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:8

> **They say, “If we return to Madinah, the mightier will surely expel the weaker therefrom.” Yet unto God belongs the might and unto His Messenger and the believers, but the hypocrites know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Claimed a City**

*Yaqūlūna la-in rajaʿnā ilā al-Madīnati la-yukhrijanna al-aʿazzu minhā al-adhall.* "They say: if we return to Madinah, the mightier will surely expel the weaker from it." The sentence is ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy's, spoken on the expedition after the quarrel between the Emigrant and the Helper, when tribal feeling flared and he thought he saw a road back to the old hierarchy. *Al-aʿazz* is the more mighty, the more glorious, the more impregnable. *Al-adhall* is the more abased, the more lowly. The comparative pair is not only about force. It is about honor. Ibn Ubayy meant: we, the resident notables, the men of numbers and of the city's ancient name, are *aʿazz*; this Makkan and his imported poor are *adhall*; a return to the city will be a return to the natural order, and we will put them out.

Some commentators note that the sentence can also be rendered "the more noble will expel the meaner." The moral claim is then even clearer. He was not only counting swords. He was ranking worth.

**The Son Who Heard the Folly**

The reports add a detail the verse itself does not need but that history has not been willing to drop. Ibn Ubayy's son, ʿAbd Allāh, was a sincere believer. When he heard what his father had said, he is reported to have barred his father's way into Madinah and to have said, in substance: you will not enter until you concede that you are the lowly and the Messenger of God is the mighty. The father did not recant as the son wished. He entered under a cloud the revelation had already named, and he died not long after. The son's stance is the verse enacted by a life: might is not the father's house. Might is where God has put it.

The episode also rescues the sūrah from being read as an anti-Madinan or anti-tribal blast. The same bloodline produced the speaker of the sentence and the man who refused it. Hypocrisy is not a genealogy. It is a claim about where ʿizzah sits.

**Where Might Actually Lives**

*Wa li-llāhi al-ʿizzatu wa li-rasūlihi wa li'l-muʾminīn.* "Yet unto God belongs the might, and unto His Messenger, and unto the believers." The sentence is among the Qur'an's great reallocations of a political word. *ʿIzzah* in Arabic is might, glory, impregnability, the capacity not to be overthrown. Pre-Islamic Arabia located it in tribe, number, horses, and poetic reputation. Ibn Ubayy located it in the resident majority of an oasis. God locates it in three nested places: in Himself absolutely; in His Messenger by gift and office; in the believers by attachment to the first two.

The same reallocation is made in Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn's sister-file, Sūrah al-Nisāʾ and Sūrah Fāṭir:

> *“And let not their speech grieve you. Indeed, the might belongs entirely to God.”* (Qur'an 10:65)

> *“Whoever desires might — then to God belongs the might entirely.”* (Qur'an 35:10)

> *“They say: ‘If we return to Madinah…’ — and God writes: ‘Might is for God, and for His Messenger, and for the believers, but the hypocrites do not know.’”*

The grammar is possessive throughout. Might is not a trophy the believers seize. It is a property they are given to stand in, the way a person stands in a house he did not build. When believers seek ʿizzah from other owners — from a party, a wealth-block, a foreign patron, a trend — they are, in the logic of this verse, momentarily talking like Ibn Ubayy: locating impregnability in a place God has not named.

**“But the Hypocrites Know Not”**

Verse 7 ended with *lā yafqahūn* (they comprehend not). Verse 8 ends with *lā yaʿlamūn* (they know not). The commentators mark the shift. *Fiqh* is penetrating understanding; *ʿilm* is knowledge of a fact. In verse 7 they failed to understand the nature of provision. In verse 8 they fail even to know a fact that the rest of the universe knows: that might is God's. Their epistemology has now been named twice, at two depths. They can neither grasp the principle nor get the elementary datum right.

Why would a man not "know" something so often repeated? Because knowledge, in the Qur'an's usage, is not the ability to recite a sentence. Ibn Ubayy could have heard *li-llāhi al-ʿizzah* a hundred times in the mosque. Knowing, here, means living as if the sentence were true. He continued to live as if ʿizzah were a tribal remainder that a return to the city would restore. The verse calls that living *not knowing*. It is a severe definition, and it is the only definition that keeps religious sentences from becoming furniture.

**The Irony History Wrote**

Within a few years the sentence had been historically inverted. The "weaker" party, by Ibn Ubayy's ranking, was in Madinah to stay. The old bid for kingship was over. Islam's ʿizzah was not a matter of the expedition's return-march. It was already, by then, the city's public order — which is why hypocrisy existed at all. Men do not swear false shahādahs to a weak cult. They swear them to a power they wish to survive inside. Ibn Ubayy's boast was, in that sense, already anachronistic when he uttered it. He was threatening to expel a force whose capacity to attract hypocrites was itself proof that it could not be expelled.

The Qur'an often lets false predictions stand in the text so that later readers can watch them fail. Pharaoh's "I am your lord most high" (79:24) is preserved for the same reason. 63:8 preserves a municipal version of the same error: the claim to own a city's future. God answered not with a counter-boast of numbers — the believers were not, at every moment, the larger faction — but with a relocation of the noun. Might is not where the census is. Might is where He is.

**Modern Relocations of ʿIzzah**

The verse's contemporary life is in every attempt to rank human worth by the metrics Ibn Ubayy used: residency against immigration, wealth against poverty, eloquence against silence, established name against revealed claim. Nations talk this way. Institutions talk this way. Families talk this way when a son or daughter follows a truth the family's rank finds embarrassing. The Qur'an's answer is not that weakness is better than strength. It is that strength has been redefined. The believer may be, in a given room, the poorest body present, and still stand inside ʿizzah because he stands where God put it. The notable may own the room and be, in the verse's vocabulary, *adhall*, because he is fighting the Owner for a title he cannot hold.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the ethical face of this reallocation:

> *“The strong man is not the one who throws others down. The strong man is the one who controls himself in anger.”* (al-Bukhārī 6114; Muslim 2609)

Might begins as self-mastery under God, then becomes, by God's gift, a communal standing that no municipal plot can expel. The hypocrites did not know this. The believers are commanded, by the mere existence of the verse, to know it well enough that a notable's threat does not relocate their sense of where they live.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:9

> **O you who believe! Let neither your property nor your children divert you from the remembrance of God. Whosoever does so, it is they who are the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns, and the Mirror Is Produced**

After eight verses in the third person — they say, they swear, they twist, they plot — the sūrah turns on its heel: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — "O you who believe." The hypocrites are no longer the grammatical subject. The believers are. The turn is the sūrah's most pastoral act. A congregation that has just been shown the coarse disease is now shown the fine one, and the fine one is near enough to piety to be missed. Ibn Ubayy's hypocrisy is a double oath. The believer's danger is a double occupancy of the heart: God named as Lord, and property and children occupying the rooms where remembrance should live.

The structure matches the end of the previous sūrah. Al-Jumuʿah closed by warning the believers not to leave the Prophet ﷺ standing in the Friday sermon for a trade caravan (62:9–11). Al-Munāfiqūn closes by warning them not to let the standing furniture of a successful life — wealth and offspring — perform the same desertion in slow motion. The two closings interpret one another. Distraction is not only a market arriving outside the mosque. Distraction is a household growing inside the chest.

**Property and Children: Named Because They Are Goods**

*Lā tulhikum amwālukum wa lā awlādukum ʿan dhikri Allāh.* "Let not your properties nor your children divert you from the remembrance of God." The verb *alhākum* is from *lahw*: diversion, preoccupation, the occupying of attention by a thing that may be pleasant. The Qur'an does not say "abandon property and children." It says do not let them *divert*. The distinction is the whole ethics of the verse. Property is a trust God named a trial and a support. Children are among the most praised of worldly gifts. Both appear in the Book as adornment:

> *“Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world, but the enduring righteous deeds are better with your Lord in reward and better in hope.”* (Qur'an 18:46)

> *“It is not your wealth or your children that bring you near to Us in nearness, except those who believe and do righteous deeds.”* (Qur'an 34:37)

And on the Day when the adornment is tested:

> *“The Day when neither wealth nor children will avail, except one who comes to God with a sound heart.”* (Qur'an 26:88–89)

The sūrah is therefore not an ascetic's charter against family life. It is a charter against a family life that has become a rival qiblah. The same Book that commands the feeding of children and the earning of lawful wealth here commands that neither of those obediences be allowed to eat the obedience they were meant to serve.

**What “Remembrance of God” Covers in This Verse**

Al-Qurṭubī notes that *dhikr Allāh* here can be read in two complementary ways, and the verse is richer for holding both. The first is *dhikr* as the practice of remembrance: prayer, recitation, the tongue's and heart's mention of God, the "remembrance of God" that 13:28 says makes hearts tranquil, and that 29:45 says prayer itself is for:

> *“Recite what has been revealed to you of the Book, and establish the prayer. Indeed, prayer forbids indecency and wrong, and the remembrance of God is greater.”* (Qur'an 29:45)

> *“Those who believe and whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of God. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of God hearts find rest.”* (Qur'an 13:28)

The second reading, which al-Qurṭubī also records, takes *dhikr* as "the reminder of God" — the Qur'an itself, one of whose names is *al-Dhikr*:

> *“Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed We will be its Guardian.”* (Qur'an 15:9)

> *“It is nothing but a reminder for the worlds.”* (Qur'an 68:52; cf. 6:90; 12:104; 38:87)

On that reading the verse says: do not let wealth and children take you away from the Book — from its recitation, its rulings, its Friday, its funeral, its claim on the calendar. The two readings are not rivals. A life diverted from the Book is a life diverted from remembrance, and a life without remembrance will not long remain a life of the Book.

**Why These Two Diversions, and Not Others**

The Qur'an could have named many distractors: status, appetite, anger, entertainment. It names property and children because they are the distractors that look most like duties. A man neglecting prayer for a forbidden pleasure knows, at some level, what he is doing. A man neglecting prayer because a business must be saved, or a child must be driven, or a household must be maintained at a certain level, can call the neglect responsibility. The verse goes to the place where the excuse is strongest.

It is also the place where love is strongest, which is why the Book elsewhere warns that the love itself can be a trial:

> *“And know that your property and your children are a trial, and that with God is a great reward.”* (Qur'an 8:28)

> *“O you who believe! Indeed, among your spouses and your children are enemies to you, so beware of them. And if you pardon and overlook and forgive, then indeed God is Forgiving, Merciful. Your property and your children are only a trial, and God — with Him is a great reward.”* (Qur'an 64:14–15)

"Enemies" in 64:14 is not a slur on families. It is a statement that those one loves most can, without intending evil, pull one off the road. A child's need is real. A fortune's demand is real. Taqwā is the art of meeting real needs without surrendering the real center. The analogy given in the standing instructions is exact: taqwā is driving a dangerous road carefully. Property and children are passengers in the car. They are not the road, and they are not the destination. A driver who stares at the passengers will leave the road with them still in the car.

**“It Is They Who Are the Losers”**

*Wa man yafʿal dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn.* "Whoever does that — it is they who are the losers." *Khusrān* is loss in the commercial sense: a trade that ends in deficit. The sūrah that began with hypocrites selling oaths for a paltry price (cf. 3:77) ends its address to the believers with the same ledger. To gain a market and a lineage and to lose remembrance is not a mixed result. It is a loss. The restriction *hum* — "they themselves" — is the grammar of exclusivity: the real losers are not necessarily the poor who remembered, but the successful who forgot.

The Prophet ﷺ fixed the definition of loss and gain in a hadith that belongs over this verse:

> *“The intelligent one is he who takes his soul to account and works for what is after death, and the incapable one is he who lets his soul follow its desires and then places hopes upon God.”* (al-Tirmidhī 2459, from Shaddād ibn Aws)

And he taught a daily counter-occupation of the heart:

> *“The world is cursed, and all that is in it is cursed, except the remembrance of God and what is connected to it, and a scholar and a student.”* (al-Tirmidhī 2322)

Remembrance is not a hobby the verse is trying to protect from a busy schedule. It is the one occupation that makes the rest of the schedule count.

**The Subtle Hypocrisy This Verse Exists to Name**

The introduction of the sūrah, and the classical commentators after it, read verses 9–11 as a warning about a hypocrisy that does not require Ibn Ubayy's plot. A man can be a believer — the verse addresses him as one — and still live a functional nifāq of attention. He says the shahādah and means it. He does not leak secrets to an enemy. He does not twist his head at the offer of forgiveness. He simply never arrives at the prayer on time because the shop is open, and he never opens the Book because the children are awake, and he never gives the charity because the children's future must be secured first, and he dies with the intention still warm and the deed still pending. Verse 10 will put that death on the page.

This is why the verse must not be preached only at the obviously worldly. It is aimed at the responsible. The shopkeeper, the parent, the saver, the ambitious professional who is "doing it for the family" — these are the people God loved enough to warn. Love of children is not the crime. Diversion is the crime. The test of diversion is practical and almost crude: when the time of remembrance comes, what actually wins? If the answer, week after week, is the property or the child, the verse has already named the result. Loss. Not because property and children are cursed, but because they were asked to be God, and they are not qualified.

**How to Keep the Passengers Without Leaving the Road**

The tradition did not hear this verse as a command to impoverish or to abandon. The Prophet ﷺ raced with ʿĀʾishah, let Ḥasan and Ḥusayn climb his back in prostration, named a child's love as a mercy, and asked God for useful wealth. The method is integration, not amputation: property earned lawfully and spent (v. 10) becomes itself a form of remembrance; children raised to pray become an extension of one's own dhikr, not its rival. The family that is organized around the prayer has not been diverted by children. It has enrolled them. The wealth that has a known outlet in zakāh and ṣadaqah has not diverted from remembrance. It has been made to remember.

What the verse will not permit is the honest-sounding postponement: I will remember when the business is stable, when the children are grown, when the house is paid. Verses 10 and 11 exist because that postponement is the most common last sentence of a diverted life.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:10

> **And spend of that which We have provided you before death comes upon one of you and he says, “My Lord, wouldst that Thou grant me reprieve till a term nigh, that I may give charity and be among the righteous!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command with Its Deadline Written on Its Face**

*Wa anfiqū min mā razaqnākum min qabli an yaʾtiya aḥadakumu al-mawt.* "And spend of that which We have provided you, before death comes to one of you." The verse continues the address to the believers and converts verse 9's prohibition (do not be diverted) into a positive act (spend). The spending in view, al-Qurṭubī records, is first of all the obligatory zakāh, and then every other right that wealth owes: the voluntary ṣadaqah, the maintenance of family, the meeting of a need one could meet. The commentators add that the verse was also heard as a general prohibition of delay in any obligatory duty. Prayer, repentance, apology, the will, the debt — all of them hide under the same *min qabl*, "before."

The phrase *mā razaqnākum* — "that which We have provided you" — is the same relocation of ownership performed in verse 7. You are not asked to spend from what is, in the last analysis, yours. You are asked to circulate a provision that arrived from a treasury you do not run. The psychology of that phrasing is merciful. Giving is easier when the hand has already admitted it is a hand, not a source.

**The Scene the Delay Produces**

The verse then stages the future the delayer is writing. Death comes. The man says: *rabbi law lā akhkhartanī ilā ajalin qarīb fa-aṣṣaddaqa wa akun mina al-ṣāliḥīn* — "My Lord, if only You would delay me for a near term, so that I may give charity and be among the righteous." Every word of the plea is a confession. *Law lā akhkhartanī*: the request for postponement, the very habit of his life, now asked of God as a last favor. *Ilā ajalin qarīb*: he does not even ask for a long life; a short extension would do — enough to write the check, enough to make the prayer, enough to repair. *Fa-aṣṣaddaqa*: so that I may give ṣadaqah — the verb of charity, doubled in form, urgent. *Wa akun mina al-ṣāliḥīn*: and be among the righteous. He knows, at last, the name of the company he is not in.

Ibn ʿAbbās, as recorded by al-Jalālayn and al-Qurṭubī, said:

> *“Every person who has fallen short in zakāh will ask to be returned to this world at the moment of death.”*

The statement is not a curiosity of one tax. Zakāh is the representative duty of the verse because it is the duty most easily postponed without immediate social shame. A missed prayer is noticed by a congregation. A missed zakāh can be a private arrears for years. Death collects the arrears in a currency the estate cannot pay.

**Why Charity Is the Deed the Dying Ask For**

It is not accidental that the dying man in this verse asks to give, not to earn more, not to be praised, not even, in so many words, to pray. Charity is the deed that most clearly requires a living hand and a still-owned provision. After death the wealth is no longer his to spend; it is an estate. The Prophet ﷺ taught the narrowness of that gate in a hadith of Abū Hurayrah:

> *“When a person dies, his deeds are cut off except from three: a continuing charity, knowledge by which benefit is had, or a righteous child who prays for him.”* (Muslim 1631)

The continuing charity (*ṣadaqah jāriyah*) is the one spending that death does not entirely end — and it can only be started before death. The man in the verse wants a near term because he has finally understood this schedule. He wants to plant at the hour when planting is over.

The same Prophet ﷺ said, when asked which charity has the greatest reward:

> *“That you give in charity while you are healthy and greedy, hoping to be rich and fearing poverty; and do not delay until the soul reaches the throat and you say: ‘To so-and-so this, and to so-and-so that,’ when it already belongs to so-and-so.”* (al-Bukhārī 1419; Muslim 1032)

That hadith is verse 10 in prophetic prose. The charity of the healthy, greedy, still-hopeful man is the charity the verse is trying to obtain. The charity of the throat-rattle is the charity the verse says will not be available.

**The Righteousness He Named Too Late**

*Wa akun mina al-ṣāliḥīn* — "and be among the righteous." Ṣalāḥ, righteousness, in the Qur'an's usage, is not a mood of piety. It is the state of a thing doing what it was made to do. A ṣāliḥ person is a person whose faith, worship, and dealings are in working order. The dying man does not ask to be known as a donor. He asks to be in that working order. The tragedy the verse records is that he always knew the name of the company. He had intended, in some foggy future, to join it. The foggy future was a theory. Death is not.

This is why the verse is a commentary on hypocrisy even though it addresses believers. The hypocrite of verses 1–8 says what he does not mean. The delayer of verse 10 means what he does not do. Both have a split: one between tongue and heart, the other between intention and hour. The second split can exist inside a real faith. That is what makes it more relevant to most reciters of the sūrah than Ibn Ubayy's plot. Most reciters will never try to starve the Emigrants. Many reciters will die with a zakāh unpaid and a reconciliation unattempted, having meant, sincerely, to get to it.

**The Logic of “Before”**

Why does God insist on *before*, when He could accept the dying plea as the most authentic moment of a life? Because a religion that accepted deathbed spending as equivalent to living spending would teach people to treat this world as a waiting room and God as a cashier who opens at the last minute. The moral muscles that charity trains — trust in the treasury of verse 7, refusal of the diversion of verse 9, the breaking of the self's panic about tomorrow — those muscles are trained by giving while tomorrow still exists to panic about. A charity that costs nothing in hope is a charity that trains nothing.

There is also a justice toward the poor. The poor cannot eat an intention. They eat bread that was transferred while the transferor could still sign. A society of delayers is a society that funds its piety with checks the grave cancels.

**The Near Term and the Modern Calendar**

The man's request for *ajalin qarīb*, a near term, is the most recognizably modern clause in the verse. Contemporary life is an apparatus for manufacturing near terms: the end of the quarter, the end of the school year, the next bonus, the retirement, the Monday after this Monday. Each near term feels like a responsible schedule. The verse places death in the middle of that calendar, unscheduled, and lets us hear how all the near terms sound from the other side. They sound like one sentence: *law lā akhkhartanī*. If only You would postpone me.

The practical obedience is correspondingly untheatrical. Calculate the zakāh and pay it when it is due, not when the cash-flow is prettier. Give the smaller ṣadaqah on the day the impulse is sincere, because the later, larger gift is the one the verse is not confident will occur. Write the will. Pay the debt. Make the apology. The "spend" of the verse is a species; the genus is *do the due thing while the hand still closes*. Verse 11 will now close the genus with a law of time that no plea rewrites.

## Sūrah al-Munāfiqūn 63:11

> **Yet God will not grant any soul reprieve when its term has come. And God is Aware of whatsoever you do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law That Does Not Negotiate the Hour**

*Wa lan yuʾakhkhira Allāhu nafsan idhā jāʾa ajaluhā.* "And God will not delay a soul when its term has come." The dying man's request in verse 10 is answered with a law, not with a scolding. The law is not particular to hypocrites or to the stingy. It is a law of *nafs* — any soul. *Ajal* is the appointed term, the dated end written with the writing of creation. When it arrives, the request for a near term meets a closed counter.

The Qur'an states this law in enough places that no reader can treat it as a local threat:

> *“And for every nation there is a term. So when their term comes, they will not remain behind an hour, nor will they precede it.”* (Qur'an 7:34; 10:49)

> *“We grant them reprieve only that they may increase in sin.”* (Qur'an 3:178)

> *“And never think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them for a Day when eyes will stare in horror.”* (Qur'an 14:42)

> *“If God were to take mankind to task for what they have earned, He would not leave a single creature on the face of the earth. But He grants them reprieve until an appointed term. And when their term comes, indeed God is Seeing of His servants.”* (Qur'an 35:45; cf. 16:61)

Two kinds of reprieve must be distinguished, and this verse is the distinction. There is the great reprieve of life itself: the years God could have ended and did not, the punishments He could have hastened and delayed, the sun that rose again on a man who had not yet repented. That reprieve is mercy, and it is already in the reader's hand. Then there is the reprieve asked *after* the *ajal* has arrived: the extra hour at the throat. That reprieve is the one *lan yuʾakhkhira* refuses. The first is the open market of tawbah. The second is the market's closing bell.

**Death as the Door That Changes the Kind of Asking**

Islamic eschatology is exact about what death does to repentance. 4:17–18, which the source commentary rightly places beside this verse, divide tawbah into the accepted and the unaccepted by timing:

> *“God accepts the repentance of those who do evil in ignorance and then repent soon after; those God will turn to, and God is Knowing, Wise. But repentance is not for those who do evil deeds until, when death confronts one of them, he says, ‘Indeed, now I repent,’ nor for those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”* (Qur'an 4:17–18)

The man of 63:10 who asks to give charity and be righteous is, in 4:18's vocabulary, the man who says "now I repent" when death is present. The present verse supplies the metaphysical reason that "now" does not function as the earlier "now" did: the term has come. The examination is over. Extra answer-sheets are not handed out. This is not cruelty. It is the only way a term can be a term. An *ajal* that always extended when asked would not be an *ajal*. It would be a negotiation, and a life spent in negotiation with death is a life that never decides.

**The Two Hands of Divine Reprieve**

The source commentary notes the balance, and it is the balance of the whole religion. On the one hand, everyone is granted reprieve until the moment of death, and that grant is already enormous:

> *“If God were to take mankind to task for what they have earned, He would not leave upon the surface of the earth a single creature. But He grants them reprieve until an appointed term.”* (Qur'an 35:45)

The Prophet ﷺ reported God's own self-description in a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *“My mercy prevails over My wrath.”* (al-Bukhārī 7404; Muslim 2751)

On the other hand, those who treat that mercy as a plan — I will take the extra years as extra credit to be spent later — meet the second sentence of the doctrine: no extra hour when the hour is the hour. The punishment of such a plan is described elsewhere as the absence of lightening and the absence of further delay:

> *“The punishment shall not be lightened for them, nor shall they be granted respite.”* (Qur'an 2:162; cf. 3:88; 16:85; 21:40)

Mercy that could be gamed would cease to train anyone. It would become a loophole. The *ajal* is the loophole's closure. The believer is meant to feel both truths at once: I have been given time; I have not been given a promise that the time will still be here at sunset.

**“God Is Aware of Whatsoever You Do”**

The sūrah's last words are *wa Allāhu khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūn* — "and God is Aware of whatsoever you do." *Khabīr* is the Name of inner awareness: knowledge of the news of a thing, of its interior, of what is not on the surface. It is the right Name to close a chapter that began with a testimony God knew was a lie. The hypocrites' speech (v. 1), the shield-oaths (v. 2), the sealed heart (v. 3), the impressive bodies (v. 4), the twisted heads (v. 5), the refused prayer (v. 6), the planned famine (v. 7), the claim of might (v. 8), the diverted remembrance (v. 9), the ungiven charity (v. 10) — every item is *mā taʿmalūn*, "what you do," and none of it is off the books.

The Name also prevents a last mistake: the mistake of thinking that because the *ajal* is fixed, deeds do not matter until it arrives. If the term is fixed and God is *Khabīr*, then the years inside the term are the whole of the examination, not a waiting room. What He is aware of is not only the deathbed sentence. It is the Monday, the transaction, the glance, the withheld zakāh, the remembrance performed when it cost, the remembrance postponed when it cost. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *“Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your illness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death.”* (al-Ḥākim, from Ibn ʿAbbās; graded authentic by a number of the later hadith authorities and acted upon in meaning across the tradition)

That list is 63:11 taught as a calendar. Each "before" is a small *ajal* inside the large one.

**How This Ending Completes the Sūrah of the Hypocrites**

It is a precise authorship that ends a chapter named "The Hypocrites" not with a curse but with a deadline. Verses 1–8 have shown the coarse split of tongue and heart. Verses 9–10 have shown the fine split of intention and hour. Verse 11 shows the moment at which both splits become unrepairable. The hypocrite who thought he could manage God with oaths, and the believer who thought he could manage God with plans, meet the same *ajal*. God is not managed. He is *Khabīr*.

The pastoral effect, if the sūrah is read as it was meant to be recited — often, in the second rakʿah of Jumuʿah, after the encouragement of Sūrah al-Jumuʿah — is a complete movement of the soul in a few minutes of prayer. First: do not be the people of the double oath. Second: do not be the people of the postponed remembrance. Third: you will not be given the minute you are counting on. The Friday congregation walks out of the mosque into a market of property and children, which is exactly the market verse 9 named, under a sky of treasuries verse 7 named, toward an *ajal* verse 11 named. The sūrah's work is to make that walk awake.

**The Last Practical Word**

Do not leave the chapter as a student of Ibn Ubayy only. Leave it as a person with a term. Pay what is due before the due date that cannot be seen. Remember God before the passengers in the car become the windshield. Spend from the provision while the provision is still in the hand that was told it does not own the treasury. And let the Name *al-Khabīr* do what it is here to do: make the inner life feel observed — not with the flinch of verse 4, in which every cry is an accusation, but with the calm of a driver who knows the road is watched and therefore drives as one who intends to arrive.

The Prophet ﷺ used to seek refuge from the state this sūrah exists to prevent:

> *“O God, I seek refuge in You from anxiety and grief, from inability and laziness, from cowardice and miserliness, from being heavy in debt and from being overpowered by men.”* (al-Bukhārī 6369)

Miserliness, delay, the domination of other men's metrics of might — the refuge-prayer names the interior of this chapter. The sūrah names the same interior from God's side. Between the prayer and the sūrah a life can be straightened: one oath, one heart, one remembrance, one spending, before one term that will not move.
