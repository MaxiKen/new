# Sūrah al-Aʿlā (Chapter 87) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Sūrah of First Things**

Sūrah al-Aʿlā, "The Most High," is the eighty-seventh chapter of the Qur'an and one of its most compact masterpieces: nineteen verses in the standard count, entirely in the rhythm of early Makkan revelation, and recited so constantly in worship that its opening phrase became the name by which Muslims of every generation know it — they call it, simply, Sūrat Sabbih, "the Chapter of 'Glorify,'" after its first word. It belongs to the thirtieth and final part of the Qur'an (juzʾ ʿamma), the portion most memorized by children and most recited in daily prayer, and its position there is fitting: it gathers, in under a page, the whole arc of the Qur'anic message — the transcendence of God, the precision of creation, the gift of revelation, the freedom and destiny of the human being, the two destinations of the Fire and the Garden, and the confirmation that this teaching is no novelty but the ancient scroll-message entrusted to Abraham and Moses. It is a sūrah of first things: the first duty of the creature (glorification), the first act of the Creator (creation and guidance), the first gift to the Prophet (a revelation he will not forget), and the first question put to every soul (which life will you prefer?). In nineteen verses the Qur'an compresses a complete theology, a complete psychology, and a complete eschatology.

The name al-Aʿlā is taken from the final words of its very first verse — "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" — and it is one of the names of God Himself, the superlative of the high: the One above every height that any mind can scale or any language can name. Early manuscripts and hadith reports also refer to the chapter by its first phrase, "Sabbih isma rabbika al-aʿlā," and the great reciters and jurists knew it as the sūrah of the prostration-verse of glorification, because it supplies the very formula a Muslim speaks in the deepest posture of worship: subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā, "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High." The sūrah's name is thus not a label attached from outside but a command placed at its own threshold — the reader cannot even pronounce the title without performing the act the chapter exists to teach.

**When It Was Revealed: The Eighth Step of a New Dawn**

The overwhelming majority of the scholars of Qur'anic chronology class al-Aʿlā as an early Makkan sūrah. Traditional orderings of revelation — the lists preserved from the earliest generations and refined by later scholars such as al-Zarkashī and Ibn Ḥajar — commonly place it eighth in the sequence of revelation, revealed in the first Makkan years, shortly after Sūrah al-Takwīr (81) and in the same period as the chapters that address the very beginning of the prophetic mission: the command to warn, the consolation of revelation, and the refutation of the pagans' charge that Muhammad had invented the Qur'an. The internal evidence agrees. The sūrah speaks to a messenger still being trained in the reception of revelation — "We shall make you recite, so that you do not forget" (87:6) — a concern that belongs to the earliest days when Gabriel's visits were new and the Prophet, as the commentators report, would hasten to repeat the words before the angel had finished, afraid of losing them. Its addresses are to the Prophet alone and to humanity at large, with no legislation for a functioning community, no detailed law, no mention of the believers as an organized polity — the marks of the first Makkan period. And its close, which locates the same message in "the scrolls of Abraham and Moses" (87:18–19), reflects the Meccan argument of those years: the Qur'an answered the charge of novelty by appealing to the ancient chain of revelation, exactly as it does in the roughly contemporary Sūrah al-Najm (53:36–37).

There is, however, a notable minority position, and it deserves honest statement because it touches the meaning of verses 14–15. Some commentators — and the source tradition records this especially from narrations reaching back to the family of the Prophet — held that the final portion of the sūrah, with its mention of "he who purifies himself" and the prayer, was revealed in the Madinan period, since they understood the purification of which the verse speaks to be the alms of ʿĪd al-Fiṭr and the prayer to be the ʿĪd prayer, institutions that took shape only after the migration to Madinah. On this view the sūrah would be composite: an early Makkan core with a late Madinan seal. The great majority, however — and this includes commentators like al-Baghawī who examined the question directly — answered that verses 14–15 can refer perfectly well to the universal duties of self-purification, the remembrance of God, and the five daily prayers, which were obligatory in Makkah itself (the five prayers having been established, in the soundest view, from the earliest period of the mission, with the nightly prayer and the two daily prayers antedating the Miʿrāj). On this reading the whole sūrah is Makkan, and its verses about the ʿĪd alms, if any reader takes them that way, are simply prophecy of practices that would later realize the verse's general principle. The disagreement is instructive in itself: it shows that the earliest Muslims read their own liturgical history into the text, and that the text was capacious enough to carry both readings.

**The Sūrah the Prophet Loved to Recite**

No sūrah of the Qur'an has a more continuous presence in the Prophet's documented prayer than al-Aʿlā. The reports are consistent across the collections and were acted upon by the community for fourteen centuries. On the two great festivals — ʿĪd al-Fiṭr, closing Ramadan, and ʿīd al-Aḍḥā, closing the pilgrimage — and in the Friday congregational prayer, the Prophet regularly recited Sūrah al-Aʿlā together with its sister chapter Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah (88). Muslim records in his Ṣaḥīḥ, on the authority of al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr and Samurah ibn Jundub:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to recite, in the two ʿĪds and on Friday, 'Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High' (Sūrah 87) and 'Has there come to you the narration of the Overwhelming' (Sūrah 88). And when the ʿĪd and Friday fell on the same day, he would recite both sūrahs in both prayers."* (Muslim 878)

The same chapter was a staple of the night worship. Reports preserved in the Sunan collections describe the Prophet reciting "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High," together with Sūrah al-Kāfirūn and Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ, in the witr prayer with which he closed his night prayers, and he is reported to have commended these same two sūrahs — 87 and 88 — to his companions for their evening prayer. The constant use of the chapter in the most public liturgies of the week (Friday), of the year (the two ʿĪds), and of the night (witr) means that for a practicing Muslim, al-Aʿlā is heard more often than almost any other passage of the Book — a design of Providence, the commentators note, for the chapter whose first verse orders the glorification of the Most High is thus glorified, in practice, thousands of times over a lifetime. In the Shiite tradition the chapter's rank was expressed with striking amplitude: it is related, according to the source tradition, that ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib said that if people knew what blessing lies in al-Aʿlā, every person would recite it ten times each day, and that one who recites it has, in effect, recited the Qur'an and the scrolls of Moses and Abraham — an allusion to the sūrah's own closing claim that its message is "in the scrolls of Abraham and Moses" (87:18–19). The report, whatever its exact chain, encodes a theological conviction shared by all schools: the chapter is a distillation, and to hold the distillation is to hold, in principle, the whole.

**The Architecture: Five Movements in Nineteen Verses**

The sūrah's nineteen verses arrange themselves into five movements of almost sculptural symmetry.

The first movement (verses 1–5) is pure theology ascending: the command to glorify the Name of the Most High, followed by four attributes that unfold what His being means for creation — He created, He proportioned, He determined and guided, He brings forth the pasture and turns it to stubble. The movement moves from the transcendent Name to the nearest blade of grass, from the One above all worlds to the green world at our feet — and its point is that the same Hand does both.

The second movement (verses 6–9) turns from creation to revelation: God addresses His Prophet directly, promising him a recitation he will not forget, an eased path, and a mission whose only duty is to remind. These are among the earliest divine assurances given to the messenger, and they answer the human anxieties that attend the first reception of the Qur'an — the fear of forgetting, the weight of the task, the doubt of effectiveness.

The third movement (verses 10–13) draws the human map: the reminder will be received by him who fears, and shunned by the most wretched — the one who will enter the greatest Fire, where he neither dies nor lives. Two verses for each destiny, balanced like scales.

The fourth movement (verses 14–17) delivers the sūrah's ethical core in two matched pictures: prosperity belongs to the one who purifies himself, remembers his Lord, and prays; while "nay, but you prefer the life of this world" stands as the diagnosis of failure, "while the Hereafter is better and more enduring" as the cure. The movement pivots on a preference — the Qur'an's most characteristic diagnosis of the human tragedy is not ignorance but preference, a will that chooses the lesser good.

The fifth movement (verses 18–19) seals the whole: "Truly this is in the earlier scrolls, the scrolls of Abraham and Moses." The message of purification, prayer, and the preference of the Hereafter is not a new invention; it is the oldest teaching of the oldest prophets, now renewed in Arabic.

Notice the elegance of the arrangement: the sūrah opens with God's Name being glorified and closes with God's Name being remembered in prayer (verse 15 is the structural hinge of the whole); it opens with creation (2–5) and closes with scripture (18–19), the two books — the book of nature and the book of revelation — that testify to the same Author; and at its exact center stands the human choice, verses 10–17, between fear and wretchedness, purification and worldliness. The sūrah thus moves from heaven down to the grass, from the grass up to the Prophet, from the Prophet to the two human destinies, and from the present revelation back to Abraham and Moses — a complete circle of descent and return in nineteen verses.

**The Thesis of the Sūrah: Worship That Purifies Against a World That Passes**

If one were to compress the whole argument of al-Aʿlā into a single sentence, it would be this: the creature's only fitting response to the Most High — who created him, proportioned him, determined his measure, and guided him — is to purify himself, remember his Lord, and pray, thereby prospering in a life better and more enduring than the one he can see and touch. Everything in the sūrah serves that sentence. The creation verses (2–5) establish the ground of gratitude and trust: the same God who proportions every creature and feeds every pasture will not abandon the servant who turns to Him. The revelation verses (6–9) establish the ground of hope: the guidance has been sent, preserved, and eased. The destiny verses (10–13) establish the ground of urgency: the alternative to remembrance is a Fire in which one neither dies nor lives. And the closing verses (18–19) establish the ground of certainty: this path is the ancient path, worn smooth by every prophet, and the one who walks it walks in the company of Abraham and Moses.

There is also a quiet but profound connection between the sūrah's opening and its middle that the commentators loved to trace. The first verse commands glorification of the Name "the Most High" — al-Aʿlā — and the fourteenth verse promises prosperity to "him who purifies himself" — tazakkā. Both verbs share the sense of elevation: tasbīḥ declares God high above every deficiency, while tazkiyah raises the soul above its own lower drives. The sūrah's grammar is thus a mirror: man cannot lift himself up by pretending to be high; he lifts himself up only by declaring the Most High high, and letting that declaration reorder his loves. Self-purification begins with God-glorification; the soul rises by looking up, not by looking at itself. This single insight — that human elevation is a by-product of divine glorification — is the spiritual engine of the entire chapter, and it explains why the sūrah that orders the believer to say "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High" in prostration, the lowest posture of the body, is at the same time the sūrah that promises the highest success: *qad aflaḥa man tazakkā* — he has prospered who purified himself. The body goes down; the soul goes up; and the pivot of the exchange is the Name.

**How This Commentary Proceeds**

Following the method fixed for this entire project, the commentary below treats every verse of the sūrah as an independent discourse, in strict order, from verse 1 to verse 19. Each verse is quoted individually — the source commentary's groupings (2–3, 4–5, 10–11, and so on) are here split so that every single verse receives its own full treatment, exactly as the workflow requires. Each section draws on the Arabic language and its roots, on the classical commentators (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Qayyim, and others), on the authenticated words of the Prophet with their collections identified, on the logical structure of the argument, and on the application of the verse to the psychology and practice of modern life. Nothing has been compressed because the sūrah is short; on the contrary, its brevity is the reason every verse can receive the full expansion it deserves. The chapter that Muslims recite at every festival and every Friday night deserves to be understood the way its first audience understood it: as a complete education in the Name of the Most High.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:1

> **Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command That Opens the Chapter and Names It**

*Sabbiḥ isma rabbika al-aʿlā* — the Qur'an's opening verb here, *sabbiḥ*, is the imperative of the second form of the root *s-b-ḥ*, and the word carries a striking history. The root's original sense, preserved by the lexicographers, is that of moving through water or air — the swimming of a fish, the movement of a horse in water — from which it came to mean being far from something, and then, in the religious vocabulary, declaring God far above and free of every deficiency: *tasbīḥ* is the assertion that God transcends whatever the mind of man might wrongly attach to Him — partners, offspring, weakness, injustice, weariness, likeness to creation. When the Qur'an says the heavens "glorify" God (57:1; 59:1; 61:1; 62:1; 64:1), it means that the universe itself testifies to the perfection of its Maker; when it commands the believer to glorify, it commands him to align his tongue, his mind, and his life with that universal testimony:

> *"The seven heavens and the earth and all that is within them glorify Him. And there is not a thing but that it glorifies His praise, but you do not understand their glorification."* (Qur'an 17:44)

The verse is one of a family of commands that punctuate the Makkan revelation — "So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Magnificent" (56:74; 69:52); "Glorify the praise of your Lord and be among those who prostrate" (15:98) — and it stands at the head of this sūrah as the foundation stone of everything that follows. Before the sūrah speaks of creation, revelation, or destiny, it commands glorification: the creature's first word to its Lord is not a request but an acknowledgment.

The grammarians and commentators — al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī among them — long debated the precise syntax: does "the Most High" (al-aʿlā) describe the Lord, so that the sense is simply "glorify your Lord, who is the Most High"? Or does it describe the Name, so that the sense is "glorify the Name that is most high — the name al-Aʿlā itself"? The majority preferred the first reading: the verse is a command to glorify God. But the second reading has a profound consequence that al-Rāzī develops at length: if the adjective qualifies the Name, then the verse instructs us about the manner of glorification — that we should not associate the Name of God with anything else, that we should not invoke it over false gods or worldly schemes, and that we should praise God by the Names He has revealed for Himself, calling upon Him as He has taught:

> *"Say: Call upon God, or call upon the Compassionate — whichever you call upon, to Him belong the most beautiful Names."* (Qur'an 17:110)

**Why the Name? The Logic of Glorifying What Is Named**

A modern reader may wonder why the verse does not simply say "glorify your Lord" and instead inserts the Name — "glorify the Name of your Lord." The classical commentators give several converging reasons. First, the Name is the means of address: we cannot stand before the Essence itself, but we can stand before the revealed Name; glorifying the Name is the humanly possible form of glorifying the Named. Second, the Name is what the servant actually utters: the command is to make the tongue, the daily instrument of praise and of slander alike, a vehicle of tanzīh — declaring God's transcendence — so that the same faculty by which man errs becomes the faculty by which he is saved. Third — and this was the insight of the spiritual commentators — the mention of the Name carries a promise of divine response, for the Prophet reported that God has said:

> *"I am with My servant's thought of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me: if he remembers Me in himself, I remember him in Myself; and if he remembers Me in an assembly, I remember him in a better assembly."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675)

There is also a subtle polemical edge to the phrase in its Makkan setting. The pagans of Makkah named their idols and attached divine names to them — al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, al-Manāt — and the Qur'an's insistence on "the Name of your Lord, the Most High" draws a line of purity around the Name: this Name belongs to One alone, and glorifying it means refusing to let it be spoken over anything else. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his discussions of the divine Names, made the same point doctrinally: the Names of God are not human inventions to be tossed about but revealed designations (tawqīfiyyah), and the first honor we owe them is not to apply them wrongly.

**Al-Aʿlā: The Name That Ends All Comparison**

The adjective al-aʿlā is the elative of ʿalā — to be high — and it means the Highest, the Most High, the One than whom nothing is higher. In the Qur'an it occurs in the very formula of prostration, and the tradition records that the Prophet made this Name the content of the deepest posture of the body. When the verse of Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah — "So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Magnificent" (56:74) — was revealed, the Prophet told his Companions to enact it in the bowing (rukūʿ); and when the present verse, "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High," was revealed, he told them to enact it in the prostration (sujūd). The reports are preserved in the Sunan collections from Ibn ʿAbbās:

> *"When 'So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Magnificent' was revealed, the Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'Make it in your bowing.' And when 'Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High' was revealed, he said: 'Make it in your prostration.'"* (Abū Dāwūd 869; Ibn Mājah 897)

The practice settled into the worship of the community: the worshipper says, in the bow, "Glory be to my Lord, the Magnificent" (subḥāna rabbiya al-ʿaẓīm), and, in the prostration, "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High" (subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā), three times each — the very phraseology reported from the Prophet's own prayer in the authentic collections:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to say in his bowing and prostration: 'Glory be to my Lord, the Magnificent,' and 'Glory be to my Lord, the Most High.'"* (Abū Dāwūd 871; al-Tirmidhī 261–262)

The choice of posture is itself a theology. The body, which can stand upright in pride, folds itself to the ground at the very moment it pronounces the Most High — the height of the Name is measured, as it were, by the depth of the bow. The Prophet said, in a saying recorded by Muslim:

> *"The nearest that a servant comes to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so multiply supplication."* (Muslim 482)

And glorification is not only a prayer-posture; it is a life-posture. The authentic collections record the Prophet's description of two phrases that summarize this verse's work in a single breath:

> *"Two words that are light on the tongue, heavy in the Balance, beloved to the Compassionate: 'Glory be to God and with His praise; glory be to God, the Magnificent.'"* (al-Bukhārī 7563; Muslim 2694)

**What Glorification Does to the One Who Glorifies**

There is a psychology hidden in the command. The human mind has two characteristic diseases regarding what is above it: it either drags the high down to its own level (fashioning gods in its image, as the pagans did with their idols and as every age does with its pantheon of celebrities, states, and appetites) or it denies that anything is high at all (reducing the universe to matter in motion, with nothing to reverence). Tasbīḥ is the exact discipline against both. To say "Glory be to God, the Most High" with understanding is to break the habit of comparison — God is not the greatest among competitors, but high above the category of competition altogether; and it is equally to break the habit of cynicism — there is, after all, something truly High, and the human soul, which was made for reverence, has found its only worthy object. Psychologically, the daily repetition of the formula in prayer functions as a repeated recalibration of the self's sense of scale: five times a day the worshipper places his forehead on the ground and names the Most High, and each repetition quietly dissolves the illusion that any earthly power, any bank balance, any reputation, or any tribulation is the highest thing in his world. The proud man is the man who has forgotten the Name; the humble man is the man whose tongue has kept it. This is why the sūrah begins where it does: every other instruction in the chapter — purify, remember, pray, prefer the Hereafter — will fail unless the soul has first settled the question of scale by glorifying the Most High. The verse is the root; the rest of the chapter is the tree; and the Name at the root is the Name the worshipper still pronounces, nineteen times in every prostration, fourteen centuries later.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:2

> **Who created, then proportioned,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Name to the Work: Why Glorification Leads to Creation**

The first verse commanded glorification; the second and third now give the reasons for it, in the Qur'an's characteristic manner — the command is followed by the evidence that makes it rational. The sequence is worth pausing on: God does not ask for blind praise but for praise that arises from understanding His work. *Alladhī khalaqa fa-sawwā* — "Who created, then proportioned." The relative clause reaches back to "your Lord": the One glorified is the One who created, and the One who created is the One who proportioned. Al-Ṭabarī and the classical exegetes note that the creation spoken of here is general in wording but that the immediate frame is the human being — created from nothing, then shaped, balanced, and finished — though the verse also embraces animals and, in the widest reading preferred by several commentators, all of creation, since everything that exists displays the double act of coming-to-be and being-brought-to-measure.

The two verbs describe two distinct acts, and the "then" (fa) between them marks a sequence of stages. *Khalaqa* — from the root *kh-l-q*, whose oldest sense is to measure and to determine — means to bring into existence from nothing, to originate. *Sawwā* — the second form of *s-w-y*, from the root meaning levelness and equality — means to level, to complete, to fashion into due proportion, to give a thing the finished form proper to it. Creation answers the question of existence: that a thing is. Proportioning answers the question of form: that it is as it should be. The Qur'an uses the pair repeatedly for the human being — "He created you, then proportioned you, then balanced you" (82:7); "He Who created you and fashioned you and proportioned you aright" (64:3) — and gives the human form a name of honor: "Surely We created man in the fairest stature" (95:4). The verses describe, in the case of the first man, the fashioning of Adam's body and the breathing into it of God's Spirit (15:29; 38:72), after which — as the commentators explain — the human form was left without irregularity or flaw, "the fairest stature."

**The Argument from Design: Proportion as Evidence**

There is a complete natural theology folded into this one verb pair. Consider what "proportioning" means in the world we actually inhabit. Every living thing comes into being and, simultaneously, arrives already fitted to its function: the bird arrives with wings whose curves are computed for its air; the fish with gills matched to its water; the eye with its lens, the ear with its drum, the heart with its four chambers — each organ a finished structure in which no part could be removed without collapse. The Qur'an invites the reader to contemplate precisely this double fact — that things exist, and that existing things are fitted — as the signature of a Creator who both originates and perfects:

> *"O man, what has deceived you concerning your Generous Lord, Who created you, then proportioned you, then balanced you, composing you in whatever form He willed?"* (Qur'an 82:6–8)

The logical force is simple and strong. Existence alone might be imagined as accident; but existence plus proportion — form fitted to function, structure matched to need, beauty superimposed on utility — is what engineers call design, and design implies a designer who measured. The Qur'an's word for this is precise: even the verb khalaqa carries the nuance of measuring, as though creation itself were an act of setting dimensions. The creature that exists is a fact; the creature that is proportioned is an argument. Al-Rāzī, in his great commentary, treats this verse as one of the Qur'an's foundational proofs of the existence and wisdom of the Creator: things are not merely present but present in measure, and measure is the signature of mind. He notes further that the "then" of sequence — created, then proportioned — corresponds to what the Qur'an elsewhere describes as the staged development of the embryo, from a drop of fluid to a clinging clot to a lump of flesh, then to bones, then to the clothing of bones with flesh:

> *"Then We made the drop into a clinging clot, then We made the clot into a lump, then We made the lump into bones, then We clothed the bones with flesh; then We produced it as another creation. So blessed be God, the Best of creators!"* (Qur'an 23:14)

The authentic hadith on the earliest stage of this process was transmitted by Ibn Masʿūd, who reported the Messenger of God as saying — and it is among the most frequently quoted hadith on creation:

> *"Each of you is gathered in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop of fluid, then for a like period as a clinging clot, then for a like period as a lump of flesh, then the angel is sent, and he breathes into him the spirit, and he is commanded with four words: to write his provision, his term, his deeds, and whether he is wretched or blessed."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

**Why the Human Being Is the Center of the Frame**

If the verse is general, why have the commentators — and the tradition generally — read the human being into its center? Because the sūrah's argument needs it. The next verses will speak of guidance and of pasture and of human destiny; the whole chapter is addressed to human beings; and so the chapter begins its evidence with the creature most immediately at hand: the reader himself. "Who created, then proportioned" is, in effect, a mirror held up before the reader's own face — you did not make yourself, and you are not a chaos; you were made, and made well. The psychological effect of this address is worth marking: it places every human being, whatever his belief, before a personal claim. It is not an argument about the origins of galaxies, which can be contemplated at a safe distance; it is an argument about the origin of the reader, which cannot. The verse's genius is that the evidence is always with us — we carry it around in the geometry of our own hands. Every time a human being marvels at the coordination of eye and hand, or at the recovery of a body from illness, or at the birth of a child with ten fingers and ten toes, he is repeating, unawares, the content of this verse: created, then proportioned.

The classical commentators add a further nuance about the order of the two acts: why "created, then proportioned" and not simply "created proportioned"? Because the two acts are distinct in kind and in time. To create is to give existence; to proportion is to perfect existence — and the Qur'an elsewhere warns that existence can precede perfection, as the embryo precedes the child and the child precedes the mature man. The sequence "created, then proportioned" thus quietly teaches two truths at once: that God's act is gradual in its unfolding (nothing in creation springs to its final form in an instant — even the heavens and the earth were created in six days, 7:54), and that the creature's duty is gratitude for the completion of its making. The human being did not exist; then existed; then was finished — and the finished form, "the fairest stature," is itself a favor:

> *"God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and gave you hearing and sight and hearts, that haply you might give thanks."* (Qur'an 16:78)

**The Verse as a Discipline of Wonder**

There is a final, practical register in which the verse has always functioned in Muslim life: as a cure for the two opposite diseases of modern consciousness — the disease of self-contempt and the disease of self-worship. Self-contempt says: I am an accident, unlovely, unbidden, without design or dignity. The verse answers: you were created, then proportioned — you exist by intent and are formed by design; the fairest stature is your endowment, and defacing it by despair is ingratitude to the One who proportioned it. Self-worship says: I am the architect of myself, the author of my own measure. The verse answers: "Who created, then proportioned" — the verb's subject is God alone, and the human being stands in the sentence not as the doer but as the done-to; the proper response to one's own existence is not pride in the clay but praise of the Potter. Between these two diseases the verse clears a narrow ridge of truth: you are not nothing, and you are not everything; you are a created thing, and a proportioned one — which means you owe your being and your form to Another, and the fitting response to both gifts is the glorification commanded in the first verse. The sūrah's logic is seamless: because He created, glorify Him; because He proportioned, glorify Him — and because He proportioned you as the fairest of stature, glorify Him with a life that honors the form He gave.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:3

> **Who determined the measure, then guided,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Attribute: Everything Has Its Measure**

*Alladhī qaddara fa-hadā* — "Who determined the measure, then guided." The verse completes a triplet of divine acts: creation (existence), proportioning (form), and now measuring-and-guiding (destiny and direction). *Qaddara* is the second form of the root *q-d-r*, the root of power (qudrah) and of measure (qadar); its second form intensifies, so that qaddara means to measure out exactly, to assign to each thing its precise scope, capacity, and term. The verse admits a second canonical reading: *qadara*, from the same root, meaning simply "He had power over" — in which case the clause teaches that after creating and proportioning, God retained direct power over all He made. Both readings converge on a single doctrine: nothing in creation is left to chance, nothing is unmeasured, and nothing escapes the power of its Maker. The measure of a thing is written into its nature — the span of its life, the reach of its strength, the limits of its habitat, the season of its flowering — and the Qur'an states this as a universal law:

> *"And there is not a thing but that with Us are its treasuries, and We do not send it down except in a known measure."* (Qur'an 15:21)

> *"Indeed, We have created everything in measure."* (Qur'an 54:49)

The verse's "then" (fa) is again significant. The commentators explain the sequence: God proportioned the creature's form, then measured out its capacities and its needs, then guided it to the use of both. Form answers the question of structure; measure answers the question of scope; guidance answers the question of direction. A creature with a stomach but no food would starve; a creature with eyes but no light would grope; a creature with capacities but no instinct to deploy them would perish in its first hour. The verse teaches that none of these is left dangling: the same Hand that built the instrument also tuned its range and supplied its compass. It is, in modern terms, a claim of total calibration — the universe is not merely designed but provisioned; not merely ordered but directed.

**Then Guided: The Many Levels of Divine Direction**

*Hadā* — "He guided" — is one of the richest words in the Qur'anic vocabulary, and the commentators distinguish the levels of guidance that this single verb carries. There is the guidance of instinct: the newly hatched turtle that moves toward the sea, the bee that returns to its hive by the sun and builds its hexagons, the spider that spins its first web without ever having seen one, the newborn that finds its mother's milk in its first hour. The Qur'an points to this universal instinct-guidance in the words of Moses, answering Pharaoh's question about the Lord of the worlds:

> *"He said: Our Lord is He Who gave everything its creation, then guided it."* (Qur'an 20:50)

There is the guidance of sensory perception, by which the animal locates its food and flees its predator. There is the guidance of reason, by which the human being infers from the ordered world the existence of its Orderer. And above all there is the guidance of revelation — the sending of prophets and books, by which the human being is guided not merely to survive but to be saved. The commentators note that the verse's sequence — measure, then guidance — is visible at every level: the bee's measure (a life of weeks, a body built for flight and wax) is matched by its guidance (the dance, the hive, the forage); the human measure (a life of decades, a soul built for meaning and worship) is matched by its guidance (the prophets, the Books, the conscience). The Prophet's own summary of the two guidances — the general and the religious — is preserved in his answer to a question about the decree, transmitted by Muslim from ʿAlī and by other collections from other Companions:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ was asked about the decree, and he said: 'God created everything, then measured it, then guided it.'"* — the wording reported by al-Ṭabarānī and others from ʿAlī; the sense is confirmed by the verse itself and by the Qur'an's statement that God guides whom He wills and leads astray whom He wills (14:4), a guidance that in the case of mankind operates through the sending of messengers.

The deepest formulation, however, is that of the Qur'an itself in the sūrah of the soul:

> *"By the soul and Him Who proportioned it, and inspired it with its wickedness and its righteousness. He has succeeded who purifies it, and he has failed who buries it."* (Qur'an 91:7–10)

The human being is not only guided from outside — by instinct, sense, reason, and revelation — but from within: the soul itself is created with an inborn compass that distinguishes its corruption from its soundness, a guidance the Qur'an elsewhere calls the primordial nature, the fiṭrah. The authentic hadith describes this native endowment and its fate at the hands of environment:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah; then his parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian — just as the camel is born whole: do you perceive any mutilation?"* (al-Bukhārī 1385; Muslim 2658)

**Measure, Guidance, and the Question of Human Freedom**

A verse about divine measuring naturally raises the question that every thinking reader will ask: if God has measured everything and guides whom He wills, what room remains for human choice and responsibility? The classical theologians debated precisely this ground, and the Qur'an's own witness draws the boundaries with care. On one side stands the universality of the divine decree: nothing occurs outside God's knowledge and will, and the Qur'an says plainly that "no calamity strikes the earth or your selves but that it is in a Book before We bring it into being" (57:22). On the other side stands the reality of human agency and accountability: the same Qur'an that speaks of the decree commands, forbids, rewards, and punishes, and addresses the human being as a responsible chooser — "Whoever wills, let him take the path to his Lord" (76:29). The two are reconciled in the tradition's mature formulations by distinguishing what God has measured from what He has commanded: the decree concerns the conditions of the act — its occasion, its capacity, its term — while the act itself remains the creature's, truly chosen and truly owned. Ibn al-Qayyim, the great expositor of this question, argued that the divine measure is not a chain dragging the will but a framework within which the will genuinely moves, like a river that is measured in its banks but free in its flow. The verse's own sequence supports the distinction: it does not say "He compelled, then drove," but "He measured, then guided" — and guidance is precisely the kind of direction that presupposes a creature capable of following. A stone needs no guidance; a compass-bearing soul does. The very existence of the word "then guided" in the verse is, on this reading, the Qur'an's quiet acknowledgment that the crown of creation is a being who can respond to direction with obedience or defiance — and who will be asked, in the end, which he chose.

**The Practical Yield: Trust and Effort in One Verse**

For daily life the verse yields a double discipline that the commentators never tired of expounding. The first is trust: because everything is measured by God, the believer is released from the anxiety of controlling outcomes. The provision that will reach him, the term that awaits him, the trials that will visit him — all are in a Book before they occur, and the Prophet said, in a saying recorded by Muslim:

> *"The pen has been lifted, and the pages have dried."* — meaning that what has been decreed is settled, and the believer's task is not to micromanage fate but to meet it with faith and action. (Muslim; from Abū Hurayrah, the wording of the decree-hadith)

The second is effort: because God has guided, the believer is summoned to walk the guided path. Measure answers the question "what is written?" with a horizon of trust; guidance answers the question "what is required?" with a map of action. The two belong together, and the Prophet's ﷺ instruction to his Companion is the classic summary: act, for everyone is eased toward what he was created for. When a man asked whether he should rely on the decree and abandon effort, the Prophet replied:

> *"Work, for everyone is eased toward that for which he was created."* (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2647)

The verse before us is, in fact, the source of the very ease the hadith speaks of: God measured, then guided — and the creature who accepts the measure and follows the guidance lives in the center of the divine intention. The scientist who studies the measured laws of nature, the farmer who plants in the measured seasons, the believer who obeys the guided path of revelation — all are living out the two verbs of this verse: qaddara, the world's calibration; hadā, the soul's compass. And both return the reader to the opening command: glorify the One whose measure never fails and whose guidance never misleads.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:4

> **and Who brings forth the pasture,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Heights to the Herbage: The Scope of the Sustaining Hand**

After three verses that scaled the heights of divine action — creation, proportioning, measuring, guiding — the fourth verse descends to the ground beneath our feet: *wa-alladhī akhraja al-marʿā* — "and Who brings forth the pasture." The drop is deliberate and theologically loaded. The Most High, whose Name the first verse commanded us to glorify, is also the One who splits the seed and raises the grass on which the grazing animal feeds. Al-Rāzī notes the beauty of the descent: the Qur'an moves from the heavens to the herbage in four verses to teach that divine care is not a distant administration of cosmic law but a proximate, continuous provision for the humblest needs of the humblest creatures. The God of the sūrah is both the Most High (al-aʿlā) and the Bringer-forth of pasture (al-mukhrij al-marʿā) — height and nearness in a single sentence, transcendence and provision in a single breath.

*Marʿā*, rendered "pasture," is from the root *r-ʿ-y*, the root of shepherding and grazing; it denotes whatever green thing is cropped by grazing animals — the grass of the field, the herbage of the steppe, and by extension, as the commentators note, all forms of vegetation that sustain life. The word's grammatical form (a verbal noun used as a noun of place and object) carries the sense of "that which is grazed," the standing crop of the earth. The Qur'an elsewhere uses the same root to describe the whole economy of rain and growth:

> *"And We sent down from the rain-laden clouds abundant water, that We may bring forth thereby grain and vegetation, and gardens thick with trees."* (Qur'an 78:14–16)

> *"Let man then consider his food: that We pour down water in abundance, then We cleave the earth in clefts, and We bring forth therein grain, and grapes, and fresh vegetation."* (Qur'an 80:24–28)

**The Invisible Chain: Rain, Soil, Seed, and the Hand Behind Them**

Consider what the verse is asking the reader to see. Pasture does not bring itself forth. The chain of its existence runs through rain from the sky, soil opened by the season, seed placed in darkness, warmth measured by the turning year — and at the head of the chain stands an act that no link in the chain can perform: the original command that made the whole cycle run. The pre-Islamic Arabs, the first audience of this verse, were a people of the desert who knew exactly how much hung between pasture and death: their herds, their milk, their meat, their very movement between water-holes followed the whim of the rain. For them, "Who brings forth the pasture" was not a poetic generality but the most practical sentence in the world — a statement about where their next meal would come from. The Qur'an repeatedly grounds its appeal in this elemental dependence:

> *"Have they not seen how We drive water to the barren land and bring forth thereby crops from which their cattle and they themselves eat? Will they not see?"* (Qur'an 32:27)

The verse before us is that appeal in miniature. And its placement here — immediately after the verse of measuring and guiding — carries a precise logic: the same God who measured the creature's needs is the God who provides their supply; the same God who guided the bee to the flower is the God who grows the flower. Measure without supply would be a mockery; provision without measure would be chaos. Together, qaddara and akhraja — He measured, and He brings forth — form the Qur'an's doctrine of rizq: provision is not an accident of the market or a conquest of the strong but a divine dispensation that reaches every creature, as the Prophet's ﷺ report of God's own words affirms:

> *"God said: O My servants, ... it is I who provide for you; and provision is by My measure, and the whole matter is in My hand."* — the sense of the hadith qudsī on providence transmitted in the collections from Abū Dharr and others, echoing the Qur'an's own assurance: *"And there is no creature on the earth but that upon God is its provision."* (Qur'an 11:6)

**The Pasture as a Sign of the Resurrection**

The classical commentators — and here the sūrah's own sequel is the best guide — read the bringing-forth of pasture not only as daily sustenance but as an annual resurrection. The earth that bears green in the spring was, months earlier, a dead expanse of dust; the same power that raises the grass from the dead ground is the power that will raise the dead from their graves. The Qur'an states the analogy explicitly where it describes the reawakening of barren land:

> *"And you see the earth barren; then when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and puts forth every lovely pair of growths. That is because God is the Truth, and because He brings to life the dead, and because He is over all things Competent — and because the Hour is coming, no doubt of it, and because God will raise those who are in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7)

The green pasture is thus a standing proof of the two doctrines the sūrah will soon need: that God can restore what has perished (for the resurrection), and that God provides for what He has created (for the trust of the worshipper). Every spring is an argument; every grazing field is a resurrection in miniature. Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this family of verses, observes that God has made the revival of the dead earth the Qur'an's most frequently repeated evidence for the revival of the dead, precisely because it is the evidence most constantly before our eyes — we do not need to travel to a miracle; we need only to walk to a pasture.

**What the Verse Asks of the Modern Reader**

Modern life has done something remarkable to our relation with this verse: it has hidden the pasture. Food arrives packaged, meat arrives portioned, and the connection between the rain and the sandwich has been severed by a thousand intermediaries — so the dependence this verse announces has become, for many, invisible. The verse's contemporary application is therefore partly an act of reconnection: to eat with the awareness that the chain of provision begins in an act of divine bringing-forth; to see, in every market stall, the far fields and the rain that filled them; to recover what the Qur'an calls remembrance of the provision. This is why the Qur'anic ethic of food begins with the Name: the basmalah over the meal is the acknowledgment that the pasture's Lord is the table's Lord, and the Prophet ﷺ taught that food over which the Name of God has been mentioned is food received in gratitude rather than taken by forgetfulness. The verse also carries a social edge that the classical commentators did not fail to draw: if God is the Bringer-forth of pasture for all His creatures, then the hoarding of provision against the need of others is a practical denial of the verse — a claim that the pasture is mine by my own power rather than God's by His. The one who glorifies the Bringer-forth shares the brought-forth; the one who forgets the Bringer-forth clutches. In the sūrah's architecture, this verse is the green base of the ladder whose top is the Name: from the grass that feeds the herd, the argument climbs back to the Most High who feeds all — and the creature who sees the pasture and does not rise to the Name has missed the point of the grass.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:5

> **then makes it dark, withered refuse.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Cycle: Beauty Handed Over to Decay**

*Fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan aḥwā* — "then He makes it dark, withered refuse." The green pasture of verse 4 does not remain green. In the span of a single season the living field is turned by the same Hand that raised it into ghuthāʾ — the Arabic word for the dried scum and debris that a flood carries on its surface and leaves strewn on the banks — rendered here as refuse, withered stubble; and aḥwā, from the root *ḥ-w-y*, describes the blackish, dusky color into which green herbage turns as it dries and rots — the dark, mottled stain of decay. The verse's image is exact and farm-true: lush green does not fade to clean yellow only; it blackens, streaks, and collapses into litter. The Qur'an paints the same cycle elsewhere with deliberate fullness:

> *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion and adornment and boasting among you and rivalry in increase of wealth and children — like the likeness of rain whose vegetation pleases the farmers; then it dries up and you see it turning yellow, then it becomes chaff."* (Qur'an 57:20)

> *"And strike for them the likeness of the life of this world: like water which We send down from the sky, and the vegetation of the earth mingles with it, then it becomes dry stubble that the winds scatter."* (Qur'an 18:45)

The pair of verses before us — bring forth, then make refuse — thus states, in two strokes, the entire career of every living green thing, and the commentators were unanimous that the image is not botanical only but existential: the pasture is the world, and the world's greenest promises are the promises that blacken. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī read the two verses as a single memento mori: look at the field in spring and you see what the world promises; look at it in autumn and you see what the world delivers. The power that turned the green to refuse is the same power that will turn every living thing to dust, and the same power again that will raise both the field and the dust — which is why this pair of verses sits exactly between the doctrine of creation (verses 2–3) and the doctrine of revelation and destiny (verses 6–13): the cycle of the pasture is the argument that the present order of things is not final, that beauty is not permanent, and that the world which cannot keep its own grass green cannot be the world the soul was made for.

**Why the Qur'an Refuses to Let Us Stop at the Green**

A lesser book would have ended the creation-portrait at verse 4: "Who brings forth the pasture" — a clean, affirmative image of providence, suitable for a devotional poster. The Qur'an deliberately refuses this comfort, and the refusal is itself a teaching. The full truth about this world includes its decay, and a religion that spoke only of the green would be a religion of illusion — it would bless the world's promises without warning of their expiry date. The inclusion of the withering in the same breath as the bringing-forth is the Qur'an's honesty, and honesty is the precondition of wisdom: no one can order his life rightly around a world he has been allowed to believe is permanent. The verse functions, then, as an inoculation against what the Qur'an calls ghurūr — self-deception by the world's glitter:

> *"Let not the life of this world delude you, and let not the Deluder delude you concerning God."* (Qur'an 31:33)

The classical commentators found in the two verses a perfect emblem of divine justice as well: the same God who brings forth is the God who causes to pass away, and His doing both is the guarantee that nothing in this world is owed permanence except by His leave. The field does not sue the farmer for its withering; the green does not accuse the Hand that raised it. And the creature who complains against his own seasons of withering — against illness, age, loss — is complaining against the very law that makes renewal possible, for a world in which nothing withered would be a world in which nothing could grow again. The cycle of green to black is not a flaw in the design; it is the design, and it runs on the same principle as the soul's own seasons: the Prophet ﷺ said, in a saying that applies the field's lesson to the heart:

> *"The likeness of the believer is like the green crop: the wind bends it this way and that, and when the wind stills it stands upright — and the believer is tested with afflictions, and the likeness of the hypocrite is like the cedar: it stands stiff until one blow fells it."* (al-Bukhārī 5644; Muslim 2809)

**The Verse as a Lens on Modern Anxiety and Modern Arrogance**

Applied to the present day, the verse diagnoses two characteristic failures at once. The first is the failure of the anxious: those who cling to the world's greenery as though it were eternal — to youth, to markets, to reputations, to health — and are therefore perpetually terrified of the season's turn. The verse's medicine for them is the knowledge that the turn is not a catastrophe but a law: the field was never promised forever green, and the soul that builds on the field builds on a lease. The second is the failure of the arrogant: those who look at the green field in its spring and say, as the Qur'an reports of a man who owned a garden, "I do not think that this will ever perish" (18:35) — and who are answered by the same night that levels the garden. The verse before us is that answer written in advance: every field carries its own funeral in its seed; every spring contains its autumn; and the only greenery that does not blacken is the one the next verses will describe — the growth of the soul that purifies itself, remembers its Lord, and prays, in a world where "the Hereafter is better and more enduring" (87:17). The sūrah has now finished its portrait of the natural world — created, proportioned, measured, guided, greened, and withered — and the portrait has a single moral: the world is a sign, not a home; an argument, not an end. The reader who has understood the pasture's rise and fall is now ready to hear what the sūrah has to say about the revelation that does not wither, the reminder that does not decay, and the Word of which God has said: *"falsehood cannot approach it from before it or from behind it"* (41:42).

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:6

> **We shall make you recite, so that you do not forget,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn from Creation to Revelation**

With verse 6 the sūrah pivots: the portrait of God's work in nature (verses 2–5) yields to God's address to His Prophet — *sanuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā* — "We shall make you recite, so that you do not forget." The pronoun changes from the third person (Who created... Who brought forth) to the first person plural of divine majesty (We shall make you recite), and the subject changes from the world to the messenger. The connection between the two halves is exact: the God who brings forth the pasture and turns it to refuse is the God who sends down revelation and preserves it from decay. The natural world withers by His law; the revealed Word does not wither by His promise. And the God who guided every creature to its pasture (verse 3) now guides His Prophet to his recitation.

The verb *sanuqriʾuka* is the first person plural of the fourth form of the root *q-r-ʾ*, the root of reading and recitation: *aqraʾa* means to cause to read, to teach to recite — and the word is the very root of the Qur'an's own name (al-Qurʾān, the Recitation) and of the first word ever revealed: *iqraʾ*, "Recite!" (96:1). The verse is thus a promise of enablement: the recitation will be placed in the Prophet's mouth and fixed in his breast by the same divine agency that first commanded it. The commentators relate the context with great tenderness. When Gabriel first brought revelation, the Prophet — a man who had never learned to read or write, receiving words of a density and beauty unlike anything human speech could produce — would hasten to repeat the words before Gabriel had finished the passage, afraid that they would slip from him before he could catch them. It is the anxiety of a man holding water in his hands. And the Qur'an addresses that anxiety here and in its two sister-passages:

> *"Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it. Indeed, upon Us is its gathering and its recitation. So when We recite it, follow its recitation. Then upon Us is its explanation."* (Qur'an 75:16–19)

> *"And do not hasten with the Qur'an before its revelation to you is completed, and say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge."* (Qur'an 20:114)

The promise "so that you do not forget" closes the circle: the preservation of the revelation is God's own undertaking, not a burden on the Prophet's memory. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭūsī record the occasion of the verse in the same terms: when Gabriel came with revelation, the Prophet would sometimes recite the beginning of the passage before the angel had finished, fearing forgetfulness — so this verse was revealed, assuring him that the One who sent the revelation would guard it in his breast.

**The Miracle of the Unschooled Prophet's Perfect Retention**

There is a factual wonder beneath the verse that the earliest community understood immediately and that modern readers can still verify: the Prophet of Islam was unlettered — ummī, the Qur'an itself says (7:157–158) — and yet he carried in his memory a book of more than six thousand verses, preserved over twenty-three years of piecemeal revelation, recited publicly, memorized by hundreds of Companions, and transmitted with such fidelity that the text in the hands of Muslims today differs from the text of the first generation only in the minutiae of recitation dialects. No other scripture in history has been preserved by the untrained memory of a single man and his community with anything approaching this precision. The Qur'an presents this very fact as a sign of its divine origin — the message was not composed by a scholar, for there was no scholar; it was not written down by a scribe at leisure, for the scribe could not write; it was received, retained, and delivered by a man whose only preparation was the divine enablement this verse announces:

> *"And you did not recite before it any Book, nor did you write one with your right hand; otherwise the falsifiers would have doubted."* (Qur'an 29:48)

The promise "you will not forget" was kept in a second sense as well, one that stretches beyond the Prophet's lifetime to the whole community: the Qur'an is the one revealed Book whose preservation was guaranteed as a continuing divine act, addressed to the community through its Prophet:

> *"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are its Guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

The science of hadith and of Qur'anic recitation grew up around this promise: generation after generation of memorizers (ḥuffāẓ) has kept the text alive exactly as it was kept alive in the first generation — by living memory — so that today, in any mosque in the world, a child of ten can recite the whole Book from the breast, the living continuation of the very "making to recite" that this verse promises. When the Prophet's ﷺ Companion Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī was told by him, in a hadith recorded in the collections, to "keep refreshing the Qur'an, for by Him in Whose hand is my soul, it escapes faster than tethered camels" (al-Bukhārī 5032; Muslim 789), the command presupposed what this verse guarantees: the Qur'an is given to be kept, and its keeping is both a divine promise and a human duty.

**The Divine Method: Recitation as the Mode of Revelation**

Notice finally the specific verb chosen: not "We shall teach you to know it" but "We shall make you recite it" — sanuqriʾuka. The mode of the revelation is recitation, and the mode of its preservation is recitation, and the mode of its transmission is recitation. The Qur'an was not sent as a set of propositions to be pondered in silence, nor as a text to be consulted on shelves; it was sent as something to be sounded — aloud, in prayer, in the dawn, in the night — and its very sound is part of its meaning. The word qurʾān itself, the Book's proper name, is the verbal noun of this same root: the Recitation. This is why the promise of verse 6 is fulfilled every time a Muslim prays: the recitation that God "made" the Prophet retain is the recitation He makes each believer retain in turn, for the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it."* (al-Bukhārī 5027)

And the sūrah that contains this very promise of retention is itself one of the portions of the Qur'an that Muslims have been commanded to retain and recite in the great assemblies of worship — on Friday, at the two ʿĪds, in the night prayer — so that the verse about not forgetting has been verified, practically, by the fact that no Muslim generation has ever forgotten the sūrah that contains it. The revelation that promised its own preservation is, by that promise, still being recited; the pasture withered as the verse foretold, but the recitation has not been forgotten, and will not be, until the last tongue is stilled. The next verse will now qualify this promise in a way that has occupied the scholars of abrogation for fourteen centuries.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:7

> **save what God wills. Truly He knows what is manifest and what is hidden.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception That Opened a Science**

*Illā mā shāʾa Allāh* — "save what God wills." The unconditional promise of verse 6 — "We shall make you recite, so that you do not forget" — is immediately qualified by an exception, and that exception has exercised the commentators more than almost any clause in the sūrah. The verse continues: *innahu yaʿlamu al-jahra wa-mā yakhfā* — "Truly He knows what is manifest and what is hidden." The scholars of tafsīr have generally understood the exception in one of two ways, and the two readings are really one doctrine viewed from two sides.

The first reading: the promise that the Prophet will not forget is absolute within its own sphere — the revelation God intends to remain in force — but it is not a veto on God's own legislative freedom. God may will that some portion of the revealed text be lifted, its recitation discontinued, or its ruling superseded — and in that case "forgetting" is not a defect in the Prophet but a divine act of supersession. This is the Qur'anic doctrine of abrogation (naskh), stated by the Book itself in its most cited formulation:

> *"No sign do We abrogate or cause to be forgotten, but that We bring that which is better than it or like unto it. Do you not know that God is over all things Competent?"* (Qur'an 2:106)

The second reading, preferred by those who read the exception as applying to the ordinary course of the Prophet's earthly life: even the Prophet, being human, could be made by God to set aside or to miss a portion of what had been recited — not through fault or negligence, but through the will of God, who alone knows all that is manifest and hidden. The scholars note in this connection the well-known report from ʿĀʾishah that the Prophet once heard a man reciting the Qur'an by night and said:

> *"May God have mercy on him — he reminded me of such-and-such a verse that I had been caused to drop from such-and-such a sūrah."* (al-Bukhārī 5036; Muslim 789)

Al-Rāzī draws the theological consequence with care: the verse is a consolation, not an accusation. If the Prophet forgot or set aside a verse, it was because God willed it — not because of any weakness in the Prophet's reception. The Qur'anic portrait of the Prophet is never that of a man struggling to hold a slippery text; it is that of a man whose retention was divinely enabled, and whose occasional "dropping" of a passage was itself a divine act, serving the divine purpose of the Book's progressive legislation. The verse is thus the theological foundation of the entire doctrine of naskh: since the Qur'an was revealed in circumstances over twenty-three years, with rulings suited to the community's growth, God reserved the right to replace a ruling with a better or like ruling — and the Prophet's freedom from blame in the process was guaranteed by the clause "save what God wills."

**The Question of Divine Knowledge: What Is Manifest and What Is Hidden**

The verse's second half — "Truly He knows what is manifest and what is hidden" — is not a pious appendage; it is the logical ground of the exception. Only a knower of both the manifest and the hidden can decide what should remain and what should be lifted. The manifest (al-jahr) is what is open and audible, the realm of what human beings see and hear — including, in the context, the public recitation of the Qur'an and the public rulings of the community. The hidden (mā yakhfā) is what lies beneath: the secrets of hearts, the unseen consequences of rulings, the wisdom concealed in abrogation, the full architecture of the divine plan that no creature perceives while it is being built. The verse tells the Prophet — and through him, the community — that the administration of revelation rests on a knowledge none of them possesses: God knows why this verse was sent now and lifted then; He knows what is gained by the replacement; He knows what the manifest text conceals of hidden wisdom. The believer's duty toward the apparent contradictions of scripture — the abrogated and the abrogating, the general and the specific, the absolute and the conditioned — is therefore not to resolve them by suspicion but to receive them by trust, for the One who legislated knows both what is open and what is concealed:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the Unseen; none knows them but He. And He knows what is on the land and in the sea. And not a leaf falls but He knows it, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything moist or dry, but that it is in a clear Book."* (Qur'an 6:59)

**What the Verse Teaches About Divine and Human "Forgetting"**

There is a subtle and important distinction the commentators draw here between God's forgetting and man's. When the verse promises the Prophet that he will not forget, and then excepts what God wills, it uses the human word for the sake of address — but God does not forget in the human sense. Human forgetting is a failure of retention, an involuntary loss; divine "causing to forget" is a deliberate act of legislation. The difference is the difference between a student who loses his notes and a teacher who revises the syllabus. The Qur'an's own language elsewhere marks the boundary: God "erases what He wills and establishes" (13:39), and the erasure is never a loss of truth but a stage in the delivery of the final form. The Prophet, for his part, was preserved from the involuntary kind of forgetting entirely — the authentic reports show him correcting reciters and retaining revelations for years — so that the only "forgetting" in his career was the divinely willed kind, which is no forgetting at all but supersession. The verse therefore protects both sides of the doctrine: the integrity of the Prophet's reception (he forgets nothing that God wills to remain) and the freedom of the divine legislation (what God wills to lift is lifted without fault).

**The Practical Yield: Trusting the Text's Full Architecture**

For the ordinary believer the verse holds a permanent lesson in the etiquette of scripture. The Qur'an, like any living constitution, contains verses whose application belongs to their context and verses of universal command; it contains the abrogating and the abrogated; and generations of readers have been tempted to scandalize themselves over the differences — to ask why a ruling here appears to be softened there, or why a verse of patience appears alongside a verse of struggle. The verse before us supplies the governing attitude: the One who revealed both knows what is manifest and what is hidden, and the reader's part is not to have every hidden wisdom disclosed but to trust the Knower of it. This is not an invitation to intellectual laziness — the Islamic scholarly tradition built entire sciences (uṣūl al-fiqh, the sciences of naskh and takhṣīṣ) on the painstaking study of just these relations; it is an invitation to humility within that study — the recognition that the full architecture of the Book is known only to its Author. The promise to the Prophet — "you will not forget" — extends, in the life of the community, to the promise that the guarded Book (15:9) will not be lost; and the verse's exception clause extends, in the life of the scholar, to the admission that some of what God knows is hidden from every creature, and that the appropriate posture before the hidden is not suspicion but submission. Between the manifest text, which every reader can study, and the hidden wisdom, which only God fully knows, the believer walks by the light of the first and trusts the second — exactly as the Prophet was taught to do by this very verse.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:8

> **And We shall ease your way to ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise of a Smooth Road**

*Wa-nuyassiruka lil-yusrā* — "And We shall ease your way to ease." The verb *nuyassiru* is the first person plural of the second form of the root *y-s-r*, the root of facility, smoothness, and wealth (in old Arabic, yusr also meant the easy circumstance, as its opposite ʿusr meant hardship); the object *al-yusrā* is the same root in its feminine elative form — the easiest state, the utmost ease. The sentence is a doubled ease: God will make easy (nuyassiru) the Prophet's path to the state of ease (al-yusrā). One can render it "We shall ease you toward the Ease" — a road made smooth that leads to a condition of smoothness. The commentators, as the source tradition records, understood the promise in several converging senses, and the senses are worth setting side by side, because each opens a different door of the verse.

First, there is the ease of the revealed law itself. The religion brought by the Prophet was not to be a religion of impossible burdens — and the Qur'an says of it explicitly: "God desires ease for you, and He does not desire hardship for you" (2:185); "He has laid no hardship upon you in religion" (22:78); "God wishes to lighten your burden, for man was created weak" (4:28). The verse before us, on this reading, is a charter of facilitation: the way of Islam is the easy way, and the One who sent it promises to keep it easy. The Prophet's ﷺ own summary of his mission is the sunnah-commentary on this verse:

> *"Religion is ease; and no one overburdens himself in religion except that it overcomes him. So be upright, draw near, give glad tidings, and seek help in the morning, the evening, and part of the night."* (al-Bukhārī 39)

Second, there is the ease of memorization and reception — the most natural reading in the immediate context of verses 6–7. After promising "We shall make you recite, so that you do not forget," the verse adds: and We shall make the whole task easy for you — the retention of the Qur'an, the bearing of the message, the endurance of opposition. The Prophet's mission was, by any human measure, impossibly hard: a single man against the entrenched cult and commerce of Makkah, unlettered, without army or treasury, bearing a word that his own tribe had sworn to extinguish. The verse tells him that the difficulty will not be borne by his resources but eased by divine facilitation at every step — and the Qur'an repeats the promise in the two famous verses of Sūrah al-Sharḥ: "For truly, with hardship comes ease; truly, with hardship comes ease" (94:5–6), which the commentators read as the fulfillment of this very assurance: two eases for one hardship, because God doubles the relief He sends with every trial.

Third — and this is the reading that lifts the verse into eschatology — there is the ease of the final account. Some of the commentators understood al-yusrā as the Garden itself, so that the verse promises that God will ease the Prophet's path to Paradise, the state of permanent ease; and they connected it with the paired verses of Sūrah al-Layl, where the same root governs the two human destinies:

> *"As for him who gives and fears God, and affirms the beauty of the reward, We shall ease him toward ease. But as for him who is miserly and self-sufficient, and denies the beauty of the reward, We shall ease him toward hardship."* (Qur'an 92:5–10)

**The Ease That Comes from the Law's Design**

The logical structure of the verse deserves attention, because it answers a question every reader of scripture eventually asks: if the religion is true, why is it demanding? Why the prayers at dawn, the fast of Ramadan, the restraint of the lawful and unlawful? The verse's answer is that the demand is itself the ease — properly understood. The Qur'anic ethics of obligation rests on the conviction that the human being was created with needs that only discipline serves and only worship satisfies; therefore the commands of religion are not arbitrary taxes on human freedom but the maintenance schedule of the human soul, and a religion that is easy in the sense of undemanding would be a religion that abandons the soul to its own decay. The ease promised here is not the ease of the hammock but the ease of the well-oiled machine, the ease of a path that has been graded so that the traveler's effort is not wasted. Islam's law is easy, the scholars say, in its entrance (the testimony of faith), easy in its proportions (five prayers in a day, not fifty), easy in its allowances (the concession for the traveler, the sick, the pregnant), and easy in its repentance (the door never closed) — and the verse before us is the guarantee that this character is not accidental but constitutional: "We shall ease your way to ease" is a promise about the nature of the religion itself, and its abrogating of hardship is written into the law's own structure:

> *"God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you."* (Qur'an 2:185)

The Prophet's ﷺ practice was the living application of the principle. The hadith collections record that whenever the Prophet was given a choice between two lawful matters, he chose the easier of them, so long as it involved no sin — the sunnah-embodiment of this verse, and the reason the classical jurists, from al-Shāfiʿī to Ibn Taymiyyah, treated facilitation (taysīr) as a root-principle of jurisprudence: where the Lawgiver's intent admits of ease, the strictest reading is not necessarily the truest one.

**The Ease of the Qur'an's Learning: A Promise Kept in Every Madrasa**

There is a remarkable factual fulfillment of this verse that any observer of Muslim civilization can verify: the Qur'an — a book of some six thousand verses in a language of formidable richness — has been, for fourteen centuries, the most widely memorized book in human history, memorized by children of seven and eight, by farmers and merchants, by generations who never saw a printed page. The verse promised the Prophet ease in retaining the revelation; the promise overflowed to his community, and the Qur'an itself states the general law of that overflow: "And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance — so is there any who remembers?" (54:17, repeated four times in that sūrah). The ease is not the ease of triviality — the memorization of the Qur'an remains a genuine achievement — but the ease of fitness: the text is so shaped to the human memory, so cadenced to the human tongue, that millions have found in it what this verse promises, a way made smooth. The same doubling — the law's ease and the learning's ease — is what the verse holds out to every believer who finds religion heavy: if it is heavy, the fault may be in the carrying, not in the load; and the promised facilitation is sought, as the Prophet taught, by drawing near rather than by straining beyond measure — "so be upright, draw near, and give glad tidings" (al-Bukhārī 39). The verse is, in the end, a personal address as much as a historical one: to the Prophet it promised the ease of the mission; to the reciter of the sūrah it promises the ease of the path; and to the soul that finds the road to God uphill, it whispers what God told His Messenger — the road has been graded; the One who called you to it has already eased it for you; walk.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:9

> **So remind — if the reminder should benefit.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mission Reduced to Its Core: A Reminder, Not a Conquest**

*Fa-dhakkir ini nafaʿati al-dhikrā* — "So remind — if the reminder should benefit." With this verse the sūrah states, in five Arabic words, the whole shape of the Prophet's task as the Qur'an conceives it. The verb *dhakkir* is the second form of the root *dh-k-r*, whose field covers memory, mention, and mindfulness; its second form means to remind, to admonish, to call to remembrance — to place before another what he already knows in the depth of his nature but has allowed to go dormant. The noun *al-dhikrā* is the reminder itself, the admonition — and the Qur'an uses the same word as a name for its own content, since revelation is, at root, a calling-back of the human being to the truth his own fiṭrah already carries. The Prophet's office is thus defined with deliberate modesty: he is not sent to compel, to coerce, to micro-manage, or to convert by force; he is sent to remind. And the Qur'an repeats the definition until it becomes a refrain of the Makkan sūrahs:

> *"So remind — you are only a reminder. You are not over them a controller."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

> *"And remind, for the reminder benefits the believers."* (Qur'an 51:55)

> *"So remind, by means of the Qur'an, him who fears My threat."* (Qur'an 50:45)

The clause "if the reminder should benefit" — *ini nafaʿati al-dhikrā* — was read by the grammarians in two ways, and both carry doctrine. Read as a condition ("if the reminder benefits, then remind"), the verse directs the reminder to those who can receive it — it spares the Prophet the exhaustion of hammering on sealed hearts and directs his energy to the furrows that can still take the seed. Read as a statement of fact ("the reminder benefits — so remind"), the verse becomes an encouragement: the reminder does benefit, does penetrate, does bear fruit, so do not withhold it out of pessimism. The classical commentators, as the source tradition notes, drew the practical conclusion that covers both readings: the duty to remind stands regardless of the apparent receptivity of the audience — for all will be held accountable for what reached them, and the reminder that seems wasted on one heart may be the very seed of another's awakening — while the wisdom of the verse directs the messenger to spend his effort where the reminder can take root. The Prophet's ﷺ own career displayed both laws: he preached to the sealed hearts of the Quraysh leadership for years with little visible yield, and the very same words that the sealed rejected were carried home by visitors, slaves, and strangers — the reminder benefiting where the messenger least expected it.

**Why Reminding Is the Right Method: The Anthropology Beneath It**

The verse's choice of method rests on a specific anthropology, and it is worth making the logic explicit. If the human being were a blank slate, education would be a matter of pure inscription — writing new content onto nothing. If the human being were a brute, training would be a matter of conditioning alone. The Qur'an's view is a third thing: the human being is a creature who already knows — in the depth of his created nature — his Lord, his obligation, his end. The fiṭrah has not been erased; it has been covered, and the Qur'an's word for the covering is itself a diagnosis of every human evil: kufr, from the root meaning to cover. On this anthropology, the prophet's work is not to install a new operating system but to remove the dust from the original one — to remind. And this is why the Qur'anic method of persuasion is not the method of the conqueror (whose subject must be broken) or the advertiser (whose customer must be deceived) but the method of the physician and the friend: the truth is called to mind, and the listener's own nature is summoned as a witness:

> *"And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them testify concerning themselves: 'Am I not your Lord?' They said: 'Yes, we testify.'"* (Qur'an 7:172)

The reminder works, when it works, because it is not introducing a foreign claim but awakening a native one — which is why the Qur'an can address its obstinate hearers with the incredulous question: "Do they not reflect?" and why the Prophet ﷺ was told that his success lay not in argumentative victory but in the patient calling-to-mind of what the soul already suspects. The story of the sūrah's own period illustrates the method's reach: the man who would become the second caliph, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, set out one day with a sword to kill the Prophet and returned converted — not by a display of force but because the reminder reached him through the very scripture he had come to silence, as he heard his sister reciting Sūrah Ṭā Hā and felt the text address the knowledge buried in his own breast.

**The Etiquette of the Reminder: Benefit, Not Conquest**

The verse also legislates the character of the one who reminds — and here its application extends far beyond prophets to every bearer of truth: the parent, the teacher, the preacher, the friend who calls another to God. Three disciplines follow from the words. First, the reminder must be for the benefit of the reminded, not for the triumph of the reminder: "if the reminder should benefit" makes the benefit of the hearer the criterion of the act, and the Qur'an generalizes the etiquette: "Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and beautiful exhortation, and argue with them in that which is best" (16:125). Second, the reminder must accept its own limits: it is a reminder, not a sentence; the one who reminds plants and waters, and the growth is God's — "You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills" (28:56). The parent who has reminded a child, the teacher who has reminded a student, may release the outcome without releasing the duty — the verse cuts the nerve of both despair and coercion at once: since the reminder is all that was asked of you, you have not failed when the hearer refuses, and since the reminder is all that was asked of you, you may not force the hearer's acceptance. Third, the reminder must be repeated without contempt: the Prophet was commanded to remind "if it benefits," and the Qur'an's own practice with the sealed is instructive — it never ceased addressing them, never ceased warning them, never ceased keeping the door of the reminder open, for no messenger knows when a heart declared sealed will crack, and the history of the first community is full of exactly such cracks: Abū Sufyān, who led the armies against Islam and died a Muslim; Khalid ibn al-Walīd, who planned its destruction and became its sword; ʿIkrimah ibn Abī Jahl, the persecutor's son, who became a warrior for the faith he had fought. The reminder benefits — the verse's second reading — and the messenger who stops reminding because the audience seems hopeless abandons the field before the hour of its harvest.

**The Verse in the Life of the Modern Reminder**

Every age produces its own forgetfulness, and every age therefore needs its own reminders — which is why this verse, addressed to a prophet in Makkah, speaks directly to the conditions of the modern world. Modern forgetfulness is not the absence of information; it is, paradoxically, a product of information's flood — the soul drowned in notifications, opinions, and entertainments forgets, not because it lacks truth, but because it lacks silence, attention, and repetition. The Qur'anic method of reminding is precisely calibrated to this condition, for the dhikr is not a lecture but a practice: the five daily prayers are a reminder repeated, the Friday sermon is a reminder renewed, the recitation of the Qur'an is a reminder sounded, and the whole liturgical life of Islam is, as the scholars say, a machinery of recollection — the soul is called back, at intervals it cannot miss, to the truths it knows and forgets. The verse's commission extends to every Muslim in the same measure: "So remind" — with a book given to a child, with a word of counsel to a colleague, with an example that outlives a sermon, with a post that calls to good — and the reminder's only condition is its own sincerity: it must benefit the hearer, not flatter the speaker. Where that condition holds, the reminder is never wasted: it may be refused by the most wretched (as the next verses will say) and received by the fearful (as they will also say) — but the one who reminds has done what the verse asked, and the outcome rests with the One who knows what is manifest and what is hidden. The sūrah now turns from the messenger to the two destinies that receive his reminder.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:10

> **He who fears shall be reminded,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Receiver: Fear as the Antenna of the Soul**

*Sa-yadhakkaru man yakhshā* — "He who fears shall be reminded." The verse is a future-tense guarantee: the reminder will do its work — will be received, pondered, and retained (yadhakkaru is the form that means to take admonition to heart) — in the one who fears (man yakhshā). The sūrah now draws its map of human responses to the reminder, and the first response is not intelligence, not education, not even curiosity: it is fear. The Arabic word is khashya, from the root *kh-sh-y*, and the Qur'an uses it with a precision that distinguishes it from the ordinary word for fear (khawf). Khawf is the fear of a threat one perceives — the fear of punishment, of loss, of danger; khashya is the fear that arises from knowledge and awe — the reverent dread of the One whose majesty has been perceived, the fear of a soul that understands what it stands before. The Qur'an itself defines the distinction in a single verse:

> *"Only those of His servants who possess knowledge fear God."* (Qur'an 35:28)

Khashya is thus not the trembling of the coward but the attentiveness of the aware. The ignorant do not feel it, because they do not know what they face; the arrogant do not feel it, because they overestimate what they are; but the one who knows — who has understood the sūrah's opening portrait of the Creator who measures and guides, who has heard of the Fire and the Garden, who has recognized the majesty of the Name — that one is visited by khashya, and khashya is the exact receptivity the reminder requires. The Qur'an describes the same receiver in its other portraits of the successful hearer:

> *"You only warn him who follows the Reminder and fears the Compassionate in the Unseen. Give him glad tidings of forgiveness and a generous reward."* (Qur'an 36:11)

> *"But as for him who feared the standing before his Lord and forbade the soul its caprice, the Garden is surely the refuge."* (Qur'an 79:40–41)

**Why Fear Opens the Ear: The Psychology of the Verse**

There is a precise psychological law beneath this verse, and the Qur'an states it with the confidence of a physician describing the body. The human mind allocates its attention by perceived stakes. A message that arrives when nothing seems to be at stake is filed as information — interesting, perhaps, but inert; a message that arrives when the hearer understands that his destiny is in the balance is filed as navigation — urgent, actionable, unforgettable. The reminder of the Qur'an — God, the account, the Fire, the Garden — is the highest-stakes message a human being can receive, but it can only function as navigation if the hearer believes the stakes are real; and that belief is exactly what khashya supplies. The fearful hearer does not listen harder because he is weak; he listens harder because he is rational — he has correctly computed that the message concerns his eternal interest, and he therefore receives it as a drowning man receives a rope. The Qur'an's psychology is confirmed by its portrait of those who do not receive the reminder: they are not described as stupid but as secure — "Leave them to eat and enjoy, and let vain hope delude them; soon they will know" (15:3) — and security (amān) against God's plan is named among the gravest of miscalculations: "Do they feel secure against the plan of God? None feels secure against the plan of God except the losing people" (7:99). Khashya is the opposite of that false security: it is the soul's admission that it is not safe by its own merits, and that admission is the door through which mercy enters — for the Qur'an's fearful ones are consistently its forgiven ones.

**Fear and Hope: The Two Wings of the Believer**

It would be a distortion of the verse to read it as commending terror as a permanent state. The Qur'anic psychology of khashya is not the psychology of the haunted; it is the psychology of the attentive child of a great king — respectful, careful, eager not to offend, yet confident of love. The Qur'an pairs fear with hope in the same breath whenever it describes the mature believer:

> *"They forsake their beds, calling upon their Lord in fear and hope, and spend of what We have provided them."* (Qur'an 32:16)

> *"Indeed, they used to hasten to good deeds and call upon Us in hope and fear, and they were humble before Us."* (Qur'an 21:90)

The classical tradition — and al-Ghazālī after it — described the two as the two wings of the bird: a bird with one wing cannot fly; a believer with fear alone despairs, and despair is itself a sin against God's mercy ("do not despair of God's mercy," 39:53), while a believer with hope alone grows careless, and carelessness is the antechamber of ruin. The khashya of this verse is the fear that operates within hope — the fear of a lover of disappointing the beloved, the care of a traveler of missing the road. The Prophet ﷺ, who was promised Paradise and forgiveness without reserve, still stood in prayer until his feet swelled, and when asked why he labored so, answered — in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim — "Shall I not be a thankful servant?" His labor was not the labor of the terrified but the labor of the grateful-aware, and it is the finest portrait in history of what this verse means: the one who truly knows his Lord cannot be casual toward Him; the one who truly fears the standing before Him cannot be careless about the preparation. Khashya is not the opposite of love; it is the form love takes when it understands the majesty of its object.

**The Promise in the Future Tense: The Reminder Will Find Its Mark**

Notice that the verse does not say "he who fears should be reminded" but "he who fears shall be reminded" — sa-yadhakkaru is a promise, not an exhortation. The verse guarantees that the reminder is never lost where khashya exists: the seed falls on the furrow prepared for it, and it grows. This is the sūrah's quiet answer to the messenger's discouragement, which verse 9 had already addressed: do not measure the value of the reminder by the reaction of the sealed, for there are hearts of khashya everywhere, in every audience — and where the fear of God exists, the reminder will find its mark, even if the harvest is delayed. The classical commentators connected this guarantee with the Qur'an's repeated refrain: "And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance — so is there any who remembers?" (54:17) — the Book is easy to remember for precisely the one described here, the one who fears. The verse thus completes the portrait of the successful life that the sūrah is painting: the one who will prosper (verse 14) is first described here as the one who fears (verse 10) — the fear that opens the ear, the reminder that enters through the opened ear, the purification that follows the received reminder, and the prayer that seals the purified life. Every stage is linked to the one before it, and the first link is this: the soul that knows its Lord cannot be indifferent to His address. He who fears shall be reminded — and the reminder he receives will not return to its Sender void, for the Qur'an has promised of itself: "a reminder for him who has a heart, or who gives ear while he is present" (50:37).

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:11

> **and the most wretched shall turn away from it —**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Receiver: The One Who Shuns the Reminder**

*Wa-yatajannabuhā al-ashqā* — "and the most wretched shall turn away from it." The verse completes the map of verse 10 with its dark twin: if the fearful receive the reminder, the most wretched — al-ashqā, the superlative of the root *sh-q-w* (wretchedness, misfortune) — actively shuns it. The verb *yatajannabu* is the form of deliberate avoidance: it means to keep oneself away, to sidestep, to refuse with effort. The choice of verb is exact and damning: the most wretched does not merely miss the reminder, does not merely fail to hear it; he turns away from it, he avoids it, he maneuvers around it — the reminder is present and he removes himself from its presence. The verse is a portrait of avoidance, and avoidance is the most telling of human responses, because it reveals the uncomfortable truth: the reminder was understood well enough to be feared; the shunning is a confession that the message struck home.

The Qur'an elsewhere names the same figure with the same word. The most wretched of the past communities was the man who hamstrung the sacred she-camel of Thamūd — the Qur'an calls him al-ashqā in the very act of his crime: "when the most wretched of them arose" (91:12) — a man who, presented with a sign of God and a prophet's warning, responded by destroying the sign. And Sūrah al-Layl describes the dwellers of the Fire in the same superlative:

> *"None shall enter it except the most wretched — who denied and turned away."* (Qur'an 92:15–16)

The pairing in that verse is itself the commentary on ours: the ashqā is defined by two acts — takdhīb, denial of the truth when it arrives, and tawallī, turning away from it. Our verse's verb, yatajannabu, is the tawallī in its active form: the shunning is not passive ignorance but an achieved avoidance. The most wretched, in the Qur'an's anatomy, is not the most ignorant but the most resistant — the one whose resistance has become so habitual that the approach of truth triggers an automatic withdrawal, like the eye's blink before a blow.

**The Logic of Wretchedness: Why Denial Hardens into Destiny**

How does a human being become al-ashqā — the most wretched — when he began, like everyone, as a creature of fiṭrah, born upon the natural knowledge of God? The Qur'an's answer, scattered across its pages and gathered by the commentators into a single law, is that wretchedness is an achievement of the will: it is built by repetition. Each refusal of a reminder strengthens the muscle of refusal; each turning away makes the next turning easier; the door that is never opened begins to rust; and the heart that is never softened begins to seal — the Qur'an's own metaphor, drawn on the same canvas:

> *"Nay, but that which they have earned has rusted upon their hearts."* (Qur'an 83:14)

> *"God has set a seal upon their hearts and their hearing, and over their eyes is a covering."* (Qur'an 2:7)

The classical theologians — and Ibn al-Qayyim is the most eloquent of them — insisted that the seal is not an arbitrary divine decree but the natural terminus of a process the sinner himself conducted: every act of turning away deposits a residue; the residue hardens; the hardened heart loses the capacity it once had, in exactly the way an unused limb atrophies or an unexercised language fades. The Qur'an says the same thing medically: "Then after that your hearts hardened, and they were like stones, or harder" (2:74) — the hardening is described as the outcome of their own history. Our verse's future tense — "shall turn away" — describes not a fate imposed on the ashqā before birth but the trajectory of a life: the one who will end as the most wretched is the one who is now, habitually, avoiding the reminder; the one who is now, habitually, avoiding it is rehearsing the posture in which he will meet his Lord.

**Why Avoidance Is Worse Than Denial: The Modern Form of Ashqā**

There is a piercing contemporary relevance in the choice of this verb. The modern world has produced an entire industry of avoidance — and the ashqā of our verse has found, in the age of entertainment and distraction, a machinery of shunning more efficient than anything the classical commentators could have imagined. The reminder is not absent from modern life; it is, if anything, omnipresent — the question of God, the reality of death, the call of conscience arise in every life — and the modern skill is precisely yatajannabu: the practiced art of not thinking about it, of filling every silence with noise, every solitude with a screen, every moment of possible reflection with consumption. The ashqā of verse 11 is not the atheist who has examined and denied; he may be, and often is, the busy person who has never examined — who shuns the reminder by the simple expedient of never being still enough to hear it. The Prophet's ﷺ description of such hearts is exact:

> *"There is a rust on the heart, like the rust on a copper pot; and its polish is the remembrance of God."* — a saying reported in the collections with a chain that scholars have discussed, whose meaning the Qur'an itself confirms in the verse of rusting (83:14) and in the command of remembrance.

The verse's warning to every reader is therefore the most practical in the sūrah: do not rehearse avoidance. Every sermon you skip, every page of scripture you scroll past, every prompting of conscience you drown — is a small installment on the posture of al-ashqā. The fearful of verse 10 and the ashqā of verse 11 begin at the same starting line; the difference is not in their creation but in their response to the reminder — the one receives it and is purified; the other avoids it and is hardened. The sūrah has now drawn the two destinies in two verses, and it will spend the next two verses describing the destination of the second — the greatest Fire, in which the one who preferred avoidance to remembrance will find that the thing he avoided has become the only thing present, forever.

---
## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:12

> **he who shall enter the greatest Fire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Destination of the Shunner: The Greatest Fire**

*Alladhī yaṣlā al-nāra al-kubrā* — "he who shall enter the greatest Fire." The relative clause identifies the ashqā of verse 11 by his destination: he is the one who will roast in the greatest Fire. The verb *yaṣlā* is from the root *ṣ-l-y*, whose field is burning and roasting — the verb the Qur'an uses for the entry of the damned into the Fire and for the burning of the body by its heat; the noun *al-nāra al-kubrā* — the greatest Fire, the Fire of the Hereafter — is distinguished from every lesser fire by the adjective kubrā, the feminine elative of the root *k-b-r*, greatness. The commentators discussed what "greatest" contrasts with, and two readings emerged, both preserved in the tradition. The first: the greatest Fire is the Fire of the Hereafter as opposed to the fires of this world — the fire of a furnace, a forest, a war, which are, for all their horror, finite, extinguishable, and endurable in comparison with what awaits. Al-Rāzī and others note that the Qur'an itself draws exactly this contrast when it speaks of the punishment of the world as a foretaste and the punishment of the Hereafter as the greater: "and the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if only they knew" (68:33). The second reading: the greatest Fire is the deepest degree of the Fire, the destination of the worst of the damned — for the Fire, as the Qur'an states, has degrees and gates: "To it are seven gates; to each gate belongs an allotted portion of them" (15:44). On this reading the verse alludes to a graduated justice: not all who enter the Fire enter the same depth, and the ashqā — the one who denied and turned away — enters the greatest depth, the Fire reserved for the most wretched, as Sūrah al-Layl had paired them: "None shall enter it except the most wretched, who denied and turned away" (92:15–16).

**The Justice of the Fire: Why the Qur'an Refuses to Soften It**

A modern reader — and even a believing one — may pause at the severity of the verse and ask the question that every generation has asked: why does the Qur'an speak of the Fire at all? Why not simply warn of loss, or of the absence of bliss? The answer that emerges from the Qur'an's own logic has several strands, and the sūrah's context supplies the first: the Fire is the necessary consequence of the freedom that verses 10–11 presupposed. If the human being is truly free to turn away from the reminder — and the whole of the sūrah's argument, from the command to remind (verse 9) to the blame of preference (verse 16), assumes that freedom — then his turning away must be able to issue in a real result. A universe in which the choice of the ashqā made no final difference would be a universe in which his choice was never real; the Fire is the seriousness of freedom taken to its end. The second strand is the Qur'an's insistence that the Fire is not arbitrary but earned and proportioned: "This is for what your hands sent ahead, and God does not wrong His servants" (3:182; 8:51); "and God does not wrong anyone so much as an atom's weight" (4:40). The third strand is mercy itself, rightly understood: the warning of the Fire is a mercy to the living, for a danger that is not announced cannot be avoided, and the entire purpose of the warning is that no one should enter it:

> *"So We have made it easy upon your tongue, that you may give glad tidings to the reverent and warn with it a contentious people."* (Qur'an 19:97)

> *"That he who perishes may perish upon clear proof, and he who lives may live upon clear proof."* (Qur'an 8:42)

The classical scholars were unanimous that the Fire is real — a place of real punishment, not a metaphor for psychological states — while insisting equally that its reality does not compromise divine mercy, for the same God who created the Fire sent the warners, revealed the Books, and kept the door of repentance open until the last breath. Al-Ashʿarī and the theologians of the Sunnah formulated the doctrine that the Fire's entry is entirely just — the recompense of denial and turning away — while its avoidance is entirely grace: no one enters Paradise by his own merit alone, and no one enters the Fire except by what his own hands sent ahead.

**The Greatness of the Fire and the Smallness of Its Causes**

There is a haunting disproportion in the verse that the commentators refused to smooth over: the cause — a creature's turning away from a reminder — seems so small, and the effect — the greatest Fire — so vast. The Qur'an does not soften the disproportion; it explains it. The cause is small in size but not in kind: what is turned away from is not a piece of information but the Lord of the worlds, the One who created and proportioned, measured and guided, brought forth the pasture and will raise the dead. The gravity of an offense is measured by the dignity of the one offended; and the Qur'an states this principle with explicit logic when it warns that the sin against the Creator is not like the sin against a creature: "They did not appraise God with the appraisal due to Him" (6:91; 39:67). The ashqā's crime, in the end, is ingratitude on a cosmic scale — to have been created, proportioned, measured, guided, fed, and warned, and to have answered the whole with avoidance. The greatest Fire is the only proportion the Qur'an can offer to the greatest ingratitude. And yet — and this is the sūrah's final mercy — the verse is spoken in the indicative of warning, not of despair: it describes the destination of the one who persists in shunning, and by describing it, it gives every hearer the chance to change the sentence while the sentence is still future. The next verse will describe the horror of that Fire in the most economical terms the Qur'an ever used — two words that have chilled readers for fourteen centuries: neither dying, nor living.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:13

> **then neither die therein, nor live.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words That Describe an Eternal Midnight**

*Thumma lā yamūtu fīhā wa-lā yaḥyā* — "then neither die therein, nor live." With four negations the Qur'an describes the state of the ashqā in the greatest Fire: he does not die, and he does not live. The verse is among the most economical sentences of scripture and among the most terrible; the commentators observed that the Arabic idiom — to say of someone that he neither dies nor lives — is the language of extremity itself, used of a person in the grip of a suffering so complete that death, which would be a mercy, is withheld, while life, which would be a capacity, is drained of every quality that makes life bearable. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse does not describe a third state between life and death; it describes the torture of a being who possesses enough life to feel and not enough to hope — consciousness without relief, sensation without end.

The Qur'an paints the same state in its other canvases, and the repetitions are worth setting side by side, because each adds a stroke:

> *"Whoever comes to his Lord as a sinner — for him is Hell, wherein he neither dies nor lives."* (Qur'an 20:74)

> *"For those who disbelieve, garments of fire have been cut out, and boiling water will be poured over their heads, melting what is in their bellies and their skins, and for them are hooked rods of iron. Whenever, in their anguish, they desire to go forth from it, they are returned into it: 'Taste the punishment of the burning!'"* (Qur'an 22:19–22)

> *"And death will come upon him from every side, yet he will not die; and ahead of him is a harsh punishment."* (Qur'an 14:17)

The last verse is the most precise: death "comes upon him from every side" — the sufferer feels death approaching, tastes its onset, prays for its arrival — "yet he will not die." The Qur'an elsewhere records the cry of the Fire's inhabitants for exactly this release, and its denial: "Therein they will cry: Our Lord, bring us out, and we will do righteousness, other than what we used to do" (35:37) — and the answer: "Did We not give you a life long enough for him who would remember to remember therein, and did not the warner come to you? So taste, for the wrongdoers have no helper" (35:37). And in the sūrah of the angels, the damned ask the keeper of Hell to end them, and are answered with the sentence of permanence: "And they will call: O Mālik, let your Lord finish us! He will say: You are remaining" (43:77).

**Why "Neither Dies Nor Lives" Is the Worst Punishment of All**

The Qur'an's psychology of punishment here is subtle and deserves to be made explicit, because it explains why this particular description is reserved for the worst state. Death is, for the suffering, the last door — the final exit from pain; the instinct that makes the tortured body lose consciousness and the tortured soul expire is nature's own mercy, the built-in limit of suffering. The greatest Fire is described as the place where that limit is removed: the sufferer is kept alive past every point at which life would naturally surrender, so that the suffering has no terminus within itself. Every other punishment in human experience carries its own clock — the pain ends, the body exhausts, death comes, or at the very least, hope of change remains; the punishment described here has no clock, and the Qur'an says so in its most chilling phrase for the Fire: "remaining" (43:77); "immortal therein" (4:169); "and they will not be brought out of the Fire" (5:37). The verse before us is the same verdict from inside: the ashqā is not granted the dignity of an ending, because an ending would be a mercy, and the justice that measured his crime — the rejection of the Merciful — withholds the mercy of extinction. Ibn Kathīr and the commentators who transmitted the descriptions of the state explained it in the plainest terms: the dwellers of the Fire are addressed by death itself, brought as a ram and slaughtered between Paradise and the Fire, and it is proclaimed: "O people of Paradise, no death! O people of the Fire, no death!" — the authentic report is recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī:

> *"Death will be brought in the form of a white ram and will be slaughtered between Paradise and the Fire. Then it will be said: O people of Paradise, no death! O people of the Fire, no death! And the people of Paradise will rejoice, and the people of the Fire will grieve."* (al-Bukhārī 4730; Muslim 2849)

The slaughter of death is the closing of the last door: for the blessed, it means the end of every fear that death could still take their bliss; for the damned, it means the end of every hope that death could still end their torment. "Neither dies nor lives" is that proclamation, written from the inside.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Warning**

It is a fixed principle of the Qur'anic message — and one this verse illustrates perfectly — that the most terrible descriptions of the Fire are themselves acts of mercy, because they are warnings delivered in advance. A physician who tells a patient the true course of an untreated disease is not cruel; the cruelty would be silence. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire are the divine diagnosis, delivered while the disease is still curable, and the verse before us is addressed to the living — its purpose is that no reader should verify it personally. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have wept when this family of verses was recited, and to have sought refuge in God from the Fire in his every prayer — the report of his seeking refuge is constant in the authentic collections:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to say: 'O God, I seek refuge in You from the punishment of the Fire, and from the punishment of the grave, and from the trial of life and death, and from the trial of the False Messiah.'"* (al-Bukhārī 6377; Muslim 588)

And the Qur'an itself places beside every description of the Fire its counterweight, the description of what the fearful may reach — which is exactly what this sūrah does next. The reader who has now seen the two destinies — the reminder received by the fearful, the Fire awaiting the shunner — stands precisely where the sūrah wants him: between the Garden and the Fire, addressed by verses 14–15 with the only question that matters — which life will he choose to live, and by what act of purification will he make his choice real?

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:14

> **Prosperous indeed is he who purifies himself,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pivot of the Whole Sūrah: Prosperity Through Purification**

*Qad aflaḥa man tazakkā* — "Prosperous indeed is he who purifies himself." With this verse the sūrah pivots from the two destinies of warning to the one path of success — and the pivot is marked by the strongest word of affirmation the Arabic language has for achievement. *Qad aflaḥa*: qad is the particle of realized certainty, and *aflaḥa* is the fourth form of the root *f-l-ḥ*, whose field is to prosper, to succeed, to break through to one's goal — the root from which falāḥ (success, salvation), and the daily call of the muʾadhdhin's cry *ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ* ("come to success"), are drawn. The verb is in the perfect tense: the prosperity is stated as an accomplished fact, as though the one who purifies himself has already won, the verdict already inscribed. The Qur'an uses the same formula at the two other summits of its moral teaching, and the repetitions frame the whole Book's definition of success:

> *"Prosperous are the believers, who are humble in their prayer."* (Qur'an 23:1–2)

> *"By the soul and Him Who proportioned it, and inspired it with its wickedness and its righteousness: he has prospered who purifies it, and he has failed who buries it."* (Qur'an 91:7–10)

The word for the act that earns this prosperity is *tazakkā* — the fifth form of the root *z-k-w*, the root of growth, increase, and purity; the fifth form means to purify oneself, to cleanse oneself, to cause oneself to grow by cleansing. The root is the same as that of zakāh, the purifying alms, and of tazkiyah, the purification of the soul that the scholars of Islamic spirituality placed at the center of the religious life. The commentators enumerated the senses in which the verse's purification was understood, and the enumeration itself is a map of the Qur'anic doctrine of salvation. Some of the earliest authorities — and this remained the view of many — understood tazakkā here as purification from idolatry: the cleansing of one's confession from every association (shirk) with God, the first and greatest impurity of the soul, washed away by the testimony that there is no god but God. Others understood it as purification through righteous deeds: the cleansing of the character and the conduct, the soul's growth by the pruning of its vices. Still others — and this reading is strongly represented in the tradition and connects the verse to its immediate sequel about prayer — understood it as the specific purification of the alms: the giving of zakāh, and in particular, in the view transmitted from the family of the Prophet and favored by several commentators, the alms of ʿĪd al-Fiṭr, given at the close of Ramadan to purify the faster from the lapses of his fast before he stands in the ʿĪd prayer. The Shiite tradition records that the sixth Imam, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, drew the order of the two verses into a liturgical instruction: the alms are given in the morning before the two cycles of the ʿĪd prayer, so that the worshipper stands before God purified by his giving.

The readings are not rivals but layers. The purification that begins with the confession of God's oneness, continues through the giving of what God has given, and is perfected in the cleansing of the soul's vices — this is tazkiyah in its full Qur'anic breadth, and the verse's promise covers the whole: qad aflaḥa man tazakkā — whoever undertakes this cleansing has succeeded.

**The Logic of Purification: Why the Soul Must Be Cleansed Before It Can Grow**

The verse rests on a principle that the Qur'an treats as self-evident and that the modern science of the mind has independently rediscovered: nothing grows in a medium that is not first cleansed. The seed of the reminder (verses 9–10) was sown in the soul; but the soul, left to itself, accumulates — in the Qur'an's vocabulary — rust, covering, disease: "in their hearts is a disease" (2:10); "nay, but what they earned has rusted upon their hearts" (83:14). Before the seed can grow, the medium must be cleared; before the soul can rise, the weights must be shed. This is the logic of tazkiyah, and it explains why the Qur'an never presents salvation as a mere legal transaction or an intellectual assent: the confession must be spoken, but the speaking must be followed by the cleansing, and the cleansing by the growth. The classical science of the soul — developed in the works of al-Ghazālī, Ibn al-Qayyim, and the masters of the spiritual path — built itself on exactly this verse's architecture: the vices are impurities (shirk, pride, envy, greed, rancor); the virtues are growth; and the religious life is the sustained project of removing the first so that the second may flourish. The Prophet ﷺ summarized the same architecture in a saying that defines the believer's daily project:

> *"Indeed, God does not look at your forms or your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds."* (Muslim 2564)

And the Qur'an states the goal of the whole prophetic enterprise in a single verse that could stand as the gloss on ours: "He it is Who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from among themselves, reciting to them His signs, and purifying them (yuzakkīhim), and teaching them the Book and the Wisdom" (62:2). The Prophet was sent to purify — and the sūrah before us tells every hearer that the purification he was sent to perform is one the hearer must enact upon himself: tazakkā, purify yourself; no one can be purified against his will, and no one who wills it is refused.

**Purification as the Door of the Hereafter: The Verse That Bridges Both Worlds**

There is a final subtlety in the verse's grammar that the commentators treasured: the verse does not say "he will prosper who purifies himself in the Hereafter," nor "he who purifies himself prospers in this world" — it says simply qad aflaḥa: he has prospered, absolutely, without specifying the world. The prosperity is total: the one who purifies himself prospers in both worlds, because the purification itself is already the beginning of the prosperity — the unburdened soul is lighter in this life, and the cleansed soul is ready for the next. The verse thereby answers the sūrah's own question before it is asked: the verses that follow will rebuke those who prefer this world to the Hereafter (16–17); but this verse has already shown that the choice is not between this world and the next, but between the self that is cleansed and growing in both, and the self that is buried under its own accumulations in both. The man who purifies himself loses nothing of this world that was worth keeping — the Qur'an is emphatic that the God-fearing are not impoverished by their fear: "And whoever fears God, He will make a way out for him, and provide for him from where he does not reckon" (65:2–3). What he gains is the only prosperity that outlasts the pasture's withering: the soul grown by its cleansing, standing before its Lord as the sūrah's next verse describes it — remembering His Name and praying.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:15

> **and remembers the Name of his Lord, and prays.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Acts That Complete the Prosperous Life**

*Wa-dhakara isma rabbihi fa-ṣallā* — "and remembers the Name of his Lord, and prays." The verse completes the definition of the prosperous one with two acts, joined by the particle fa, which marks sequence and consequence: he remembers the Name of his Lord, and then he prays. The order is deliberate, and the commentators drew the full doctrine from it. The remembrance (dhikr) comes first, and the prayer (ṣalāh) follows — not because prayer is secondary, but because prayer without remembrance is a form without a soul, while remembrance without prayer is a feeling without a form. The purified soul of verse 14 expresses itself in the two directions that exhaust the religious life: upward, in the remembrance of the Name — the tongue and heart turned to God; and outward, in the prayer — the whole body turned to God in the ordered worship that the Prophet called the pillar of the religion. Some commentators noted that the remembrance here includes the very basmalah — the mention of God's Name — with which the prayer begins, and every act of the day is consecrated; others — and this is the deeper reading preserved by al-Ālūsī and the spiritual exegetes — insisted that the remembrance must be of the heart and the tongue together, for the remembrance of the tongue while the heart is heedless has no weight and no fruit: the dhikr that precedes prayer is the mind's arrival before the body's.

The verse's dhikr is the echo of the sūrah's own first verse — "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" (87:1). The sūrah opened with the command to remember the Name; it here closes the portrait of the successful life with the same act performed by the purified soul. The symmetry is exact: glorification commanded at the beginning, glorification accomplished at the end; the Name that the Most High commanded the Prophet to glorify is the Name the prosperous one remembers and prays by. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same pairing the summary of all worship: "So remember Me; I will remember you; and give thanks to Me, and do not be ungrateful to Me" (2:152) — and states the supremacy of this very act: "the remembrance of God is greater" (29:45). The Prophet's ﷺ description of the one who remembers and the one who does not is the verse's practical commentary, recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī:

> *"The likeness of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is the likeness of the living and the dead."* (al-Bukhārī 6407; Muslim 779)

**The Prayer as the Seal of Remembrance**

Why does the verse join prayer to remembrance as its necessary sequel? The answer lies in the Qur'an's doctrine of the human being: the human creature is not a detached mind that can worship by intention alone; he is a body and a soul, and his worship must engage both or it engages neither for long. The remembrance that does not issue in the prayer evaporates — a mood, a sentiment, a passing warmth; the prayer gives the remembrance a body, a schedule, a posture, a community — it turns the intention into an institution of the soul. The ṣalāh is, in the Qur'an's own definition, the remembrance made durable: "Indeed, prayer prohibits indecency and evil, and the remembrance of God is greater" (29:45); it is the appointment at which the servant stands before his Lord five times a day, and the Prophet ﷺ described its centrality in terms that admit no compromise:

> *"The covenant between us and them is the prayer; whoever abandons it has disbelieved."* (al-Tirmidhī 2621; Ibn Mājah 1078)

> *"The first matter concerning which the servant will be called to account on the Day of Resurrection is the prayer; if it is sound, the rest of his deeds will be sound, and if it is corrupt, the rest of his deeds will be corrupt."* (al-Tirmidhī 413; al-Nasāʾī 465)

The sūrah's own career in the Prophet's worship enacted this verse more literally than any other passage of the Qur'an: the chapter that commands the remembrance of the Name and the prayer is the chapter the Prophet recited in the two ʿĪd prayers, in the Friday prayer, and in his night worship — so that the command of verse 15 has been performed, liturgically, tens of thousands of times by the community that received it, on the very occasions when the purified and the praying stand before God in the largest assemblies of the year. Muslim records the practice from Samurah ibn Jundub and al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr, as cited in the introduction to this sūrah: the Prophet recited "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" and Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah in the two ʿĪds and on Friday (Muslim 878). And the verse's pairing of remembrance and prayer in that order is what makes the famous practice of the ʿĪd complete: the worshipper purifies himself by the alms (verse 14), remembers his Lord in the takbīrs of the morning, and stands in the prayer — the very sequence this sūrah describes, which is why those who read verses 14–15 as the ʿĪd verses found their reading so natural.

**The Psychology of the Pair: Why Ritual Completes Intention**

There is a profound psychological realism in the verse's insistence that the remembrance must become prayer. Every human intention — the intention to be grateful, to be mindful, to be good — decays unless it is given a scheduled form; the mind forgets what the body does not repeat. The prayer is the body's repetition of the soul's intention: five times a day the purified believer stands, bows, and prostrates in the direction of the House of God, pronouncing the very glorification with which this sūrah opened — subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā in the prostration — so that the dhikr of verse 15 is not left to the fluctuating moods of the heart but is secured in the unmissable appointments of the day. The Prophet ﷺ described the five prayers as a cleansing river at the door of the man who prays:

> *"What do you think — if a river ran at the door of one of you, and he bathed in it five times a day, would any dirt remain on him? They said: No. He said: That is the likeness of the five prayers, by which God erases sins."* (al-Bukhārī 528; Muslim 667)

The prayer, in this image, is the daily re-purification that verse 14's tazakkā begins: the soul that purifies itself and prays is the soul that is washed, and re-washed, until the washing becomes its second nature. And the sūrah's own promise stands over the whole: qad aflaḥa — he has prospered. Not "he will prosper" but "he has prospered": the verdict on the one who purifies himself, remembers the Name, and prays is already written, as surely as the verdict on the shunner of verse 11 is already written. Between the two verdicts the sūrah has placed the whole of the human freedom: purify or bury, remember or avoid, pray or prefer — and the next two verses will name, with a bluntness that has startled readers for fourteen centuries, the precise failure of those who choose the wrong side of the choice.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:16

> **Nay, but you prefer the life of this world,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis of Failure: A Preference, Not an Error**

*Bal tuʾthirūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* — "Nay, but you prefer the life of this world." The verse opens with the particle of correction, bal — "nay, rather" — and the correction is aimed like an arrow at the reader. After the sūrah has shown the prosperous one (verses 14–15) and the shunner (verse 11), it now turns directly to its audience with the second person plural: *tuʾthirūna* — "you prefer." The verb is from the root *athara*, which means to give precedence, to choose one thing over another, to favor — and the Qur'an's choice of this verb is the heart of the verse's diagnosis. The failure it names is not ignorance: the verse does not say "you do not know" or "you have not been told." It says "you prefer." The human tragedy, in the Qur'an's analysis, is never finally a failure of information; it is a failure of preference — the will choosing the lesser good while knowing the greater. The same diagnosis is made elsewhere in the same vocabulary: "Nay, but you love the immediate, and forsake the Hereafter" (75:20–21); "And the Hereafter is better for you than the first" (93:4). The word al-ḥayātu al-dunyā — "the life of this world" — is itself a Qur'anic coinage of immense resonance: dunyā is the feminine elative of the root *d-n-w*, meaning the nearer or the lower, and the phrase means "the nearer life," the life that is immediately at hand — as opposed to al-ākhirah, the last life, the life that is ultimate. The very names tell the story: one life is near, low, and passing; the other is final, and it abides.

The commentators discussed whom the "you" addresses. Some read it as directed to the disbelievers of Makkah — the ones whose preference for the world expressed itself in their rejection of the reminder and the account. Others — and this reading is the more searching one, and the one that has kept the verse alive in Muslim consciences ever since — read it as an address to humanity at large, believers included, since the preference for the near over the final is a temptation that does not respect the boundary of faith. The second reading is supported by the verse's own position: it follows immediately after the portrait of the prosperous believer and his prayer, so that the "you" falls on every hearer, including the one who has just heard the definition of success — for hearing the definition and living it are separated by exactly this preference. Al-Ṭabarī and the classical exegetes note that the verse is a rebuke that cuts across every category: the believer who prays can still prefer the world in his ambitions, his anxieties, his loves; and the verse's plural address leaves no one outside its scope. The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same warning about his own community with a directness his Companions found sobering:

> *"By God, it is not poverty that I fear for you, but that the world will be spread out before you as it was spread out before those before you, and you will vie for it as they vied for it, and it will destroy you as it destroyed them."* (al-Bukhārī 4015; Muslim 2961)

**What "Preferring the World" Actually Means: A Precise Definition**

The verse's accusation would be merely rhetorical if it did not carry a precise content, and the Qur'an supplies the precision across its pages. To prefer the life of this world is not to live in it — everyone lives in it, and the Qur'an explicitly permits and even commands the enjoyment of its good things: "Say: who has forbidden the adornment of God which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things of His provision?" (7:32); "and do not forget your share of this world" (28:77). The preference the verse condemns is a structure of the heart: to make the near life the final reference point of one's choices — to measure success by its standards, to invest one's ultimate hope in its returns, to arrange one's deeds for its applause, to fear its losses more than the loss of God, to postpone the soul's business to the world's convenience. The Qur'an's portrait of this preference in its most concentrated form is the famous passage that begins with the world's own self-description:

> *"Know that the life of this world is but play and diversion and adornment and boasting among you and rivalry in increase of wealth and children — like the likeness of rain whose vegetation pleases the farmers; then it dries up and you see it turning yellow, then it becomes chaff. And in the Hereafter is a severe punishment, and forgiveness from God and approval; and the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 57:20)

The verse's word for the world's enjoyment — matāʿ al-ghurūr, "the enjoyment of delusion" — is the Qur'an's most compressed diagnosis of the preference: the world is not worthless in itself — the rain and the green are real — but it is delusive as a destination, because it promises permanence and delivers decay, exactly as this sūrah's own pasture taught in verses 4–5. The preference of verse 16 is thus the precise irrationality of the farmer who, having watched every crop of every year blacken into refuse, decides to build his house on the field. The Qur'an says of this choice, in a verse of stunning finality: "You desire the fleeting goods of this world, while God desires the Hereafter" (8:67) — the preference for the world is not merely a miscalculation; it is a contradiction of what God Himself desires and has built the human being for.

**The Psychology of the Preference: Why the Near Always Tempts**

Why do human beings so consistently prefer the near over the final, when the final is admitted to be better? The Qur'an's answer, scattered through its analysis of the soul, is that the preference is a function of the visible over the unseen, and the immediate over the delayed — a law of the mind that modern psychology has renamed temporal discounting: the nearer reward exerts a pull disproportionate to its size, and the distant reward, however great, is discounted by its distance. The world's goods are visible, tangible, and immediate; the Hereafter is unseen, and its arrival is deferred. The verse before us is the Qur'an's diagnosis of this law, and the Qur'an's cure for it is everywhere the same: to make the unseen present to the heart — by faith, by remembrance, by prayer, by the contemplation of death — until the deferred reward becomes more real to the soul than the immediate one. The Prophet ﷺ described the world's smallness with an image that has governed Muslim detachment for fourteen centuries:

> *"The world compared to the Hereafter is like one of you dipping his finger into the sea: let him see what it brings back."* (Muslim 2858)

And the same Prophet described the man whose preference has been cured — the man for whom the Hereafter has become the present:

> *"Whoever makes the Hereafter his concern, God will place sufficiency in his heart, gather his affairs for him, and the world will come to him humbled; and whoever makes the world his concern, God will place poverty before his eyes, scatter his affairs, and nothing of the world will come to him but what was written for him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2465)

The verse before us is thus not a condemnation of the world but a call to reorder the heart within it: to live in the near life without preferring it — to use it as a field, not a home; to pass through it, in the Prophet's ﷺ image, "like a stranger or a wayfarer" (al-Bukhārī 6416). And the next verse will give the reason that makes the reordering rational, in the sūrah's simplest and most decisive sentence: the Hereafter is better, and it is more enduring.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:17

> **while the Hereafter is better and more enduring.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Settles the Comparison in Seven Words**

*Wa-al-ākhiratu khayrun wa-abqā* — "while the Hereafter is better and more enduring." The verse is the sūrah's verdict, delivered in seven Arabic words, on the preference that verse 16 condemned. The two adjectives are chosen with mathematical care, because they cover the two axes on which any good can be compared: khayrun — "better" — names the Hereafter's superiority in quality, and abqā — "more enduring" — names its superiority in duration. The Qur'an's argument is that the Hereafter beats the world on both axes at once: it is better in kind, and it lasts longer in time; and when one good exceeds another in both quality and duration, the preference for the lesser is not a matter of taste but of error. The verse thereby converts the moral rebuke of verse 16 into a logical argument: you prefer the world, but the world is worse and shorter; your preference contradicts your own standard of preference, for no one who genuinely compares goods chooses the worse and the shorter when the better and the longer is available. Al-Ṭabarī and the commentators note the quiet superlatives: the Qur'an does not say the Hereafter is "good" but that it is "better" — better than the world — and the comparison itself is the argument, since the thing compared is not an abstraction but the actual content of the two lives: the world's goods, however real, are the goods of a passing season; the Hereafter's goods are the goods of the soul's eternal morning.

The same double adjective recurs in the Qur'an wherever the comparison is drawn, and the repetitions form a single sustained argument against the preference:

> *"Whatever you have been given is but the enjoyment of the life of this world; but that which is with God is better and more enduring, for those who believe and put their trust in their Lord."* (Qur'an 42:36)

> *"What is with you runs out, while what is with God remains; and We shall surely recompense the patient according to the best of what they used to do."* (Qur'an 16:96)

> *"And the Hereafter, with your Lord, is for the reverent."* (Qur'an 43:35)

> *"And truly the Hereafter is greater in degrees and greater in eminence."* (Qur'an 17:21)

The verse of Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā — revealed, like this sūrah, in the early Makkan years — states the same verdict with a personal tenderness: "And the Hereafter is better for you than the first" (93:4), addressed to the Prophet in the very period of this sūrah's revelation, when the world's balance sheet showed only loss — persecution, exile, poverty — and the Hereafter's balance sheet showed everything. The verse before us generalizes the Prophet's consolation to every believer: whatever the world's account of your life, the comparison that matters is the one this verse draws, and in that comparison the Hereafter wins on both counts.

**The Two Adjectives as a Complete Philosophy of Time**

It is worth dwelling on the word abqā, because it carries a subtlety that modern life has made urgent. The root *b-q-y* means to remain, to abide, to outlast; abqā is the elative — "more remaining," "longer-lasting," "more abiding." The world, the Qur'an says in its own vocabulary, is fānin or dhāhib — perishing, passing away; the Hereafter is bāqin — abiding. The comparison of durations is not merely quantitative — a longer life against a shorter one — but qualitative: the world's goods are the kind of thing that passes, not only because they end but because they are the sort of good that decays while it lasts — health fades, youth flees, pleasure cloys, wealth loses its taste, and the very enjoyment of the world is haunted by the world's transience. The Hereafter's good is the kind that abides without decay — the Qur'an describes the Garden promised to the reverent as one whose "produce is perpetual, and its shade" (13:35), whose inhabitants are those upon whom "no fear shall come, nor shall they grieve" (2:112, and throughout) — so that the difference between the two lives is not the difference between a short pleasure and a long one but the difference between a good that carries its own end inside it and a good that carries its own eternity. This is why the Prophet described the believer's relation to the world in the famous saying recorded by Muslim:

> *"The world is the prison of the believer and the paradise of the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

The saying is not a counsel of misery but an exact description of the two comparisons in our verse: for the believer, who knows the better and more enduring good, the world's goods are too small to be his home — to be confined in them, unable to reach the true home, is a prison; for the disbeliever, who has no other home, the world's goods are the only paradise he will ever know — and he is given it freely, as the Qur'an says: "Whoever desires the life of this world and its adornment, We will repay them in full for their deeds therein, and they will not be shortchanged therein — those are the ones for whom there is nothing in the Hereafter but the Fire" (11:15–16).

**The Rational Choice and Its Modern Obstacles**

The verse's argument — choose the better and more enduring good — is, on its face, the most rational counsel ever given; and the history of human behavior is the history of not taking it. The obstacles are worth naming honestly, because the verse itself is the remedy for them. The first obstacle is invisibility: the Hereafter is unseen, and the human mind discounts the unseen with a rigor that no argument entirely overcomes — which is why the Qur'an's cure for the preference is not argument alone but the disciplines that make the unseen present: the prayer that rehearses the standing before God, the remembrance that keeps the account in view, the contemplation of death that the Prophet ﷺ urged: "Remember often the destroyer of pleasures — death" (al-Tirmidhī 2307). The second obstacle is delay: the world's goods are now, the Hereafter's later; and the mind's discounting of the delayed is the very mechanism verse 16 diagnosed. The third obstacle is the world's own propaganda: the life of this world is advertised, in every age, as the only life — its scale enlarged until the Hereafter seems a rumor — and the Qur'an names this propaganda precisely: "the enjoyment of delusion" (57:20). Against all three obstacles the verse sets the double adjective: better, and more enduring — quality and duration — the two criteria by which every rational chooser chooses, applied to the only choice that outlasts every other.

**The Practical Yield: Living with the Comparison Constantly in View**

The verse yields a practical discipline that the classical scholars called "preferring what abides over what passes" (īthār al-bāqī ʿalā al-fānī), and its daily form is a simple habit of translation: when the world offers its goods, ask what they are worth on the scale of the abiding; when the world threatens its losses, ask whether they touch the abiding; when a deed presents itself, ask which of the two lives it serves. The Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions a supplication that makes the verse's comparison the content of daily prayer — the prayer for good in both lives, without confusion about which is which:

> *"Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire."* (Qur'an 2:201 — the verse the Prophet ﷺ most often used in supplication, and the companion of this sūrah's own verdict)

The believer who prays this prayer has not renounced the world — the prayer asks for the world's good — but has ordered the world under the Hereafter: the world's good is sought as provision for the journey, the Hereafter's good as the journey's end. And the sūrah's verdict stands over the whole ordering: al-ākhiratu khayrun wa-abqā — the Hereafter is better, and it abides. The pasture of verses 4–5 blackened and passed, as all pastures do; the remembrance of verses 14–15 abides; and the sūrah now closes its argument by revealing that this verdict — the preference for the abiding — is not a new teaching but the oldest teaching in the world, written in the scrolls of Abraham and Moses, confirmed by every prophet, and now renewed in the recitation of this sūrah.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:18

> **Truly this is in the earlier scrolls,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seal of Continuity: This Teaching Is Ancient**

*Inna hādhā la-fī al-ṣuḥufi al-ūlā* — "Truly this is in the earlier scrolls." With the emphatic particle inna and its accompanying lām — a double emphasis ("truly... indeed") — the sūrah seals its argument by revealing its pedigree. *Hādhā* — "this" — points back to everything the sūrah has delivered: the purification of verse 14, the remembrance and prayer of verse 15, the rebuke of the world's preference in verse 16, and the verdict that the Hereafter is better and more enduring in verse 17. Some of the commentators — al-Ṭūsī among them — understood "this" to refer specifically to those closing themes; others, including al-Ṭabarī, extended it to the whole content of the sūrah — glorification, creation, revelation, the two destinies, the way of success; and still others extended it further to the entire Qur'anic message, since every essential of the Qur'an is a renewal of what earlier revelation carried. Al-Ālūsī connected this verse with the Qur'an's own claim about the universality of its message's ancestry: "And truly it is in the scriptures of those of old" (26:196).

*Al-ṣuḥuf* is the plural of ṣaḥīfah — a scroll, a written page, a scripture — and *al-ūlā* means the first, the earlier, the former. The phrase "the earlier scrolls" is the Qur'an's name for the body of revelation that preceded it — the books and scrolls given to the prophets before Muhammad — and the Qur'an's use of the word ṣuḥuf is deliberately broader than the named books of the Torah and the Gospel, because it embraces the scroll-literature of the earlier prophets, including scriptures that the communities of Judaism and Christianity do not preserve or do not count among their canonical books. The verse's claim is precise and remarkable: the teaching that has just been delivered — purify yourself, remember your Lord, pray, and prefer the abiding over the passing — is not an innovation of the Makkan preacher, not the invention of a seventh-century reformer, but the perennial message of the prophetic tradition, written in the oldest scriptures known to the Arabs' spiritual ancestry. The Qur'an's answer to the charge that Muhammad had brought a novelty — a charge its opponents repeated through the Makkan years — is not merely to deny the novelty but to produce the pedigree: this is in the earlier scrolls.

The Qur'an makes the same argument elsewhere with the same appeal to ancient testimony, and the repetitions show how central the claim was to the Makkan preaching:

> *"And before it is the Book of Moses, a guide and a mercy; and this is a confirming Book, in an Arabic tongue, to warn those who do wrong and as glad tidings for the doers of good."* (Qur'an 46:12)

> *"Is it not a sufficient sign for them that the learned of the Children of Israel know it?"* (Qur'an 26:197)

> *"Those to whom We gave the Book before it — they believe in it."* (Qur'an 28:52)

**Why the Qur'an Grounds Its Claim in Ancient Scripture**

The logical function of the verse is worth making explicit, because it explains a feature of the Qur'an that modern readers sometimes find puzzling: why does a book that claims to be a direct revelation from God spend so much energy establishing its continuity with earlier scriptures? The answer lies in the nature of the claim itself. The Qur'an does not present itself as the first installment of a new religion but as the final confirmation of an ancient covenant — the same covenant of which it says: "He has ordained for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah, and what We have revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: that you establish the religion and do not be divided in it" (42:13). On this view, the truth is one because God is one; the prophets are a single chain because the message is a single message; and a revelation that contradicted the essential teaching of Abraham and Moses would disprove itself, while a revelation that confirmed it would authenticate itself. The verse before us is therefore an argument from coherence: the doctrine of this sūrah — the purification, the prayer, the preference of the Hereafter — is exactly the doctrine that the oldest scriptures taught; and the reader who knows the ancient scrolls is invited to recognize in this Makkan sūrah not a stranger but the return of a familiar, the renewal of the oldest promise. The Qur'an states the point of the whole argument in a verse whose finality has never been improved upon:

> *"Say: O People of the Book, you stand on nothing until you uphold the Torah and the Gospel and what has been sent down to you from your Lord."* (Qur'an 5:68)

**The Verse as an Interfaith Charter**

There is a further implication of the verse that deserves to be drawn with care, because it bears on the relations of the communities of faith in every age. When the Qur'an says that its message is in the earlier scrolls, it is not engaging in a merely polemical claim; it is establishing a theological principle of immense consequence: the earlier scriptures are witnesses to the truth, carriers of the divine message, and objects of respect and study for Muslims. The Qur'an repeatedly instructs the believers to affirm what was revealed before: "Say: We believe in God, and in what has been sent down to us, and in what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes, and in what was given to Moses and Jesus and the prophets from their Lord — we make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we submit" (2:136; 3:84). The Prophet ﷺ lived out this principle in his own dealings with the People of the Book — he affirmed the Torah's authority in its own sphere, accepted the testimony of learned Jews when it agreed with the revelation, and instructed his followers to argue with the People of the Book only in the fairest manner: "And do not argue with the People of the Book except in that which is best" (29:46). The verse before us anchors that whole ethic in the sūrah's closing claim: the Muslim who recites this sūrah is not reciting a scripture that stands over against the earlier scrolls; he is reciting a scripture that stands with them, confirming what they taught, renewing what they carried, and calling their communities back to the common core — the purification, the prayer, and the preference of the abiding. And with that claim established, the sūrah speaks its last two words, naming the scrolls themselves.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:19

> **the scrolls of Abraham and Moses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Witnesses Named: Abraham and Moses**

*Ṣuḥufi Ibrāhīma wa-Mūsā* — "the scrolls of Abraham and Moses." The sūrah ends by naming the two prophets whose scrolls carry its message, and the choice of the two names is anything but accidental. Abraham and Moses stand at the two ends of the prophetic history that the Arabs knew: Abraham, the father of the monotheists, the friend of God (al-Khalīl), the builder of the very Kaʿbah toward which the sūrah's worshippers pray, the prophet of pure submission (islām) before the division of the communities; and Moses, the lawgiver, the bearer of the Torah, the prophet of the exodus and the covenant, the most frequently named prophet in the Qur'an. Between them they span the entire arc of the earlier revelation: Abraham is the root, Moses is the trunk, and the Qur'an presents itself as the fruit that confirms both. The Qur'an elsewhere names the two together in exactly this way when it wants to establish the antiquity of a doctrine:

> *"Or has he not been informed of what is in the scrolls of Moses — and of Abraham, who fulfilled [his commission]? — that no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another, and that man has nothing but what he strives for, and that his striving will be seen, then he will be recompensed for it with the fullest recompense, and that to your Lord is the final end."* (Qur'an 53:36–42)

That passage of Sūrah al-Najm is the Qur'an's own commentary on this verse, for it states what the scrolls contained: individual responsibility, the fruit of striving, the certainty of recompense, and the return to God — the very doctrines this sūrah has delivered. The commentators noted that the two sūrahs belong to the same period of revelation and make the same argument: the Makkan audience, surrounded by the ruins of earlier communities and in contact with Jews and Christians who carried the scriptures, was being told that the message of the Qur'an was not a novelty but the content of the oldest books — the scrolls of Abraham and Moses.

**The Scrolls of Abraham: The Scripture That History Did Not Preserve**

The verse raises a historical question that the commentators addressed with candor: the scriptures of Judaism and Christianity do not include a book ascribed to Abraham, and the Torah itself does not claim that Abraham received written scrolls. Yet the Qur'an here affirms that Abraham had scrolls, and the affirmation is repeated in the passage of Sūrah al-Najm just quoted. The Islamic tradition resolved the question in two ways. First, it accepted the Qur'anic affirmation as the primary datum: if the Qur'an says the message is in the scrolls of Abraham, then Abraham received a scripture, even if the community that preserved his memory did not preserve his book. Second, it supplemented the Qur'anic affirmation with prophetic reports about the ancient books. The well-known report from Abū Dharr, transmitted in the hadith literature and cited by the commentators — among them al-Ālūsī in his Rūḥ al-Maʿānī — describes the Prophet's answer when he was asked how many books God had revealed:

> *"He said: One hundred and four books: God sent down fifty scrolls to Seth, thirty scrolls to Idrīs, ten scrolls to Abraham, and ten scrolls to Moses before the Torah; and He sent down the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms, and the Criterion."* (reported from Abū Dharr; cited by al-Ālūsī and the commentators, with a chain that some later hadith critics discussed)

The report gives Abraham ten scrolls and Moses ten scrolls before the Torah — and the Qur'an's own phrase "the scrolls of Abraham and Moses" matches it precisely, since the Qur'anic word ṣuḥuf (scrolls) is distinguished from the great named books (the Torah, the Gospel), suggesting a body of smaller scriptures belonging to the earlier prophets. The Shiite tradition, as the source commentary notes, preserves a slightly different narration of the same report, and some authorities counted the scrolls of Abraham as twenty. The differences in count are of no doctrinal moment; what matters is the unanimous conviction of the tradition: revelation did not begin with the Torah or the Gospel, nor even with Moses, but ran through the prophets from the beginning of the human story, and Abraham — the friend of God — received his own portion of it. The Qur'an's own naming of the content of Abraham's scrolls is preserved in the Sūrah of the Most High's sister-passage in Sūrah al-Najm, and in the traditions that describe the counsel of the ancient scrolls — the same counsel this sūrah has delivered: purification, prayer, and the preference of the abiding. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ confirmed the unity of the whole when he said, as recorded in the collections:

> *"I am the nearest of people to Jesus son of Mary in this world and the Hereafter — the prophets are brothers: their mothers differ, but their religion is one."* (al-Bukhārī 3443; Muslim 2365)

**The Scrolls of Moses: The Law and Its Heart**

The mention of Moses completes the genealogy of the message with the prophet whose scripture the Arabs most associated with the People of the Book — the Torah, the great law-book of the ancient world. The verse's point in naming Moses is twofold. First, it claims the Torah's own witness: the law of Moses, whatever its later communities made of it, taught the same essentials — purification, prayer, and the precedence of the service of God over the service of the world. The Qur'an says of the Torah: "Indeed, We sent down the Torah, in which was guidance and light, by which the prophets who submitted judged" (5:44), and it describes Moses himself as the model of the very preference this sūrah teaches: "And Moses said: O my people, if you have believed in God, then rely upon Him, if you are submitting" (10:84). Second, it answers the objection of novelty from the side of the law itself: the Arabs of Makkah dealt with Jewish communities who carried the Torah; the Qur'an's appeal to "the scrolls of Abraham and Moses" was an appeal to a witness they could, in principle, consult — and the Qur'an makes the appeal explicit elsewhere: "Ask the People of the Reminder if you do not know" (16:43; 21:7). The deepest point, however, is doctrinal rather than historical: the Qur'an's message is the religion of Abraham and Moses because it is the religion of God, and God does not change His religion — "the religion of your father Abraham" (22:78) is the Qur'an's own name for Islam, and the Prophet ﷺ was commanded to follow the creed of Abraham: "Then We revealed to you: Follow the creed of Abraham, the upright, and he was not of the idolaters" (16:123).

**The Closing of the Sūrah: The Message Returns to Its Source**

The sūrah that opened with the command to glorify the Name of the Most High closes with the scrolls of Abraham and Moses — and the architecture of the whole is now visible in a single glance. The glorification of verse 1 is the worship that the scrolls commanded; the creation of verses 2–5 is the evidence that the scrolls' God is the only God; the revelation of verses 6–9 is the renewal of the scrolls' message; the two destinies of verses 10–13 are the scrolls' warning; the purification and prayer of verses 14–15 are the scrolls' way; the preference of the Hereafter in verses 16–17 is the scrolls' wisdom; and the whole is sealed by the claim of verses 18–19: this is not new — this is the oldest word, carried by Abraham, carried by Moses, and now carried by the unlettered Prophet of Makkah, who recited it and did not forget, because the One who sent it down promised to preserve it. The tradition preserved in the sūrah's own introduction — the saying attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib that whoever recites al-Aʿlā has, in effect, recited the Qur'an and the scrolls of Moses and Abraham — is the community's own recognition of the sūrah's claim: this short chapter is a distillation of the whole prophetic message, and the one who holds the distillation holds, in principle, the chain that runs from Adam to Muhammad. The believer who recites the nineteen verses of al-Aʿlā — at Friday, at the ʿĪd, in the night prayer — stands, in the words of the commentators, at the meeting point of all revelation: glorifying the Name that Abraham glorified, praying the prayer that Moses prayed, purified by the tazkiyah that all the prophets taught, preferring the Hereafter that all the scrolls promised, in the recitation that God promised would not be forgotten — until the day when the pasture of this world is turned to refuse and only the abiding remains, and the Name that the sūrah glorified is seen as it is: the Name of the Most High, the First and the Last, the One who was worshiped by Abraham and Moses and Muhammad, and who is worshiped still.
