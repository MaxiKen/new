# Sūrah al-Aʿlā (Chapter 87) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Chapter Built Like a Complete Argument**

Sūrah al-Aʿlā, "The Most High," is a Makkan chapter of nineteen brief verses whose brevity conceals an unusually complete structure. It begins with a command to glorify God, moves through a compressed account of how God creates, proportions, decrees, guides, feeds, and finally withers His creation, turns to reassure the Prophet ﷺ about the revelation entrusted to him, states the purpose of prophetic preaching, divides humanity into the one who heeds and the one who avoids, names the destinies of each, defines success in three practical acts, diagnoses the disease that keeps most people from that success, and closes by declaring that this entire teaching is not new — it stood already in the oldest scriptures given to Abraham and Moses. In nineteen verses the sūrah supplies a theology of God, a theology of creation, a theology of revelation, an ethic of preaching, a psychology of human response, an eschatology, a practical program of worship, a critique of materialism, and a doctrine of the unity of all prophecy. Very few passages of comparable length in any scripture attempt so much.

The name of the sūrah is taken from its first verse: *sabbiḥ isma rabbika al-aʿlā*, "Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Most High." Muslims also refer to it simply as *Sabbiḥ*, after its opening word, and the early community frequently identified chapters by their opening rather than by a theme — a reminder that these were living recited texts before they were catalogued.

**Place and Time of Revelation**

The great majority of scholars classify al-Aʿlā as an early Makkan revelation, and it is commonly placed eighth in the chronological order of revelation, immediately after Sūrah 81, al-Takwīr. Everything about its style supports that placement: short rhythmic verses, end-rhyme that carries the ear forward, oaths of divine action rather than legal clauses, direct address to a Prophet still in the first years of his mission, and an audience being introduced to the very idea of resurrection rather than being legislated for.

A minority held that part of the sūrah — specifically verses 14 and 15, mentioning purification, remembrance, and prayer — belongs to the Madinan period, because certain reports interpret those verses as referring to the alms of ʿĪd al-Fiṭr and the ʿĪd prayer, neither of which was instituted before the Hijrah. Al-Ṭabarī records this view. Others, such as al-Baghawī, answered that a Makkan verse may perfectly well describe in general terms what a later Madinan institution would embody in specific form: revelation frequently lays down a principle first and gives it legal shape afterward. Fasting, charity, and prayer all existed in general Makkan commands long before their detailed Madinan regulation. On this reading — which is the stronger one — verses 14–15 mean purification, remembrance, and prayer as such, and the ʿĪd application is an instance rather than the meaning.

**The Prophet's Own Use of This Sūrah**

Few chapters are attached to as many concrete practices of the Prophet ﷺ as this one, which tells us how the earliest community experienced it: not as a text to be studied occasionally but as a companion of the weekly and yearly rhythm of worship.

Muslim records from al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr that the Prophet ﷺ used to recite this sūrah together with Sūrah al-Ghāshiyah (88) on the two ʿĪds and at the Friday congregational prayer:

> *The Messenger of God ﷺ used to recite in the two ʿĪds and in the Friday prayer: "Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Most High" and "Has there come to thee the tiding of the Overwhelming Event?" And when the ʿĪd fell on a Friday, he would recite both of them in both prayers.* (Muslim 878)

He also used it in the witr prayer. It is recorded from ʿĀʾishah and from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb that the Prophet ﷺ would recite in the three units of witr: *Sabbiḥ isma rabbika al-aʿlā*, then *Qul yā ayyuhā al-kāfirūn*, then *Qul huwa Allāhu aḥad* (recorded by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī, and Ibn Mājah).

And when Muʿādh ibn Jabal lengthened the ʿIshāʾ prayer for his congregation until a working man withdrew from the prayer, the Prophet ﷺ named this sūrah among those suitable for an imam leading tired people:

> *"Are you a man of trials, O Muʿādh? When you lead the people in prayer, recite 'By the sun and its morning brightness,' and 'Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Most High,' and 'Read in the Name of thy Lord,' and 'By the night when it covers.'"* (al-Bukhārī 705; Muslim 465)

It is further reported that when the verse was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ instructed his Companions to make its words the formula of prostration, saying, *"Make it in your prostration"* (recorded by Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah), which is why Muslims to this day say *subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā* — "Glory be to my Lord, the Most High" — while their foreheads are on the ground. Every believer who has ever prayed has therefore been reciting this sūrah's first verse, several times a day, for fourteen centuries, whether or not he knew its source.

In the Shīʿī tradition it is related from Imām ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib that if people knew the blessing contained in al-Aʿlā they would recite it ten times daily, and that whoever recites it has in a sense recited the Qur'an together with the scriptures of Moses and Abraham — a statement that reads the sūrah exactly as its own final verses invite: as a distillation of the perennial message.

**The Architecture of the Chapter**

The sūrah unfolds in five clearly marked movements.

*Verses 1–5: The God who is glorified and why.* The command to glorify is immediately grounded in five divine acts, each expressed in a tight verb: created, fashioned, measured out, guided, brought forth pasture — and then, decisively, made it withered debris. Glorification is not asked as blind obedience; it is asked of creatures who can look at their own bodies and at the grass under their feet and see the evidence.

*Verses 6–8: The Prophet's reassurance.* God will teach him to recite, he will not forget, his path will be eased. Amid the pressure of Makkan hostility and the fear of losing the words themselves, the sūrah pauses to steady the one carrying the message.

*Verse 9: The mandate.* "So remind" — the entire vocation of prophecy, and after him of every teacher and parent, condensed into two words.

*Verses 10–15: The two responses and the two ends.* The one who fears remembers; the wretched turns away; the fire; the deathless torment; and against that darkness, three simple acts of success — purify, remember, pray.

*Verses 16–19: Diagnosis and authentication.* Why most people fail — they prefer the immediate — followed by the correction that the Hereafter is better and lasts longer, and the closing assurance that this message is as old as revelation itself, present in the scriptures of Abraham and Moses.

**Why the Sequence Matters**

There is a logic running through the sūrah that is easy to miss when the verses are read as isolated fragments. God is first shown as the One who makes things and then perfects them, decrees for them and then guides them, feeds them and then ends them. That is precisely the pattern of a human life: made, formed, allotted a portion, guided, sustained, and finally reduced to dust. Once that pattern is established, the reminder of the Hereafter is not an alien doctrine imposed on a person from outside; it is the last term of a series he has already accepted in every other case. The one who has watched green pasture turn into blackened stubble knows exactly what will become of his own body. The sūrah then simply asks: given this, what should you prefer?

Everything after verse 5 is the working-out of that question. Revelation is given (6–8) so that the answer can be known; reminder is commanded (9) so that the answer can be heard; humanity divides (10–13) according to the answer it gives; success is defined (14–15) as the answer lived out; and the failure of most people is named for what it is — not ignorance, but preference (16–17).

**A Note on This Commentary**

What follows treats each of the nineteen verses as an independent and complete discussion, even where the source material groups verses in pairs. Each verse is examined through its Arabic vocabulary and morphology explained in plain language, its cross-references within the Qur'an with the relevant texts quoted, the authenticated words of the Prophet ﷺ with their collections identified, the readings of classical commentators such as al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Baghawī, and others, the doctrinal issues each verse raises, and — because a scripture that is not lived is not understood — the psychological, social, and practical consequences of taking each verse seriously in ordinary modern life.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:1

> **Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Most High,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Opening a Book with a Verb, Not a Statement**

The sūrah does not begin by informing us of something; it begins by asking something of us. *Sabbiḥ* — "glorify," "declare free of every imperfection" — is an imperative, and it is the first word the listener hears. This is characteristic of the early Makkan revelation: before doctrine is elaborated, an act is commanded, because the Qur'an consistently treats worship as the doorway to understanding rather than the reward for it. A person who begins glorifying God will come to know Him; a person who waits until he fully knows God before glorifying Him will wait forever.

There is a second reason for the imperative form. The Makkan audience was not irreligious. They spoke constantly of God, swore by Him, and called Him the creator of the heavens and the earth. What they lacked was not belief in a deity but a correct conception of Him — a God unmixed with partners, immune from need, above the images and intermediaries they had built around the Kaʿbah. *Sabbiḥ* strikes precisely at that: the first corrective the Qur'an offers is not "believe" but "purify your idea of God."

**What Tasbīḥ Actually Means**

The verb *sabbaḥa* comes from the root *s-b-ḥ*, whose primary physical sense in Arabic is to swim, to move swiftly and freely through water or space, to travel a distance away from a starting point. The Qur'an uses the root in this literal sense of celestial motion:

> *"And He it is who created the night and the day, and the sun and the moon, each swimming (yasbaḥūn) in an orbit."* (Qur'an 21:33)

From this root the religious meaning develops naturally. To perform *tasbīḥ* is to declare God *distant from* — to move Him far away in one's conception from — every defect, deficiency, need, partner, and resemblance to created things. Where *ḥamd* (praise) affirms what God is, *tasbīḥ* negates what He is not. Together they form the two halves of correct thinking about God, which is why the two are so often joined in the Qur'anic and prophetic formula *subḥāna Allāhi wa bi-ḥamdih* — "Glory be to God, and praise be to Him" — a phrase the Prophet ﷺ described in terms of extraordinary weight:

> *"Two words are light on the tongue, heavy on the Scale, beloved to the Most Merciful: subḥāna Allāhi wa bi-ḥamdih, subḥāna Allāhi al-ʿaẓīm."* (al-Bukhārī 6682; Muslim 2694)

Notice the structure of that formula: negation first, then affirmation. Clear away the false picture, then affirm the true one. All sound theology follows this order.

There is a further nuance in the intensive form *sabbaḥa* (form II). Arabic verbs of this pattern often carry the meaning of doing something repeatedly, thoroughly, or with effort. *Tasbīḥ* is thus not a single mental note that God is perfect; it is a sustained, repeated purification of one's conception of Him — because the human mind constantly slips back into imagining God as a larger version of a creature: a ruler who can be flattered, a partner who can be bargained with, a force that can be manipulated by ritual technique.

**Why "the Name of thy Lord" and Not Simply "thy Lord"**

The verse does not say "glorify thy Lord" but "glorify the *Name* of thy Lord." Commentators have offered several complementary explanations, each illuminating.

The first, favored by al-Rāzī and others, is that the word *ism* here is not an addition of meaning but a mark of reverence and a pointer to how glorification is actually performed. Human beings cannot reach God's Essence; what they can reach is His Name — the revealed word by which He has permitted Himself to be addressed. To glorify the Name is to glorify the only aspect of God that is placed within our grasp. In practice, tasbīḥ happens through speech, and speech works with names.

The second is that the phrase specifically forbids misusing God's Name: do not attach it to falsehood, do not swear by it deceitfully, do not use it to sanctify greed or cruelty, do not couple it with the names of false objects of worship. On this reading the verse is a command of linguistic purity. The name of God should not appear in a sentence that shames it. This is a far-reaching instruction in any age, and a pointed one in ours, where religious language is routinely conscripted to justify what religion forbids.

The third is that the verse instructs us to call upon God by the Names He Himself has disclosed, not by names we invent for Him. The Qur'an makes this explicit:

> *"Say: Call upon God, or call upon the Compassionate. Whichever you call upon, to Him belong the most beautiful Names."* (Qur'an 17:110)

> *"And to God belong the most beautiful Names, so call upon Him by them; and leave those who deviate concerning His Names."* (Qur'an 7:180)

Human beings have a long history of inventing gods and then inventing names for them. Revelation reverses the direction: God names Himself, and the servant uses what he has been given.

**Al-Aʿlā: The Superlative That Admits No Rival**

*Al-Aʿlā* is the superlative form (*afʿal*) of the root *ʿ-l-w*, meaning height, elevation, ascendancy. It means "the Highest," "the One above whom there is nothing." Grammatically the superlative in Arabic can express comparison ("higher than") or absolute pre-eminence ("the highest without qualification"); here the classical commentators are unanimous that the second is intended, because comparison would imply a scale on which God occupies the top position among others — and there is no such scale.

The commentators identify several senses in which God is *al-Aʿlā*:

*Highness of Essence* — He is above His creation, transcendent, not contained by it or mixed with it. The Qur'an affirms this without allowing the imagination to picture it: *"There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing"* (42:11). Ibn Taymiyyah insisted that affirming God's elevation is part of what revelation actually says and must not be explained away, while equally insisting that it be affirmed without likening God to anything created — the two principles held together, negation of resemblance and affirmation of what God said about Himself.

*Highness of rank and worth* — every perfection belongs to Him in its complete form, and every perfection found in creation is a borrowed trace of His.

*Highness of power and dominance* — nothing overrules Him; every other authority is derivative and temporary. *"And He is the Subjugator over His servants, and He is the Wise, the Aware"* (6:18).

There is also a grammatical question the commentators discuss with care: does *al-Aʿlā* describe "thy Lord" or "the Name"? Most, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī, take it as describing the Lord, so the verse means: glorify the Name of your Lord, who is the Most High. A minority read it as describing the Name — glorify the highest Name of your Lord — which yields the instruction to use the loftiest and most fitting expressions when speaking of God. Both readings are sound Arabic, and the Qur'an frequently permits such layered constructions so that a short verse yields more than one lawful meaning. The first is the plainer sense; the second adds a useful discipline of speech.

**"Thy Lord": Intimacy Inside Transcendence**

It would have been easy to read this verse as a purely intimidating declaration of divine remoteness, except that the verse deliberately says *rabbika* — "thy Lord" — before it says *al-aʿlā*. *Rabb* is not a title of distance. Its root, *r-b-b*, carries the sense of nurturing, raising, correcting, and bringing something step by step to its completion, which is why the same root gives *tarbiyah*, the ordinary Arabic word for the upbringing of a child.

So in a single line the Qur'an holds together the two truths that most religious systems fail to keep in balance. God is *al-Aʿlā*, infinitely above us — which prevents religion from degenerating into familiarity, superstition, and the manipulation of a manageable deity. And He is *rabbuka*, your own Lord, the One personally engaged in raising you — which prevents transcendence from collapsing into cold deism, a God too exalted to care. The believer's God is neither a distant abstraction nor a domesticated companion. He is the Most High who is nevertheless *yours*.

**The Prostration Connection: Highest Name, Lowest Posture**

The most striking practical outcome of this verse is its placement in the prayer. It is reported that when *"So glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Magnificent"* (56:74) was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ said, *"Make it in your bowing,"* and when this verse was revealed he said, *"Make it in your prostration"* (recorded by Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah; cited by al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī in their commentaries on this verse). From that instruction come the two formulas every praying Muslim repeats: *subḥāna rabbiya al-ʿaẓīm* in *rukūʿ*, and *subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā* in *sujūd*.

The pairing is deliberate and psychologically brilliant. The moment a human being is physically at his absolute lowest — forehead, nose, palms, knees, and toes on the ground, the seat of his intellect placed level with the dust he walks on — is the moment he is instructed to pronounce the Name of the Most High. The body says *I am low*; the tongue says *He is high*. The two statements verify each other. Anyone who has prostrated attentively knows that this is not humiliation but relief: the posture removes the exhausting human project of self-elevation and puts elevation where it actually belongs.

This is also why the Prophet ﷺ taught that prostration is the point of greatest nearness:

> *"The closest a servant is to his Lord is while he is prostrating, so increase supplication therein."* (Muslim 482)

The One declared highest is met at the lowest point. Height and nearness, in the divine case, do not compete.

**Why Glorification Is Psychologically Necessary, Not Merely Owed**

A modern reader may reasonably ask why an infinitely perfect God would command creatures to declare His perfection. The classical answer is straightforward: He does not need it; we do. God is *al-Ghanī*, free of all need, and the Qur'an says so bluntly in the ḥadīth qudsī recorded by Muslim:

> *"O My servants, were the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, to be as pious as the most pious heart of any one of you, that would not increase My kingdom in anything."* (Muslim 2577)

The benefit runs entirely in our direction, and it runs along at least three channels.

*It corrects the scale of one's problems.* A person who spends the day inside his own difficulties gradually loses proportion; the unanswered message, the unpaid bill, the office rivalry expand until they fill the whole horizon. Saying *subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā* restores the true relative sizes of things. Nothing shrinks anxiety faster than the accurate recollection that the One in charge is above all of it.

*It protects against pride.* The human ego is naturally expansionist. Left unchecked it converts talent into entitlement and success into superiority. Repeated glorification is the counterweight — a scheduled reminder, several times a day, that height belongs to Another.

*It keeps the concept of God clean.* Every religious community drifts toward making God resemble itself: tribal, partisan, purchasable. *Tasbīḥ* is the ongoing maintenance work that keeps the concept from corroding.

**A Practical Analogy: The Instrument That Must Be Retuned**

A stringed instrument left alone does not stay in tune; heat, humidity, and use pull it flat. It is not enough to tune it once at the factory. A musician retunes before every performance, and often between pieces. The human conception of God behaves the same way. Life pulls it out of tune: suffering makes God seem indifferent, success makes Him seem unnecessary, culture makes Him seem like a projection of our preferences. *Tasbīḥ* is the retuning — brief, frequent, and non-negotiable — and the Qur'an schedules it at exactly the moments when a person is most likely to drift:

> *"So be patient over what they say, and glorify with praise of thy Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting; and during periods of the night glorify Him, and at the ends of the day, that thou mayest be satisfied."* (Qur'an 20:130)

Note the closing purpose in that verse: *that thou mayest be satisfied*. Glorification is prescribed as a route to the servant's own contentment.

**Living the Verse**

The verse asks three concrete things of anyone who takes it seriously. First, that the words *subḥāna rabbiya al-aʿlā* in prostration stop being a reflex and become a statement one actually means — a small change that transforms the prayer from an exercise into a conversation. Second, that God's Name be kept out of speech that dishonors it: casual oaths, dishonest assurances, cruelty dressed in religious language. Third, that when circumstances press hardest, the first move be upward rather than inward. The order of the sūrah is instructive here: before it says anything about the fire, about preference for the world, about the scriptures of Abraham and Moses, it says *glorify*. Everything else in the chapter is built on the foundation laid by that one imperative.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:2

> **Who created, then fashioned,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why a Command Is Immediately Given a Reason**

The sūrah has just issued its imperative: glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High. It does not leave that command hanging as bare authority. Beginning here, and continuing through verse 5, the Qur'an attaches to the command a chain of divine acts, each introduced by the relative pronoun *alladhī* — "the One who." Glorify the Lord *who created, then fashioned; who measured out, then guided; who brought forth pasture, then made it blackened stubble.*

This is a pattern of enormous significance for how the Qur'an teaches. It rarely says "obey because you are told to." It says: here is who He is, and now draw the conclusion. Al-Rāzī observed that the four verses that follow the opening command function as evidence supporting it, in the manner of a proof following a claim. The listener is not asked to worship an unknown power; he is asked to consider what has demonstrably been done for him and to respond accordingly. This is an argument, not merely an order.

**Two Verbs, Two Distinct Divine Acts**

*Khalaqa fa-sawwā* — "created, then fashioned." Two verbs joined by the connective *fa-*, which in Arabic indicates immediate sequence and often causal order: this happened, and then straightaway that.

*Khalaqa*, from the root *kh-l-q*, means to bring into existence, but the root also carries the sense of measuring out and shaping material — an Arab would use it for a leatherworker cutting a sandal to size. In Qur'anic usage applied to God it means origination: bringing a thing from non-existence into existence.

*Sawwā*, from the root *s-w-y*, means to make even, level, balanced, and proportionate. The same root yields *siwā* (equal), *mustawī* (level), and *taswiyah* (leveling, proportioning). Applied to creation, it means the arrangement of the created thing so that its parts are correctly sized, correctly placed, and correctly related to one another.

So the verse describes two stages: existence, then form. First God brings the thing into being; then He proportions it so that it functions. This distinction matters more than it may seem. A pile of the exact chemical elements of a human body is not a human body. Existence alone is raw material; *taswiyah* is the arrangement that turns material into a functioning creature. The verse thus refuses the notion that creation is a single crude act of bringing stuff into being. Creation includes design.

**Who or What Was Created?**

The commentators offer three levels of reference for the unstated object of "created," and al-Rāzī presents them as concentric rather than competing.

*The human being in particular.* This is the most common reading, and it is supported by the Qur'an's parallel use of the very same verbs about man:

> *"O man, what has deceived thee concerning thy generous Lord, who created thee, then fashioned thee, then proportioned thee? In whatever form He willed He assembled thee."* (Qur'an 82:6–8)

Al-Ṭūsī and others explain that God fashioned the first human being, breathing into him of His Spirit and leaving him without irregularity or defect, and thereafter continued the same fashioning in every descendant.

*Human beings and animals together*, as the living creatures whose forms display evident proportioning.

*The whole of creation*, in which case the verse describes the cosmos: brought into being, then ordered into a functioning system of heavens, orbits, elements, and laws.

All three are defensible, and the Qur'an's habit of leaving an object unspecified is often intentional: the meaning expands to fill whatever the reader can perceive. A shepherd in seventh-century Arabia reading this verse thought of his own limbs and his flock. A physician reads it and thinks of the coordination of organ systems. An astronomer reads it and thinks of the fine balances that make a stable universe possible. The verse accommodates all of them without altering a letter.

**The Evidence Written Into the Human Body**

Since the primary reference is the human being, it is worth pausing on what *taswiyah* means concretely, because the argument of the verse depends on a person actually noticing it.

Consider the arrangement of the human head. The eyes are set high, where a scanning organ belongs, and paired, which produces depth perception. They are placed in bony sockets for protection, lubricated continuously, shaded by brows that divert sweat, and shuttered by lids that close reflexively faster than conscious thought. The ears are placed on opposite sides of the skull, which allows the brain to locate a sound by the microsecond difference in its arrival. The mouth is set below the eyes so that what is eaten can be inspected first. The nostrils face downward — a small detail with obvious utility in rain.

Consider the hand: four fingers and an opposable thumb, an arrangement that permits both the power grip that swings a hammer and the precision grip that threads a needle. Consider the spine: not a straight rod, which would transmit every footfall directly into the skull, but a curved column that acts as a spring. Consider that the human infant is born with a soft skull that can compress through the birth canal and then fuses afterward.

The Qur'an is explicit that this proportioning is meant to be read as evidence:

> *"And in the earth are signs for those of assured faith, and in yourselves. Will you then not see?"* (Qur'an 51:20–21)

> *"We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth."* (Qur'an 41:53)

The argument the verse presses is simple and logically respectable: arrangement of this order, sustained across every generation and every organ system, calls for an Arranger. One may debate mechanism endlessly; the Qur'an's claim concerns agency, and it asks the listener to face the coherence of what he is walking around inside.

**The Fashioning of Every Individual, Not Only the First**

Some might imagine *taswiyah* as a one-time event at the beginning of the human species. The Qur'an corrects this by describing it as continuous and personal, occurring in the womb of every mother:

> *"Then We made the drop into a clinging clot, and We made the clot into a lump, and We made the lump into bones, and We covered the bones with flesh; then We produced it as another creation. So blessed is God, the best of creators."* (Qur'an 23:14)

> *"He it is who fashions you in the wombs as He wills. There is no god but He, the Mighty, the Wise."* (Qur'an 3:6)

And the Prophet ﷺ described the stages with the same sense of a supervised process:

> *"Verily, the creation of each one of you is brought together in his mother's womb for forty days as a drop, then he becomes a clinging clot for the like of that, then a lump of flesh for the like of that; then an angel is sent to him who breathes the spirit into him..."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

The theological consequence is direct: no one is an accident. A person's existence is not the residue of an unattended process but the outcome of a deliberate act of forming. That conviction — that one was *made*, and made on purpose — is the foundation of every claim the sūrah goes on to make about accountability. Made things have purposes; accidents do not.

**Ibn Kathīr and the Perfection of Form**

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this verse, connects it to the Qur'anic declaration:

> *"Truly We created man in the most beautiful stature."* (Qur'an 95:4)

and to the divine address in 82:7, understanding *sawwā* as God's making the human being upright and well-proportioned, with faculties fitted to one another and to the world outside. The classical commentators frequently drew attention to the human upright posture as the sign of a creature designed to look outward and upward rather than downward at the ground — a physical form matching a spiritual vocation.

A note of care is required here, because the verse can be misread cruelly. That God fashions in perfect proportion does not mean that every body conforms to a single standard of appearance or ability. The Qur'an itself acknowledges variation as part of divine wisdom and specifically removes blame and burden from those whose bodies differ:

> *"There is no blame upon the blind, nor any blame upon the lame, nor any blame upon the sick."* (Qur'an 48:17)

Proportioning in the Qur'anic sense refers to the fitting of a creature to its purpose and to the wisdom in how it was made, not to cosmetic uniformity. A person with a disability is no less *fashioned*, and the tradition treats trials of the body as a station of nearness rather than a mark of divine neglect — the Prophet ﷺ taught, *"No fatigue, illness, anxiety, sorrow, harm, or distress befalls a Muslim, even the prick of a thorn, but that God expiates some of his sins thereby"* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573).

**The Ethical Weight of Being Made**

If a person is fashioned, three moral consequences follow immediately, and the tradition drew all three.

*The body is a trust, not a possession.* One does not have the standing to damage what one did not make. This underlies the Islamic prohibitions on suicide, self-mutilation, and intoxication, and equally the Prophetic instruction of moderation in eating: *"No human being fills a vessel worse than his stomach"* (al-Tirmidhī 2380, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). The Prophet ﷺ also rebuked the neglect of the body's rights: *"Your body has a right over you"* (al-Bukhārī 1975).

*Contempt for one's own form is contempt for the Maker's work.* This is a pointed teaching for an era in which self-image is shaped by curated photographs and algorithmic comparison. The verse relocates the standard: your form was fashioned by the Most High, not submitted for public approval. The Prophet ﷺ directed attention away from the surface entirely: *"God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds"* (Muslim 2564).

*Mockery of another's appearance is a serious offense*, since it is a criticism aimed past the creature at the One who fashioned him. The Qur'an forbids it explicitly: *"Let not a people ridicule another people; perhaps they may be better than them... and do not insult one another by nicknames"* (49:11).

**The Logic Being Built**

The verse also lays the first stone of the resurrection argument the Qur'an makes repeatedly. If creating and fashioning were done once, they can be done again; the second is not harder than the first. The Qur'an makes this inference explicitly elsewhere:

> *"And it is He who originates creation, then repeats it — and that is easier for Him."* (Qur'an 30:27)

> *"Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes indeed — We are able to proportion (nusawwiya) his very fingertips."* (Qur'an 75:3–4)

That last verse uses the same verb as ours, *sawwā*, and applies it to the fingertips — the ridged patterns that modern forensics would eventually discover to be unique to each individual. The point being made is that the One who fashioned at that resolution of detail is not troubled by reassembly.

**Practical Reflection: A Daily Inventory of Proportion**

There is an exercise implicit in this verse that costs nothing and changes a great deal. Each morning, before the day's demands begin, take an unhurried inventory of what has been fashioned: eyes that opened, lungs that filled without instruction, a heart that beat some hundred thousand times overnight without supervision, a mind that reassembled a coherent self out of sleep. None of these was purchased, earned, or arranged by the person benefiting from them.

The Prophet ﷺ built exactly this recognition into the first words spoken upon waking:

> *"Praise be to God who gave us life after having caused us to die, and unto Him is the resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 6312)

Even that short formula contains the sūrah's logic in miniature — creation, restoration, and return. A person who begins the day with an accurate account of what was given him has already answered the command of verse 1. He glorifies the Most High not out of obligation, but because he has just finished counting the evidence.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:3

> **Who measured out, then guided,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Form to Destiny**

The previous verse described what God did to bring a creature into being and give it shape. This verse describes what God did with its life. *Wa alladhī qaddara fa-hadā* — "and who measured out, then guided." Existence and form are not enough for a living thing to survive: it needs a portion allotted to it, and it needs to know how to reach that portion. The verse supplies both, and again in the tight sequence marked by *fa-*: first the measuring, then the guiding.

The pairing is precise. A measure without guidance would be a treasure buried where no one can find it. Guidance without a measure would be a map to nowhere. Together they describe a world in which every creature has both a share and a way to it.

**Qaddara: The Word Behind Qadar**

*Qaddara* comes from the root *q-d-r*, which carries two closely related families of meaning: measuring or determining an amount, and having power or capacity. From it come *qadar* (divine decree), *miqdār* (quantity, measure), *qudrah* (power), and *qadīr* (all-powerful). The intensive form *qaddara* used here means to determine precisely, to allot a definite amount, to set a thing's specifications.

The Qur'an uses the root repeatedly to describe a universe run by exact quantities rather than approximations:

> *"Indeed, all things We created with a measure (bi-qadar)."* (Qur'an 54:49)

> *"And there is not a thing but that with Us are its depositories, and We do not send it down except in a known measure (bi-qadarin maʿlūm)."* (Qur'an 15:21)

> *"...and He created everything and determined it with precise determination (fa-qaddarahu taqdīrā)."* (Qur'an 25:2)

The commentators note that the word is read in two ways in the recognized recitations: *qaddara* with doubling, meaning "measured out precisely," and *qadara* without it, which can carry the meaning of "had power over" or "determined." Al-Rāzī draws out the second sense: after God created and fashioned, He retained direct power over everything He made. The two readings are not in conflict; they name two aspects of the same reality. God set the measures, and God still holds the authority over them. Nothing was set running and then abandoned.

**What Has Been Measured**

The reach of *taqdīr* is easiest to grasp by descending from the cosmic to the personal.

At the level of physical law, the universe operates on quantities so specific that any significant deviation would make it uninhabitable — the strength of the forces that hold atoms together, the rate at which stars burn, the tilt of the earth's axis that produces seasons, the proportion of gases in the air. The Qur'an points to precisely this kind of quantitative order in the celestial bodies: *"The sun and the moon by exact calculation (bi-ḥusbān)"* (55:5), and *"the moon We have determined (qaddarnāhu) in phases, until it returns like an old date stalk"* (36:39).

At the biological level, every creature has a determined size, lifespan, diet, and habitat. A bee's body is measured to the flower it visits; a whale's to the ocean depth it dives.

At the personal level, taqdīr covers a person's lifespan, provision, and circumstances. The Prophet ﷺ described the fixing of these in the womb, in a report that follows immediately upon his account of the stages of embryonic formation:

> *"...then an angel is sent to him who breathes the spirit into him, and who is commanded with four matters: to write down his provision, his lifespan, his deeds, and whether he will be wretched or happy."* (al-Bukhārī 3208; Muslim 2643)

And belief in this decree is a pillar of faith itself. In the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel, when the visitor asked about faith, the Prophet ﷺ answered:

> *"That you believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day, and that you believe in the decree, its good and its evil."* (Muslim 8)

**Hidāyah: The Many Levels of Being Guided**

*Hadā*, "guided," from the root *h-d-y*, means to show the way, to lead toward a goal, to direct. The Qur'an uses it across a striking range, from the instinct of an insect to the illumination of a prophet's heart. The commentators, and al-Ṭūsī explicitly on this verse, identify at least two primary senses here, and the tradition as a whole distinguishes four or five levels.

*Instinctual guidance.* Every creature is equipped with the drives and knowledge it needs to find what has been measured out for it. The newborn seeks the breast without instruction. The migrating bird navigates thousands of miles it has never flown. The spider builds a geometrically precise web on the first attempt, having been taught by no one. The Qur'an states this principle in the mouth of Moses, in a verse that is almost a commentary on ours:

> *"He said: Our Lord is He who gave everything its created form, then guided it."* (Qur'an 20:50)

That verse contains the identical structure — creation, then guidance — and it is Moses' entire answer to Pharaoh's demand to identify his Lord. The Lord is the One who both makes a thing and equips it to live.

The Qur'an gives a memorable specific case in the bee:

> *"And thy Lord revealed to the bee: Take habitations in the mountains and in the trees and in what they build. Then eat of all the fruits and follow the ways of thy Lord made easy."* (Qur'an 16:68–69)

*Sensory and rational guidance.* Human beings are given faculties — sight, hearing, reason, moral intuition — by which they can investigate reality and reach conclusions. The Qur'an regards these as gifts that carry responsibility: *"And God brought you out of the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and made for you hearing and vision and hearts, that perhaps you would be grateful"* (16:78).

*Revealed guidance.* Beyond instinct and reason, God sent messengers and scriptures to make plain what unaided reason cannot settle — the nature of God, the details of worship, the shape of the afterlife. This is the sense al-Ṭūsī highlights: God guides people to His religion and to knowledge of His Oneness by manifesting signs and clear proofs.

*Guidance of enablement (tawfīq).* Knowing the way and walking it are different things. The final level of guidance is the divine assistance that turns knowledge into action — which is precisely why the Muslim, who already knows the path, still asks *ihdinā al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, "guide us upon the straight path," in every unit of prayer.

**Why Decree and Guidance Belong in the Same Sentence**

Placing *qaddara* and *hadā* in one breath resolves a difficulty that troubles many people about divine decree. If everything is already measured, why act at all?

The verse answers structurally: the decree includes the guidance. God did not merely allot outcomes; He allotted a path to them, and walking the path is part of what was decreed. A person's provision is written, and so is the fact that he obtains it by working. The Prophet ﷺ made this vivid:

> *"If you were to rely upon God with the reliance He is due, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out hungry in the morning and return full in the evening."* (al-Tirmidhī 2344, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

The birds are provided for — and they go out. Reliance in Islam has never meant passivity, and the Companions understood this immediately. When ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb was asked why he turned back from a plague-stricken land, and someone objected that he was fleeing God's decree, he answered:

> *"We are fleeing from the decree of God to the decree of God."* (al-Bukhārī 5729)

The logic is exact. Taking precautions is not a rebellion against destiny; it is participation in it.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked the same question in its starkest form — whether medicines and precautions can change what God has decreed — and his answer preserved both truths at once:

> *When the Companions asked whether the remedies they used and the precautions they took could repel anything of God's decree, he said: "They are from the decree of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 2065; Ibn Mājah 3437)

And in the well-known exchange after he had described the writing of destinies, when a Companion asked whether they should therefore abandon action and rely on what is written:

> *"Act, for everyone is facilitated toward that for which he was created."* (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2647)

That single sentence — *iʿmalū fa-kullun muyassarun li-mā khuliqa lah* — is the practical resolution. You cannot read the decree; you can only read your own choices. Effort is the only access any human being has to what has been written.

**Free Will Within a Measured World: The Classical Position**

The theological debate over decree and human freedom occupied Muslim thinkers for centuries, and the mainstream position that emerged holds several truths together without collapsing any of them.

God's knowledge is complete and eternal; He knows what every person will choose. But knowing a choice is not the same as forcing it. A teacher who has taught a student for years may know with certainty how he will answer a question; the knowledge does not write the answer.

Human beings possess real agency — the Qur'an addresses commands, warnings, and praise to them throughout, which would be meaningless if they were machines. *"Whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve"* (18:29).

At the same time, that agency is itself created and sustained by God; the capacity to act is granted moment by moment, so no one can claim independence from Him.

Ibn Taymiyyah's formulation is often cited: divine decree is to be used as a lens for understanding what has already happened, not as an excuse for what one is about to do. Before an action, a person has commands to follow. After a calamity, he has a decree to accept. Reversing these two — accepting sin as "decreed" and resenting misfortune as unjust — is the root of most confusion on the subject.

The Prophet ﷺ taught this ordering explicitly:

> *"Be eager for what benefits you, seek help from God, and do not despair. If something befalls you, do not say, 'If only I had done such-and-such,' but say, 'God decreed, and what He willed He did' — for 'if only' opens the work of Satan."* (Muslim 2664)

Note the placement: *"be eager for what benefits you"* comes first. Decree is invoked only after the outcome, and only to close the door on corrosive regret.

**The Psychological Gift of Believing in a Measure**

There is a practical benefit to this doctrine that no other worldview supplies as cleanly. A person who believes his provision and lifespan are measured is released from two exhausting fears: the fear that someone else's success has consumed his share, and the fear that his death depends on his own vigilance.

Envy, in particular, becomes irrational. If shares are allotted by the Most High, then another person's portion was never available to me, and resenting it is resenting the Allotter rather than the recipient. The Qur'an addresses this directly:

> *"And do not wish for that by which God has made some of you exceed others... and ask God of His bounty."* (Qur'an 4:32)

Notice the instruction that replaces envy: do not covet the allotment — *ask the Allotter*. The energy is redirected from horizontal comparison to vertical request.

Similarly, anxiety about the future is bounded. The believer still plans, saves, insures, and takes medicine — those are the guidance — but he does so without the metaphysical terror of a person who believes the entire outcome rests on the adequacy of his own foresight.

**An Analogy: The Well-Provisioned Journey**

Imagine a traveler sent on a long expedition by someone who has already surveyed the route. Before he sets out, supplies have been cached along the way at measured intervals — that is *taqdīr*. He is also given a map, a compass, and a set of instructions for reading the terrain — that is *hidāyah*. The caches do not walk to him; he must reach them, and he can only reach them by using the map. If he sits down and declares that the supplies were measured for him anyway, he starves within sight of provisions that were genuinely his.

That is exactly the relation between decree and effort in this verse. The measure is real, the guidance is real, and the walking is real. All three were arranged by the same Lord.

**Living the Verse**

Three habits follow from taking this verse seriously. The first is to work with full seriousness while holding the outcome loosely, since the effort is one's responsibility and the result is not. The second is to look for the guidance already provided before demanding new signs: the intellect, the scripture, the counsel of the knowledgeable, and the moral intuition planted in every person are the guidance the verse describes, and most of what people ask for in prayer has already been answered in one of these forms. The third is to treat one's allotted circumstances — the family, era, body, and constraints one did not choose — as the specific arena in which one's own path was measured, rather than as an obstacle standing between oneself and some other life that was never on offer.

The verse ends the way it does for a reason. It does not say "measured out" and stop, leaving creatures alone with a destiny they cannot read. It says *measured out, then guided*. The One who set the terms also showed the way.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:4

> **and Who brought forth pasture,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Descending from the Cosmic to the Grass Underfoot**

Three verses have described God as creator, fashioner, decreer, and guide — vast claims. Now the sūrah does something unexpected. Instead of ascending further into abstraction, it points at grass. *Wa alladhī akhraja al-marʿā* — "and who brought forth the pasture."

This is a deliberate rhetorical descent, and it is one of the Qur'an's most effective teaching methods. The great arguments are grounded in the smallest and most familiar things. To an audience of Makkan traders and herders, nothing was more ordinary than pasture, and nothing was more essential. Their wealth walked on four legs, and those legs depended entirely on what came out of the ground after rain. The verse takes the abstract claim "God is your Lord" and translates it into the concrete fact that they ate that month because grass grew.

There is also a logical function to the descent. It is easy to hold a distant theology and never let it touch daily life. By naming pasture, the verse makes divine sustenance a matter of what is under one's feet rather than what is beyond the stars.

**Akhraja: Bringing Out What Was Hidden**

*Akhraja* is the causative form of the root *kh-r-j*, "to go out." It means to bring out, extract, produce, or cause to emerge. The choice of verb is significant: God is not described as manufacturing vegetation but as *bringing it out* — drawing forth from the earth something that was concealed within it.

This matches the Qur'an's persistent way of describing plant growth as an emergence, and it carries an argument with it:

> *"And a sign for them is the dead earth: We give it life and bring forth from it grain, so that they eat of it."* (Qur'an 36:33)

> *"Then let man look at his food — how We poured down water in abundance, then split the earth in fissures, and caused to grow within it grain, and grapes and herbage, and olives and palm trees, and gardens of dense shrubbery, and fruit and grass — as provision for you and your grazing livestock."* (Qur'an 80:24–32)

That last passage is almost an expansion of our verse, ending on the identical note: pasture as provision, for people and for their animals alike.

The verb *akhraja* is also the exact verb the Qur'an uses for the resurrection, and this is not accidental:

> *"And God has caused you to grow from the earth a good growth. Then He will return you into it and bring you out (yukhrijukum) an actual bringing out."* (Qur'an 71:17–18)

The listener who accepts that God brings life out of dead soil every spring has already accepted the principle he denies when he laughs at resurrection. The Qur'an presses that comparison explicitly:

> *"And it is He who sends the winds as good tidings before His mercy, until, when they have carried heavy rainclouds, We drive them to a dead land and send down rain therein and bring forth thereby fruits of every kind. Thus will We bring forth the dead — perhaps you may take heed."* (Qur'an 7:57)

**Marʿā: The Word for Everything That Grazing Animals Eat**

*Al-marʿā* is a noun of place and object from the root *r-ʿ-y*, meaning to graze, to pasture, and — in its human extension — to tend, watch over, and take care of. From the same root come *rāʿī*, a shepherd, and *riʿāyah*, custodial care.

Linguistically *marʿā* denotes the place of grazing or what is grazed upon: herbage, grass, and by extension all vegetation that sustains animals. The commentators note that it covers all forms of vegetation, dry and fresh, cultivated and wild.

The root's double meaning — grazing and guardianship — is used by the Prophet ﷺ in one of the most famous statements on responsibility in the Islamic tradition, which turns the pastoral image into a universal ethic:

> *"Every one of you is a shepherd (rāʿin), and every one of you is responsible for his flock. The ruler is a shepherd and is responsible for his subjects. The man is a shepherd of his household and is responsible for his flock. The woman is a shepherd in her husband's house and is responsible for her flock. The servant is a shepherd of his master's property and is responsible for it."* (al-Bukhārī 893; Muslim 1829)

The connection is worth noticing. God brings forth the pasture; human beings are appointed shepherds. Provision comes from above; stewardship of it is delegated below.

**Animals Named Before Human Beings**

There is a quiet theological point in the choice of *marʿā* rather than a word for human food. Pasture is what animals eat. The verse could have said "brought forth grain" or "brought forth fruit," addressing the human listener directly. Instead it names the food of livestock.

Al-Rāzī and others observe that this widens the scope of divine lordship beyond the human species. God's provision is not a human welfare program; it is universal. The Qur'an states this as a general law:

> *"And there is no creature on earth but that upon God is its provision, and He knows its place of dwelling and place of storage. All is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 11:6)

That verse has consoled anxious believers for fourteen centuries, and its logic is simple: a God who has undertaken the feeding of every insect, fish, and grazing animal on the planet is not likely to have overlooked the person worrying about next month's rent.

There is also an indirect benefit to humans in the mention of pasture, since human sustenance depends on animal sustenance. Milk, meat, wool, leather, and transport in a pre-industrial economy all rested on grass. The Qur'an traces that chain elsewhere with unusual precision:

> *"And indeed, for you in grazing livestock is a lesson: We give you drink from what is in their bellies — between excretion and blood — pure milk, palatable to drinkers."* (Qur'an 16:66)

The verse is asking the listener to trace his dinner back through the animal to the field to the rain to the One who sent it.

**The Water Cycle as a Chain of Dependence**

Pasture does not appear on its own. It requires rain, which requires evaporation, wind, cloud formation, and condensation — a sequence the Qur'an describes repeatedly and challenges its listeners to claim credit for:

> *"Have you seen the water that you drink? Is it you who brought it down from the clouds, or is it We who bring it down? If We willed, We could make it bitter — so why are you not grateful?"* (Qur'an 56:68–70)

> *"Say: Have you considered — if your water were to become sunken into the earth, then who could bring you flowing water?"* (Qur'an 67:30)

These verses work by exposing a dependency that habit conceals. Nothing feels more automatic than the availability of water, until the day it stops. The person who turns a tap and receives clean water has, in that moment, been the beneficiary of a planetary system he did not design, could not maintain, and cannot replace. The verse asks him to notice.

**Green as a Mercy for the Eye and the Soul**

The commentators of a more contemplative bent noted that vegetation serves purposes beyond nutrition. Green landscapes rest the eye, calm the nervous system, and lift the mood — an observation the Arabs of the desert made long before it was measured in clinical studies of stress and green space.

The Qur'an itself draws attention to beauty in vegetation as a distinct favor, alongside its usefulness:

> *"And We sent down from the sky blessed water and caused to grow thereby gardens and grain from the harvest, and lofty palm trees having fruit arranged in layers — as provision for the servants; and We gave life thereby to a dead land. Thus is the emergence."* (Qur'an 50:9–11)

> *"...and We caused to grow therein gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and We caused springs to gush forth, that they may eat of His fruit. And their hands have not produced it — so will they not be grateful?"* (Qur'an 36:34–35)

The phrase *"their hands have not produced it"* is a direct rebuke of the illusion of self-sufficiency, and it lands harder in an industrialized economy than it did in an agrarian one. A farmer at least knows he did not make the rain. A city dweller who buys sealed produce off a shelf can go a lifetime without confronting the fact that no human being can make a seed.

**An Environmental Ethic Grounded in Ownership**

If pasture is brought forth by God, then it is His, entrusted to human beings for use rather than for destruction. The classical jurists built a substantial body of law on this foundation: rules against polluting water, against cutting trees in the sacred precincts, against wastefulness even at the riverbank, and the important category of *ḥimā* — protected pasture reserved from overgrazing, a practice the Prophet ﷺ and the early caliphs maintained.

The Prophetic teaching on planting is remarkable for making cultivation itself an act of continuing charity, regardless of who benefits:

> *"There is no Muslim who plants a tree or sows a crop, and then a bird or a human being or an animal eats from it, but that it is charity for him."* (al-Bukhārī 2320; Muslim 1553)

And the same ethic is extended to the very last moment of history in the well-known report:

> *"If the Hour arrives while one of you has a palm shoot in his hand, let him plant it if he is able before he stands."* (Musnad Aḥmad; graded authentic by scholars of ḥadīth)

Wastefulness is condemned even where the resource seems limitless. The Prophet ﷺ passed by Saʿd while he was performing ablution and said:

> *"What is this extravagance?" Saʿd said: "Is there extravagance even in ablution?" He said: "Yes, even if you are on the bank of a flowing river."* (Ibn Mājah 425)

Overgrazing, deforestation, aquifer depletion, and soil exhaustion are, in the framework of this verse, not merely economic errors. They are the mismanagement of something brought forth by the Most High and handed to a shepherd who will be asked about his flock.

**Kindness to the Animals That Graze**

The verse also indirectly grounds the tradition's treatment of animals. God feeds them; the human being who takes them into his service inherits an obligation to do the same. The Prophet ﷺ told of a man forgiven entirely for giving water to a thirsty dog:

> *"A man felt very thirsty while on a journey, so he went down a well and drank. On coming out he saw a dog panting and eating mud from extreme thirst. The man said: This dog is suffering the thirst I suffered. So he went down the well again, filled his shoe with water, held it in his teeth as he climbed out, and gave the dog a drink. God thanked him for that and forgave him." They said: "O Messenger of God, is there reward for us in animals?" He said: "In every living being there is reward."* (al-Bukhārī 2466; Muslim 2244)

And conversely, of a woman punished for the opposite:

> *"A woman was punished on account of a cat which she imprisoned until it died. She entered the Fire because of it: she neither fed it nor gave it water when she confined it, nor did she release it to eat of the vermin of the earth."* (al-Bukhārī 3482; Muslim 2242)

**Why This Verse Sits Here**

Structurally, verse 4 is the hinge on which the next verse turns. The sūrah has established a God who creates, forms, decrees, guides, and now feeds. Everything so far is benevolent, expansive, life-giving. The listener is being brought to a comfortable place — and in the very next line, the pasture will be turned to blackened debris.

That reversal is the point, and it only works because this verse first establishes the fullness of what will be taken away. A person cannot be taught the lesson of transience by being shown something already dead. He must first be shown the field at its greenest.

**Living the Verse**

Practically, this verse asks for a discipline of noticing. Provision arrives so reliably that gratitude atrophies; the Qur'an's method is to interrupt the automatic by naming it. Before eating, the believer is taught to say the Name of God — a small verbal act that reinserts the source into the transaction. Upon finishing, the Prophet ﷺ taught a supplication of striking humility:

> *"Whoever eats food and then says, 'Praise be to God who fed me this and provided it for me without any might or power on my part,' is forgiven his past sins."* (al-Tirmidhī 3458; Abū Dāwūd 4023)

The operative phrase is *"without any might or power on my part."* A person may have earned the money, driven to the market, and cooked the meal — and still not have grown a single grain of what he ate. The verse ends there: God brought forth the pasture. Everything else in the supply chain is transportation.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:5

> **then made it as blackened stubble.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn the Listener Did Not Expect**

Everything in the preceding four verses moved in one direction: existence, form, allotment, guidance, food. The listener has been carried along a rising sequence of divine generosity. Then, in five Arabic words — *fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan aḥwā* — the direction reverses. The pasture that was just brought forth is turned into dark, dried, worthless debris.

This reversal is the emotional and argumentative pivot of the first movement of the sūrah. Its purpose is not to depress but to complete the picture. A God who only gives and never takes would be understood as a supplier; a God who both gives and takes is understood as a Lord. And a creature who only ever saw growth would never ask what happens after growth stops.

Note also the grammatical shift. Verses 2–4 used the relative pronoun *alladhī*, "the One who" — a description. Verse 5 does not begin a new description; it continues the same clause with *fa-*, tying withering to growing as parts of a single divine act. Growth and decay are not two policies. They are one arrangement.

**Ghuthāʾ: The Word for What the Flood Leaves Behind**

*Ghuthāʾ* is a rich and unpleasant word. It refers to the scum, froth, and debris carried on the surface of a torrent — twigs, dead leaves, straw, and refuse that a flood sweeps along and deposits, dried and tangled, when the water recedes. By extension it means dried vegetation and worthless matter, the residue of what was once living.

The Qur'an uses the word in exactly this sense to describe the fate of a destroyed nation:

> *"So the shriek seized them in truth, and We made them as debris (ghuthāʾ). So away with the wrongdoing people."* (Qur'an 23:41)

There the metaphor is applied to a civilization; here to grass. The lesson is identical and the Qur'an intends the resonance: peoples wither exactly as pastures do.

The choice of *ghuthāʾ* rather than a neutral word for dryness is deliberate. It carries connotations of worthlessness and of being swept away by a force one cannot resist. Flood debris is not merely dead; it is dead, dirty, and in motion, going wherever the water sends it. That is a precise image for what time does to bodies, wealth, buildings, and reputations.

**Aḥwā: The Colour of Withering**

*Aḥwā* comes from the root *ḥ-w-y*, denoting a dark colour — blackish, dusky, dark brown, or a green so deep it appears black. The commentators disagreed productively about which stage of the plant's life the word describes, and both readings are instructive.

Some said *aḥwā* describes the pasture at its peak: intensely dark green from abundance and moisture, and then it withered. On this reading the sequence is: God brought forth pasture, deep green and lush, and then made it debris. The adjective belongs to the height of its beauty, and the tragedy is sharpened.

Others said *aḥwā* describes the debris itself: blackened, as dead vegetation turns when it rots and dries. On this reading the greenness is implied and the blackness is stated.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī both record the two views. Either way, the verse compresses the entire arc of a growing thing into a single line — brought forth, flourishing, blackened.

**The Full Cycle Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

This image is one of the Qur'an's most frequent, and its other occurrences make explicit what verse 5 leaves implicit: the cycle of vegetation is a parable of worldly life itself.

> *"Know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion and adornment and boasting to one another and competition in increase of wealth and children — like the example of a rain whose resulting plant growth pleases the tillers; then it dries and you see it turned yellow; then it becomes scattered debris. And in the Hereafter is severe punishment and forgiveness from God and approval. And what is the worldly life except the enjoyment of delusion."* (Qur'an 57:20)

That verse is a complete commentary on ours. Notice its sequence: rain, growth, delight of those who cultivate it, yellowing, debris — and then, immediately, the Hereafter. The Qur'an makes the same move that Sūrah al-Aʿlā will make eleven verses later at 87:16–17.

> *"And present to them the example of the life of this world: like rain which We send down from the sky, and the vegetation of the earth mingles with it, and then it becomes dry remnants scattered by the winds. And God is ever, over all things, Perfect in Ability."* (Qur'an 18:45)

> *"The example of the life of this world is but like rain which We have sent down from the sky, by which the vegetation of the earth is intermixed — of that which men and livestock eat — until, when the earth has taken on its adornment and is beautified, and its people suppose that they have complete capability over it, there comes to it Our command by night or by day, and We make it as a harvest, as if it had not flourished yesterday."* (Qur'an 10:24)

The phrase *"as if it had not flourished yesterday"* deserves to be read slowly. It captures the specific quality of loss: not merely that a thing ends, but that its ending erases the memory of its fullness so completely that the ground looks as though nothing was ever there. Anyone who has revisited a childhood home, an old workplace, or the site of a demolished building knows the sensation exactly.

**Why the Qur'an Teaches Mortality Through Plants Rather Than Through Corpses**

There is a pedagogical wisdom in the Qur'an's preference for the vegetable image. Death is a subject the human mind is expert at avoiding. Told directly that he will die, a person agrees intellectually and forgets within the hour. Shown a field that was green last month and is black stubble now, he understands something the intellect alone does not deliver.

The plant image also carries none of the horror that would make the lesson unbearable. It teaches transience gently, in a form the listener sees every year without trauma. And it is universal — every human being in every climate has watched something green go brown.

Above all, the plant image contains a second half. Dead vegetation is not the end of the story of vegetation; the field greens again after the next rain. That is precisely why the Qur'an uses the same imagery for the resurrection:

> *"And you see the earth barren, but when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and swells and grows of every beautiful kind. That is because God is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is over all things competent — and because the Hour is coming, no doubt about it, and because God will resurrect those in the graves."* (Qur'an 22:5–7)

So verse 5 is not despair. It is the first half of a cycle whose second half the sūrah will supply from verse 12 onward.

**Blackened Stubble Is Not Waste**

There is an ecological observation here that the classical commentators could not have framed in modern terms but whose sense they grasped: the withered plant is the raw material of the next generation of plants. Decayed vegetation becomes the humus that feeds the soil; dried stubble shelters seeds and returns nutrients. The Arabs used dried herbage as fodder and as fuel — the "blackened stubble" of the verse was gathered, not merely mourned.

This is a useful correction to a purely pessimistic reading. God does not turn the pasture into debris out of indifference to it, but as one stage of a system in which nothing is wasted. Endings feed beginnings. The same is true of human generations: each one clears the ground for and fertilizes the next, which is why the Qur'an speaks of successive peoples inheriting the earth from one another (cf. 6:133, 7:69).

**What the Verse Says About Human Life**

Applied to a human life, the four-verse sequence reads as a biography. *Khalaqa* — conceived. *Sawwā* — formed in the womb and born whole. *Qaddara fa-hadā* — allotted a lifespan and a portion, and equipped to seek it. *Akhraja al-marʿā* — nourished, grown, brought to strength. *Fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan aḥwā* — aged, weakened, and returned to the earth. The Qur'an spells out this parallel explicitly:

> *"God is the One who created you from weakness, then made after weakness strength, then made after strength weakness and white hair. He creates what He wills, and He is the Knowing, the Competent."* (Qur'an 30:54)

> *"And he to whom We grant long life We reverse in creation. So will they not understand?"* (Qur'an 36:68)

The honesty of this is worth pausing on. Modern culture is organized around the concealment of exactly this arc. Aging is treated as a technical problem, death as a medical failure, and the elderly are frequently removed from the visible centre of social life. The verse refuses the concealment, and there is relief in the refusal: a person who has already accepted that he is a seasonal plant is not devastated when autumn arrives.

**The Prophetic Vocabulary of Transience**

The Prophet ﷺ used the same register constantly, and his images have the same brevity as the verse.

> *"Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a wayfarer."* (al-Bukhārī 6416)

Ibn ʿUmar, who transmitted that ḥadīth, would add his own gloss: *"When evening comes, do not expect the morning; and when morning comes, do not expect the evening. Take from your health for your illness, and from your life for your death."*

> *"What is my relationship with this world? I am in this world only like a rider who shades himself under a tree, then goes on and leaves it."* (al-Tirmidhī 2377, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ)

> *"Remember often the destroyer of pleasures" — meaning death.* (al-Tirmidhī 2307; al-Nasāʾī 1824)

None of this is a call to withdraw from the world. The rider does shelter under the tree; he simply does not build a house in its shade.

**The Psychological Function of Contemplating Decay**

There is a widespread modern assumption that thinking about mortality produces anxiety and paralysis. The tradition's experience — and, interestingly, a good deal of contemporary observation — points the other way when the contemplation occurs inside a framework of meaning.

Awareness of endings clarifies priorities. Questions that seem agonizing at length settle quickly when measured against a finite lifespan: whether to repair a relationship, whether to pursue a career for status or for usefulness, whether an insult is worth years of resentment.

Awareness of endings reduces the sting of loss. Someone who has internalized that everything given was on loan grieves genuinely but is not destroyed, because he was never operating under the illusion of permanent ownership. The formula Muslims say upon hearing of a death states the principle exactly: *"Indeed we belong to God, and indeed to Him we return"* (2:156). Note the first clause — the return is not the surprise; the belonging was never in question.

Awareness of endings motivates action. The Prophet ﷺ turned it directly into productivity:

> *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your illness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your preoccupation, and your life before your death."* (al-Ḥākim; graded authentic by scholars of ḥadīth)

**An Analogy: The Hotel Room**

A useful way to hold both halves of this teaching is the difference in how a person treats a hotel room and a home he owns. In the hotel, he uses everything — the bed, the desk, the shower — fully and without guilt. He simply does not renovate it, does not argue with the management about the colour of the walls, and does not weep when checking out. He knows what the arrangement is.

The Qur'anic attitude to the world is precisely this. Use it thoroughly; the sūrah has just spent three verses describing the goods provided. But recognize the terms of the stay. The distress people feel at loss is very often not the loss itself but the collapse of a false assumption of permanence that they never examined.

**Living the Verse**

Two practices flow naturally from this verse. The first is the deliberate visiting of the reminders of transience — attending funerals, visiting graves, sitting with the elderly — which the Prophet ﷺ recommended for exactly this reason: *"Visit graves, for they remind you of the Hereafter"* (Ibn Mājah 1569; and in Muslim 976 with the wording, *"I had forbidden you from visiting graves, but visit them"*). The second is an audit of investment: how much of one's effort, worry, and time is being spent on the part of life that turns to debris, and how much on the part that does not. The sūrah itself will make that comparison explicit at verse 17 — *"while the Hereafter is better and more enduring."* Verse 5 has already supplied the evidence for the first half of that claim. Everyone has watched the pasture blacken.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:6

> **We shall teach thee to recite, that thou wilt not forget,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sudden Turn from Creation to the One Carrying the Message**

The first five verses looked outward at the universe. Verse 6 turns inward and personal, and the pronoun changes with it. Until now God has been described in the third person — *the One who created, the One who measured*. Now God speaks in the first person plural of majesty and addresses the Prophet ﷺ directly: *sanuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā* — "We shall make you recite, and you will not forget."

This shift is emotionally significant. Between the cosmic verses before and the eschatological verses after, the Qur'an pauses to reassure one anxious man. The God who arranges galaxies and grasslands stops to address a private worry in the heart of His messenger. That the Book preserves this moment tells us something about how God deals with those who carry His work: the general does not eclipse the particular.

**Sanuqriʾuka: The Verb and What It Reveals**

*Sanuqriʾuka* is built from the root *q-r-ʾ*, the same root that gives *Qurʾān* — "recitation," "that which is read aloud." The form used here is the causative (form IV, *aqraʾa*), which means to make someone read or recite, hence "to teach recitation." The prefix *sa-* indicates the future, and the plural *nu-* is the divine plural of majesty.

Three things follow from the verb's construction.

First, the Prophet ﷺ is the recipient, not the author. He is made to recite; he does not compose. This is exactly consistent with the Qur'an's insistence throughout on the passivity of the Prophet with respect to the text: *"Nor does he speak from desire; it is nothing but a revelation revealed"* (53:3–4).

Second, teaching by recitation is oral. The Qur'an was not handed over as a manuscript; it was recited into a memory. The very first word revealed was *iqraʾ* — "Recite!" (96:1) — and the Prophet's own reply, according to the reports of the first encounter in the cave of Ḥirāʾ, was *"I am not one who reads."* The word chosen for the whole revelation is a word for speech.

Third, the future tense is a promise about what has not yet happened. Much of the Qur'an was still to come. God is committing, at an early stage of the mission, to complete what He has begun.

**The Anxiety Being Addressed**

The commentators are unanimous that this verse responds to a specific fear. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭūsī record that when Gabriel came with revelation, the Prophet ﷺ would sometimes begin repeating the words while Gabriel was still speaking, hurrying to fix them in memory out of fear that he would lose them. This verse, and the parallel passages, were revealed to stop him.

The parallels are explicit:

> *"Move not thy tongue with it to hasten with it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation. So when We have recited it, then follow its recitation. Then upon Us is its clarification."* (Qur'an 75:16–19)

> *"And hasten not with the Qur'an before its revelation is completed to thee, and say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge."* (Qur'an 20:114)

Al-Bukhārī records Ibn ʿAbbās' explanation of the first of these:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ used to suffer great difficulty when revelation came down, and he used to move his lips. So God revealed: 'Move not thy tongue with it to hasten with it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation' — meaning, upon Us is to collect it in your breast, and then that you recite it."* (al-Bukhārī 5)

This is a portrait of a human being under enormous strain. He has been entrusted with words he believes to be the speech of God, delivered orally, in a society that keeps no systematic written records, and he is terrified of losing a syllable. The verse takes that terror and answers it in three words.

**"Thou Wilt Not Forget": Statement or Command?**

The grammarians and commentators discuss whether *fa-lā tansā* is informative (*khabar*) — "you will not forget" — or prohibitive (*nahy*) — "so do not forget."

The majority take it as informative: a divine guarantee. On this reading God is not adding a duty to an already burdened man; He is removing a fear. The grammatical support is that if it were a prohibition, the final letter would normally be dropped in this construction (*lā tansa*), whereas the recitation preserves it.

A minority read it as a gentle prohibition — do not neglect it, do not abandon its recitation — which would make it a call to maintain the Qur'an actively. Al-Rāzī notes that even this reading does not undercut the reassurance, since a command to retain implies the ability to retain.

The stronger view is the first, and it is confirmed by the outcome: the Prophet ﷺ retained the entire revelation across twenty-three years, recited it back to Gabriel annually, and in the final year of his life reviewed it with him twice, as recorded in al-Bukhārī from Fāṭimah's report of what her father told her.

**The Preservation of the Qur'an as a Divine Undertaking**

This verse is one link in a chain of Qur'anic statements assigning the preservation of the text to God rather than to human diligence:

> *"Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

> *"Falsehood cannot approach it from before it or from behind it — a revelation from One Wise and Praiseworthy."* (Qur'an 41:42)

The claim is unusual among scriptures, and it is worth stating plainly what actually happened historically, because the verse's promise is a testable one. The Qur'an was memorized in its entirety by numerous Companions during the Prophet's lifetime, written on available materials under his direction, collected into a single codex under Abū Bakr after the deaths of many memorizers at the battle of al-Yamāmah, and standardized in copies dispatched to the major cities under ʿUthmān. Alongside the written line, an unbroken oral line has run for fourteen centuries: children in every generation memorize the entire text from teachers who memorized it from teachers, with the pronunciation, elongations, and pauses transmitted by ear.

The result is a text with a redundancy no other ancient book possesses. Even if every manuscript vanished, the text could be restored from memory; even if every memorizer vanished, it could be restored from manuscript. The two systems check each other continuously, which is precisely what makes systematic alteration impossible: a change in a written copy is instantly audible to anyone who has memorized, and a slip in recitation is instantly visible against the page.

**Why an Unlettered Prophet Matters Here**

The Qur'an describes its recipient as *al-nabī al-ummī*, the unlettered Prophet (7:157–158), and connects this to the credibility of the revelation:

> *"And thou didst not recite before it any scripture, nor didst thou inscribe one with thy right hand; then the falsifiers would have had cause for doubt."* (Qur'an 29:48)

The verse before us fits this framework. A man who cannot read and write is entirely dependent on memory, which is why the fear of forgetting was so acute — and why the divine guarantee is so significant. The preservation of a long, complex, rhythmically intricate text in the memory of an unlettered man over twenty-three years, delivered piecemeal across changing circumstances yet internally consistent, is itself part of the Qur'an's argument for its own origin.

**Memory as a Divine Gift, Not Merely a Skill**

The verse also has a general application beyond the Prophet's person. Memory is presented here as something God grants, not something the human being manufactures. This is a corrective for both the arrogant and the despairing.

For the arrogant: the scholar who has memorized the text and the sciences did not generate his own capacity, and he can lose it. The tradition took this seriously. Al-Shāfiʿī's famous lines complaining of a weakened memory and being told that knowledge is a light God does not grant to a sinner reflect a whole ethic in which retention is tied to conduct, not merely to technique.

For the despairing: God's assistance in memorizing is genuinely available and has been sought by students of the Qur'an in every century. The Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions supplications for retention, and the entire ḥifẓ tradition rests on the assumption that this is a cooperative enterprise between effort and divine facilitation.

At the same time, the Prophet ﷺ warned that memory requires maintenance, using an image any herder would understand:

> *"Keep refreshing your knowledge of the Qur'an, for by Him in whose hand is my soul, it escapes more quickly than a camel from its tether."* (al-Bukhārī 5033; Muslim 791)

There is no contradiction between the divine guarantee to the Prophet ﷺ and the human obligation to review. The guarantee concerns the preservation of the revelation as such; the obligation concerns the individual's personal share of it.

**The Broader Principle: God Equips Those He Assigns**

Read as a principle rather than a report, this verse says something with wide application: when God assigns a task, He supplies the capacity for it. The Prophet ﷺ was not handed an impossible responsibility and left to manage. The same assurance is given to Moses when he protested his inadequacy for the mission to Pharaoh:

> *"He said: My Lord, expand for me my breast, and ease for me my task, and untie the knot from my tongue, that they may understand my speech... He said: You have been granted your request, O Moses."* (Qur'an 20:25–36)

Every person who has been placed in a role that exceeds his felt ability — a new parent, a young doctor, a caregiver for a dying relative, a teacher facing a difficult class — can hear this verse as addressed to that condition. The pattern is consistent: the assignment comes with provision, but the provision often becomes visible only after one has begun.

**Practical Application: Anxiety, Preparation, and Trust**

The verse also models a healthy relationship with anxiety about performance. The Prophet's ﷺ instinct — repeat it immediately, do not risk losing it — was not condemned as faithless; it was corrected as unnecessary. He was told to listen first and follow afterward: *"So when We have recited it, then follow its recitation"* (75:18).

This is directly useful. A great deal of human anxiety consists of trying to secure an outcome while the process that produces it is still underway — rehearsing an answer while the question is still being asked, planning a response while the other person is still speaking, worrying about a result while the work is still in progress. The instruction is to be present for the input and trust that the retention will follow.

For anyone memorizing the Qur'an today, the verse carries an additional encouragement. The Qur'an describes itself as made accessible for exactly this purpose, and repeats the statement four times in a single chapter as though anticipating disbelief:

> *"And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance, so is there any who will remember?"* (Qur'an 54:17, 22, 32, 40)

That millions of people, including very many who do not speak Arabic and many who are children, have committed to memory a book of over six thousand verses is an ongoing empirical footnote to that claim.

**Living the Verse**

The concrete takings from this verse are three. Approach the Qur'an as something received rather than mastered — the posture of a student being taught, which changes both the humility and the attentiveness one brings to it. Maintain what one has been given, since the tether metaphor of the Prophet ﷺ makes clear that retention is not automatic. And when carrying a responsibility that feels beyond one's capacity, recall that the verse's promise was made not to a man who felt equal to his task, but to one who was afraid he would drop it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:7

> **save what God wills. Truly He knows that which is open and that which is hidden.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Guarantee That Keeps Its Door Open**

The previous verse gave an unambiguous promise: *you will not forget*. This verse immediately attaches a qualification: *illā mā shāʾa Allāh* — "except what God wills."

At first hearing this may seem to weaken the promise. In fact it strengthens the theology behind it, and understanding why goes to the heart of how the Qur'an speaks about God's freedom. A promise that could not be altered by the One who made it would imply a constraint upon Him. By adding the exception, the verse preserves the absolute sovereignty of God even over His own gifts. He guarantees preservation — and He remains free, because He is God and not a mechanism.

**The Grammar and Force of the Exception**

*Illā* is the standard Arabic particle of exception. *Mā shāʾa Allāh* means "what God wills." The construction appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in exactly this role — attaching a divine reservation to a stated outcome:

> *"They will abide therein as long as the heavens and the earth endure, except what thy Lord wills. Indeed, thy Lord is the effecter of what He intends."* (Qur'an 11:107)

> *"And say not of anything: Indeed, I will do that tomorrow — except that God wills. And remember thy Lord when thou forgettest."* (Qur'an 18:23–24)

That second passage is particularly relevant. It is the Qur'anic basis for the universal Muslim habit of saying *in shāʾ Allāh* — "if God wills" — after any statement about the future. Our verse applies the same discipline to a divine promise, which teaches a striking lesson: if even God's own stated outcome is framed with the reservation of His will, then no human plan should ever be stated without it.

**How the Classical Commentators Understood the Exception**

The commentators offered several readings, and they are complementary rather than contradictory.

*The exception establishes divine capability without implying actual forgetting.* Many scholars, including a large group cited by al-Rāzī, held that the exception affirms God's power to cause forgetting without asserting that He did so. Arabic uses such constructions to establish a principle: the outcome is guaranteed, and the guarantor remains free. On this reading, nothing was in fact forgotten; the clause exists to prevent the listener from imagining that the Prophet's memory operated independently of God's ongoing will.

*The exception refers to abrogation.* Others understood it to mean verses whose recitation God deliberately withdrew. The Qur'an itself describes this process:

> *"No sign do We abrogate or cause to be forgotten but that We bring one better than it or like unto it. Dost thou not know that God is over all things competent?"* (Qur'an 2:106)

> *"And when We substitute a verse in place of a verse — and God is most knowing of what He sends down — they say: You are but an inventor. Rather, most of them do not know."* (Qur'an 16:101)

Notice the crucial safeguard built into 2:106: nothing is removed except that something better or equal replaces it. Abrogation in the Qur'anic sense is not loss; it is substitution under supervision. The commentators distinguished several types — abrogation of a ruling while the wording remains (the most common), and the much-discussed and far rarer case of wording withdrawn from recitation. In either case the agency is God's, exercised deliberately, and never leaves the community with less than it had.

*The exception removes blame from the Prophet ﷺ.* Al-Rāzī records this as a central point: if a verse ever left the Prophet's recall, it was because God willed its removal, not because of any deficiency in him. This is pastorally significant. It closes off in advance the possibility that the Prophet ﷺ, or anyone observing him, would read a lapse as a failure of character or attention.

**The Ḥadīth That Illustrates the Principle**

There is a well-known incident that shows both the reality of momentary lapse and the manner in which it was treated:

> *ʿĀʾishah reported that the Prophet ﷺ heard a man reciting in the mosque at night and said: "May God have mercy on him — he has reminded me of such-and-such a verse, which I had been caused to forget from such-and-such a chapter."* (al-Bukhārī 5042; Muslim 788)

Two details in this report repay attention. First, the wording used is passive — *unsītuhā*, "I was caused to forget it" — matching the language of 2:106 exactly and confirming that this was understood as a divine act rather than a human failing. Second, the Prophet's response is a prayer of mercy for the man who reminded him. There is no embarrassment and no defensiveness; the community functions as a mutual check, and the correction is received as a gift.

This is a model for religious learning generally. In a healthy tradition, being corrected is not humiliation, and correcting is not attack. The most authoritative human being in the community publicly acknowledged a gap in his recall and blessed the person who filled it.

At the same time, the Prophet ﷺ discouraged a certain way of speaking about lapse:

> *"How wretched is it for one of you to say, 'I have forgotten such-and-such a verse' — rather, he has been made to forget."* (al-Bukhārī 5032; Muslim 790)

The correction is subtle and telling. The problem is not the lapse; it is a phrasing that treats the Qur'an as something one has casually mislaid. The instruction is to speak of it in a way that keeps both the seriousness of the text and the agency of God in view.

**"He Knows That Which Is Open and That Which Is Hidden"**

The second half of the verse supplies the reason the exception can be trusted: *innahu yaʿlamu al-jahra wa mā yakhfā*.

*Al-jahr* comes from the root *j-h-r*, meaning to be loud, open, public, plainly visible. It covers spoken words, visible actions, and everything a person does in the open.

*Mā yakhfā* — "what is hidden" — comes from *kh-f-y*, to be concealed. It covers whispered speech, private conduct, and, crucially, the interior life: intentions, motives, the thoughts one has never voiced.

Placed here, the phrase does at least three jobs.

*It justifies the exception.* If God removes something, He does so with complete knowledge of what its removal will accomplish. Divine decisions about the text are not arbitrary; they are made by the One who knows both the visible circumstances and the hidden needs of every generation that will read it.

*It reassures the Prophet ﷺ about his own inner state.* His anxiety over the revelation was private; nobody saw it but God. The verse tells him that his hidden effort is known, which is the deepest consolation available to anyone whose struggle is invisible to others.

*It universalizes the warning.* The one who conceals unbelief behind polite agreement, or charity behind a hidden desire for reputation, is not concealing anything at all.

**The Qur'anic Doctrine of Total Divine Knowledge**

The Qur'an returns to this attribute constantly, and always with a practical edge:

> *"And with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them except Him. And He knows what is on the land and in the sea. Not a leaf falls but that He knows it. And no grain is there within the darknesses of the earth, and no moist or dry thing, but that it is in a clear register."* (Qur'an 6:59)

> *"And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than his jugular vein."* (Qur'an 50:16)

> *"He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal."* (Qur'an 40:19)

That last verse is remarkable in its precision. *Khāʾinat al-aʿyun* — "the treachery of the eyes" — refers to the glance that is quick enough to be deniable, the look one gives when one believes no one is watching. Divine knowledge is described at that resolution.

**The Ethical Consequence: A Life Without a Backstage**

Modern life is arranged into visible and invisible zones. There is the public self — the professional demeanour, the social media presence, the face presented at the mosque or the office — and there is the private self, the browsing history, the tone used with family behind closed doors, the resentments never spoken.

This verse abolishes the distinction at the only level that ultimately matters. There is no backstage before God. And the tradition treats the gap between the two selves as the central spiritual danger. The Prophet ﷺ warned in the strongest terms about people whose public religiosity conceals private violation:

> *"I certainly know people of my nation who will come on the Day of Resurrection with good deeds like the white mountains of Tihāmah, and God will make them scattered dust." Thawbān said: "O Messenger of God, describe them to us, make them clear to us, that we may not be of them unknowingly." He said: "They are your brothers and from your race, and they take from the night as you do; but they are a people who, when they are alone with the sacred limits of God, violate them."* (Ibn Mājah 4245; graded authentic by scholars of ḥadīth)

The corrective virtue is *iḥsān*, defined by the Prophet ﷺ in the ḥadīth of Gabriel:

> *"That you worship God as though you see Him; for if you do not see Him, He surely sees you."* (al-Bukhārī 50; Muslim 8)

This is not a doctrine of surveillance and dread. It is an invitation to consistency. A person whose private and public selves converge is, quite apart from any theological reward, a person with far less internal friction — no maintenance of a performance, no fear of exposure, no exhausting management of two identities.

**The Comfort Hidden Inside the Warning**

It would be a mistake to read total divine knowledge only as a threat. It is also the deepest form of being understood, and the tradition treats it that way.

The person whose good deeds go unrecognized is fully seen. The one who was misjudged, whose motives were slandered, whose patience was mistaken for weakness, whose quiet care for a difficult parent or sick child was never noticed by anyone — that entire hidden record is held by the One who *"knows that which is open and that which is hidden."*

The Prophet ﷺ specifically praised the servant whose worship is concealed:

> *"Seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His... and a man who gives in charity and conceals it, such that his left hand does not know what his right hand has given; and a man who remembered God in private and his eyes overflowed."* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031)

The hidden is not merely audited; it is preferred. In a culture organized around visibility and public accounting of one's virtues, this verse quietly reopens the possibility of a life whose best parts nobody sees.

**An Analogy: The Examined Manuscript**

Imagine a scholar who has spent years producing a manuscript, and who fears above all that pages will be lost. He is told by the one who commissioned it: the archive is guaranteed, and if any page is ever removed, it will be removed by me, for a reason, and replaced by something better. That is the first half of the verse. The second half adds: and I have read every page you have written, including the notes in the margins you never intended anyone to see.

The combination is exactly what a person carrying a serious responsibility needs — the security that the work will not be lost by accident, and the awareness that it is being read with complete attention.

**Living the Verse**

Three applications follow. Attach the divine reservation to one's own plans, as the Qur'an instructs in 18:23–24: *in shāʾ Allāh* is not a verbal tic but an accurate description of who controls outcomes. Treat one's lapses of memory, energy, and ability as arranged rather than as personal failures, while continuing to make the effort that is one's part. And work toward the closing of the gap between public and private conduct, beginning with a single honest question: what would change in my week if the hidden were as visible as the open? The verse's answer is that it already is.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:8

> **And We shall ease thy way unto ease.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Promise in a Row**

This is the third consecutive divine commitment made to the Prophet ﷺ. We shall teach you to recite; you will not forget; and now — *wa nuyassiruka lil-yusrā* — "We shall ease you toward the ease."

Read together, these three verses form a complete answer to a messenger's three fundamental worries. *Will I receive the message?* — We shall teach you. *Will I keep it?* — You will not forget. *Will I be able to bear the weight of it?* — We shall ease your way. The Qur'an does not merely command the Prophet ﷺ to be strong; it undertakes to make him capable.

**Yassara and Yusrā: The Vocabulary of Ease**

The root *y-s-r* carries the meanings of ease, facility, gentleness, and the removal of difficulty. It also gives *yasār* (the left hand, and by extension prosperity), *maysūr* (made easy), and *maysarah* (a time of ease, as in 2:280, *"then postponement until a time of ease"*).

*Nuyassiru* is the intensive causative: We will make easy, We will smooth the path, We will facilitate.

*Al-yusrā* is the feminine superlative — "the easiest," "the way of greatest ease." The definite article gives it the sense of a specific known thing: not merely "ease" in general, but *the* ease, the state or path that is easiest in the truest sense.

The construction is unusual: literally, "We will facilitate you *to* the easiest." The verb usually takes a direct object — you facilitate a *thing*. Here the person himself is the object of facilitation. The commentators note this: the Prophet ﷺ is not merely given easy tasks; he himself is made easy for the easiest path. His nature, his capacity, and his inclinations are being shaped so that the right road becomes the natural one.

**Three Classical Readings, All True**

Al-Rāzī and others record three main interpretations, and there is no need to choose between them.

*The revealed law itself is easy.* On this reading — favored by the authors of the Jalālayn and many others — the verse promises the Prophet ﷺ that the religion he is bringing will be a religion of manageable obligations rather than crushing ones. This links directly to:

> *"God intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship."* (Qur'an 2:185)

> *"And He has not placed upon you in the religion any hardship."* (Qur'an 22:78)

> *"God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity."* (Qur'an 2:286)

*Memorization will be made easy.* Read in light of the two preceding verses, the promise continues the theme of revelation: God will make retention effortless. This reading has the merit of continuity with the immediate context.

*The way to Paradise will be eased.* Some understood *al-yusrā* as the ultimate ease — the Garden — and the verse as a promise that the road to it will be smoothed. This finds support in the parallel passage in Sūrah al-Layl, where the same superlative appears:

> *"As for he who gives and is conscious of God and believes in the best reward, We shall ease him toward ease (fa-sanuyassiruhu lil-yusrā). But as for he who withholds and considers himself self-sufficient and denies the best reward, We shall ease him toward hardship (fa-sanuyassiruhu lil-ʿusrā)."* (Qur'an 92:5–10)

That parallel is the most illuminating cross-reference to our verse, and it deserves its own treatment.

**The Moral Law of Facilitation**

Sūrah al-Layl states a psychological and spiritual law that experience confirms in every domain of human behaviour: whichever direction a person chooses, that direction becomes easier for him. Give, and giving becomes easy. Withhold, and withholding becomes easy — indeed, giving becomes hard.

This is the mechanism of habit stated in theological terms. Every action lays down a track. The first act of charity is difficult; the hundredth is reflexive. The first lie is uncomfortable; the hundredth is unnoticed. The first missed prayer requires an excuse; after a year, no excuse is needed. The Qur'an describes this as divine facilitation in both directions — which is significant, because it locates even the hardening of a heart within God's arrangement while leaving the initial choice with the person.

The Prophet ﷺ expressed the same law with the same verb in the context of destiny:

> *"Act, for everyone is facilitated toward that for which he was created."* (al-Bukhārī 4949; Muslim 2647)

And in the practical realm of learning and worship, the same principle appears as the counsel of consistency:

> *"The most beloved of deeds to God are those that are most consistent, even if they are few."* (al-Bukhārī 6464; Muslim 783)

Consistency is precisely the mechanism by which a path becomes eased. Small repeated acts convert difficulty into second nature; sporadic heroic efforts do not.

**The Religion of Ease: The Prophetic Evidence**

If the first reading is correct — that the verse promises an easy law — then the Prophetic practice is its commentary, and the evidence is abundant.

> *"Religion is ease, and no one makes the religion difficult except that it overwhelms him. So aim for what is right, come close to it, give glad tidings, and seek help in the morning, the evening, and a part of the night."* (al-Bukhārī 39)

> *"Make things easy and do not make them difficult; give glad tidings and do not repel people."* (al-Bukhārī 69; Muslim 1734)

> *ʿĀʾishah reported: "The Messenger of God ﷺ was never given a choice between two matters except that he chose the easier of the two, so long as it was not a sin. If it was a sin, he was the furthest of people from it."* (al-Bukhārī 3560; Muslim 2327)

> *"I have been sent with the primordial, tolerant way (al-ḥanīfiyyah al-samḥah)."* (Musnad Aḥmad)

The Prophet ﷺ also acted decisively against excess even when it wore religious clothing. When three men came to his household and, upon hearing of his moderate practice, resolved to pray all night, fast permanently, and never marry, he addressed them publicly:

> *"Are you the ones who said such-and-such? By God, I am the most fearful of God among you and the most conscious of Him — yet I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my way is not of me."* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401)

The legal tradition institutionalized this. The concessions (*rukhaṣ*) are not grudging allowances but recognized parts of the law: shortening and combining prayers when travelling, breaking the fast when ill or on a journey, praying seated when standing is not possible, dry ablution when water is unavailable or harmful, and the general principle that necessity permits the otherwise prohibited. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"God loves that His concessions be taken, just as He dislikes that disobedience to Him be committed."* (Musnad Aḥmad; Ibn Ḥibbān)

The jurists distilled these texts into maxims still taught in every school: *hardship brings facilitation*, *harm must be removed*, *necessity renders the forbidden permissible*, and *when a matter becomes constricted, it expands*. These are not modern accommodations; they are formulations from the classical period, derived from verses like this one.

**Ease Is Not the Absence of Effort**

A serious misreading must be blocked. The verse promises facilitation, not the removal of exertion. The same Prophet ﷺ whose way was eased prayed until his feet swelled, endured the boycott in the valley, buried six of his seven children, and returned from Ṭāʾif bleeding. The revelation itself came upon him with such weight that he sweated on cold days.

Ease in the Qur'anic sense means proportion: obligations matched to capacity, exemptions where capacity fails, and divine assistance in bearing what remains. It does not mean comfort. Sūrah al-Sharḥ, revealed in the same Makkan period, states the relationship precisely:

> *"For indeed, with hardship comes ease. Indeed, with hardship comes ease."* (Qur'an 94:5–6)

The preposition matters. The Arabic *maʿa* means "with," not "after." Ease is not merely a reward that arrives once hardship has ended; it accompanies the hardship, embedded inside it. Anyone who has come through a serious difficulty recognizes this — the unexpected help, the capacity discovered mid-crisis, the compressed clarity that a hard season produces.

The classical commentators also noted the definite and indefinite forms in those two verses: the hardship is definite (*al-ʿusr*, the same hardship mentioned twice), while the ease is indefinite (*yusran*, twice, and indefinites repeated are read as two distinct things). Hence the saying transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and others: *one hardship will never overcome two eases*.

**Why an Easy Religion Is the Rational Design**

There is a logic to divine facilitation that goes beyond mercy.

A law that most people cannot keep produces one of two outcomes: mass abandonment, or mass hypocrisy. Neither serves the purpose of religion, which is the actual formation of human beings. A sustainable law is one that a working farmer, a nursing mother, a travelling merchant, and an elderly man with failing joints can all keep in their respective conditions. Islamic worship is scaled exactly this way — the same five prayers, but shortened for the traveller, seated for the infirm, and gestured for the bedridden.

Ease also protects sincerity. When obligations are proportionate, keeping them says something real about a person's commitment. When they are impossible, everyone fails, and the distinction between the sincere and the negligent collapses.

And ease preserves the long game. Religion is not a sprint; it is a lifetime. The Prophet ﷺ used exactly this image:

> *"This religion is strong, so go into it gently; and do not make the worship of God hateful to yourself, for the one who drives himself hard neither covers the ground nor spares his mount."* (Musnad Aḥmad; and in al-Bukhārī 39 in the shorter form)

The image is of a rider who exhausts his animal in the first hours of a long journey and is left stranded with a broken mount and most of the distance still ahead. Every teacher, parent, and person who has ever attempted a sudden total religious reformation recognizes the picture.

**Applying the Verse Today**

Three applications are worth naming.

*In personal practice*: build the small and sustainable rather than the dramatic and temporary. Two units of night prayer maintained for a year outweigh a month of all-night vigils followed by collapse. This is not laziness; it is the Prophetic method.

*In teaching and preaching*: the Prophet's instruction to *"make things easy and do not make them difficult"* was addressed to Muʿādh and Abū Mūsā as they were sent to teach a new population. Those who present the religion to others — parents especially — have a specific mandate to lead with what is manageable and encouraging. A generation driven away by harshness was not saved by the strictness of its teachers.

*In self-judgment*: many sincere people live under a permanent sense of religious failure, measuring themselves against an imagined standard nobody meets. This verse, and the corpus behind it, is the corrective. God legislated for human beings as they actually are — tired, distracted, embodied, and limited — and then undertook to ease them toward the easier way.

**Living the Verse**

The promise is stated in the future tense: *We shall ease*. Ease, in the Qur'an's framing, is something that comes to meet a person who has started walking. It is not found by waiting for the road to improve. Begin the practice at the level you can actually sustain, hold it steady, and the verse's mechanism — the same one described in 92:5–7 — begins to operate: the direction chosen becomes the direction facilitated.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:9

> **So remind, should the reminder benefit:**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Reassurance to Assignment**

Three verses of divine promise now issue in a command. The particle *fa-* at the head of the verse — "so," "therefore" — makes the logical connection explicit: because We will teach you, because you will not forget, because We will ease your way, *therefore remind*.

This is the pivot of the entire sūrah. Everything before it concerned God and the Prophet ﷺ. Everything after it concerns the audience and their two possible responses. The single word *dhakkir* — "remind" — is the hinge, and it defines the entire vocation of prophecy in two syllables.

**Dhakkir: Reminding, Not Informing**

The verb comes from the root *dh-k-r*, which covers remembering, mentioning, recalling, and bringing to mind. The intensive form *dhakkara* means to cause someone to remember — to remind. From the same root come *dhikr* (remembrance, and also a name for the Qur'an itself), *dhikrā* (a reminder or admonition), and *tadhkirah* (a reminder, a memorandum).

The choice of "remind" rather than "inform" or "teach" is theologically loaded. To remind implies that the hearer already possesses the content in some form and has forgotten it. This matches the Qur'an's fundamental anthropology: human beings are not blank surfaces awaiting religious information; they are creatures who once knew and then lost the knowledge.

The Qur'an locates this original knowledge in the primordial covenant:

> *"And when thy Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them testify concerning themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we have testified — lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection, We were unaware of this."* (Qur'an 7:172)

And it names the innate disposition *fiṭrah*:

> *"So direct thy face toward the religion, inclining to truth — the natural disposition of God upon which He created mankind. There is no altering the creation of God. That is the correct religion, but most people do not know."* (Qur'an 30:30)

The Prophet ﷺ described the same primordial orientation:

> *"Every child is born upon the fiṭrah, and it is his parents who make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian."* (al-Bukhārī 1358; Muslim 2658)

This has an enormous practical consequence for how one speaks to others about God. The task is not to install a foreign system in a hostile mind but to awaken something dormant. That changes the posture from argument to appeal, from conquest to recognition. It also explains a common human experience: the moment when a religious statement lands not as new information but as something the listener realizes he already suspected.

**The Prophet's Job Description in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an repeatedly limits the messenger's mandate to reminding, and the limitation is emphatic:

> *"So remind — thou art only a reminder. Thou art not over them a controller."* (Qur'an 88:21–22)

> *"And remind, for indeed the reminder benefits the believers."* (Qur'an 51:55)

> *"Thou dost not guide whom thou lovest, but God guides whom He wills."* (Qur'an 28:56)

> *"There is no compulsion in religion; right guidance has become distinct from error."* (Qur'an 2:256)

Taken together, these verses draw a boundary that has protected both preacher and audience for fourteen centuries. The one who conveys is responsible for conveying, not for producing belief. He is a *mudhakkir*, not a *musayṭir* — a reminder, not a controller.

The psychological effect of accepting that boundary is considerable. A great deal of religious frustration, and much religious coercion, arises from taking responsibility for an outcome that was never assigned. The Prophet ﷺ himself grieved so intensely over those who rejected the message that God addressed his grief directly:

> *"Then perhaps thou wouldst kill thyself through grief over them, if they do not believe in this message, out of sorrow."* (Qur'an 18:6)

> *"Perhaps thou wouldst kill thyself because they will not be believers."* (Qur'an 26:3)

If the most sincere human being who ever lived had to be told that the outcome was not his responsibility, then the ordinary parent, teacher, or friend can accept the same relief.

**The Conditional Clause: "Should the Reminder Benefit"**

The second half of the verse — *in nafaʿati al-dhikrā* — has generated significant discussion, because on its surface it appears to limit the command: remind *only if* it will help.

The commentators offer several resolutions.

*The condition is not restrictive but affirmative of a general fact.* Al-Baghawī's reading, reflected in the source material, is that reminding is to be carried out whether or not it appears beneficial, since all people will be held accountable and all must therefore be given the reminder. On this understanding the clause functions as encouragement rather than limitation: remind, for reminder does benefit.

*The condition indicates priority, not exclusion.* Where reminder is likely to be received, it is more urgent; where it is refused, the obligation may reduce to the minimum of conveyance. This is a matter of wisdom in allocation, not permission to abandon people. A teacher with limited hours reasonably invests more in the student who attends than in the one who has walked out — without expelling the latter.

*The condition concerns manner and timing.* Some read it as guidance on method: remind in the way, and at the moment, in which reminder can actually benefit. The same words delivered at the wrong time, in the wrong tone, or in front of the wrong audience will not benefit; delivered well, they will.

*The condition is a rhetorical rebuke of the audience.* On this reading the phrase implies: remind them — if reminding such people can do any good at all. The clause then exposes the hardness of the rejecters rather than limiting the preacher.

All four readings are defensible, and together they yield a balanced practical rule: never stop conveying, but be intelligent about when, how, and how much.

**The Qur'an's Own Instructions on Method**

If reminder is the mandate, method is not optional, and the Qur'an is unusually specific about it.

> *"Invite to the way of thy Lord with wisdom and beautiful preaching, and argue with them in the way that is best."* (Qur'an 16:125)

Three instruments are named: *ḥikmah* (wisdom — the right thing, to the right person, at the right time), *mawʿiẓah ḥasanah* (beautiful admonition — appeal to the heart, not merely the intellect), and *mujādalah bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* (argument conducted in the best manner). Notice that even the adversarial mode is bound by "the best manner."

> *"And by the mercy of God thou wast lenient with them. Had thou been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around thee."* (Qur'an 3:159)

That verse is a diagnosis of failure in advance. Harshness scatters people. The Qur'an says it plainly, and every community that has ignored it has watched the prediction come true.

And in the most striking case of all, when Moses and Aaron are sent to Pharaoh — a tyrant who claimed divinity and murdered infants — the instruction is:

> *"And speak to him a gentle word, that perhaps he may be reminded or fear."* (Qur'an 20:44)

If gentleness was the prescribed register for addressing Pharaoh, no one else qualifies for an exemption.

**The Prophetic Practice of Measured Reminding**

The Prophet ﷺ was as careful about frequency as about content:

> *Ibn Masʿūd used to admonish the people every Thursday. A man said: "O Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, we love your talk and we wish you would speak to us daily." He said: "What prevents me is that I dislike wearying you. I ration admonition for you as the Prophet ﷺ used to ration it for us, fearing weariness on our part."* (al-Bukhārī 68)

This is an extraordinary piece of pedagogical wisdom preserved in the ḥadīth corpus. Even when the audience *requests* more, the teacher moderates, because saturation dulls the ear. Anyone who has grown up under continuous religious lecturing knows the effect: the words stop being heard as words.

He also insisted on beginning with foundations rather than details. Instructing Muʿādh ibn Jabal as he sent him to Yemen:

> *"You are going to a people from the People of the Book. Let the first thing you call them to be the testimony that there is no god but God" — and in another wording, "to the worship of God alone." "If they obey you in that, then inform them that God has obligated upon them five prayers in every day and night. If they obey you in that, then inform them that God has obligated upon them a charity taken from their wealthy and returned to their poor..."* (al-Bukhārī 1395; Muslim 19)

The sequence is deliberate and gradual: creed first, then the pillar of worship, then financial obligation — each introduced only after the previous is accepted. Reminder that ignores sequence is not reminder; it is unloading.

**Reminder Is Not Restricted to Scholars**

Although the verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ, the tradition has always read the obligation of conveying as shared by the community according to each person's capacity:

> *"Convey from me, even a single verse."* (al-Bukhārī 3461)

> *"Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."* (Muslim 49)

> *"Whoever guides to good has the reward of the one who does it."* (Muslim 1893)

> *"By God, that God guides through you a single man is better for you than red camels."* (al-Bukhārī 3009; Muslim 2406 — red camels being the most prized wealth of the Arabs)

This universalizes the verse. A parent reminding a child, a friend raising an uncomfortable subject, a colleague setting a visible example, a person who shares a beneficial word — all are performing the function the verse commands, and all are bound by its accompanying wisdom about method.

**Why Human Beings Need Reminding at All**

There is a realism in this verse worth dwelling on. The Qur'an does not assume that a truth once accepted stays active. Human attention decays; convictions held firmly at a funeral evaporate within a week of ordinary routine. This is why the same book that revealed the doctrine also commands its constant repetition, and why worship in Islam is structured as recurrence rather than as a one-time initiation: five daily prayers, a weekly assembly, an annual fast, a lifetime pilgrimage.

The Arabic word for the human being, *insān*, was connected by some early lexicographers to *nisyān*, forgetfulness — a linguistic observation that captures the condition. The Qur'an reports the failure of the first human in precisely these terms:

> *"And We had already taken a covenant from Adam before, but he forgot; and We found not in him determination."* (Qur'an 20:115)

If forgetting is the default, then reminding is not an occasional intervention. It is maintenance.

**An Analogy: The Warning Sign on the Familiar Road**

Consider a sharp bend on a road where accidents have repeatedly occurred. The authorities install a warning sign. The drivers who use the road daily know the bend perfectly well — the sign tells them nothing they do not already know. Yet the sign reduces accidents, because knowledge that is not active at the moment of decision is functionally absent.

That is exactly what *dhikrā* is. The reminder does not supply new information to the believer; it activates information he already has at the moment he needs it. Which is also why the sign must be placed *before* the bend, must be legible, and must not be one of forty competing signs on the same post. Timing, clarity, and restraint are the whole art.

**Living the Verse**

Three concrete commitments follow. Speak the reminder — say the useful thing rather than assuming someone else will, since the mandate is distributed. Study the method as seriously as the message, because the Qur'an spends as much text on how to invite as on what to invite to. And release the outcome: the verse commands reminding, not converting, and the person who accepts that boundary will still be speaking kindly ten years later, while the one who demands results will have burned out or turned harsh long before.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:10

> **he who fears will remember;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Explains Why Some People Listen**

The command has been given: remind. Now the sūrah describes what happens when the reminder reaches human ears. It does not describe uniform acceptance or uniform rejection. It describes a sorting: *sa-yadhdhakkaru man yakhshā* — "he who fears will remember."

This is one of the Qur'an's most compact statements about the psychology of religious response. The same words, delivered by the same speaker, in the same room, produce entirely different results — and the variable is not intelligence, education, or exposure. The variable is *khashyah*.

**Sa-yadhdhakkaru: The Certainty of the Future Tense**

The verb is prefixed with *sa-*, the particle of the near future, and the form *yadhdhakkaru* (originally *yatadhakkaru*, with assimilation) is the reflexive of the same root as the command in the previous verse. The Prophet ﷺ was told *dhakkir* — remind; here the response is *yadhdhakkaru* — he will take the reminder, receive it, let it work in him.

The future tense functions as a promise, and it is meant to encourage. God is telling His messenger, at an early and discouraging stage of the mission when the believers were few: the reminder will land. Not on everyone, but it will land. There exist, in every audience, hearts already prepared for it.

The reflexive form is also significant. *Yadhdhakkaru* is something the listener does, not something done to him. The preacher reminds; the listener remembers. Two agents, two responsibilities.

**Khashyah: The Fear That Comes From Knowing**

*Yakhshā* comes from *kh-sh-y*, and the tradition distinguishes it carefully from *khawf*, the more general word for fear.

*Khawf* is fear of a thing — of pain, loss, danger. It can be felt by anyone, including an animal, and it does not require any knowledge of what is feared beyond its threat.

*Khashyah* is fear grounded in knowledge and reverence: the awe felt toward someone whose greatness one has genuinely apprehended. It combines fear with love, respect, and a desire not to displease. The Qur'an makes the connection to knowledge explicit in one of its most quoted verses:

> *"Only those of His servants who have knowledge fear God (innamā yakhshā Allāha min ʿibādihi al-ʿulamāʾ)."* (Qur'an 35:28)

The restriction particle *innamā* makes the statement exclusive: real *khashyah* belongs to those who know. And the knowledge intended here is not academic accumulation but genuine apprehension of who God is. A person who has actually grasped the magnitude of the One he is dealing with cannot be casual about Him.

There is a useful human analogy. A child running through a laboratory feels no fear of the equipment; the chemist who understands what is on the shelves moves carefully. His caution is not cowardice — it is competence. He knows what he is standing among. *Khashyah* is that kind of fear: the alertness of someone who understands the stakes.

**Why Fear Is the Precondition of Hearing**

The verse asserts a causal relationship: fear produces receptivity. Why should this be?

Because a reminder is only useful to someone who believes there is something to lose. Warning a man about a fire is pointless if he does not believe fire burns. The whole apparatus of religious instruction — accountability, reward, consequence, the Hereafter — presupposes that the listener takes seriously the possibility that his life is being evaluated. Remove that, and the reminder becomes at best interesting and at worst irritating.

Fear also produces the specific mental state in which learning happens: humility. A person who assumes he is fine has no reason to revise anything. The Qur'an identifies this self-satisfaction as the root disease in the very next verses of this sūrah and elsewhere:

> *"Nay, indeed man transgresses because he sees himself as self-sufficient."* (Qur'an 96:6–7)

Note the diagnosis: transgression follows from perceived self-sufficiency. *Khashyah* is the antidote precisely because it dissolves the illusion of independence.

**The Parallel in Sūrah Qāf**

The Qur'an makes almost the same statement in another Makkan chapter, using the same two elements:

> *"So remind by the Qur'an him who fears My warning."* (Qur'an 50:45)

The pairing of "remind" with "him who fears" in both places suggests a deliberate teaching. The Qur'an consistently identifies its own primary audience not as the clever but as the concerned.

And it says so about itself at its opening:

> *"This is the Book in which there is no doubt, a guidance for the God-fearing (al-muttaqīn)."* (Qur'an 2:2)

That verse has puzzled readers: if it guides the God-fearing, what does it do for everyone else? The answer this verse supplies is that guidance requires a receptive substrate. Rain is the same rain; the soil determines the harvest.

**The Ḥadīth of the Rain and the Three Soils**

The Prophet ﷺ gave this exact image, and it is the finest single commentary on our verse:

> *"The parable of the guidance and knowledge with which God has sent me is like abundant rain falling on the earth. Some of it was fertile soil that absorbed the water and brought forth vegetation and grass in abundance. Another portion of it was hard and held the water, and God benefited the people with it: they drank from it, gave drink to their animals, and irrigated with it. And a portion of it was barren land which could neither hold the water nor bring forth vegetation. Such is the example of the one who understands the religion of God and benefits from what God has sent me with, so he learns and teaches it — and the example of the one who does not raise his head to it and does not accept the guidance of God with which I was sent."* (al-Bukhārī 79; Muslim 2282)

Three responses to identical rain. The first soil absorbs and produces — the person who learns and acts. The second holds without producing directly but supplies others — the person who transmits knowledge faithfully even if his own practice is modest. The third rejects everything.

Our verse identifies what makes the difference between the first two categories and the third. It is not the quality of the rain, and it is not the eloquence of the preacher. It is whether the ground has been prepared by *khashyah*.

**Fear and Hope Must Travel Together**

Islamic spirituality has always insisted that fear alone is unbalanced. The classical scholars described the believer as flying with two wings, *khawf* and *rajāʾ* — fear and hope — and warned that a bird cannot fly with one.

The Qur'an models the pairing constantly:

> *"Inform My servants that I am the Forgiving, the Merciful — and that My punishment is the painful punishment."* (Qur'an 15:49–50)

> *"Their sides part from their beds; they call upon their Lord in fear and hope, and from what We have provided them they spend."* (Qur'an 32:16)

The Prophet ﷺ visited a young man dying and asked how he found himself. The man said he hoped in God and feared for his sins, and the Prophet ﷺ replied:

> *"These two do not gather in the heart of a servant in such a situation except that God grants him what he hopes for and secures him from what he fears."* (al-Tirmidhī 983; Ibn Mājah 4261)

The imbalance in either direction is damaging. Fear without hope produces despair, and despair is itself condemned: *"Do not despair of the mercy of God"* (39:53). Hope without fear produces presumption, equally condemned: *"Do they then feel secure from the plan of God? None feels secure from the plan of God except the losing people"* (7:99).

**What Khashyah Looks Like in Practice**

Because the word has been misused to justify a joyless religiosity, it is worth stating what it actually produces in a person's behaviour.

It produces carefulness in small things — the accuracy of a statement, the fairness of a price, the honesty of an excuse — because the one who is watching sees the small things too.

It produces responsiveness to correction. A person with *khashyah* who is told he has wronged someone does not defend; he checks.

It produces private consistency, since the fear is of God rather than of exposure.

It produces gentleness rather than harshness toward others, because the person who genuinely fears his own accounting is slow to feel superior about anyone else's. This is one of the surest tests: religiosity that increases contempt for others is not *khashyah*; it is something else wearing its clothes.

And it produces tears at the appropriate moment. Among those shaded on the Day of Judgment, the Prophet ﷺ named *"a man who remembered God in seclusion and his eyes overflowed"* (al-Bukhārī 660; Muslim 1031), and he said:

> *"Two eyes will not be touched by the Fire: an eye that wept from fear of God, and an eye that kept watch in the path of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 1639)

**Cultivating Receptivity**

If *khashyah* is the condition for benefiting from reminders, the practical question becomes how a person acquires it. The tradition suggests several avenues, all consistent with 35:28's linkage of fear to knowledge.

*Learn about God.* Study of the divine names and attributes is not an abstract exercise; it is the direct route to *khashyah*, because fear tracks understanding.

*Attend to the reminders already built into life.* Illness, funerals, aging parents, near-accidents, the sight of a hospital ward — each is a *dhikrā* delivered without words. The Qur'an notes that most people receive such reminders and let them pass: *"Do they not see that they are tried every year once or twice, yet they do not repent, nor do they take heed?"* (9:126)

*Read the Qur'an as addressed to oneself.* The Qur'an describes its own effect on prepared hearts: *"God has sent down the best statement — a consistent Book wherein is reiteration. The skins shiver thereat of those who fear their Lord; then their skins and their hearts soften at the remembrance of God"* (39:23). Note the sequence: shiver, then soften. Fear is the entry, not the destination.

*Keep company with those who have it.* Receptivity is contagious. So is its absence.

**An Analogy: The Smoke Alarm and the Sleeper**

A smoke alarm sounds at three in the morning in two adjacent apartments. In the first, a light sleeper wakes instantly, checks the kitchen, finds the smouldering appliance, and prevents a fire. In the second, the occupant hears it, assumes it is malfunctioning as it has before, pulls the blanket over his head, and goes back to sleep.

Same alarm, same volume, same information. What differs is the internal state of the hearer: one takes the possibility of fire seriously; the other has decided in advance that alarms are noise.

Verse 10 describes the first sleeper. Verse 11, which follows immediately, will describe the second — and will use a word far stronger than "sleeper."

**Living the Verse**

The verse offers an unusually direct instrument of self-examination. When a reminder reaches you — a verse in a sermon, a warning from a friend, a passage read at random — watch the first movement of your heart. Does it turn toward the reminder, or does it start manufacturing reasons the reminder does not apply?

That first movement is a more accurate reading of one's spiritual state than any amount of self-description. The verse promises that the one who fears will remember. The corollary is exact: if reminders consistently bounce off, the deficiency is not in the reminders.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:11

> **while the most wretched shall avoid it;**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Sorting**

Verse 10 described the receptive hearer. Verse 11 describes his opposite: *wa yatajannabuhā al-ashqā* — "and the most wretched will avoid it."

The two verses are deliberately parallel and deliberately unequal in tone. The first uses a mild descriptive term, *man yakhshā*, "he who fears." The second uses a superlative of condemnation, *al-ashqā*, "the most wretched." The Qur'an is not describing two neutral preferences. It is describing a choice and naming its consequence in the very word used for the chooser.

**Yatajannabu: Avoidance Is an Activity**

The verb *tajannaba* comes from the root *j-n-b*, whose basic sense is the side or flank of something. From it come *jānib* (side), *ijtanaba* (to avoid, literally to put something to one's side), and *janb* (flank).

*Tajannaba*, in this form, means to deliberately keep something to one side, to steer around it, to give it a wide berth. It is not passive ignorance. A person who has never heard the reminder cannot be said to avoid it. Avoidance requires knowing where the thing is in order to stay away from it.

This is the crux of the verse. The Qur'an is not describing someone who missed the message; it is describing someone who located it precisely and then arranged his path around it. Al-Rāzī and others note that this active sense distinguishes the *ashqā* from the merely uninformed, and it is exactly what makes him blameworthy. Ignorance may be an excuse; evasion cannot be.

The same root appears in the Qur'an's positive commands — *"and avoid (ijtanibū) the abomination of idols"* (22:30), *"avoid much suspicion"* (49:12) — which shows that the faculty of steering away is morally neutral in itself. What matters is what one steers away from. The believer avoids what harms him; the wretched avoids what would save him. The mechanism is identical; the target is inverted.

**Al-Ashqā: The Superlative of Misfortune**

*Shaqiyy* comes from the root *sh-q-w*, meaning wretchedness, misery, hardship, and ruin. It is the standard Qur'anic antonym of *saʿīd*, the happy or fortunate one. The Qur'an pairs them explicitly in describing the two outcomes of the Day of Judgment:

> *"The day it comes, no soul will speak except by His permission. Among them will be the wretched (shaqiyy) and the happy (saʿīd)."* (Qur'an 11:105)

*Al-ashqā* is the superlative: the most wretched of all.

The word choice carries a striking argument. The one who avoids the reminder no doubt considers himself to be protecting his interests — his freedom, his pleasures, his social standing, his self-image. The Qur'an names him with the word for the person who has ended up worst off. His avoidance is not clever self-preservation; it is the most catastrophic bargain available to a human being.

The same root is used of the ultimate destiny: *"He will avoid it who is most wretched (al-ashqā), who denies and turns away"* (92:15–16) — the very same superlative in a parallel Makkan passage — and, on the other side, of the one who follows guidance: *"Whoever follows My guidance will not go astray nor be wretched (yashqā)"* (20:123).

**Who Was Meant, and Who Is Meant**

Some commentators identified the immediate reference as the leaders of Makkan opposition — men like Abū Jahl and Abū Lahab, who were not confused about the message but organized against it. Al-Rāzī notes the general framing: those who deny the return to God are the most wretched, in contrast to those who are certain of it or who at least admit its possibility, who are the ones who fear.

But the Qur'an's habit of using descriptive superlatives rather than personal names keeps the category permanently open. The verse names a type, not an individual. In every generation, and within every individual, there is a capacity for exactly this behaviour: to identify the truth accurately and then to route one's life around it.

**The Psychology of Deliberate Avoidance**

Why would a rational being avoid something he suspects is true and important? The Qur'an itself supplies several answers scattered through its pages, and they map with uncomfortable accuracy onto observable human behaviour.

*Because truth carries a cost.* Accepting a message means changing a life. The Makkan elite understood that submitting to the Prophet ﷺ meant surrendering religious authority, revenue from pilgrimage, tribal precedence, and the standing of their ancestors. The Qur'an records the calculation:

> *"And they say: If we were to follow the guidance with you, we would be swept from our land."* (Qur'an 28:57)

*Because it wounds pride.* Accepting a reminder means admitting one was wrong. The Qur'an identifies arrogance as the root refusal:

> *"And when it is said to him, Fear God, pride carries him off to sin. Sufficient for him is Hell, and how wretched is that resting place."* (Qur'an 2:206)

*Because desires resist.* The Qur'an is blunt that unbelief is often the servant of appetite rather than of argument: *"Rather, man wishes to continue in sin ahead of him"* (75:5). The one who intends to keep doing something finds it convenient not to hear that it is forbidden.

*Because facing it means facing death.* Reminder in this sūrah leads immediately to the Fire and the Hereafter. Avoidance of religious reminders is often, at bottom, avoidance of mortality.

The behaviours produced are recognizable in any century. The Qur'an describes physical avoidance:

> *"And indeed, every time I invited them that You might forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears, covered themselves with their garments, persisted, and were arrogant with great arrogance."* (Qur'an 71:7 — Noah's report of his people)

And it describes the manufacture of distraction as a deliberate tactic:

> *"And those who disbelieve say: Do not listen to this Qur'an, and speak noisily during it, that perhaps you will overcome."* (Qur'an 41:26)

That verse is worth pausing over. It records a strategy: not refutation, but noise. The rejecters did not attempt to answer the Qur'an; they attempted to prevent it from being heard. Anyone who has watched a serious question drowned in mockery, or noticed his own reflex to reach for a phone the moment a quiet thought becomes uncomfortable, will recognize the technique. Modern life offers the *ashqā* far more sophisticated instruments of avoidance than the seventh century could supply. A person can now guarantee that he will never have thirty consecutive seconds of silence in which a reminder might reach him.

**The Hardening That Follows Avoidance**

The Qur'an teaches that repeated avoidance is not neutral: it progressively damages the capacity to respond.

> *"No! Rather, the stain has covered their hearts of that which they were earning."* (Qur'an 83:14)

The Prophet ﷺ explained this verse in mechanical terms:

> *"When the servant commits a sin, a black spot is placed upon his heart. If he desists, seeks forgiveness, and repents, his heart is polished. But if he returns, it is increased until it covers his heart — and that is the rust which God mentioned: 'Rather, the stain has covered their hearts.'"* (al-Tirmidhī 3334; Ibn Mājah 4244)

And in another image of two hearts:

> *"Trials are presented to hearts like a reed mat, strand by strand. Any heart that absorbs them has a black mark put in it, and any heart that rejects them has a white mark put in it — until hearts become of two kinds: a white heart like a smooth stone, which no trial harms as long as the heavens and earth endure; and another black and overturned like an inverted vessel, which does not recognize good nor reject evil, except what is soaked into it of its desires."* (Muslim 144)

The image of the inverted vessel is precise: no matter how much is poured, nothing enters. This is the endpoint of the trajectory the verse names. Avoidance repeated becomes incapacity.

**A Warning Against Misapplication**

There is a way of reading this verse that the Qur'an itself forbids: using it as a label for other people.

Nothing in the text authorizes an individual to identify a particular living person as *al-ashqā*. Final states are known only to God, and the tradition is emphatic on this point. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever says, 'The people are ruined,' is the most ruined of them."* (Muslim 2623)

And he related the story of a man who swore that God would not forgive another, whereupon God said:

> *"Who is it that swears by Me that I will not forgive so-and-so? Indeed, I have forgiven him and nullified your deeds."* (Muslim 2621)

History supplies the decisive illustration: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who set out with a sword to kill the Prophet ﷺ, became the second caliph; Khālid ibn al-Walīd, who commanded the enemy at Uḥud, became the commander of Muslim armies. Anyone confidently classifying either of them at the time would have been catastrophically wrong.

The verse is therefore a mirror, not a telescope. Its correct use is self-examination.

**Honest Self-Examination**

Applied inward, the verse asks an uncomfortable and useful question: *what am I steering around?*

Most people who take religion seriously still maintain small zones of deliberate avoidance. The subject one never brings up with a knowledgeable friend, because one suspects the answer. The category of income one has decided not to investigate. The relationship one knows requires repair and keeps postponing. The verse of the Qur'an one skips past.

The tradition's counsel is to move toward the discomfort rather than away from it, and to do so early, while the heart is still responsive. ʿUmar's famous instruction captures the method:

> *"Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed for you."*

Self-audit performed voluntarily is far easier than the audit described in the following verses of this sūrah.

**An Analogy: The Unopened Letter**

A person receives a letter from the tax authority. He recognizes the envelope, knows roughly what it says, and puts it in a drawer. Each day he sees the drawer and does not open it. The debt does not pause; it accrues. Interest compounds. Deadlines pass. The eventual reckoning is far worse than it would have been on the first day.

His avoidance felt, at every moment, like relief. That is the deception at the heart of it: evasion pays immediately and charges later. This is exactly why the sūrah moves directly from this verse to verses 12 and 13, where the bill is presented in full. The Qur'an does not leave the analogy hanging; it opens the envelope.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:12

> **he who enters into the greatest Fire,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Consequence Named Without Delay**

The previous verse identified the one who steers around the reminder. This verse tells him where the road he chose actually leads. *Alladhī yaṣlā al-nāra al-kubrā* — "the one who will enter into the greatest Fire."

The grammatical link is important: this verse is a relative clause describing *al-ashqā* from the preceding verse. It is not a separate subject. The Qur'an is saying that the wretchedness already named is not an insult but a description of outcome — he is called "the most wretched" precisely because this is where he ends.

This immediacy is characteristic of Makkan revelation. Cause and consequence are placed adjacent, with nothing intervening, so that the listener cannot separate the act from its result.

**Yaṣlā: To Be Made to Endure Fire**

The verb *ṣaliya* / *yaṣlā*, from the root *ṣ-l-y*, has a specific and physical meaning in Arabic. It is used for roasting meat over a fire, for warming oneself at a flame, and for being scorched. From the same root come *ṣilāʾ* (the act of being burned) and *taṣliyah*.

The verb thus conveys entering into direct contact with fire and remaining in it — not passing by it or being near it, but being subjected to it. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly in this sense:

> *"He will burn in a Fire of blazing flame."* (Qur'an 111:3)

> *"I will drive him into Saqar."* (Qur'an 74:26)

> *"Indeed, those who consume the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire, and they will be burned in a Blaze."* (Qur'an 4:10)

The choice of a verb from the vocabulary of cooking is deliberately unsparing. The Qur'an does not soften its eschatology into metaphor, and it does not permit the listener to imagine punishment as a vague spiritual condition. Whatever the ultimate nature of that reality — and the tradition affirms that it exceeds our categories — the language chosen is language a human being feels in his body.

**Al-Nār al-Kubrā: Why "the Greatest"**

*Al-kubrā* is the feminine superlative of *kabīr*, "great." It means "the greatest," "the major," "the supreme."

The commentators offer two principal explanations for the superlative, both preserved in the classical works.

*The greatest Fire as opposed to the lesser fire of this world.* This is the most widely reported reading, found in the Jalālayn, al-Rāzī, and others. The fire of this world is real and painful, but it is a diminished sample. The Prophet ﷺ stated the comparison in explicit quantitative terms:

> *"This fire of yours, which the son of Adam kindles, is one part of seventy parts of the fire of Hell." They said: "O Messenger of God, this one alone would have been enough!" He said: "It has been given an excess of sixty-nine parts, each of them like its heat."* (al-Bukhārī 3265; Muslim 2843)

The reaction of the Companions preserved in that report — *this one alone would have been enough* — is the natural human response, and the Prophet ﷺ did not soften it.

*The greatest Fire as the deepest level of punishment.* Al-Rāzī records the alternative that the superlative distinguishes degrees within the Hereafter itself: the punishment is not uniform, and *al-nār al-kubrā* is the portion reserved for the most wretched. This reading is strongly supported by the Qur'an's statement about the hypocrites:

> *"Indeed, the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire, and never will you find for them a helper."* (Qur'an 4:145)

Graded punishment is in fact a requirement of justice, and the Qur'an affirms gradation on both sides of the ledger: *"For all are degrees according to what they have done"* (6:132). A tyrant who killed thousands and a man who died in casual neglect cannot receive identical treatment without justice being violated.

**Why Punishment at All? The Argument from Justice**

Modern readers frequently find this the hardest material in scripture, and the discomfort deserves a serious answer rather than either denial or shouting.

Begin with the alternative. Consider a world in which every human being, regardless of conduct, arrives at the same destination. In such a world the man who spent his life sheltering refugees and the man who trafficked them share an identical end. The woman who raised children alone through poverty without compromising her integrity and the man who defrauded her of her savings are treated the same. Whatever else such a universe might be, it is not just — and something in every human being, including those who reject religion entirely, protests against it. The universal human demand for justice is one of the few genuinely cross-cultural moral facts, and the Qur'an appeals to it directly:

> *"Or should We treat those who believe and do righteous deeds like the corrupters in the land? Or should We treat those who fear God like the wicked?"* (Qur'an 38:28)

> *"Then is one who was a believer like one who was defiantly disobedient? They are not equal."* (Qur'an 32:18)

The Qur'an also insists that this world does not settle accounts. Tyrants die comfortable; victims die unknown. If the ledger closes at death, it closes unbalanced. The Hereafter is the Qur'an's answer to the problem of unpunished injustice, and it is worth noticing that this makes divine justice a doctrine of consolation for the oppressed before it is a doctrine of threat to the comfortable.

**The Safeguards That Frame Divine Punishment**

The Qur'an surrounds its warnings with limits that must be stated alongside them, or the doctrine is misrepresented.

*Nobody is punished without warning.*

> *"And We never punish until We have sent a messenger."* (Qur'an 17:15)

*Nobody is punished beyond what they did.*

> *"Whoever comes with a good deed will have ten times the like thereof, and whoever comes with an evil deed will not be recompensed except the like thereof — and they will not be wronged."* (Qur'an 6:160)

The asymmetry here is striking and is easy to overlook: good is multiplied tenfold and beyond; evil is recorded once, at exact value. This is not a system tilted toward condemnation.

*Nobody is punished for another's crime.*

> *"And no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another."* (Qur'an 6:164)

*Nobody is wronged in the least.*

> *"Indeed, God does not wrong the people at all, but it is the people who wrong themselves."* (Qur'an 10:44)

*And mercy is declared to outweigh wrath.* In a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"When God decreed the creation, He wrote in His Book, which is with Him above the Throne: 'My mercy prevails over My wrath.'"* (al-Bukhārī 3194; Muslim 2751)

The Prophet ﷺ also described the scale of divine mercy in a way that reframes the entire subject:

> *"God has one hundred mercies, of which He sent down one between jinn, mankind, animals, and insects — by which they are compassionate to one another and by which the wild animal is kind to its offspring. And God has withheld ninety-nine mercies with which He will be merciful to His servants on the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Bukhārī 6469; Muslim 2752)

Every act of human kindness ever performed — every mother nursing a child, every stranger stopping to help — belongs to a single portion of divine mercy. Ninety-nine remain in reserve for the Day of Judgment. Any presentation of Islamic eschatology that leaves this out has misrepresented it.

**Punishment as the Natural Continuation of a Choice**

There is a further way of understanding the Fire that the tradition supports and that resolves much of the moral discomfort: the punishment is not an arbitrary penalty attached to an unrelated act, but the manifestation of what the act already was.

The Qur'an frequently uses language suggesting continuity rather than external imposition:

> *"Indeed, those who consume the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire."* (Qur'an 4:10)

The fire is described as being eaten *now*, in this life; the Hereafter reveals what the meal actually consisted of. Similarly:

> *"On the Day when their foreheads, their flanks, and their backs will be branded with it: This is what you hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard."* (Qur'an 9:35)

*What you hoarded for yourselves* — the punishment is identified as the hoard. On this reading, Hell is not a place God builds to house people He is angry with; it is the final and undisguised form of a life spent turning away from the source of all good. The Qur'an's phrase for the disbelievers on that Day is: *"Nay, but they will be veiled from their Lord that Day"* (83:15). Deprivation of God is named before the burning is described.

**The Warning as an Act of Mercy**

It is worth asking why the Qur'an describes the Fire at all, and the answer is contained in the structure of this sūrah. The description arrives immediately after the command to *remind* and the naming of the one who *avoids*. It is placed in front of people while they still have time to change course.

Warning is what one does for people one wants to save. The Prophet ﷺ described his own position in exactly these terms:

> *"My example and the example of what God sent me with is like a man who came to a people and said: 'O people, I have seen the army with my own eyes, and I am the naked warner — so save yourselves!' A group of them obeyed him and set off at nightfall and escaped. Another group disbelieved him and stayed where they were, and the army came upon them in the morning and destroyed them."* (al-Bukhārī 6482; Muslim 2283)

And in a second image of unbearable poignancy:

> *"My example and your example is like a man who kindled a fire, and when it lit up what was around it, moths and insects began falling into it, and he was trying to drive them away — and I am holding you back by your waists from the Fire, while you are slipping from my hands."* (al-Bukhārī 6483; Muslim 2284)

That is the emotional register in which this verse should be read. It is not the voice of a judge relishing a sentence. It is the voice of someone grabbing at people as they walk toward an edge they cannot see.

**Living the Verse**

Two practical responses follow, and the tradition names both.

The first is to use the fear productively rather than being paralyzed by it. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to ask for protection explicitly and constantly — the supplication *"O God, save me from the Fire"* was to be said after the final testimony in every prayer, and he told his Companions:

> *"Whoever asks God for Paradise three times, Paradise says: O God, admit him into Paradise. And whoever seeks refuge from the Fire three times, the Fire says: O God, save him from the Fire."* (al-Tirmidhī 2572; al-Nasāʾī 5521)

The second is to shield oneself and others with the smallest available deeds, since the tradition insists that no act of protection is too minor to count:

> *"Protect yourselves from the Fire, even with half a date."* (al-Bukhārī 1417; Muslim 1016)

Half a date. The verse describes the greatest Fire; the remedy offered is a fragment of fruit given to someone hungry. That disproportion is the whole point — the danger is enormous and the door out of it is deliberately set low enough for anyone to step through.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:13

> **then neither dies therein nor lives.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Closes the Last Exit**

Verse 12 named the destination. Verse 13 removes the one escape a suffering creature always assumes is available: *thumma lā yamūtu fīhā wa lā yaḥyā* — "then he neither dies therein nor lives."

Every earthly torment has a natural limit. Pain either ends because the cause is removed, or because the body adapts, or because the sufferer loses consciousness, or because he dies. The last of these is the ultimate guarantee: no human agony on earth can exceed the endurance of the body, because the body gives out. This verse states that in that condition the guarantee has been withdrawn.

**Thumma: The Weight of "Then"**

The connective is *thumma*, which in Arabic indicates sequence with an interval — "then, after a while" — as distinct from *fa-*, which indicates immediate succession. The sūrah has used *fa-* repeatedly in its opening verses (created *then* fashioned, measured *then* guided, brought forth pasture *then* made it debris), where the acts follow instantly upon one another.

Here the connective changes, and the change carries meaning. The entering of the Fire is one thing; the endless condition that follows is described as extending beyond it, stretched out in time. The grammar itself lengthens the sentence.

**"Neither Dies nor Lives": An Arabic Idiom of Extremity**

Al-Rāzī notes that in Arabic usage, to say of a person that he neither lives nor dies is to indicate that he is undergoing severe and continuous trial. The phrase describes a suspended condition: too afflicted for the state to be called living, yet not released into death.

The Qur'an uses the identical formula elsewhere, in the description of Pharaoh's people at the resurrection:

> *"Indeed, whoever comes to his Lord as a criminal — for him is Hell; he will neither die therein nor live."* (Qur'an 20:74)

And it expands the picture in Sūrah Ibrāhīm:

> *"Before him is Hell, and he will be given a drink of purulent water. He will gulp it but will hardly be able to swallow it, and death will come to him from every side, yet he is not to die; and before him is a massive punishment."* (Qur'an 14:16–17)

The phrase *"death will come to him from every side, yet he is not to die"* is one of the most vivid in the Qur'an. Death approaches from all directions — that is, every kind of pain that would normally kill arrives — and still the release does not come.

Sūrah Fāṭir records the resulting plea:

> *"And those who disbelieved will have the fire of Hell. It will not be decreed for them to die, nor will its torment be lightened for them. Thus do We recompense every ungrateful one. And they will cry out therein: Our Lord, take us out; we will do righteousness, other than what we were doing! But did We not grant you life enough for whoever would remember therein to remember? And the warner came to you. So taste, for the wrongdoers there is no helper."* (Qur'an 35:36–37)

Two things in that passage deserve attention. First, the request is not for pardon but for a second chance — and the answer given is that the first chance was sufficient. *"Did We not grant you life enough for whoever would remember to remember?"* Every human being who has lived to adulthood has had time. Second, the phrase *"and the warner came to you"* closes the excuse of ignorance, the same point the Qur'an makes at 17:15.

And the final request, when the plea for release is abandoned and only annihilation is sought:

> *"And they will call: O Mālik, let your Lord put an end to us! He will say: Indeed, you will remain."* (Qur'an 43:77)

Mālik is the angel appointed over the Fire. The request is for extinction, and it is refused.

**Why Death Is a Mercy — and Its Removal a Punishment**

The verse contains, by implication, one of the Qur'an's least noticed teachings: death in this world is a mercy.

We do not usually experience it that way, and the Qur'an does not pretend otherwise. But consider the alternative. Endless aging without release, chronic pain with no terminus, grief that can never resolve, a body degrading indefinitely — that is what deathlessness in a fallen condition would mean. Death draws a line under both suffering and sin. It stops the accumulation. For the believer it is described as an opening rather than an ending, and the Prophet ﷺ said of the deceased believer:

> *"When the funeral is ready and the men carry it on their shoulders, if it was righteous it says: Present me quickly! And if it was not righteous, it says: Woe to it! Where are they taking it?"* (al-Bukhārī 1314)

The removal of death in the Hereafter is therefore the removal of a mercy, and the tradition marks this with one of the most striking images in the ḥadīth corpus:

> *"Death will be brought forth in the form of a black and white ram, and a caller will call: O people of Paradise! They will crane their necks and look. He will say: Do you recognize this? They will say: Yes, this is death — and all of them will have seen it. Then he will call: O people of the Fire! They will crane their necks and look. He will say: Do you recognize this? They will say: Yes, this is death — and all of them will have seen it. Then it will be slaughtered, and he will say: O people of Paradise, eternity and no death; O people of the Fire, eternity and no death."* (al-Bukhārī 4730; Muslim 2849)

The narration adds that the people of Paradise increase in joy upon their joy, and the people of the Fire in grief upon their grief. The slaughtering of death is the sealing of both destinations.

**"Nor Lives": The Second Half of the Denial**

The verse denies two things, not one. He does not die — and he does not live.

This second denial is easily passed over, but the commentators drew from it that whatever exists in that state cannot properly be called life. Life in the Qur'anic sense involves more than the persistence of sensation. It involves hope, growth, relationship, purpose, the possibility of change. Strip all of these away and what remains is bare continuation.

The Qur'an in fact reverses the usual categories, describing spiritual deadness in this world as a form of non-life:

> *"Is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him a light by which to walk among the people like one who is in darknesses, never to emerge therefrom?"* (Qur'an 6:122)

> *"And the home of the Hereafter — that is the true life, if only they knew."* (Qur'an 29:64)

So the categories are consistent: real life is life connected to God, and its absence is death whether or not the heart is beating. The condition described in verse 13 is that absence made permanent and undisguised.

**The Question of Eternity, and What the Tradition Actually Says**

The permanence of punishment has been the subject of serious discussion among Muslim scholars, and honesty requires setting out what the sources say.

For those who die in disbelief, the Qur'an uses the language of permanence: *"abiding therein forever" (khālidīna fīhā abadā)*, at 4:169, 33:65, 72:23 and elsewhere.

For believers who enter the Fire on account of their sins, the tradition is emphatic that this is temporary, and the texts on the matter are among the most hopeful in the corpus:

> *"There will come out of the Fire whoever says 'There is no god but God' and has in his heart good equal to the weight of a barley grain; then there will come out of the Fire whoever says 'There is no god but God' and has in his heart good equal to the weight of a wheat grain; then there will come out of the Fire whoever says 'There is no god but God' and has in his heart good equal to the weight of an atom."* (al-Bukhārī 44; Muslim 193)

And the description of their restoration:

> *"Then God will say: The angels have interceded, the prophets have interceded, and the believers have interceded, and none remains but the Most Merciful of the merciful. He will take a handful from the Fire and bring out a people who never did any good... They will be cast into a river at the mouth of Paradise called the River of Life, and they will sprout as a seed sprouts in the silt carried by a flood."* (Muslim 183; al-Bukhārī 7439, with variant wording)

The image of people growing back like seedlings after a flood is deliberately gentle, and it is placed at the end of the most severe eschatological material in the tradition.

A minority of scholars — notably Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, on the basis of statements reported from ʿUmar and Ibn ʿAbbās and of Qur'anic phrases such as *"except what thy Lord wills"* (11:107) — discussed whether even the Fire itself might have a term known to God. The majority did not adopt this view. What the discussion demonstrates is that the tradition treated divine mercy as a live question even at its most severe boundary, rather than as an afterthought.

**The Function of This Verse in a Life**

Verses of this kind are not given to produce despair, and the Qur'an itself forbids the despair they might seem to invite:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (Qur'an 39:53)

Ibn Kathīr notes that this verse was revealed concerning those who had committed the gravest offenses and thought themselves beyond forgiveness. *All sins* — with repentance, the door does not close in this life.

The function of verse 13 is therefore to convey urgency about the one thing that is finite: time. The condition it describes has no exit, which is exactly why the present life, which has many, is so valuable. The Prophet ﷺ located the whole opportunity in the interval before death:

> *"God accepts the repentance of a servant as long as the death rattle has not reached his throat."* (al-Tirmidhī 3537; Ibn Mājah 4253)

And the Qur'an closes the door precisely there, at the last breath, not before:

> *"But repentance is not accepted of those who continue to do evil deeds until, when death comes to one of them, he says: Indeed, I repent now."* (Qur'an 4:18)

**An Analogy Handled Carefully**

Analogies for eternity necessarily fail, but one comparison is instructive rather than merely frightening. Consider the difference between a patient told that a painful treatment will last six months and a patient told there is no end date. The physical sensation may be identical; the psychological experience is not remotely comparable. Human endurance runs almost entirely on the arithmetic of remaining time. Take away the arithmetic and endurance has nothing to hold.

That is what verse 13 removes. And the appropriate response is not to attempt to imagine it — the imagination fails — but to notice that we are, at this moment, in the period during which the arithmetic still works.

**Living the Verse**

The practical yield of this verse is a reordering of urgency. Human beings routinely treat reversible problems as emergencies and irreversible ones as background noise. A missed payment produces immediate action; an unrepented wrong produces a vague intention to deal with it later.

The verse inverts the priority. Debts can be renegotiated, careers rebuilt, illnesses treated, reputations repaired. The condition described here admits of no revision — but only after the point of transition. Before it, everything is revisable, and revising it is the cheapest transaction available to a human being: a sincere turning back, at any hour, at any age, after anything.

> *"And turn to God in repentance, all of you, O believers, that you might succeed."* (Qur'an 24:31)

The sūrah's next verse begins with exactly that word — *aflaḥa*, "he has succeeded" — and describes what that turning back actually looks like in practice.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:14

> **He indeed prospers who is purified,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Turning from the Fire to the Way Out**

After two verses of unrelenting warning, the sūrah changes direction entirely. *Qad aflaḥa man tazakkā* — "He has indeed succeeded who purified himself."

The placement is intentional. Having shown the destination of avoidance, the Qur'an immediately shows the alternative, and it does so in the most positive grammatical construction available in Arabic: *qad* followed by a perfect-tense verb, which conveys certainty and completion. The success is stated as an accomplished fact, not a possibility. Not *he may succeed*, but *he has succeeded*.

This is the Qur'an's characteristic rhythm — warning followed immediately by the open door. It never leaves the listener with the Fire as the final word.

**Aflaḥa: Success as Breaking Through Soil**

*Aflaḥa* comes from the root *f-l-ḥ*, whose primary physical meaning is to split, cleave, or break open. The Arabic word for a farmer is *fallāḥ*, from the same root — the one who splits the soil.

This etymology is one of the most illuminating in Qur'anic vocabulary. *Falāḥ*, the noun, is usually translated "success" or "prosperity," but the buried image is agricultural: success is what happens when a seed breaks through the earth that was covering it, and when a farmer breaks the ground so that growth becomes possible.

Several consequences follow from this image.

Success requires breaking something. The seed must rupture its own shell; the crust of the soil must be split. Nothing grows without a barrier being broken through. The barriers in human life are habits, comforts, pride, and the accumulated sediment of a settled way of living.

Success is a process with stages, not a single event. Ploughing, sowing, watering, waiting, weeding, harvest. The farmer who expects a crop the week after planting has misunderstood agriculture, and the person who expects transformation in a weekend has misunderstood the soul.

Success takes time and patience. This is why the same call appears in the *adhān* five times a day: *ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ* — "come to success." Every call to prayer summons the listener to farming work.

The Qur'an opens Sūrah al-Muʾminūn with the same verb and then lists the crop:

> *"Certainly will the believers have succeeded (qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn): they who are during their prayer humbly submissive, and they who turn away from ill speech, and they who are observant of zakāh..."* (Qur'an 23:1–4)

**Tazakkā: Purification and Growth in One Word**

*Tazakkā* is the reflexive form of the root *z-k-w* / *z-k-y*, and this root carries two meanings simultaneously: purity and growth. Something that is *zakiyy* is both clean and thriving. The obligatory charity is called *zakāh* for exactly this reason — it purifies wealth of greed and causes it to grow.

The union of the two meanings is not accidental; it states a principle. Purification is not subtraction only. Removing what is corrupt is precisely what permits growth. A gardener who prunes is not damaging the plant; the pruned branch is removed so that the plant's energy goes where it produces fruit. A person who cuts a destructive habit does not become smaller; the capacity previously consumed by it becomes available.

The reflexive form *tazakkā* means "he purified himself." The verb assigns the action to the person. God guides, enables, and accepts — but the verb here is not passive. Someone did something.

**Three Classical Readings of "Purified"**

The commentators identified three principal meanings, recorded in al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Ṭūsī, and others.

*Purification from idolatry by affirming God's Oneness.* On this reading *tazakkā* is the foundational purification: cleansing the heart of associating anything with God. This is the primary and most defensible reading, because the sūrah is Makkan and its central conflict is over *tawḥīd*. The Qur'an describes idolatry itself as a kind of filth: *"So avoid the uncleanliness of idols"* (22:30). And no subsequent purification is possible while the foundation is polluted, since deeds are validated by the orientation behind them.

*Purification through righteous deeds.* On this reading *tazakkā* is the general moral cleansing of a person's character and conduct — the ongoing work of *tazkiyat al-nafs*.

*Purification through the alms of ʿĪd al-Fiṭr.* This is the reading attributed to a number of early authorities, and it is connected to the specifically Madinan institution of *zakāt al-fiṭr*. Ibn ʿUmar and ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz are among those reported to have read the verse this way, and the reading is strengthened by the sequence with the next verse: give the alms, then remember God's Name, then pray — which is precisely the order of the ʿĪd morning. In Shīʿī tradition, the sixth Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq is reported to have stated that the ordering of the verses indicates that the alms are given in the morning before the two cycles of the ʿĪd prayer.

These readings are not in competition. The general principle is the purification of the self; the ʿĪd charity is a beautiful specific instance of it, and the Prophet ﷺ described that charity in exactly the language of purification:

> *"The Messenger of God ﷺ enjoined zakāt al-fiṭr as a purification for the fasting person from idle talk and obscenity, and as food for the poor."* (Abū Dāwūd 1609; Ibn Mājah 1827)

**The Qur'anic Doctrine of Self-Purification**

This verse belongs to a family of Qur'anic statements that make purification of the self the central determinant of a person's fate.

> *"By the soul and He who proportioned it, and inspired it with its wickedness and its righteousness — he has succeeded who purifies it (qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā), and he has failed who instils it with corruption (wa qad khāba man dassāhā)."* (Qur'an 91:7–10)

The parallel is nearly verbatim with our verse, and it supplies the antonym. *Dassāhā* comes from a root meaning to bury, conceal, or push down out of sight — the same root used for the pre-Islamic practice of burying infant girls (16:59). The failed person is the one who buries his own soul: covers over its innate sense of right, smothers its capacity to respond.

The Qur'an also names purification as a core purpose of prophecy itself:

> *"It is He who has sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves, reciting to them His verses and purifying them (yuzakkīhim) and teaching them the Book and wisdom, although they were before in clear error."* (Qur'an 62:2)

Notice the placement of *yuzakkīhim* — purification is listed between recitation and teaching, not after them. Prophecy is not merely an information delivery service. It is a programme of character formation.

**What Purification Involves in Practice**

The tradition developed an entire science around this verse's imperative. Its outline is worth stating in plain terms, because "purify yourself" can otherwise remain a pious abstraction.

*Purification of belief* — clearing the mind of false conceptions of God: that He is unjust, that He is indifferent, that He can be bribed by ritual, that He is a projection of one's own preferences, or that anything shares His authority.

*Purification of intention.* The Prophet ﷺ placed this at the foundation of everything:

> *"Actions are only by intentions, and every person will have only what he intended."* (al-Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907)

The placement of this ḥadīth as the first in al-Bukhārī's collection was deliberate on the compiler's part: nothing that follows counts without it.

*Purification of the heart from its recognized diseases* — envy, arrogance, rancour, ostentation, love of praise, and greed. The Prophet ﷺ was asked which people are best and answered:

> *"Every one with a sealed heart and a truthful tongue." They said: "A truthful tongue we know, but what is a sealed heart?" He said: "It is the pure and God-fearing heart, in which there is no sin, no transgression, no rancour, and no envy."* (Ibn Mājah 4216)

*Purification of the limbs from wrongdoing* — the outward conduct that follows from the inward state.

*Purification of wealth* through obligatory and voluntary giving, which is why the same root names the charity.

*Purification of the body*, since Islam ties ritual purity to worship. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Purification is half of faith."* (Muslim 223)

The tradition consistently treats outer and inner cleanliness as connected rather than as competing priorities: the ablution before prayer is a physical rehearsal of what the prayer is meant to do internally.

**The Mechanism: How Purification Actually Happens**

The Qur'an and Sunnah name specific instruments, and they are all ordinary and available.

*Repentance*, which the tradition treats as the primary cleaning agent:

> *"The one who repents from sin is like one who has no sin."* (Ibn Mājah 4250)

*Prayer*, described by the Prophet ﷺ with a domestic image:

> *"If there were a river at the door of any one of you and he bathed in it five times a day, would any dirt remain on him?" They said: "No dirt would remain on him at all." He said: "That is the example of the five prayers: by them God wipes away sins."* (al-Bukhārī 528; Muslim 667)

*Charity*, whose purifying function is stated in the Qur'an directly: *"Take from their wealth a charity by which you purify them and cause them increase"* (9:103) — again both meanings of the root in a single verse.

*Difficulty patiently borne*, which the Prophet ﷺ described as expiation: *"No fatigue, illness, anxiety, sorrow, harm, or distress befalls a Muslim, even the prick of a thorn, but that God expiates some of his sins thereby"* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573).

*Good character*, which the Prophet ﷺ weighted heavily: *"Nothing is placed on the Scale that is heavier than good character"* (Abū Dāwūd 4799; al-Tirmidhī 2003).

**The Order Matters: Why Purification Comes First**

There is a sequence in verses 14 and 15 that is easy to miss and important to notice. Purification is mentioned first; remembrance and prayer follow.

Al-Ālūsī and others draw the lesson: the outward acts of worship rest on an inward foundation. Remembrance with a tongue while the heart is elsewhere, and prayer performed by a person whose life is unpurified, are structures built on unstable ground. The Qur'an says of prayer that it is meant to produce exactly this effect: *"Indeed, prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing"* (29:45) — and where it visibly does not, something in the foundation requires attention.

This is not a licence to postpone worship until one is pure, which would invert the relationship entirely; prayer is itself one of the chief instruments of purification, as the river ḥadīth states. The point is rather that purification and worship are meant to reinforce each other, and that a person who performs the outward acts while making no attempt at the inward work has taken only half the prescription.

**An Analogy: The Filter and the Water**

Consider a household water filter. Its function is to remove contaminants so that what passes through is drinkable. Two failures are possible. The filter may be clogged, in which case nothing flows. Or the filter may be bypassed, in which case everything flows but nothing is cleaned.

The human heart in this verse is the filter through which a person's whole life passes — perceptions coming in, actions going out. Purification is filter maintenance. It is unglamorous, requires regular attention, and is invisible to everyone except the person doing it and the One who sees the hidden. And it determines the quality of everything downstream: how a person hears criticism, treats a subordinate, spends an unobserved hour, or responds to another's good fortune.

**Living the Verse**

The verse offers a definition of success that competes directly with every other definition a person will encounter. Wealth, status, recognition, followers, security — the Qur'an does not deny that these exist or that they matter in their place, but it refuses to call them *falāḥ*. Success is purification, full stop, and it is available to a person of any income, any education, and any social position.

The practical instrument is a regular audit, and it needs no elaborate technique: identify one impurity, name it honestly, and work on it deliberately until it loosens. One habit of speech. One grudge. One category of consumption. One relationship being neglected. The verb is *tazakkā* — reflexive, active, and personal. Nobody else can conjugate it on your behalf.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:15

> **remembers the Name of his Lord and prays.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second and Third Movements of Success**

Verse 14 named the first component of success: purification. This verse names the two that complete it — *wa dhakara isma rabbihi fa-ṣallā*, "and remembers the Name of his Lord and prays."

Three acts, in a deliberate order: purify, remember, pray. Al-Ālūsī reads the two verses together as describing worship with the whole of one's being — purifying the heart, remembering with the tongue, and performing with the limbs the actions God commanded, foremost among them the prayer. Inner, verbal, and physical: nothing is left out, and none of the three stands alone.

The order is also a movement outward. Purification is invisible and internal. Remembrance is verbal and semi-private. Prayer is embodied and communal. Religion in this sūrah begins in the heart, passes through the tongue, and ends in the body — which is precisely the sequence in which any genuine conviction becomes real.

**Remembering "the Name" of His Lord**

The verse says *dhakara isma rabbihi* — he remembered the *Name* of his Lord — echoing the sūrah's opening verse, which commanded glorification of the Name of the Lord, the Most High. This inclusio binds the chapter together: the command of verse 1 is fulfilled in the practice of verse 15.

Why "the Name"? For the reasons discussed at verse 1, and one more that is specific here: remembrance operates through language. The Name is the handle by which a limited creature takes hold of an unlimited reality. To remember God is, in practice, to say something.

The commentators offer several specifications of what remembering the Name means in this context. Al-Ṭūsī records that some understood it as mentioning God's Name in the *basmalah* at the beginning of each prayer — the formula *bismi Llāhi al-Raḥmāni al-Raḥīm* with which the recitation opens. Others, including al-Ālūsī, insist on the broader and deeper sense: remembering God with heart and tongue together, since remembrance by the tongue while the heart is heedless carries no benefit and earns no reward.

That last point is worth stating in the strongest terms, because it protects the whole practice from degenerating into mechanical repetition. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Know that God does not accept a supplication from a heedless, inattentive heart."* (al-Tirmidhī 3479)

**Dhikr: The Practice and Its Place in Islamic Life**

*Dhikr* — remembrance — is one of the widest categories in Islamic devotion. It includes the recitation of the Qur'an, the formulas of glorification and praise, supplication, the mention of God upon eating, entering a house, boarding a mount, waking, and sleeping, and the simple internal awareness of God during ordinary activity.

The Qur'an commands it in unusually expansive terms:

> *"O you who believe, remember God with much remembrance, and glorify Him morning and evening."* (Qur'an 33:41–42)

> *"Those who remember God standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and who reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth: Our Lord, You did not create this aimlessly."* (Qur'an 3:191)

Note the postures listed in that second verse: standing, sitting, lying down. Between them they cover the entire waking and resting life. Remembrance is not scheduled into a slot; it saturates the day.

And the Qur'an states the effect plainly:

> *"Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of God. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of God hearts are assured."* (Qur'an 13:28)

The word *taṭmaʾinn* — assurance, tranquillity, the settling of something restless — describes a psychological state, and the Qur'an names its cause without hedging.

The Prophet ﷺ made remembrance the highest-value activity relative to effort:

> *"Shall I not inform you of the best of your deeds, the purest in the sight of your Sovereign, the highest in your ranks, better for you than spending gold and silver, and better than meeting your enemy and striking their necks and their striking yours?" They said: "Yes." He said: "The remembrance of God."* (al-Tirmidhī 3377; Ibn Mājah 3790)

And he described the contrast between the living and the dead in exactly these terms:

> *"The example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the example of the living and the dead."* (al-Bukhārī 6407)

In a ḥadīth qudsī, God describes His side of the exchange:

> *"I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he remembers Me within himself, I remember him within Myself; and if he remembers Me in a gathering, I remember him in a gathering better than it."* (al-Bukhārī 7405; Muslim 2675)

**Fa-ṣallā: The Consequence, Not Merely the Sequel**

The verse joins prayer to remembrance with *fa-*, the connective of immediate consequence: he remembered the Name of his Lord *and so* he prayed.

This grammatical detail carries the logic of the verse. Prayer is presented as the natural result of genuine remembrance, not as an unrelated additional duty. A person who has actually brought God to mind will pray; someone who never prays has, whatever he says, not been remembering.

And the Qur'an describes prayer itself as remembrance, closing the circle:

> *"Indeed, I am God. There is no deity except Me, so worship Me and establish prayer for My remembrance."* (Qur'an 20:14)

**Which Prayer Is Meant**

Most commentators take *ṣallā* here as referring to the five canonical prayers. Al-Baghawī records that some understood it specifically as the congregational prayer of the morning of ʿĪd al-Fiṭr, in keeping with the reading of verse 14 as referring to the ʿĪd charity.

The general reading is stronger and includes the specific. Prayer in the Qur'anic sense covers the obligatory five, the voluntary prayers, the night prayer, the ʿĪd and Friday prayers, and — in the root's original sense — supplication itself, since *ṣalāh* before its technical Islamic meaning denoted invocation and blessing.

The root *ṣ-l-w* is discussed by lexicographers with several suggested derivations, including a connection to the bending of the back in bowing and prostration, and a connection to invocation. Both senses are present in the Islamic practice: the prayer is a supplication performed with the body.

**Why the Order Is Purify, Remember, Pray**

The sequence of these two verses rewards attention because it maps a genuine psychological progression.

*Purification* is the removal of obstruction. A heart occupied with idols — whether stone ones or the modern equivalents of wealth, status, and appetite — has no room to remember.

*Remembrance* is the reorientation of attention. Once the obstruction is cleared, the mind turns toward God. This is a matter of thought and speech, and it is portable: it can happen in traffic, in a hospital corridor, at a desk.

*Prayer* is the commitment of the body. Attention that never reaches the limbs remains sentiment. Prayer takes an internal orientation and forces it into a schedule, a direction, a posture, and a set of words, at fixed hours that do not consult one's mood.

Reverse the order and the structure fails. A person who prays without remembering performs calisthenics; a person who remembers without purifying is divided; a person who purifies without praying has cleared a space and left it empty.

**The Centrality of Prayer in Islam**

The tradition places prayer at the top of practice after belief itself, and the evidence is extensive.

It was legislated directly, without an intermediary, during the Night Journey, unlike every other obligation.

It is the first matter of accounting:

> *"The first thing for which a servant will be brought to account on the Day of Resurrection is his prayer. If it is sound, he has succeeded; and if it is corrupt, he has failed and lost."* (al-Tirmidhī 413; al-Nasāʾī 465)

It is the dividing line the Prophet ﷺ drew most sharply:

> *"Between a man and disbelief and idolatry is the abandonment of prayer."* (Muslim 82)

It was the last instruction he gave. In the final hours, as reported in the accounts of his death, he kept repeating: *"The prayer, the prayer, and what your right hands possess."*

And it is described as the comfort rather than the burden of the one who prays:

> *"Beloved to me from your world are women and perfume, and the coolness of my eye has been placed in the prayer."* (al-Nasāʾī 3939)

That phrase — *qurrat ʿaynī*, "the coolness of my eye," an Arabic idiom for the deepest delight and rest — is the corrective to any conception of prayer as a tax. He also said to Bilāl, when calling for the prayer to be established: *"Give us rest by it, O Bilāl"* (Abū Dāwūd 4985). Rest *by* it, not rest *from* it.

**Presence of Heart: The Difference Between Two Identical Prayers**

Since verse 15 links prayer to remembrance, the quality of attention within prayer becomes the central issue rather than a refinement. Two people may perform identical movements and identical words with entirely different outcomes. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"A man may finish his prayer and have recorded for him only a tenth of it, a ninth, an eighth, a seventh, a sixth, a fifth, a quarter, a third, or a half."* (Abū Dāwūd 796)

This is a sobering measurement, and it is also liberating, because it redirects effort from quantity toward quality. Practical means of improving presence are well established in the tradition: praying at the beginning of the time rather than at its edge; understanding the meaning of what is recited; a slow, unhurried pace, since the Prophet ﷺ rebuked hasty prayer and told a man who prayed quickly, *"Go back and pray, for you have not prayed"* (al-Bukhārī 757; Muslim 397); the removal of distractions before starting; and treating the words as address rather than recitation.

**Remembrance in a Distracted Age**

The specific difficulty of this verse in the present era deserves naming. The obstacle to remembrance today is rarely hostility to religion; it is saturation. The average person's attention is contested continuously by devices engineered by skilled professionals to capture it. Silence, in which remembrance naturally arises, has become something one must deliberately construct.

This makes the fixed structure of the five prayers more valuable rather than less. Precisely because they arrive at set times regardless of mood or schedule, they interrupt the flow of distraction five times a day. A person who observes them cannot go more than a few hours without being pulled out of the stream and set facing a different direction. The rhythm is defensive architecture built into the day.

The portable forms of remembrance matter here too, and their brevity is the point. *Subḥāna Llāhi wa bi-ḥamdih* takes three seconds. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Whoever says 'Glory be to God and praise be to Him' one hundred times a day will have his sins forgiven even if they were like the foam of the sea."* (al-Bukhārī 6405; Muslim 2691)

These formulas were designed for the intervals of ordinary work — the walk, the queue, the commute — which is exactly the territory now occupied by the phone.

**An Analogy: The Compass Check**

A hiker crossing open terrain does not check his compass once at the start and then walk for eight hours. Small unnoticed deviations accumulate; a drift of a few degrees becomes miles of error by evening. So he checks repeatedly — briefly, at intervals — and each check costs seconds and corrects everything downstream.

*Dhikr* is the compass check, and the five prayers are its scheduled form. The person who thinks he has settled the question of direction once and can now proceed unmonitored has misunderstood how drift works. Nobody drifts deliberately. That is what makes the correction necessary.

**Living the Verse**

The verse supplies a programme rather than an ideal. Purify something specific. Remember God verbally at fixed points in the day — waking, eating, leaving the house, sleeping — using the short formulas the Prophet ﷺ taught for those exact moments. And pray, on time, with attention, treating the appointment as the immovable object in the schedule rather than the flexible one.

The sūrah has now defined success in three verbs, and none of them requires wealth, status, health, or leisure. That is the mercy of this definition: it is universally achievable. Which makes the diagnosis in the next verse — that most people nonetheless choose otherwise — all the more pointed.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:16

> **Nay, but you prefer the life of this world,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis Delivered Face to Face**

The sūrah has just laid out a programme of success so accessible that no one could plead inability: purify, remember, pray. Then comes the confrontation. *Bal tuʾthirūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā* — "Nay, but you prefer the life of this world."

Two features of this verse make it land harder than anything before it.

First, the particle *bal*. In Arabic it is the particle of retraction and correction — "rather," "no, but," "on the contrary." It sets aside what preceded and substitutes the truth. Here it functions as a turn from what people *should* do to what they *actually* do. The programme has been described; now the real behaviour is named.

Second, the grammatical person changes. The entire sūrah until now has spoken in the third person about the one who fears and the one who avoids. Suddenly the address becomes second person plural: *tuʾthirūna* — "you prefer." The listener who has been comfortably observing other people's failures is abruptly included in the sentence.

**Who Is Being Addressed?**

The commentators note a difference of view. Some held that the address is to the disbelievers specifically, since they are the subject of the preceding verses. Others held that it addresses all of humanity, believers included.

The second view is the more penetrating one and is well supported by the sūrah's structure. The verse follows immediately after a description of the successful person — purified, remembering, praying — and it names the reason most people fall short of that description. That reason is not applicable only to unbelievers. Anyone who has intended to pray and postponed it for something more urgent, or intended to give and delayed until the impulse cooled, has performed the act named in this verse.

There is also a recognized recitation of the verse in the third person — *yuʾthirūna*, "they prefer" — which some early readers transmitted. The variation does not change the substance; it only shifts the angle of address.

**Tuʾthirūna: The Verb of Preference and Its Surprising Range**

The verb *āthara*, from the root *ʾ-th-r*, means to prefer one thing over another, to give something priority. The related noun *īthār* has an interesting double life in Arabic and in Islamic ethics. Applied to one's own interests, it denotes selfishness — preferring oneself. Applied to others, it denotes the highest form of generosity: preferring another over oneself.

The Qur'an uses the noble sense in its famous praise of the Anṣār of Madinah:

> *"And they give preference (yuʾthirūna) over themselves, even though they are in privation."* (Qur'an 59:9)

The same verb, therefore, describes both the summit of virtue and the root of failure. The difference lies entirely in what is preferred over what. This is the moral drama of a human life compressed into a single verb: everyone practises *īthār* constantly; the question is only in which direction.

Note also that the verb is not "you disbelieve in the Hereafter" or "you are ignorant of it." Preference presupposes comparison, and comparison presupposes knowledge of both options. The verse describes people who know there is something better and choose the nearer thing anyway. That is a far more accurate description of most religious failure than ignorance is. Very few people abandon prayer because they have concluded that God does not exist. They abandon it because something more immediate was in front of them at the time.

**Al-Ḥayāt al-Dunyā: The Nearer, Lower Life**

The Qur'anic term for this world, *al-dunyā*, is itself an argument. It is the feminine superlative of the root *d-n-w*, which yields two related meanings: nearness and lowness. *Adnā* means both "nearer" and "lower."

So *al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* means simultaneously "the nearer life" and "the lower life," and the Qur'an exploits both senses. It is nearer — immediate, visible, tangible, available now. And it is lower — of lesser value, positioned beneath the life that follows.

That single word contains the whole explanation of human behaviour that this verse describes. People prefer the *dunyā* precisely because it is *dunyā* in the first sense: near. Its rewards arrive today. The Hereafter, whatever its magnitude, arrives later.

The Qur'an names this preference for the immediate elsewhere with equal directness:

> *"No! But you love the immediate and leave the Hereafter."* (Qur'an 75:20–21)

The word there is *al-ʿājilah*, "the hastening one," the thing that comes quickly. The diagnosis is identical: it is not that people reject the greater good, but that they discount it because it is delayed.

**Why the Immediate Wins: The Mechanics of the Error**

This verse describes something that is now among the best-documented features of human decision-making, though the Qur'an stated it fourteen centuries before it was measured: human beings systematically overvalue immediate rewards and undervalue delayed ones, even when they know the delayed reward is larger.

Everyone recognizes the pattern from ordinary life. A person knows that regular exercise extends and improves life, and chooses the sofa. A smoker knows the statistics and lights another. A student knows the exam is in three weeks and watches one more episode. In each case the knowledge is not defective. The preference is.

Several features of the immediate give it this unfair advantage.

*It is concrete.* The pleasure available now can be seen, tasted, and felt. The reward promised later is described in words. The nervous system responds to the first far more strongly than to the second.

*It is certain.* Whatever is in hand cannot be lost by waiting. Delayed rewards feel probabilistic, even when the promise is from God — which is why the Qur'an so frequently reinforces the *certainty* of the Hereafter rather than merely its desirability.

*It requires no effort of imagination.* Preferring the Hereafter requires holding in mind something one has never seen. That is cognitively expensive, and human beings economize.

Understanding the mechanism is useful, because it identifies the remedy. If the problem is that the delayed good is abstract and distant, then the solution is to make it vivid and near — which is precisely what the Qur'an does by describing the Garden and the Fire in physical detail, and precisely what the Prophet ﷺ did by instructing frequent remembrance of death. These are not scare tactics; they are corrections to a known bias in human perception.

**Not a Condemnation of the World Itself**

It is essential to state what this verse does not say. It does not say the world is evil, that possessions are forbidden, or that enjoyment is sinful. The verb is *tuʾthirūna* — "you *prefer*." The error is one of ranking, not of use.

Islam has never taught world-renunciation of the monastic type, and the Qur'an rejects it explicitly:

> *"But monasticism they invented — We did not prescribe it for them."* (Qur'an 57:27)

> *"Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of God which He has produced for His servants, and the good things of provision?"* (Qur'an 7:32)

> *"And seek, through that which God has given you, the home of the Hereafter; and do not forget your share of the world."* (Qur'an 28:77)

That last verse is decisive. *Do not forget your share of the world* — spoken as instruction, in the same breath as the command to seek the Hereafter. The two are ordered, not opposed.

The Prophet ﷺ was married, traded, ate well when food was available, enjoyed perfume, raced with his wife, and prayed for worldly good as well as the good of the Hereafter — the most frequently repeated of all his supplications being:

> *"Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire."* (al-Bukhārī 6389; Muslim 2690, citing Qur'an 2:201)

He also said plainly:

> *"There is no harm in wealth for the one who is conscious of God."* (Ibn Mājah 2141)

And, in the clearest statement of the correct ordering:

> *"Whoever's concern is the Hereafter, God places contentment in his heart, gathers his affairs for him, and the world comes to him despite its reluctance. And whoever's concern is the world, God places his poverty before his eyes, scatters his affairs, and nothing of the world comes to him except what has been decreed for him."* (al-Tirmidhī 2465; Ibn Mājah 4105)

Notice the practical claim being made: right ordering does not cost a person the world; it delivers it more reliably, and without the anxiety.

**The World as Prison and Garden**

The source material cites the ḥadīth that captures the relative valuation:

> *"The world is the prison of the believer and the paradise of the disbeliever."* (Muslim 2956)

The statement requires careful reading. It does not mean the believer must be miserable. It means that whatever the believer experiences here, however good, is confined relative to what awaits him — as a comfortable cell is still a cell to someone who knows what lies outside. And whatever the disbeliever experiences here, however good, is the maximum he will receive.

The Prophet ﷺ illustrated the scale with an image of arresting simplicity:

> *"By God, this world in comparison to the Hereafter is nothing but as though one of you dipped his finger in the sea — let him consider what he brings back."* (Muslim 2858)

A drop clinging to a fingertip, against the whole ocean. That is the ratio the verse is asking people to keep in mind when they choose.

And in a still sharper image of relative worth:

> *The Prophet ﷺ passed by a dead lamb with deformed ears and said: "Which of you would like to have this for a dirham?" They said: "We would not want it for anything." He said: "By God, the world is more insignificant to God than this is to you."* (Muslim 2957)

**Detachment of the Heart, Not of the Hand**

The classical formulation of the balance, articulated by many scholars, is that *zuhd* — detachment — is a state of the heart rather than an absence of possessions. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is reported to have expressed it as not grieving over what one has lost and not exulting over what one has gained, which is close to the Qur'anic phrasing:

> *"...so that you may not despair over what has eluded you, nor exult over what He has given you."* (Qur'an 57:23)

Sulaymān was a king with unmatched wealth, and Yūsuf administered the treasury of Egypt; both are prophets. Wealth in the hand is not the issue. Wealth in the heart — occupying the place where God should be — is.

**Practical Correction of the Ranking**

If the disease is misordered preference, the treatment is a set of practices that adjust the ranking, and they are all ordinary.

*Test the ranking with the calendar.* An honest account of where a week's hours and a month's money actually went is a more accurate statement of one's priorities than any declaration of belief.

*Attach the delayed good to the immediate act.* The Prophet ﷺ constantly reframed daily acts as investments in the Hereafter — a smile as charity, removing an obstacle from a path as a branch of faith, feeding one's family as reward. This closes the gap between the immediate and the delayed by making the immediate act itself the delayed investment.

*Make the Hereafter concrete.* Visiting graves, attending funerals, reading the descriptions in the Qur'an slowly, and sitting with the dying all convert an abstraction into something the imagination can hold.

*Practise small delays.* Fasting is the classic training instrument, because it rehearses the exact skill the verse requires: declining an available, permitted, immediate good for a delayed one. A person who can leave a glass of water on a hot afternoon has trained the faculty that this verse says most people lack.

**An Analogy: The Advance and the Salary**

Consider an employee offered a choice: a hundred units of currency handed over immediately, or ten thousand deposited in one year. Nobody deliberating calmly takes the hundred. Yet if the hundred is placed physically on the table and the ten thousand exists only as a written undertaking, a surprising number of people reach for the cash — and every one of them can explain afterward why they needed it at the time.

The verse is describing exactly that reach. It is not describing a considered philosophical position that the world is better than the Hereafter. Nobody holds that view. It is describing a hand moving toward what is on the table.

The next verse supplies the number the hand is ignoring.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:17

> **while the Hereafter is better and more enduring.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Correction Stated as Two Facts**

Having named the error, the sūrah supplies the correction — not as an exhortation but as a statement of fact: *wa al-ākhiratu khayrun wa abqā*, "and the Hereafter is better and more lasting."

Two claims, joined by a single conjunction. The Hereafter is superior in *quality* — *khayr*, better. And it is superior in *duration* — *abqā*, more enduring. Between them these two words exhaust the dimensions along which any good can be evaluated. Whatever the world offers can be assessed by how good it is and by how long it lasts, and on both measures the verse asserts a decisive verdict.

**Khayrun: Better in Kind**

*Khayr* is the general Arabic word for good, and in this comparative construction it means "better." The comparison is not close.

The Qur'an details the qualitative difference throughout, and its descriptions consistently address the specific defects of worldly pleasure rather than merely amplifying its quantities.

Worldly pleasure is mixed with pain; the Hereafter's is not:

> *"No fatigue will touch them therein, nor from it will they ever be removed."* (Qur'an 15:48)

Worldly satisfaction is diluted by anxiety about its loss; the Hereafter's is secure:

> *"They will not taste death therein except the first death, and He will have protected them from the punishment of Hellfire."* (Qur'an 44:56)

Worldly relationships are damaged by resentment and rivalry; those of the Hereafter are cleansed:

> *"And We will remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment, brothers, on thrones facing each other."* (Qur'an 15:47)

Worldly pleasure diminishes with repetition; the Hereafter's does not weary:

> *"Abiding eternally therein, they will desire no transfer from it."* (Qur'an 18:108)

And above all the Qur'an names a good that has no earthly counterpart at all:

> *"For those who have done good is the best reward and extra."* (Qur'an 10:26)

The Prophet ﷺ explained *"the extra"* (*ziyādah*) in that verse as the vision of God's Face (Muslim 181) — and the tradition treats this, not the gardens or rivers, as the summit of the Hereafter's superiority. The pleasures are the setting; the meeting is the point.

The scale of the qualitative difference is stated in a ḥadīth qudsī:

> *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never occurred to a human heart."* (al-Bukhārī 3244; Muslim 2824)

That last clause is the significant one. It concedes the limits of description. Whatever a person imagines when told about Paradise is by definition insufficient, because the raw material of his imagination is drawn entirely from a world that does not contain anything comparable.

**Abqā: More Enduring**

*Abqā* is the comparative of *bāqin*, from the root *b-q-y*, meaning to remain, to persist, to be left over. From this root come *bāqiyāt* (things that remain) and *baqāʾ* (permanence), the classical theological term contrasted with *fanāʾ* (perishing).

This is the argument that requires no faith in the unseen to appreciate. Even a person uncertain about the Hereafter can evaluate the durability of what he has chosen instead — and the sūrah has already given him the image. Verse 5 showed the pasture turned into blackened stubble. Whatever a person accumulates here is on the schedule described in that verse.

The Qur'an drives the contrast repeatedly, and the phrasing is worth noticing: it usually pairs quality with duration exactly as our verse does.

> *"And whatever you have will end, but what God has is lasting."* (Qur'an 16:96)

> *"Whatever you have been given is but the enjoyment of worldly life, and what is with God is better and more lasting for those who believe and rely upon their Lord."* (Qur'an 42:36)

> *"But the enduring good deeds (al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt) are better with thy Lord for reward and better for hope."* (Qur'an 18:46; also 19:76)

> *"But you prefer the worldly life, while the Hereafter is better and more enduring."* — our verse, and its near-twin at 93:4: *"And the Hereafter is better for thee than the first."*

The phrase *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* — "the enduring righteous deeds" — is particularly instructive. It names a category of action that survives the transition. The Prophet ﷺ identified specific members of this category:

> *"When a human being dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: an ongoing charity, knowledge from which benefit is derived, and a righteous child who prays for him."* (Muslim 1631)

This is the practical answer to the question the verse implies. If the durable is better, what can a person actually build that is durable? Three things: something that keeps serving people after him, something true that he taught, and a person he raised well.

**Everyone Already Believes the Logic of This Verse**

An important observation: nobody actually disputes the principle the verse states. Every human being already prefers the better and more lasting good whenever both are visible.

This is why people save for retirement, endure years of training for a career, undergo painful surgery for later health, and study for examinations instead of sleeping. Every one of these is the same trade the verse asks for: present cost, future benefit. The capacity is universal.

The failure, then, is not a failure of principle but of perception. People make this trade readily when the future benefit is visible to them and reluctantly when it is not. Which means the religious task is not to install a new decision rule in a person's mind — the rule is already there — but to make the delayed good real enough to trigger it.

Ibn al-Qayyim and others noted that this is precisely the function of the Qur'an's detailed descriptions of the next life. They are not decoration. They are the mechanism by which an abstraction becomes vivid enough to compete with what is on the table.

**The Comparison Is Not Between Nothing and Something**

There is a version of religious rhetoric that presents the choice as one between deprivation now and reward later. The Qur'an does not present it that way, and the verse's own wording resists it.

The believer is not asked to have a worse life now in exchange for a better one later. He is promised a good life in both:

> *"Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while he is a believer — We will surely cause him to live a good life, and We will surely give them their reward according to the best of what they used to do."* (Qur'an 16:97)

*Ḥayātan ṭayyibah* — a good life, here. The tradition understood this as contentment, sufficiency, the removal of the anxiety that comes from having staked everything on what cannot be secured. This is not a metaphor. A person whose primary investment is in something no thief can steal, no market can devalue, and no illness can take from him is measurably less frightened than a person whose entire net worth is exposed to circumstance.

The Prophet ﷺ described this directly:

> *"Whoever among you wakes up secure in his dwelling, healthy in his body, having his food for the day — it is as though he has been given the whole world."* (al-Tirmidhī 2346; Ibn Mājah 4141)

And:

> *"Richness is not in having many possessions; richness is the richness of the soul."* (al-Bukhārī 6446; Muslim 1051)

So the trade is not sorrow now for joy later. It is anxiety now for contentment now, plus permanence later.

**What the Comparison Does to Suffering**

For those whose portion in this world has been genuinely hard — the chronically ill, the bereaved, the poor, the oppressed — this verse functions differently than it does for the comfortable. To them it is not a warning but a promise, and the tradition has always treated it as such.

The Prophet ﷺ described the moment of arrival:

> *"The most affluent person of this world, from among the people of the Fire, will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and dipped once in the Fire. Then it will be said: O son of Adam, did you ever see any good? Did you ever have any pleasure? He will say: No, by God, my Lord. And the most miserable person of this world, from among the people of Paradise, will be brought and dipped once in Paradise. Then it will be said: O son of Adam, did you ever see any hardship? Did you ever have any distress? He will say: No, by God, my Lord — no hardship ever passed over me, and I have never seen any distress."* (Muslim 2807)

A single immersion erases a lifetime of memory in both directions. That is the practical meaning of *khayrun wa abqā* for a person whose life has been mostly difficulty: not that the suffering is denied, but that its proportion to what follows is such that it will not survive the comparison.

**What the Comparison Does to Ambition**

The verse is often read as a brake on ambition. Read carefully, it is a redirection of it.

The person who has internalized that the lasting is better does not become passive; he becomes strategic. He still builds, earns, studies, and competes — but he selects projects by a different criterion. He asks of an opportunity not only what it pays but what remains of it. This is why the great institutions of Islamic civilization — the endowed hospitals, the water fountains, the libraries, the schools maintained for centuries by *waqf* — were built by people who took this verse literally. They were not renouncing the world; they were investing in the only part of it that carries over.

**An Analogy: Two Currencies at the Border**

Imagine a traveller in a country he is certain to leave within a fixed period, holding local currency that cannot be exchanged at the border. He has two options with his earnings: spend them on immediate comforts, or convert them, at an available exchange, into the currency of the country he is going to.

Everything about his behaviour will depend on whether he genuinely believes he is leaving. A traveller who half-believes it will convert a token amount and spend the rest. One who is certain will convert as much as he can, keeping only what he needs for the remaining stay.

The exchange desk in this analogy is charity, worship, learning, and service — the mechanisms by which the currency of this world is converted into the currency of the next. The Prophet ﷺ made the metaphor almost explicit when his family slaughtered a sheep, gave nearly all of it away, and ʿĀʾishah reported that only the shoulder remained. He said:

> *"All of it remains except its shoulder."* (al-Tirmidhī 2470)

What was given away was the part that remained; what was kept was the part that was lost. That single sentence is the whole of verse 17 rendered into a household example.

**Living the Verse**

The verse asks for one concrete act of ranking. Take a decision currently pending — how to spend a sum of money, an evening, or a year — and evaluate the options by the verse's two criteria rather than by the usual ones: which is better in kind, and which lasts longer?

Most people find that applying this test even once changes an answer they thought was settled. That is the verse doing its work. It does not ask anyone to hate the world. It asks them to do the arithmetic honestly, once, and then act on the result.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:18

> **Truly this is in the scriptures of old,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Closing Argument From Antiquity**

The sūrah ends with an appeal that changes the register entirely. Having warned, promised, diagnosed, and corrected, it now says: *inna hādhā la-fī al-ṣuḥufi al-ūlā* — "Indeed, this is in the earlier scriptures."

The rhetorical move is significant. One of the standing accusations against the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah was novelty — that he had invented a religion, broken with ancestral tradition, and introduced something unheard of. The Qur'an answers not by claiming originality but by denying it. The message is not new. It is the oldest thing there is.

The verse also carries emphasis in its grammar: *inna* at the head of the sentence, and the emphatic *la-* prefixed to the prepositional phrase. Both are markers of assertion in Arabic — the equivalent of "indeed this most certainly is." The Qur'an is not making a tentative historical suggestion.

**What Does "This" Refer To?**

The demonstrative *hādhā* — "this" — is unspecified, and the commentators identified three levels of reference.

*The immediately preceding subjects*, namely the teaching of verses 14–17: that success lies in purification, remembrance, and prayer, and that the Hereafter is better and more lasting. This is the reading attributed to al-Ṭūsī and others, and it has the merit of proximity.

*The entire sūrah.* Al-Ṭabarī records this view: everything from the command to glorify through the description of the two destinies stood already in the older revelations.

*The whole Qur'an.* Al-Ālūsī notes this widest reading, supported by the Qur'an's own statement elsewhere:

> *"And indeed, it is in the scriptures of former peoples."* (Qur'an 26:196)

All three readings converge on a single claim, differing only in scope: the core of what is being taught here is continuous with what was taught before.

**Ṣuḥuf: The Word for Written Sheets**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet, page, or written document — from the root *ṣ-ḥ-f*, which also gives *muṣḥaf*, the term Muslims use for the physical bound copy of the Qur'an.

The word denotes written material rather than oral tradition, which is significant for the verse's argument. The claim is not merely that similar ideas circulated in antiquity but that they were inscribed in revealed documents.

The Qur'an uses *ṣuḥuf* for revealed scriptures generally:

> *"A messenger from God, reciting purified scriptures (ṣuḥufan muṭahharah)."* (Qur'an 98:2)

And it uses the same word for the records of human deeds that will be distributed on the Day of Judgment:

> *"And when the pages (al-ṣuḥuf) are spread out."* (Qur'an 81:10)

The double usage is quietly appropriate here: the scriptures record what God said, and the scrolls record what we did. Both are read out.

*Al-ūlā* means "the first," "the earliest," the feminine form agreeing with *ṣuḥuf*. The reference is to revelations preceding the Qur'an.

**The Qur'anic Doctrine of Continuous Revelation**

This verse is a compact statement of one of Islam's most distinctive doctrines: that God has been sending the same essential message to humanity from the beginning, through a long succession of messengers, and that the Qur'an is the final instalment rather than the first.

The Qur'an states the universality of prophecy explicitly:

> *"And there was no nation but that a warner had passed among it."* (Qur'an 35:24)

> *"And for every nation is a messenger."* (Qur'an 10:47)

> *"And We certainly sent into every nation a messenger, saying: Worship God and avoid false gods."* (Qur'an 16:36)

It states the identity of the core message:

> *"And We sent not before thee any messenger except that We revealed to him that there is no deity except Me, so worship Me."* (Qur'an 21:25)

It requires belief in the earlier prophets as an article of faith rather than as a courtesy:

> *"Say: We have believed in God and in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes, and in what was given to Moses and Jesus and what was given to the prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them, and we are Muslims in submission to Him."* (Qur'an 2:136)

> *"The Messenger has believed in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and so have the believers. All of them have believed in God, His angels, His books, and His messengers: We make no distinction between any of His messengers."* (Qur'an 2:285)

And it places the Prophet ﷺ in a line rather than at a beginning:

> *"Say: I am not something original among the messengers."* (Qur'an 46:9)

The Prophet ﷺ gave the classic image of his position in that sequence:

> *"My example and the example of the prophets before me is like a man who built a house and made it excellent and beautiful, except for the place of one brick in a corner. People would go around it and marvel at it, and say: If only this brick were put in place! I am that brick, and I am the seal of the prophets."* (al-Bukhārī 3535; Muslim 2286)

**Why This Doctrine Matters Theologically**

The claim of continuity is not merely a historical footnote. Several consequences follow from it, and each answers a serious question.

*It vindicates divine justice.* If salvation depended on receiving one particular message at one particular moment in history, then the overwhelming majority of human beings who lived before it, or beyond its reach, would have been condemned by geography and chronology. The Qur'anic doctrine removes this problem entirely: every nation received a warner, and *"We never punish until We have sent a messenger"* (17:15).

*It establishes the consistency of God.* A God who taught contradictory things to different peoples would be either capricious or evolving. The Qur'an's claim is that the essential message — one God, accountability, purification, worship — has never changed, and that what varied was the specific law suited to each community's circumstances:

> *"To each of you We prescribed a law and a method. Had God willed, He would have made you one nation, but He intended to test you in what He has given you."* (Qur'an 5:48)

*It provides a criterion for evaluating religious claims.* If the core has always been the same, then a teaching that contradicts it — that multiplies deities, denies accountability, or dispenses with moral purification — is by that fact identifiable as a deviation from the original rather than a legitimate variant.

*It relocates the argument about the Qur'an's origin.* The Makkans could accuse the Prophet ﷺ of many things, but they could not plausibly accuse an unlettered man in an isolated trading town of having researched the content of Abrahamic and Mosaic scripture. The Qur'an makes this point elsewhere: *"And thou didst not recite before it any scripture, nor didst thou inscribe one with thy right hand"* (29:48).

**Where the Continuity Is Visible**

The claim of verse 18 is testable to a degree, and the correspondences are real.

The prohibition of idolatry, the command to honour parents, the prohibitions of murder, theft, adultery, and false witness, the obligation of charity, the institution of prayer, the fast, and the affirmation of a final accounting — these appear across the scriptural traditions descended from the prophets named here.

The Qur'an itself notes specific parallels. Of the Torah:

> *"And We ordained for them therein a life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth, and for wounds is legal retribution. But whoever gives it up as charity, it is an expiation for him."* (Qur'an 5:45)

Of the Psalms:

> *"And We have already written in the Psalms after the Reminder that the land is inherited by My righteous servants."* (Qur'an 21:105)

Of the Gospel and the Torah together, in describing the believers:

> *"That is their description in the Torah. And their description in the Gospel is as a plant which produces its shoot and strengthens it, so it grows firm and stands upon its stalk, delighting the sowers."* (Qur'an 48:29)

At the same time, the Qur'an maintains that the earlier texts as transmitted have suffered alteration and loss:

> *"So woe to those who write the Scripture with their own hands, then say: This is from God, in order to exchange it for a small price."* (Qur'an 2:79)

> *"They distort words from their places and have forgotten a portion of what they were reminded of."* (Qur'an 5:13)

This is why the Qur'an describes itself as *muhaymin* — a guardian and criterion over what came before:

> *"And We have revealed to thee the Book in truth, confirming that which preceded it of the Scripture and as a criterion over it."* (Qur'an 5:48)

The position is therefore neither wholesale endorsement nor wholesale rejection of prior scripture, but a claim to be the authenticated version of a shared original.

**The Argument From Universality**

There is a further argument implicit in this verse that deserves drawing out, because it is genuinely persuasive.

If a moral teaching appears in one culture only, it is reasonable to suspect that it reflects that culture's particular circumstances. If the same teaching appears independently across widely separated peoples and eras — prohibitions on murder and theft, obligations of honesty and generosity, reverence for a transcendent reality, a conviction that conduct matters beyond death — then either human nature converges on it, or it was placed there.

The Qur'an's answer is that both are true, and that they are the same fact: the teaching corresponds to human nature because the One who made human nature also sent the teaching. This is the doctrine of *fiṭrah* meeting the doctrine of continuous revelation. The reason the message rings true in verse 10 — *"he who fears will remember"* — is the reason it appears in the ancient scriptures in verse 18. It was never foreign.

**Practical Consequences for How Muslims Relate to Others**

This verse and the one after it have shaped Muslim engagement with other religious communities in concrete ways.

They generate a presumption of respect toward the prophets and scriptures of others rather than dismissal. Muslims are required to honour Moses and Jesus, and insulting them is a violation of Islamic belief, not merely of manners.

They provide common ground for dialogue, which the Qur'an itself proposes:

> *"Say: O People of the Scripture, come to a word that is equitable between us and you — that we will not worship except God and not associate anything with Him and not take one another as lords instead of God."* (Qur'an 3:64)

And they impose intellectual humility on Muslims themselves. A community that believes it inherited an ancient message rather than invented a new one has less warrant for arrogance toward those who inherited earlier portions of the same deposit.

**An Analogy: The Restored Original**

Consider a text composed by a single author and copied by hand across centuries. Different regional copies accumulate different errors — a line dropped here, a marginal note absorbed into the body there, a passage rewritten by a later editor who found it difficult. Each copy still contains a great deal of the original, and their agreements are strong evidence of a common source. Then a definitive edition is issued, which confirms the copies where they preserve the original and corrects them where they drifted.

That is precisely the relationship the Qur'an claims for itself in relation to earlier scripture, and verse 18 states the premise on which the claim rests: *this is in the earlier scriptures*. The next verse identifies two of them by name.

## Sūrah al-Aʿlā 87:19

> **the scriptures of Abraham and Moses.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naming the Witnesses**

The final verse of the sūrah does not introduce a new subject; it identifies precisely what the previous verse asserted in general terms. *Ṣuḥufi Ibrāhīma wa Mūsā* — "the scriptures of Abraham and Moses."

A general claim about ancient revelation could be dismissed as vague. By naming two figures whose authority the Prophet's audience — including the Jews and Christians of Arabia and the Arabs who claimed descent from Abraham — could not dispute, the Qur'an makes the claim specific and checkable in principle.

Ending a chapter on this note is also a deliberate act of closure. The sūrah began with a command to glorify the Most High; it ends by placing that command in a lineage stretching back to the earliest revealed books. The reader is left not with novelty but with continuity.

**Why Abraham and Moses in Particular**

The choice of these two names is not arbitrary. Several reasons converge.

*Abraham is the acknowledged ancestor and the model of pure monotheism.* The Makkans traced their lineage to him through Ishmael, revered the Kaʿbah as his construction, and considered themselves the custodians of his legacy — even as they filled his sanctuary with idols. The Qur'an makes constant and pointed use of this claim:

> *"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was one inclining toward truth, a Muslim, and he was not of the polytheists."* (Qur'an 3:67)

> *"And who is better in religion than one who submits himself to God while being a doer of good and follows the way of Abraham, inclining toward truth? And God took Abraham as an intimate friend."* (Qur'an 4:125)

Naming Abraham at the close of a sūrah about glorifying the One God, purifying oneself, and preferring the Hereafter is therefore an argument aimed directly at the audience's self-understanding: *this is your own ancestor's religion, and you have departed from it.*

*Moses is the archetype of law and liberation.* He received the most detailed body of revealed legislation before the Qur'an, confronted the greatest tyrant in the Qur'anic narrative, and led a community out of bondage. He is mentioned more frequently by name in the Qur'an than any other prophet.

*Together they represent the two poles of prophetic mission.* Abraham represents the founding of monotheism against a pagan environment; Moses represents the establishment of a law and a community. Between them they cover the whole scope of what the Prophet ﷺ was doing in Makkah and would do in Madinah.

*And both are named elsewhere as the two great precedents.* The Qur'an pairs them in a passage that closely parallels ours:

> *"Or has he not been informed of what is in the scriptures of Moses and of Abraham, who fulfilled his obligations — that no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another, and that there is not for man except that for which he strives, and that his effort is going to be seen, then he will be recompensed for it with the fullest recompense, and that to thy Lord is the finality."* (Qur'an 53:36–42)

This passage is the single most valuable cross-reference for our verse, because it actually specifies contents of the earlier scriptures. And what it lists is instructive: personal responsibility, the reality of effort, the certainty of accounting, and the finality of return to God. These are precisely the themes of Sūrah al-Aʿlā. The passage continues with a series of divine attributes — that He causes laughter and weeping, death and life, creates the pairs, and gives wealth — echoing the same creation-and-decree sequence found in verses 2–5 of our sūrah.

The correspondence is close enough to constitute an internal Qur'anic commentary on what verse 19 means.

**The Scriptures of Abraham: A Book Not Preserved**

An honest treatment must acknowledge a fact the source material raises: Judaism and Christianity do not ascribe a revealed book to Abraham. The Torah presents him as a recipient of covenant and promise, not as the bearer of a scripture.

The Qur'an nonetheless affirms that Abraham had one, and it does so consistently — at 53:36–37 and here. The tradition supplements this with a report about the number of revealed books, which the source cites as transmitted by al-Ālūsī:

> *When the Prophet ﷺ was asked how many revealed books there were, he responded: "One hundred and four books. He sent down fifty scrolls to Seth, thirty scrolls to Idrīs, ten scrolls to Abraham, and ten scrolls to Moses before the Torah. And He sent down the Torah, the Gospel, the Psalms, and the Criterion."*

This report is transmitted in the well-known long ḥadīth of Abū Dharr on the number of prophets and scriptures, and it is important to note that scholars of ḥadīth have criticized its chain of transmission; it is generally classified as weak. It appears in the commentary literature as an explanatory tradition rather than as an evidentiary proof. Shīʿī sources transmit a variant, and some reports give the number of Abraham's scrolls as twenty rather than ten.

The Qur'anic affirmation does not depend on that report. What the Qur'an affirms is simply that Abraham received revelation in written form, and this is entirely consistent with its broader position that many prophets and scriptures existed of which no detailed record survives:

> *"And messengers We have already mentioned to thee before, and messengers We have not mentioned to thee."* (Qur'an 4:164)

The absence of Abraham's scrolls from later tradition is, in the Qur'anic framework, unremarkable. Texts are lost. What is remarkable, and what the verse asserts, is that their content survives — because it was re-sent.

**What Survives Is the Message, Not the Manuscript**

This is the deepest point of the closing verse and worth stating plainly.

The scriptures of Abraham are gone. The original text given to Moses is not available in the form he received it. Yet the sūrah says *this is in the scriptures of Abraham and Moses* — present tense, as a live claim. What is being asserted is the survival of content, not of parchment.

The implication is that revelation is not fundamentally a documentary phenomenon. God does not preserve pieces of writing; He preserves a message, and He re-sends it whenever it has been sufficiently obscured. The Qur'an describes exactly this pattern of renewal:

> *"Then We sent Our messengers in succession. Every time there came to a nation its messenger, they denied him, so We made them follow one another in destruction and We made them narrations. So away with a people who do not believe."* (Qur'an 23:44)

The Prophet ﷺ likewise described the renewal of religion within his own community:

> *"God will send to this community, at the head of every hundred years, one who will renew for it its religion."* (Abū Dāwūd 4291)

The same logic operates within the Muslim tradition as operated across the prophetic succession: the message requires periodic restoration, because human beings reliably obscure it.

**The Distinctive Claim of the Qur'an**

The Qur'an makes one exception to the pattern of loss, and it is exactly the promise given earlier in this same sūrah at verse 6:

> *"Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian."* (Qur'an 15:9)

The sūrah's structure is therefore complete in a way that only becomes visible at the last verse. At verse 6 the Prophet ﷺ was promised he would not forget. At verse 19 we are told that earlier scriptures carried this message. Between the two lies an implicit argument: what was given before was lost; what is being given now is guaranteed. The final revelation is the one that does not need replacing, which is why the Prophet ﷺ is the seal of the prophets (33:40).

**The Sūrah's Complete Arc**

Standing at the end, the shape of the whole chapter can be seen.

It opened with a command — *glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Most High* — and grounded it in five acts of the Lord: creation, formation, decree, guidance, and the giving and withdrawing of sustenance.

It reassured the one carrying the message: he would receive it, retain it, and be eased in his task.

It defined his mandate in a single word: remind.

It described the two possible responses — the one who fears and remembers, and the one who avoids — and named their destinations.

It defined success in three plain acts: purify, remember, pray.

It diagnosed the reason most people fail: they prefer what is near.

It corrected the diagnosis with two facts: better, and more lasting.

And it closed by declaring that all of this is old — as old as Abraham, as old as Moses, as old as revelation.

The nineteen verses thus move from God, to the messenger, to the audience, to the two destinies, to the practical programme, to the human obstacle, and finally to history itself as witness. Very little of consequence in the religion is left untouched.

**What the Ending Asks of the Reader**

The closing verse leaves the reader in a specific position. He has been told what to do — glorify, purify, remember, pray, and prefer the enduring over the immediate. And he has been told that these instructions are not a local innovation he may take or leave as a matter of cultural preference, but the standing content of divine communication to the human race since its earliest recorded revelation.

That removes a particular escape route. It is possible to dismiss a novelty. It is much harder to dismiss something that every messenger God ever sent has been saying.

**A Final Analogy: The Message Repeated Down a Long Line**

Imagine a message passed down a line of runners across generations. Each runner delivers it to the next community; each community holds it for a time, and over time the wording drifts, portions are dropped, and local additions creep in. Periodically the sender dispatches a fresh runner with the original text to correct the accumulated changes.

The last runner arrives with the message and adds one thing the others did not: this copy will be kept intact, and no further runner is coming. He also says something that authenticates him — *check the old copies; you will find this is what they said too.*

That is verse 19. The audience is being invited to compare, and the comparison is offered as evidence.

**Living the Verse**

Three things follow for a reader today.

*Honour the line.* Belief in Abraham, Moses, and all the prophets is a requirement of faith, not an ecumenical gesture. A Muslim who speaks disrespectfully of them has contradicted his own creed.

*Recognize the antiquity of the demand.* The instruction to purify oneself, remember God, and prefer the lasting is not a seventh-century Arabian custom. It has been asked of every generation, and it will be asked of ours in the same terms.

*Take the last word of the sūrah as pointing backwards to its first.* The chapter began with *sabbiḥ* — glorify — and ends with the names of two prophets who spent their lives doing precisely that. The instruction and its precedent bracket everything in between. What remains is to join the line.

> *"Say: Indeed, my Lord has guided me to a straight path — a correct religion, the way of Abraham, inclining toward truth. And he was not among those who associated others with God."* (Qur'an 6:161)
