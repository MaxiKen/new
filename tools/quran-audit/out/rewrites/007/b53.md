**The Question of the Waiting**

*Hal yanẓurūna illā taʾwīlahu* — do they wait for anything but its fulfilment? The verb *yanẓurūn* here is not the word for looking but the word for waiting in suspense, and the noun is one of the Qur'an's most interesting technical terms. *Taʾwīl* is the coming-to-be of a statement: the point at which a proclamation stops being a prediction and becomes a fact. Sūrat Yūsuf uses it for the fulfilment of the dream (12:100); Sūrat Yūnus uses it for the fulfilment of a warning that the mockers had asked to be hurried (10:39).

The question is thus a rebuke in the form of an observation. Everyone alive is waiting for something; the sūrah asks what else these particular people could be waiting for, given that the thing they deny is the only thing on the horizon. Sūrat al-Zukhruf puts the same question to the mockers — *“Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise, when they do not even notice?”* (43:66) — and Sūrat al-Anʿām adds the warning that belief on the morning of arrival will be of no use (6:158).

**"Those Who Forgot It Beforehand"**

*Yaqūlu alladhīna nasūhu min qabul* — those who had forgotten it beforehand will say. The verb is the same one used in verse 51, where God says He will forget them as they forgot the meeting of this Day of theirs. The sūrah's argument is now complete and it circles: their forgetting was the offense; God's forgetting was the requital; and the Day itself is the memory that cannot be suppressed. The men who "forgot" were not people with a defective memory. The verse immediately preceding described them as those who had denied the signs they knew — a deliberate forgetting, maintained until the moment it could no longer be maintained.

**The Confession the Sūrah Extracts**

*Laqad jāʾat rusulu rabbinā bi-l-ḥaqq* — the messengers of our Lord certainly came with the truth. This is the fourth time the sūrah has placed a confession in the mouth of the condemned. Verse 37 had the dying say that their patrons had forsaken them; verse 39 had the leaders and followers abolish each other's rank; verse 44 had the population of the Fire answer the Garden's question with a single *yes*; verse 53 has them affirm the truth of the messengers whose coming they spent a lifetime denying.

The Book's insistence on this pattern is pastoral, and the sūrah has made no secret of it. The warnings, the signs, the scales, the conversations across barriers are all in service of the same outcome: the truth will eventually be acknowledged by everyone, and the only question is when. Sūrat al-Naml puts the recognition in the middle of history rather than the end of it — the people of the signs rejected them *“in their hearts though they were convinced of them”* (27:14) — and Sūrat al-Anʿām records the confession of the people of the Fire without any softening: *“If only we were sent back, we would not deny the signs of our Lord”* — followed immediately by the Book's verdict, *“No! They would return to what they were forbidden”* (6:27–28).

**"Are There Any Intercessors Who Can Plead for Us?"**

*Fa-hal lanā min shufaʿāʾa fa-yashfaʿū lanā* — is there anyone to intercede for us? The plea is the one the Qur'an deals with most thoroughly, because it was the central hope of the Makkan religion. Its gods were intercessors; its ancestors were intercessors; the pilgrimage was made in their names. The Book answers the hope on the highest theological level and refuses the version of it the sūrah's audience was banking on.

Intercession in the Qur'an is not a transaction that any creature can arrange. *“Who can intercede with Him except by His permission?”* (2:255). *“None can intercede except by His permission”* (10:3). *“To Him belongs all intercession”* (39:44). *“He knows what is before them and behind them; and they do not intercede except for those whom He approves”* (21:28; cf. 20:109; 53:26). The Book does not deny that intercession exists; it removes it from the possession of the influential and makes it entirely dependent on the will of God. And for those whose whole case was built on it, Sūrat al-Muddaththir records the verdict that ends the hope: *“the intercession of intercessors will be of no benefit to them”* (74:48).

**"Or We Could Be Sent Back"**

*Aw nuraddu fa-naʿmala ghayra alladhī kunnā naʿmal* — or be sent back, so that we may do other than what we did. The request for return is the Book's most repeated final plea: *“Our Lord, let us out so we may do good, unlike what we did”* (35:37); *“My Lord, send me back, so that I may do good in that which I left behind”* (23:99–100); *“If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us”* (2:167); *“Our Lord, we have seen and heard, so send us back, we will do good”* (32:12). Sūrat al-Zumar records the same request in the first person: *“If only I had a second chance, I would have been one of the good-doers”* (39:58) — and the Book states elsewhere the ransom such a speaker would offer: *“Even if the wrongdoers were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves”* (39:47; cf. 13:18; 3:91).

And the Book's refusal is equally consistent, and it is not cruelty. Sūrat al-Anʿām has already given the reason with a precision that makes the plea collapse: if they were returned they would return to exactly what was forbidden them (6:28). The request is not a change of will; it is a change of circumstances. What they want is not to become different but to have another opportunity to be the same with better odds. The sūrah of Yūnus puts the same anatomy of repentance on record: the repentance of the drowning is not counted as faith (10:90–91).

**"They Have Ruined Themselves"**

The verse's last sentence ends the scene with an accounting: *qad khasirū anfusuhum* — they have lost themselves — *wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn* — and what they used to fabricate has failed them. The word *khasira* is the trader's word for losing his capital, and the Book uses it for those whose capital was the self: *“those whose scale is light have lost themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs”* (7:9); *“the losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Judgment”* (39:15). The parable is exact. Investment is what a person does with a life; the profit and loss statement is the balance of the Day; and the people in this verse have lost the principal.

The second half of the sentence is the quieter horror. *Mā kānū yaftarūn* — what they fabricated — covers the idols they carved, the doctrines they invented, the ancestral customs they defended, and the status they built on those foundations. The Book's statement is that none of it will be there. *“Whatever they had fabricated will fail them”* (16:87); *“their partners will fail them, and that was their lie”* (46:28); *“the gods they fabricated will abandon them”* (7:53 here; 41:48). Sūrat al-Anʿām puts the whole thing in six words: *“Whatever they were inventing was lost to them”* (10:30).

The passage that began with a question about what the mockers were waiting for ends with the answer: they were waiting to discover that nothing they had built existed, and that they had spent an entire life on the only available opportunity to build something that did.
