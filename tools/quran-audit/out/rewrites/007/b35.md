**Messengers From Among You**

The verse returns to the address that has organized this whole passage: *O children of Adam.* And this time the subject is the arrival of revelation in human form: *immā yaʾtiyannakum rusulun minkum* — should there come to you messengers from among yourselves. The phrase *min anfusikum*, from your own selves, is the sūrah's standing answer to the Makkans' most persistent objection.

That objection is recorded in the Qur'an's own words in at least four places. The Makkans said, *“What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places?”* (25:7). They said that a messenger should be accompanied by an angel (17:92–93). They asked why the Qur'an had not been given to some great man of influence (43:31). The Book's answer is consistent and this verse is part of it: the messenger is *from among you*, because a revelation addressed to human beings must reach them through a channel that shares their condition. Sūrah Yūsuf states the underlying principle more than once: *“We have not sent before you except men to whom We gave revelation”* (12:109; 16:43; 21:7).

The wisdom is worth spelling out. A messenger who was not one of us could not demonstrate that the life he demands is livable; his purity would be a fact about a different kind of being. A messenger who is one of us can be scrutinized, followed, touched, and copied — and the Qur'an's insistence on the humanness of its messengers is precisely the Islamic answer to every later doctrine that made a mediator of another nature. Islam's Prophet is a man who ate in the marketplace, whose wives and neighbors knew him, and whose conduct has been transmitted with a biographical precision unmatched in the history of religion.

**"Recounting My Signs Unto You"**

The message is described with the verb *yaquṣṣūna*, from the same root as *qiṣṣah* — the step-by-step recounting. Revelation in this verse is not a formula to be accepted but a narrative to be followed: the messengers *recount* the signs, one after another, so that the hearer can see the pattern. It is the same verb the sūrah used in verse 7 for the divine recounting on the Day of Judgment, and the echo is intended: what is recounted here in warning will be recited there in judgment.

**The Two Conditions**

The promise that follows has two conditions: *fa-mani ittaqā wa-aṣlaḥa* — then whoever is reverent and makes amends. The pair deserves to be examined, because it is the sūrah's summary of what is demanded of the hearer of a prophet, and it names the two dimensions of a life that is being renewed.

*Ittaqā* is the verbal form of *taqwā* — the reverent caution that guards against the divine displeasure, the inward disposition the sūrah named two verses ago as the best of all garments (7:26). It is the vertical axis, and it comes first, because the horizontals of religion are parasitic on it.

*Wa-aṣlaḥa* is the form of *iṣlāḥ*, to repair. Its object is deliberately unspecified, and the commentators have drawn the silence out: the person who is reverent repairs whatever in his condition needs repair — the wrong he has done, the harm he has caused, the property he has taken, the relationship he has broken. The Qur'an uses the word for the making of peace between people (49:9–10), for the improvement of one's own conduct (4:128), and in the mouth of Shuʿayb as the banner of his mission: *“I desire naught but reform, so far as I am able”* (11:88).

The order matters. Reverence without repair produces a piety that leaves its damages in place; repair without reverence produces an ethics with no anchor. The verse asks for both, and it puts the fear of God first for the reason the whole sūrah has been demonstrating: a person who does not measure himself against the command will never quite see what needs repairing.

**The Formula of Security**

The verse closes with a phrase that recurs in the Qur'an in almost identical form: *fa-lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn* — no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve. It appears in al-Baqarah for the followers of guidance (2:38), for the believers in the broadest sense (2:62; 5:69), for those who reform (6:48), for the friends of God (10:62), and for those who remain steadfast (46:13). The tradition's commentators found a distinction in its two halves: no fear for what lies ahead, and no grief for what is behind — the two directions in which human anxiety runs, closed by one clause.

The word *khawf* means fear of a coming evil; *ḥuzn* means sorrow for something lost. Between them they cover the whole of what unsettles a human life. And the verse locates their removal not in external security but in the two conditions it has just named. The reverent and repairing person has nothing to fear from the future, because his account is in order, and nothing to grieve over in the past, because what was amiss he has mended. That is the promise held out to the children of Adam at the end of the passage that began with a whisper in a garden.
