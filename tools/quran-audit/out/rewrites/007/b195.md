**"Do They Have Feet to Walk With?"**

*A-lahum arjulun yamshūna bihā am lahum aydin yabṭishūna bihā am lahum aʿyunun yubṣirūna bihā am lahum ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā quli dʿū shurakāʾakum thumma kīdūni fa-lā tunẓirūn* — do they have feet to walk with? Or hands to hold with? Or eyes to see with? Or ears to hear with? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Call upon your associate-gods and conspire against me without delay!"

**The Inventory of the Idols' Bodies**

The verse examines the idols' anatomy and finds nothing: no feet, no hands, no eyes, no ears. The Book's critique of idolatry includes this almost clinical inventory, and its effect is to make the objects of worship visible as objects. The sūrah's readers are invited to picture what stands in the sanctuary and to compare it with what the word divine requires.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Use of the Same Faculties**

The sūrah had used the vocabulary of feet, hands, eyes, and ears in its description of the heedless, who have hearts, eyes, and ears and do not use them (7:179), and in the catalogue of the instruments of knowledge (7:172's testimony). The Book's readers are thus given the human being made with functioning faculties and the idol made with none, and the contrast is the verse's argument.

**The Book's Catalogue of the Idols' Deficiencies**

The Qur'an returns to the theme in its own comparisons: the idols who cannot respond to those who call them (46:5), the gods who cannot protect themselves (21:43), and the challenge to those who claim divine partners to produce them (34:22). The sūrah's inventory belongs to the same dossier, and its specificity gives it a rhetorical force the general statements lack.

**"Call Upon Your Associate-Gods and Conspire Against Me"**

The verse's second half turns the tables: the Prophet is commanded to invite the idolaters to summon their gods against him, with no delay. The Book's confidence in its messenger is stated in the imperative, and the sūrah's readers are shown the contrast between the idols' incapacity and the Prophet's certainty.

**The Messenger's Confidence**

The Qur'an's challenges to its opponents are among its most striking features, and their ground is always the same: the messenger relies on the One who commissioned him. The sūrah's readers are given the invitation to attack in the same passage in which the idols are shown without feet to walk with, and the two statements are meant to be heard together.

**The Book's Polemic and Its Tone**

The verse's tone is not anger but assurance, and the Book's polemics in its Makkan sūrahs frequently sound this way: the argument is made, the challenge is offered, and the messenger continues his work. The sūrah's readers are shown a confidence that rests on God's protection rather than on any human advantage.

**What the Worshippers' Gods Lack**

The inventory's items are not random: walking, grasping, seeing, and hearing are the capacities by which a being engages the world, and the idols have none of them. The sūrah's readers are told that the objects of the cult cannot act, cannot show themselves, and above all cannot hear the prayers addressed to them.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Narratives in the Same Key**

The sūrah's accounts of the towns and of Israel had repeatedly shown communities relying on what could not save them — the calf, the court of Egypt, the numbers of a tribe — and the verse now states the anatomy of that reliance. The Book's narratives and its polemics serve the same teaching, and the sūrah's readers are given both forms.

**The Next Verse's Declaration**

The command to the Prophet to issue the challenge is followed immediately by his own declaration of protection (7:196), and the sequence gives the passage its shape: the idols are dismissed, the challenge is issued, and the ground of the messenger's security is named. The sūrah's readers are shown the answer to the challenge before it is made.

**The Reader's Own Test**

The verse's questions are directed at every reader who has ever placed his confidence in something other than God, and the inventory can be applied to whatever that thing is. The Book's argument is not confined to statues, and the sūrah's closing instructions about remembrance and recitation describe the alternative practice.

**The Passage's Movement**

The sūrah's final treatment of the idols now proceeds from their creation (7:191) through their help (7:192), their response (7:193), their servitude (7:194), and their bodies (7:195), and the next verses repeat and complete the examination. The Book's method in the passage is exhaustive on purpose, and the reader is meant to reach the end of the list without finding a case.

**The Confidence That Replaces Fear**

The sūrah's readers are shown a messenger who can invite conspiracy against himself and then state that his Protector is the One who revealed the Book. The Book's teaching about reliance is thus delivered in the form of a life rather than a proposition, and the passage's closure is a declaration of trust.

The verse's inventory also serves the sūrah's closing contrast, since the next verses name the Protector who hears and acts, and the final verse of the sūrah describes the angels who worship and prostrate. The Book's readers are given three orders of being in a few lines: the idle image, the protected messenger, and the angers who serve — and the passage's argument is complete when the three are compared.
