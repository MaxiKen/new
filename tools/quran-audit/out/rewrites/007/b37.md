**The Two Crimes That Head the List**

*Fa-man aẓlamu mimman iftarā ʿalā Allāh kadhiban aw kadhdhaba bi-āyātihi* — and who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God or denies His signs? The interrogative is rhetorical in form and absolute in force: the question has no answer other than the one the verse has just named. The sūrah has been circling this pair for ten verses. The lie against God was committed by those who said *God has commanded us thus* (7:28) and by those who forbade what God had made lawful (7:32). The denial of the signs was the disdain of verse 36.

The Book repeats this superlative with striking consistency. *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed”* (6:21). *“Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah or claims, ‘I have received revelations!’ — although nothing was revealed to him”* (6:93). *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah? They will be brought before their Lord, and the witnesses will say, ‘These are the ones who lied against their Lord’”* (11:18). *“Who then does more wrong than those who lie about Allah and reject the truth after it has reached them?”* (39:32). The cumulative effect of the repetition is to establish a hierarchy in the Qur'anic understanding of sin: the gravest wrong is not violence but the corruption of the channel through which God is known — either by inventing a message in His name or by refusing the message He sent.

**"Their Portion of the Book Will Reach Them"**

The next clause has been read in two ways by the commentators, and both readings are instructive. *Fa-ulāʾika yanāluhum naṣībuhum min al-kitāb* — those, their portion of the Book will reach them.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record the first reading: the *kitāb* is the decree, the record of what has been written for each soul, and the verse states that whatever share of provision or punishment has been apportioned will be delivered in full — nothing withheld, nothing adrift. The second reading takes *kitāb* as the scripture itself: their portion of what was revealed will reach them, whether they receive it as guidance and reject it, or hear it only as a testimony against them at the reckoning.

The two readings converge on a single idea that the sūrah has been building since its first verses. Nothing owed to a person fails to arrive. The record of verses 6 to 9 — the question, the recounting, the weighing — is a description of an accounting that cannot lose any part of its data. The phrase *will reach them* is the Book's reassurance that the encounter with the truth is not optional and not avoidable: a message that has been delivered is delivered, and the one who walks away from it has not escaped the meeting, only deferred it.

**The Messengers Who Come to Take Them Away**

*Ḥattā idhā jāʾathum rusulunā yatawaffawnahum* — until, when Our messengers come to take them away. The messengers here are the angels of death, and the Qur'an gives their work a vocabulary of precision. Sūrat al-Sajdah names the office: *“Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you; then to your Lord you will be returned”* (32:11). Sūrat al-Anʿām describes the process of the *tawaffī*, the taking of the soul: *“He reigns supreme over all of His creation, and sends recording-angels, watching over you. When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, and they never neglect their duty”* (6:61). And where the death is a punishment, the account is harsher: *“If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and their backs”* (8:50; cf. 47:27).

There is a deep congruence between the sūrah's structure and this verse. Its first scene was a courtroom — the question, the book, the scale. The same court is now shown convening at the bedside of a single person, and the questions are asked in the past tense of a life that has just closed.

**The Question and Its Answer**

*Ayna mā kuntum tadʿūna min dūni Allāh* — where is that which you used to call upon apart from God? The interrogation is the exact fulfillment of what the sūrah warned in verse 30: the patrons who were taken as protectors instead of God are now the objects of a demand. And the answer the dying person gives is the shortest sentence in the verse: *qālū ḍallū ʿannā* — they said, they have forsaken us.

The Book records this scene from other angles, and the additional detail is always the same. *“with Allah? They will cry, ‘They have failed us. In fact, we did not invoke anything real before’”* (40:74). *“Why then did those they took as gods… not help them? Rather, they failed them, and that was their lie”* (46:28). *“Those against whom the decree of torment is justified will cry, ‘Our Lord, these are the ones we misled; we misled them as we ourselves were misled. We now disown them before You’”* (28:63). *“They have taken other gods, instead of Allah, seeking strength and protection. No! They will deny their worship of them, and become opponents to them”* (19:81–82). The consistency of the record matters: at the moment of extraction, no patron answers the call. The theology of *tawḥīd* is not a philosophical preference; it is a description of what a person will discover he had access to when he has finally stopped being able to pretend.

**"They Bear Witness Against Themselves"**

The verse closes with self-condemnation: *wa-shahidū ʿalā anfusihim annahum kānū kāfirīn* — and they bear witness against themselves that they were disbelievers. The courtroom that has stamped the whole sūrah now gets its confession, and it is not extracted by the prosecutor. Sūrat al-Anʿām has the same formula: *“You have certainly come to Us individually as We created you the first time… and they will bear witness against themselves that they were disbelievers”* (6:130; cf. 41:21, where the skin itself testifies). Sūrat al-Qiyāmah puts the principle as a general law of the human constitution: *“In fact, people will testify against their own souls, despite the excuses they come up with”* (75:14–15).

The verse thus ends with the last form of the argument this sūrah has been making since verse 16. Iblīs blamed God; the townspeople of verse 5 confessed too late; the fabricators of verse 28 denied knowing better. This verse shows the door closed on every one of those positions at the hour of death, when the excuses have run out because the memory that produced them is intact and its owner is the one reading it aloud to himself. The passage began with the children of Adam and a whisper in a garden; it ends with their descendants bearing witness against themselves, and no one in the room to blame.
