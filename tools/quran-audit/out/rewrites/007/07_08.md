## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:8

> **And the weighing on that Day is true. So those whose balance is heavy, it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day That Weighs**

This verse sets the third action of the sūrah's courtroom: after the question (v. 6) and the recounting (v. 7), the weighing. The syntax puts the verdict first: *wa-l-waznu yawmaʾidhin al-ḥaqq* — and the weighing on that Day is the truth. *Ḥaqq* does double duty here. It means the scales are real, not a metaphor that dissolves on inspection; and it means they are true — exact, uncorrupted, incapable of registering a false weight.

The choice of the scale as the instrument of judgment answers the audience of this Makkan sūrah. Al-Aʿrāf speaks to a mercantile city whose life ran on balances: measured grain, weighed silver, a handshake over a pan. The Qur'an takes the instrument of the market and moves it to the last Day, where it is operated by the One who cannot be bribed. The point is sharpened in another sūrah, where woe is pronounced on traders who demand full measure when they buy and short it when they sell (83:1–3). The market's scale is precisely where men learn to weigh for advantage — which is why it is a scale that is set up on the Day of Judgment, and why the verse insists that the measuring there is *ḥaqq*.

**The Scale That Weighs What Scales Cannot**

The Qur'an describes the operation of this balance with mechanical precision: *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth”* (21:47). It will register an atom's weight (4:40; 99:7–8), and its verdicts are given in the very phrasing used here: *“As for those whose scale is heavy, it is they who will be successful. But those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves”* (23:102–103); and in Sūrat al-Qāriʿah, *“So as for those whose scale is heavy, they will be in a life of bliss”* (101:6–7).

What, then, is weighed? The Prophet ﷺ answered with an image no arithmetic can hold. A man is brought before God with ninety-nine scrolls of sins, each stretching as far as the eye can see, and a single card bearing the witness *there is no god but God*. The scrolls are placed on one pan and the card on the other: the scrolls rise, the card descends — *“nothing is heavier than the Name of God”* (al-Tirmidhī 2639, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr; graded ḥasan; Ibn Mājah records it as well).

That image is this verse's commentary. The scales of the hereafter do not weigh quantity, so that whoever piles up the most acts wins; they weigh reality — the actual worth of what a life was aimed at. A mountain of activity performed for display can rise like chaff, while one sincere confession of the One God can descend with the weight of a lifetime. This is why the Day can be called *the truth*: it is the moment when things are measured as they are, and not as they were advertised.

**The Prosperity of the Heavy**

The verse closes with the outcome for those whose pan descends: *fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn*. *Mufliḥūn* comes from *f-l-ḥ*, the root of the farmer — *fallāḥ* — who splits the earth, plants, and waits. *Filāḥ*, prosperity, is the same word as cultivation, because in the Qur'an's vocabulary success is not what a man extracts from the world but what grows out of what he has planted in it. The Arabic quietly answers the mercantile image: the real trade is not the one that closes when the caravan departs, but the one whose harvest is delivered on the Day the scales are set.

Heaviness, in that grammar, is not a burden. It is the weight of the things that survive — a confession guarded in secret, a charity no one recorded, an injustice refused at the moment when it would have paid. All of it descends in the pan, in the sight of no one except the One who was never absent.
