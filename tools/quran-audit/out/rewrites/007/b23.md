**The Words That Were Given**

This verse is the turning point of the entire narrative, and the Qur'an tells us where the words came from. *“Then Adam was inspired with words of prayer by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance”* (2:37). The sentence in al-Baqarah uses *talaqqā*, to receive — the words were not composed by the fallen man in the Garden; they were given to him. The tradition's commentators read a mercy in that grammar. A creature who had just broken a command does not know what to say; the Book's answer is that the formula of return is itself supplied from above, which is why the Prophet ﷺ taught his community to ask God to teach them how to ask Him.

**"Our Lord" — The Address That Survives the Fall**

The sentence opens with *rabbanā*. It would have been possible for a shame-stricken creature to address God by the Name of power, of judgment, of wrath; the pairing chosen is the Name of nurture and cultivation. The first word after the failure is therefore a claim on the relationship, not a renunciation of it. The Qur'an's model of repentance begins here, and this is why the same opening recurs on the lips of the returning: *“Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves”* (7:23), *“Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us”* (3:16), *“Our Lord, forgive us our sins”* (3:193).

**"We Have Wronged Ourselves"**

*Ẓalamnā anfusanā.* Three words, and the whole architecture of Islamic accountability is inside them. The object of the wrong is the self — *anfusanā* — not the majesty of God in the abstract, and not another party. The verse thus contains a diagnosis before it contains a request: sin is first of all an injury to the one who commits it, and the Qur'an uses this self-directed language consistently. *“Whoever does wrong, does wrong against himself”* (4:111); *“whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger harms no one but himself”* (33:36); *“Indeed, you have wronged yourselves”* (2:54).

The verb is in the perfect tense, with the *nā* of the first person plural. It reports a finished act and accepts it as one's own. Now compare it with the sentence spoken by Iblīs eleven verses earlier — *“because You have caused me to err”* (7:16) — and the contrast the sūrah has built becomes unavoidable. One creature files the blame upward; the other files the blame inward. The Book does not present both as mistakes with different flavors. It presents one as a position from which no return was ever sought, and the other as the exact posture from which return always begins.

**The Two Requests and Their Precision**

*In lam taghfir lanā wa tarḥamnā* — if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us. The two verbs cover the two halves of what a wrongdoer needs. *Ghufrān* is covering and cancellation: the record erased, the debt discharged. *Raḥmah* is positive: the gift, the restoration, the good supplied where nothing was owed. The pair runs through the Book's prayers — *“My Lord, forgive and have mercy, for You are the Best of the merciful”* (23:118) — and its order is significant. Forgiveness comes first because mercy has nothing to land on until the account is cleared.

The conditional formulation is worth a moment. *If You do not forgive us…* is not doubt; the Arabic conditional here expresses surrender rather than uncertainty, a placing of the matter entirely in God's hand. The servant does not argue his entitlement to pardon. He does not present the balance of his good deeds, or the extenuation of a tempter's oath. He states the consequence of not being forgiven — *we shall surely be of the losers* — and leaves the decision where it belongs.

**The Same Words from a Different People**

The sūrah does something remarkable with this formula: it puts the identical sentence in the mouths of a later generation. When the Children of Israel repent of the calf, the Qur'an reports: *“And when they regretted it and saw that they had gone astray, they said, ‘If our Lord does not have mercy upon us and forgive us, we shall surely be of the losers’”* (7:149). The parallel is deliberately exact, and it makes a doctrinal point that the sūrah wants carried forward through its long history of Israelite failure: the *mechanism* of return never changes. It is not a Christian or a Jewish rite, not a particular shrine, not an intercession purchased from a priest. It is the acknowledgment of self-inflicted harm, addressed by a creature to his Lord, without a mediator and without an excuse.

**The Master Formula**

The Prophet ﷺ gave this pattern its most complete written form in the *sayyid al-istighfār*, the master supplication: *“O God, You are my Lord; there is no god but You. You created me and I am Your servant, and I abide by Your covenant and promise as much as I am able. I seek refuge with You from the evil I have done. I acknowledge before You Your favour upon me and I acknowledge my sin, so forgive me, for none forgives sins but You.”* (al-Bukhārī 6306, from Shaddād ibn Aws). The components are exactly those of 7:23 — lordship addressed, fault owned, mercy requested, credit given to God alone — and the hadith adds the detail that makes the earlier scene intelligible: the one who says this in the morning and dies that day enters the Garden.

The verse therefore closes the first fall with hope rather than condemnation, and the sūrah's readers should note carefully what is *not* in the sentence. No protest of manipulation, no appeal to the tempter's oath, no minimizing of the act. Adam and his wife do the one thing the previous episode established as decisive: they speak to God, and they tell the truth about themselves. The Book's verdict on that turning is given in the verse that follows in Sūrah al-Baqarah — *“Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”* — and the sūrah of the Heights will spend the rest of its length teaching the children of Adam to use the same door.
