## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:14

> **He said, “Grant me respite till the Day they are resurrected.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Request That Reveals the Man**

Iblīs has been sentenced and evicted, and this is the first thing he says afterward. He does not ask for pardon. He does not ask to be returned. He does not ask a question about the judgment that has just passed over him. He asks for *time*. The request is the clearest window the sūrah offers into what had happened inside him: the verdict fell, and nothing in him bent.

The comparison with the story's other fall is exact and deliberate. When Adam and his wife ate from the tree, the first words they spoke were a petition of a different kind: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers”* (7:23). Two creatures broke a command in the same narrative; one asks for mercy, the other asks for a longer rope. The sūrah places them side by side to make the distinction visible: what makes a fall fatal is not the fall itself but what the fallen one says next.

**anẓirnī: The Language of a Debtor**

The verb *anẓirnī* comes from *n-ẓ-r* in its second form, *inẓār*: to grant a delay, to let a term run on. It is the vocabulary of the creditor who gives a debtor more time, and the Qur'an uses it precisely there: *“and if someone is in hardship, then let there be postponement (*naẓirah*) until it is easy”* (2:280). Iblīs, then, phrases his demand in the technical language of a man who owes and asks for an extension.

The wording exposes him. He has just been sentenced as the wrongdoer; he speaks as though he were a debtor with a claim to accommodation. And the extension he requests is not for the purpose a debtor asks for time — to gather means and repay. It is time to work against the creditors. The vocabulary of the verse is therefore doing two things at once: it shows the formal courtesy of the request, and it shows that the courtesy is the outer layer of a plan he will announce in two verses.

**The Day He Believes In**

The most unsettling phrase in the sentence is its endpoint: *till the Day they are resurrected.* Iblīs is the arch-rebel of the Qur'an, and he asks to be kept alive until the Resurrection — which means he does not doubt it. There will be a raising, he accepts it, and he asks for his term to stretch all the way to it.

The sūrah's lesson is embedded in that detail. Iblīs was never an atheist. He spoke with God, heard the command, and reasoned about creation; his rebellion was not a denial of God's existence but a refusal of God's authority over him. The Qur'an's own diagnosis in another sūrah names this posture exactly: *“When it is said to him, 'Fear Allah,' pride seizes him with sin”* (2:206). Disbelief in practice is a different thing from disbelief in theory, and it is far more common. A person may affirm the Resurrection in his creed, and still conduct his life as if the Day were a rumor — and this verse puts that inconsistency in the mouth of the first rebel in the universe. He did not argue that the Day was unreal. He asked for a schedule that would let him keep working until it came.

**The Respite as a Workshop**

The request is granted — the next verse is the answer — and the granting is not a concession to his will but the opening of the test. That is the pattern the Qur'an states as law: *“And do not think that God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do; He only gives them respite until a Day on which the eyes will stare”* (14:42), and *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them; they are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178). The same delay that is a workshop of repentance for one person is a workshop of accumulation for another; the clock itself is neutral, and time is what the human being is made of.

There is a mercy folded into this verse that its words do not advertise. Al-Ḥijr and Ṣād report the same exchange, and there the answer adds a refinement: the respite runs *“until the appointed Day”* (15:38; 38:81) — the known time whose knowledge belongs to God alone, not the Day of Resurrection as such. The Qur'an leaves that ambiguity in place, and the effect is instructive. No one — not the believer, not the rebel, not the one who asks for time — ever knows how much time he has. Every term is fixed, as the sūrah itself will state later: *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it”* (7:34; cf. 10:49; 63:11).

The verse thus hands the reader the one thing that no creature can negotiate. Iblīs was given his extension because the trial required it; the human being is given his days for the same reason. The difference is what the two build with them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:15

> **He said, “Truly thou art among those granted respite.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Bare Answer to a Large Request**

The answer is a single sentence, without a rebuke attached and without a condition: *innaka min al-munẓarīn* — you are among those granted respite. In the other accounts of this exchange the granting is given with the same economy, and in al-Ḥijr and Ṣād it is completed by the clause *“until the appointed Day”* (15:37–38; 38:80–81).

The brevity deserves attention because of what it does not contain. No lecture follows, no warning, no invitation to reconsider. The request is granted as stated. This is the Qur'an's way of teaching the difference between guidance and coercion: God's will is that creatures act freely, and freedom has a cost that the divine government does not suppress. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the respite was granted because the trial of humanity required a tempter who is present — the human being is being tested with his eyes open, not trapped.

**"Among Those Granted Respite"**

The phrasing is partitive: *min al-munẓarīn*, *among* the deferred, a member of a class. Iblīs is not handed a unique exemption; he is enrolled in a standing law. Everything mortal lives on borrowed time: *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time”* (3:145); *“For each community there is an appointed term”* (7:34); *“Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes”* (63:11). The indefinite plural is the point. He had asked to be made an exception to the general rule of ends; the answer tells him that he has been given a longer rope, not a different rope.

It is worth pausing on the subtlety the grammarians saw in the construction. Had the verse said *you are deferred* without the partitive, the sentence would still have been true; the addition of *min* places him inside a category shared by all creatures whose terms are set. The proudest being in the story is told, in the very sentence that grants his wish, that he is one of a crowd.

**The Delay That Builds Two Different Men**

The Qur'an treats delay as a moral substance, and it divides its users. For the wrongdoer it is accumulation: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178). For the rebellious community it is a trap that feels like kindness: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise”* (6:44). And against that, the same delay is the space in which a person returns: *“Say: O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy”* (39:53).

The three verses describe one clock with three faces. Iblīs' respite produced a strategy against humanity (vv. 16–17). A human being's respite can produce either the same ruthless accumulation or the quiet correction of a life. Nothing in the calendar tells the difference; only the use does.

**istidrāj: The Respite That Looks Like Generosity**

Arabic gives this delay a technical name, and the Qur'an builds a doctrine on it. *Istidrāj* is the drawing-on of a person stage by stage, so that he is led further while believing he is being blessed. *“Leave to Me those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend”* (68:44), and the verse that follows seals it: *“I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (68:45). The sūrah of the Heights says the same thing in its own voice: *“I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (7:183), immediately after describing those who reject the signs and are given increase.

The psychological mechanism is what makes the doctrine so uncomfortable, and the Qur'an states it without softening: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired”* (6:44). The gifts keep arriving; the warnings stop feeling urgent; the recipient concludes that the silence of heaven is approval. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly forbids the interpretation of prosperity as a verdict — the wealthy denier's comfort is not a certificate, and the sufferer's hardship is not a sentence. The delay granted to Iblīs, extended to the end of time, is the purest case of *istidrāj* in the Book: an unlimited opportunity, mistaken for an endorsement.

**The Appointed Day and the Hidden Hour**

The specification in the parallel accounts — *“until the appointed Day”* — has occupied the commentators. Some take it as the Day of Resurrection, which is what Iblīs had asked for in 7:14; others read it as the end of the world as it is known, the first blast at which all things die, the moment after which only God remains. Al-Ṭabarī records both positions and their chains. What the disagreement reveals is a deliberate silence in the text: the term is *known* — fixed, recorded, unpostponable — and *unknown* to the creature who holds it. That combination is the structure of human responsibility. If the hour were known, the life before it would become a countdown, and virtue would be arithmetic; if no hour existed, nothing would carry consequence.

The verse therefore does two things at once. It answers Iblīs with a grant, and it answers every reader with a warning, using the same words. Whatever one is building, the permission to keep building has a ceiling. The creature who asked for the extension believed he had obtained a room to work in; the sūrah's later verses will show what he built in it — an ambush on the road to God — and will also show, in the same sūrah, what the refuge from that ambush is: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”* (7:200).

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:16

> **He said, “Because Thou hast caused me to err, I shall surely lie in wait for them on Thy straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Causal Clause: "Because You Caused Me to Err"**

Iblīs' first act with his new lease is to file an appeal, and its argument is that the fault is God's. *Bi-mā aghwaytanī* — *because You have caused me to err*. The Arabic causal *mā* makes the sentence a claim of cause and effect: my deviation is traced to its author, and the sentence that follows is presented as the natural consequence of it.

Two things must be said about this clause, and the sūrah says both by placing it as it does. The first is that the Qur'an reports the words of its characters without adopting them. Iblīs' theology is his own, and the Book records it as evidence against him, not as doctrine. The second is that the Qur'an elsewhere defines what God's "causing to stray" means, and it never means what Iblīs wants it to mean. *“He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”* (2:26) — the straying is the consequence of a settled character, not an injection into an innocent one. *“So when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5) — the deviation of the heart follows a prior deviation of the will. The Book's law of recompense runs in kind: a person who leans away is let lean.

Al-Rāzī draws out the difference with care. There is a *straying* that God decrees as a trial and a *straying* that He decrees as a punishment, and both are consequences interior to a path the creature has chosen, not replacements for the creature's choice. Iblīs, facing the results of his own refusal, describes them in the passive voice of another's agency. The sūrah lets the reader see the maneuver for what it is: the first blame-shift in the moral history of the universe.

**The Two Who Took the Blame**

The counter-text sits a few verses away. When Adam and Eve broke the command, they said: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves”* (7:23). Two creatures, two falls, two grammars: one sentence pushes the fault upward and outward, the other keeps it where the act was performed.

This is why the Adam narrative in the Qur'an is a story about hope and the Iblīs narrative is a story about ruin — not because their acts differed in magnitude, but because their confessions differed in direction. Islam's whole science of repentance is built on the second grammar. The Prophet ﷺ described the returning servant in the terms of that sentence: a man who says, *O my Lord, I have wronged myself*, and receives in answer a forgiveness that God announces with joy (Muslim 2747, from Ibn Masʿūd). The clause in this verse is the anti-model of every such prayer.

**The Ambush on the Road**

Then the vow: *la-aqʿudanna lahum ṣirāṭaka al-mustaqīm* — I shall surely *sit* for them on Your straight path. The verb *qaʿada* gives the picture of a seated watcher, a highwayman at his post; the emphatic *nūn* of *la-aqʿudanna* matches the certainty of the divine oaths, as though the creature were claiming the same decisiveness for his vendetta. The translations render it "lie in wait," and that is the sense: not a direct assault but an interception.

The target he names is startling. He does not say "the path of the believers" or "their road"; he says *Your* straight path — the road God made, claimed, and commanded. The audacity is the doctrine. The enemy of humanity does not deny that the road exists or that it leads to God; he stations himself on it, because that is where the travelers are. The sūrah's image of the road recurs throughout the Book — *“Indeed, that is My Path — perfectly straight. So follow it”* (6:153); the Fātiḥah's central petition — and the ambush explains why the petition asks not only for the road but for guidance upon it, in the company of those who walked it.

**The Exception He Cannot Breach**

The other accounts complete the vow with a limit Iblīs was forced to concede: *“except Your chosen servants among them”* (15:40; 38:83). And the Qur'an states the boundary as a standing law: *“you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you”* (15:42); *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord”* (16:99); *“You will truly have no authority over My faithful servants”* (17:65). Even the permission given to the tester is bounded by the freedom of the tested. His weapon is suggestion (*waswasah*), and suggestion works only on a will that agrees with it.

That is why the sūrah's own remedy is not a wall or a weapon but a turning: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah”* (7:200); and the verse that follows it describes the escape precisely — *“when Satan whispers to those mindful of Allah, they remember their Lord, then they start to see things clearly”* (7:201). The ambush in 7:16 is real. So is the road's protection, and the protection is that the traveler keeps looking at where he is going.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:17

> **Then I shall come upon them from in front of them and from behind them, and from their right and from their left. And Thou wilt not find most of them grateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Approaches and One Road**

The vow begun in the previous verse is now diagrammed. Iblīs announces that he will come at humanity from four quarters: *min bayni aydīhim* — from in front of them; *wa-min khalfihim* — from behind; *wa-ʿan aymānihim* — from their right; *wa-ʿan shamāʾilihimm* — from their left. The picture is of encirclement. He is not promising a single temptation but a surrounding, an approach from whichever side the traveler leaves unguarded.

The verse therefore explains the practical shape of the trial that the previous verses set up. A test conducted by frontal assault would be easy to recognize and easy to repel; a test that arrives from four directions at once means that the danger is rarely where a person is looking. The sūrah's later remedy fits this image exactly — the refuge is not a fortification on one flank but a turning to God from any flank: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah”* (7:200).

**What the Commentators Read into the Directions**

The classical exegetes, beginning with reports traced to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ṭabarī, treated the four directions as a map of the ways a person is assailed, and their readings are worth recording because they have worn well.

From *in front*, they said, comes the temptation of the future: doubt about what lies ahead, the promise that there will be time later, the fear of what one may lose by obeying now. From *behind* comes the weight of what is past: inherited custom, the practice of ancestors, the argument that no one in one's family ever did this, the guilt that says the record is already too spoiled to repair. From the *right* comes corruption of religion itself: excess, self-satisfaction, the transformation of worship into display, the confidence of the person who is certain he is among the saved. From the *left* comes the pull of appetite: the open sins, the small indulgences, the countless daily permissions.

The classifications differ between al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn Kathīr in their details, and the differences are useful. They show that the exegetes did not treat the four directions as a code to be decoded but as a comprehensive figure: the whole perimeter of a human life. The enemy's claim is total, and the commentators' fourfold reading is simply an acknowledgment that the claim is not obviously false.

**The Directions He Did Not Name**

A widely transmitted observation, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās in the commentary literature, notes that Iblīs named four directions and left out two: he did not say he would come from above or from below. The explanation offered is that from above comes mercy, and from below comes the humility of the servant in prostration — the two postures the rebel cannot occupy.

The point should be held with a light hand. It is an exegetical insight rather than a revealed datum; the verse says what it says, and its silence about two directions is suggestive rather than dogmatic. But the suggestion is consistent with everything else the sūrah has established. The arrogant creature was ruined by refusing to lower himself, and the remedy for those he attacks is the very thing he would not do: to bow. The unguarded quadrant of the human being is not a blind spot in space but a missing posture in the soul.

**The Siege and Its Psychology**

The Prophet ﷺ described the siege from the inside, as a condition of every human life: *“Each one of you has a devil-companion from the jinn over him.”* When the companions asked whether this included him, he answered: *“Even me, yet Allah helped me against him until he submitted; he does not order me to do anything but good.”* (Muslim 2814, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd).

Two lessons follow from that report, and both answer this verse. The first is that the presence of the whisperer is not a sign of divine abandonment; it is the human condition, the same condition under which the Prophet ﷺ lived. The second is that the companion can be reduced to a whisperer who no longer commands — *“he does not order me to do anything but good”* — by help that comes from God. The four-sided approach of Iblīs is real; so is the possibility that a man reaches a state in which the approaches no longer find an entrance.

**His Verdict on Humanity — and the Remnant**

The verse closes with the enemy's own summary of the human race: *“and You will not find most of them grateful.”* The clause is an echo, and the sūrah intends it. Verse 10 had already told humanity the same thing in God's own words — *Little do you give thanks!* — and there the diagnosis stood as a charge against the ungrateful. Here the same charge appears in the mouth of the accuser, which is the sharpest possible way of stating it: the ingratitude is the entry point he is counting on.

The Qur'an confirms the statistic without confirming his conclusion. *“Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful”* (10:60; cf. 27:73; 16:83) — and the same Book says of the divine bounty that it reaches the grateful and the ungrateful alike, since *“He has granted you all that you asked Him for”* (14:34). The word *most* is where the hope lives. The enemy's sentence leaves a remainder, and the remainder is the company he himself was forced to acknowledge and exclude from his reach: *“except Your chosen servants among them”* (15:40; 38:83). Gratitude, in this sūrah's architecture, is not a virtue added to faith. It is the door the ambush was designed to block, and the shield that shuts it is a single remembered favour at the right moment.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:18

> **He said, “Go forth from it, disgraced and banished! Whosoever among them follows you, I shall surely fill Hell with you all.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Expulsion, Sharper Than the First**

The sūrah contains two removal commands addressed to Iblīs, and the commentators read them as the two halves of one sentence. The first was *ihbiṭ* — get down (7:13), spoken in answer to his arrogance; the second is *ukhruj* — go forth (here), spoken after he has declared his plan to besiege humanity. The first removed him from the place in which the transgression occurred. The second confirms the state: *ukhruj minhā* — out of it, and out of the rank that went with it.

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note the deepening: in the first command he is told to descend; in the second, after the vow of verse 16 and the four-sided siege of verse 17, he is told to leave and is given two descriptions that settle what he now is. The sentence does not grow because God's anger grows — the divine judgment does not fluctuate — but because the accused has now declared himself. He was given the chance to say nothing further; he used the opportunity to pronounce a war.

**madhʾūman and madḥūran: Two Roots, One Verdict**

The two words are precise, and the older commentary in this file garbled them; stated correctly, they are *madhʾūman* and *madḥūran*.

*Madhʾūm* comes from the root *dh-ʾ-m*, which carries the sense of a driven-away disgrace, the foul reproach that attaches to one who has forfeited standing. *Madḥūr* comes from *d-ḥ-r*, to repel with force, to drive off violently — the root used of scattering an enemy. Together they describe an inside and an outside: the honor is stripped, and the person is expelled. Because both words are passive participles, the construction also says who did it. He is not merely disgraced as a condition and banished as an event; he is disgraced and banished *by the sentence of another*. The creature who claimed autonomous greatness ends the exchange as the object of two verbs he did not choose.

The mirror with his own crime is exact. He had made himself great in his own estimation (*ana khayrun minhu*, 7:12) and had refused to be moved by a command; the sentence accordingly reduces him and moves him by force. The rule the sūrah keeps demonstrating is that God answers self-exaltation with reduction, and refusal with expulsion.

**The Oath of the Filling**

*La-amlaʾanna jahannama minkum ajmaʿīn* — I shall surely fill Hell with you, all together. The first-person oath-pronoun is God's, and the same declaration is repeated at three other places in the Book: *“I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together”* (11:119); *“My Word will come to pass: I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together”* (32:13); and the address to Iblīs, *“I will surely fill up Hell with you and whoever follows you from among them, all together”* (38:85).

The repetition has drawn theological commentary for centuries, and the commentators have been careful about what it does and does not mean. It does not describe a deficiency that creatures must supply, as though the Fire were a container awaiting its quota. Sūrat al-Sajdah states the logic explicitly: *“Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul. But My Word will come to pass”* (32:13). The filling is the consequence of a world created with genuine choice — a world in which guidance is offered and can be refused. The declaration is therefore a statement about the realism of the test: God does not pretend that everyone will pass.

The sūrah also states the population of that sentence with precision in the verse following al-Ḥijr's parallel: *“and surely Hell is their destined place, all together. It has seven gates, to each a group of them is designated”* (15:43–44). The imagery of gates and groups is not a decoration of the threat; it is a refusal to describe the Fire as a mass. Those who enter are not a crowd but named categories of a chosen road.

**"Whoever Follows You" — the Condition That Keeps It Just**

The threat is conditional, and the condition is doing the work: *man ittabaʿaka minhum* — whoever among them follows you. What is threatened is not humanity but the *following*. The Qur'an states the same boundary as a law of the enemy's power: he has *no authority* over the sincere (15:42; 16:99; 17:65), and his capacity to lead is itself part of the test — *“He does not have any authority over them, but Our Will is only to distinguish those who believe in the Hereafter from those who are in doubt about it”* (34:21). The tempter tempts; he does not compel. A man who enters the Fire has walked there, however subtly the walking was arranged.

**Mercy Standing at the Door of the Next Verse**

It is important to notice the sequence of the sūrah after this sentence. The very next verses return to Adam, and they end with forgiveness: *“Then Adam was inspired with words of prayer by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”* (2:37; cf. 7:23). The banishment of Iblīs is followed immediately by the pardon of the human being, and the juxtaposition is the sūrah's message in miniature. The same mercy that God declared to Moses in this very sūrah — *“My mercy encompasses all things”* (7:156) — stands open to the descendants of Adam, while the one who refused to ask for it stands outside. The chapter that begins its narrative with the most final-sounding verdict in the Book ends its narrative with a returning man forgiven, and the reader is placed between the two doors.
