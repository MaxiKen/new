## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:18

> **He said, “Go forth from it, disgraced and banished! Whosoever among them follows you, I shall surely fill Hell with you all.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Expulsion, Sharper Than the First**

The sūrah contains two removal commands addressed to Iblīs, and the commentators read them as the two halves of one sentence. The first was *ihbiṭ* — get down (7:13), spoken in answer to his arrogance; the second is *ukhruj* — go forth (here), spoken after he has declared his plan to besiege humanity. The first removed him from the place in which the transgression occurred. The second confirms the state: *ukhruj minhā* — out of it, and out of the rank that went with it.

Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr note the deepening: in the first command he is told to descend; in the second, after the vow of verse 16 and the four-sided siege of verse 17, he is told to leave and is given two descriptions that settle what he now is. The sentence does not grow because God's anger grows — the divine judgment does not fluctuate — but because the accused has now declared himself. He was given the chance to say nothing further; he used the opportunity to pronounce a war.

**madhʾūman and madḥūran: Two Roots, One Verdict**

The two words are precise, and the older commentary in this file garbled them; stated correctly, they are *madhʾūman* and *madḥūran*.

*Madhʾūm* comes from the root *dh-ʾ-m*, which carries the sense of a driven-away disgrace, the foul reproach that attaches to one who has forfeited standing. *Madḥūr* comes from *d-ḥ-r*, to repel with force, to drive off violently — the root used of scattering an enemy. Together they describe an inside and an outside: the honor is stripped, and the person is expelled. Because both words are passive participles, the construction also says who did it. He is not merely disgraced as a condition and banished as an event; he is disgraced and banished *by the sentence of another*. The creature who claimed autonomous greatness ends the exchange as the object of two verbs he did not choose.

The mirror with his own crime is exact. He had made himself great in his own estimation (*ana khayrun minhu*, 7:12) and had refused to be moved by a command; the sentence accordingly reduces him and moves him by force. The rule the sūrah keeps demonstrating is that God answers self-exaltation with reduction, and refusal with expulsion.

**The Oath of the Filling**

*La-amlaʾanna jahannama minkum ajmaʿīn* — I shall surely fill Hell with you, all together. The first-person oath-pronoun is God's, and the same declaration is repeated at three other places in the Book: *“I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together”* (11:119); *“My Word will come to pass: I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together”* (32:13); and the address to Iblīs, *“I will surely fill up Hell with you and whoever follows you from among them, all together”* (38:85).

The repetition has drawn theological commentary for centuries, and the commentators have been careful about what it does and does not mean. It does not describe a deficiency that creatures must supply, as though the Fire were a container awaiting its quota. Sūrat al-Sajdah states the logic explicitly: *“Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul. But My Word will come to pass”* (32:13). The filling is the consequence of a world created with genuine choice — a world in which guidance is offered and can be refused. The declaration is therefore a statement about the realism of the test: God does not pretend that everyone will pass.

The sūrah also states the population of that sentence with precision in the verse following al-Ḥijr's parallel: *“and surely Hell is their destined place, all together. It has seven gates, to each a group of them is designated”* (15:43–44). The imagery of gates and groups is not a decoration of the threat; it is a refusal to describe the Fire as a mass. Those who enter are not a crowd but named categories of a chosen road.

**"Whoever Follows You" — the Condition That Keeps It Just**

The threat is conditional, and the condition is doing the work: *man ittabaʿaka minhum* — whoever among them follows you. What is threatened is not humanity but the *following*. The Qur'an states the same boundary as a law of the enemy's power: he has *no authority* over the sincere (15:42; 16:99; 17:65), and his capacity to lead is itself part of the test — *“He does not have any authority over them, but Our Will is only to distinguish those who believe in the Hereafter from those who are in doubt about it”* (34:21). The tempter tempts; he does not compel. A man who enters the Fire has walked there, however subtly the walking was arranged.

**Mercy Standing at the Door of the Next Verse**

It is important to notice the sequence of the sūrah after this sentence. The very next verses return to Adam, and they end with forgiveness: *“Then Adam was inspired with words of prayer by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”* (2:37; cf. 7:23). The banishment of Iblīs is followed immediately by the pardon of the human being, and the juxtaposition is the sūrah's message in miniature. The same mercy that God declared to Moses in this very sūrah — *“My mercy encompasses all things”* (7:156) — stands open to the descendants of Adam, while the one who refused to ask for it stands outside. The chapter that begins its narrative with the most final-sounding verdict in the Book ends its narrative with a returning man forgiven, and the reader is placed between the two doors.
