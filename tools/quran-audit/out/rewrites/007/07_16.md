## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:16

> **He said, “Because Thou hast caused me to err, I shall surely lie in wait for them on Thy straight path.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Causal Clause: "Because You Caused Me to Err"**

Iblīs' first act with his new lease is to file an appeal, and its argument is that the fault is God's. *Bi-mā aghwaytanī* — *because You have caused me to err*. The Arabic causal *mā* makes the sentence a claim of cause and effect: my deviation is traced to its author, and the sentence that follows is presented as the natural consequence of it.

Two things must be said about this clause, and the sūrah says both by placing it as it does. The first is that the Qur'an reports the words of its characters without adopting them. Iblīs' theology is his own, and the Book records it as evidence against him, not as doctrine. The second is that the Qur'an elsewhere defines what God's "causing to stray" means, and it never means what Iblīs wants it to mean. *“He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”* (2:26) — the straying is the consequence of a settled character, not an injection into an innocent one. *“So when they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5) — the deviation of the heart follows a prior deviation of the will. The Book's law of recompense runs in kind: a person who leans away is let lean.

Al-Rāzī draws out the difference with care. There is a *straying* that God decrees as a trial and a *straying* that He decrees as a punishment, and both are consequences interior to a path the creature has chosen, not replacements for the creature's choice. Iblīs, facing the results of his own refusal, describes them in the passive voice of another's agency. The sūrah lets the reader see the maneuver for what it is: the first blame-shift in the moral history of the universe.

**The Two Who Took the Blame**

The counter-text sits a few verses away. When Adam and Eve broke the command, they said: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves”* (7:23). Two creatures, two falls, two grammars: one sentence pushes the fault upward and outward, the other keeps it where the act was performed.

This is why the Adam narrative in the Qur'an is a story about hope and the Iblīs narrative is a story about ruin — not because their acts differed in magnitude, but because their confessions differed in direction. Islam's whole science of repentance is built on the second grammar. The Prophet ﷺ described the returning servant in the terms of that sentence: a man who says, *O my Lord, I have wronged myself*, and receives in answer a forgiveness that God announces with joy (Muslim 2747, from Ibn Masʿūd). The clause in this verse is the anti-model of every such prayer.

**The Ambush on the Road**

Then the vow: *la-aqʿudanna lahum ṣirāṭaka al-mustaqīm* — I shall surely *sit* for them on Your straight path. The verb *qaʿada* gives the picture of a seated watcher, a highwayman at his post; the emphatic *nūn* of *la-aqʿudanna* matches the certainty of the divine oaths, as though the creature were claiming the same decisiveness for his vendetta. The translations render it "lie in wait," and that is the sense: not a direct assault but an interception.

The target he names is startling. He does not say "the path of the believers" or "their road"; he says *Your* straight path — the road God made, claimed, and commanded. The audacity is the doctrine. The enemy of humanity does not deny that the road exists or that it leads to God; he stations himself on it, because that is where the travelers are. The sūrah's image of the road recurs throughout the Book — *“Indeed, that is My Path — perfectly straight. So follow it”* (6:153); the Fātiḥah's central petition — and the ambush explains why the petition asks not only for the road but for guidance upon it, in the company of those who walked it.

**The Exception He Cannot Breach**

The other accounts complete the vow with a limit Iblīs was forced to concede: *“except Your chosen servants among them”* (15:40; 38:83). And the Qur'an states the boundary as a standing law: *“you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you”* (15:42); *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord”* (16:99); *“You will truly have no authority over My faithful servants”* (17:65). Even the permission given to the tester is bounded by the freedom of the tested. His weapon is suggestion (*waswasah*), and suggestion works only on a will that agrees with it.

That is why the sūrah's own remedy is not a wall or a weapon but a turning: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah”* (7:200); and the verse that follows it describes the escape precisely — *“when Satan whispers to those mindful of Allah, they remember their Lord, then they start to see things clearly”* (7:201). The ambush in 7:16 is real. So is the road's protection, and the protection is that the traveler keeps looking at where he is going.

---

The Book's readers are meant to notice the difference between the two confessions: Iblīs attributes his ruin to God, while Adam and Eve attribute theirs to themselves. The sūrah places the two sentences a few verses apart, and the contrast is the whole doctrine of responsibility in miniature.
