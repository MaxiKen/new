# Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:80–7:104 — rewritten sections

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:80

> **And Lot, when he said to his people, “What! Do you commit an indecency such as none in the world committed before you?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lot and the Crime Without Precedent**

*Wa-Lūṭan idh qāla li-qawmihi a-taʾtūna al-fāḥishata mā sabaqakum bihā min aḥadin mina al-ʿālamīn* — and Lot, when he said to his people: do you commit an indecency that no one in the worlds has ever done before you? The sūrah's fourth town is introduced without the word *brother*, and the omission is itself a comment: Lot's relationship to Sodom is one of residence rather than kinship, and the Book's account elsewhere notes that he was a stranger among them (21:74; 29:26).

The phrase the verse uses for the crime is *al-fāḥishah* — the sūrah's standard term for sexual immorality, used at 7:28 for the circumambulation, at 6:151, and at 4:22. What distinguishes Sodom's offense in the Book's telling is the qualifier: *mā sabaqakum bihā min aḥadin mina al-ʿālamīn* — no one before you among the worlds had done it. Whether the clause means that no people before them had committed this particular act, or that no one had committed an indecency of this brazenness in public, the effect is the same: Sodom's crime is presented as historically unprecedented, which is to say that the town had reached a refinement of transgression its neighbours had not imagined.

**The Crime in the Book's Other Accounts**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ has Lot name the deed and its inversion of creation: *“Why do you ˹men˺ lust after fellow men, … leaving the wives that your Lord has created for you? In fact, you are a transgressing people.’”* (26:165–166). Sūrat al-Naml's version has the town's own view of the matter: *“And ˹remember˺ Lot, when he rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people, ‘Do you commit that shameful deed while you can see ˹one another˺?”* (27:54), and Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt adds the detail of their public councils: *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.’”* (29:29). The picture the Book builds is not of a private vice but of a town that had made the deed part of its public culture.

The Qur'an's rhetoric on the point is worth noting for what it does not do. The Book never argues that the act is unnatural in the modern sense of a category violation; its arguments are those it makes against every sin in the sūrah: the deed is described as a transgression of bounds, a corruption in a land that had been set right, and — decisively — an act performed against the messenger sent to correct it. The town's judgment in the sūrah hangs on that last point, and verse 82 will show the town conflating the two.

**The Difference Between Sodom and Its Neighbours**

The sūrah's towns have so far at least argued with their messengers. ʿĀd demanded proof, Thamūd killed a sign, Noah's chiefs pronounced a verdict. Sodom's response, recorded in the next verse, will contain no theological content at all — no gods are defended, no ancestors are cited, no claim is made about the messenger's truthfulness. What the town says is that the messenger and his household must leave. The Book's narratives treat that difference as the town's most damning feature: a community so settled in its practice that the only question raised by a prophetic summons was the inconvenience of the summons.

Sūrat al-Ṭūr and Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt preserve the town's atmosphere with the same emphasis: *“He had already warned them of Our ˹crushing˺ blow but they disputed the warnings.”* (54:36 vicinity), and the Book repeatedly describes the towns as being destroyed *while they were still in their transgression, asleep at dawn* (15:66, 51:35 in the vicinity). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf gives the town one verse of speech in the next lines, and the whole town has nothing to say about God.

**The Messenger as a Resident**

Lot's standing in the sūrah's sequence is unusual because he is not a founder of the town he addresses. The Qur'an nowhere claims that Sodom received a legislative covenant from him; the Book's portrait is of a righteous man living among a people committed to a practice he denounced, so that the town's history became a contest between a resident's conscience and its demand for conformity. Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ and Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt both record that Lot was a recipient of judgment and knowledge (21:74) and that he emigrated for the sake of his Lord (29:26), and the Book's account in Sūrat al-Ṭahrīm names his wife as an example of a believer's household member who failed the test (66:10).

That last detail is doing important work in the Book's teaching about family and faith, and the sūrah will state it factually in verse 83: the rescue passed through the household and took all but one. The narrative therefore has two poles — a man out of place in his city, and a wife out of place in the ark of her family — and the sūrah reports both with the same flatness, as facts about which community a person's life actually belonged to.

**A Verse About Precedent**

One further observation belongs to the verse's opening clause. The phrase *no one among the worlds has done it before you* contains an argument that the sūrah's readers cannot miss: the towns of the prophetic era are being compared with one another, and Sodom's failure is a novel rather than inherited form of corruption. The Book's view of moral history is not cyclical; it allows for moral invention, and it treats novelty in evil as a distinctive kind of responsibility.

That is why the sūrah's account of the town is so compressed and yet so severe. Sodom did not fall because its people were weaker than the populations destroyed before them, or because they had been given less light. It fell because a community that had been warned chose to make its practice the measure of its life, and the sūrah's next verse shows what such a community says when a prophet interrupts its evening.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:81

> **Verily you come with desire unto men instead of women. Indeed, you are a prodigal people!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Specification of the Charge**

*Innakum la-taʾtūna al-rijāla shahwatan min dūni al-nisāʾ* — you approach men with desire, instead of women. Lot's opening address continues with the detail that makes the town's crime specific: the substitution. The Book's phrasing is careful. It is not a general accusation of sexual disorder; it names the act, the object, and the perversion of the design — *min dūni al-nisāʾ*, to the exclusion of women. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version adds the created order to the charge: *“leaving the wives that your Lord has created for you? In fact, you are a transgressing people.’”* (26:166). The argument in the Book is thus about authorship: the pairing of male and female was a creation, and the town's practice was a repudiation of the arrangement in favour of an invention.

The Qur'an records the same indictment against Sodom across six sūrahs, and its constancy is itself part of the teaching: this is not a prohibition the Book is embarrassed about, and it never softens the account under pressure. Sūrat al-Naml puts the accusation in the mouth of the town rather than of the prophet — *“And ˹remember˺ Lot, when he rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people, ‘Do you commit that shameful deed while you can see ˹one another˺?”* (27:54) — and Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt expands the catalogue with the town's other practices: the cutting of the road, the shameful assembly (29:29).

**"You Are a People Transgressing"**

*Bal antum qawmun musrifūn* — rather, you are a people exceeding the limits. The word *musrif* is the sūrah's vocabulary for the one who goes past the boundary in any domain: the spendthrift in property, the tyrant in power, the glutton in appetite, and here the community that has made excess its norm. The Book uses *isrāf* across the whole field of conduct, and its consistent point is that the sin is not located in a single appetite but in the decision that there is no limit to be respected.

The Quranic treatment of Sodom's offense is thus framed by the same doctrine the sūrah applied to the towns' religion. Noah's people thought worship was inherited property; ʿĀd thought strength was authorization; Thamūd thought they could define the sign. Sodom thought that desire was the only law there was, and the town's culture had been arranged around the assumption. The verse names the underlying error with a single adjective.

**The Town's Public Dimension**

It is worth noting how many of the Book's accounts of Sodom place the crime in the street or in an assembly. Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt's version has the town's own question — *“Do you really lust after ˹other˺ men, abuse the travellers, and practice immorality ˹openly˺ in your gatherings?” His people’s only response was to say ˹mockingly˺: “Bring Allah’s punishment upon us, if what you say is true.’”* (29:29) — and Sūrat al-Naml has the deed performed with the knowledge of the community, which is what its rhetorical question about *seeing* implies (27:54). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records the response of the townsmen to Lot's guests as a mob action (26:165). The Book's picture is of a vice that had become an institution, defended as a culture, and enforced by the town's collective will.

That is the context in which the next verse is to be read. When a community arranges itself around an inversion, the presence of a man who will not conform becomes a scandal rather than an eccentricity. The Book's judgment against Sodom, in its own framing, is not pronounced on private acts discovered in the dark; it is pronounced on a public order that had decided to define itself by them.

**The Comparative Context**

The Qur'an's severity on this subject has been noted by every generation of its readers, and the Book's own comparative treatment is instructive. Sūrat al-Nisāʾ, in legislating against an unrelated sexual offense, uses a term that connects the two: *“If two of you commit it, punish them both; but if they repent and mend their ways, leave them alone”* (4:16 in the reading of the majority, where the verse is understood in some commentaries of a general indecency). Sūrat al-Nūr establishes the evidentiary and penal structure for illicit sexual conduct and closes the door on slander (24:4). The Book's approach to sexual ethics is thus consistent and structural: a fixed boundary, a heavy penalty, and a strong guard against accusation.

Sodom's singularity lies then in its scale. The town had made of the transgression a civilization. It is the same judgment the sūrah has applied to every one of its five towns: the crime is almost never the individual act; it is the collective decision to arrange a community — its laws, its gatherings, its walls — on the wrong side of a limit.

**"Instead of Women" and the Book's Argument From Design**

The clause *min dūni al-nisāʾ* has traditionally been read as the Book's argument from design, and the sūrah of al-Shuʿarāʾ makes that reading explicit by naming the mates as *what your Lord created* (26:166). The Book's sexual ethic in this verse is therefore anchored in the creation order rather than in tribal convention, which is precisely why the sūrah's presentation is so direct: the town did not merely break a law; it rewrote the human arrangement in the ledger of created things.

The point has a corollary that the sūrah's narrative will press. If the arrangement of the sexes is a creation, then a community's authority over the practice runs only as far as a creature's authority over the design of its own species — which is to say, it has none. The town's response, in the next verse, is to expel the one resident who said so, and the Book records the sentence without ironic comment. What follows — the rain, the stones, the overturned towns — is the sūrah's answer to a community that thought its culture could overrule its Creator.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:82

> **And the reply of his people was but to say, “Expel them from your town! Truly they are a people who keep themselves pure.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Drive Them Out — They Want To Be Pure"**

*Wa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū akhrijūhum min qaryatikum innahum unās yataṭahharūn* — and the only answer of his people was to say: expel them from your town; they are people who want to be pure. The sentence is the sūrah's finest specimen of the rhetoric of resentment, and its cruelty lies in the last word.

The town could have defended its practice, cited its ancestors, disputed the messenger's claims, or demanded a sign — as the previous four towns had done. It did none of it. What it said instead was that the presence of people who insist on purity is intolerable. The mockery is not directed at Lot's message about God; it is directed at the household's chastity. The Book's other account of the same remark makes the verb even blunter: *“But his people’s only response was to say, ‘Expel Lot’s followers from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!’”* (27:56).

**Purity as an Accusation**

The word *yataṭahharūn* is the fifth form of the root *ṭ-h-r*, with the reflexive and intensive sense: they seek to purify themselves, they make a show of purity. The town's sentence treats moral cleanliness as an affectation — something a person pretends to, something that embarrasses an honest community, something to be expelled for. The sūrah's reader already knows how the Book regards that reversal, because its opening had made purity of heart one of the definitions of the saved (7:42 in the vicinity), and because the Book's own community would spend its history being told that *“Allah loves those who purify themselves”* (9:108).

There is a further indictment in the grammar. The townspeople do not say *Lot is a hypocrite*; they say the household *wants* to be pure, as though the desire itself is the offense. What Sodom objected to was not the performance of virtue — a town can accommodate a few performers — but its presence as a standing contradiction of their own order. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn's picture of the mockers at the gate — *“Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers,”* (83:29 vicinity) — is the same social mechanism at a smaller scale; the sūrah's verse is the mechanism with a decree attached.

**The Town That Could Not Tolerate a Neighbour**

The Book's interest in the towns of the prophets consistently turns on this question: what does a community do with a member who will not conform? The five towns answer in escalating terms — a label, a taunt, a killed animal, an expulsion — and the sūrah arranges their answers so that the reader can see them as a scale of intolerance. Sodom's answer is the most radical because it begins from the premise that the community has the right to define the conditions of belonging, and the one condition it cannot meet is the presence of a conscience.

That is why the Book's account of Sodom has always been read by its communities as a description of something more than a sexual history. Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt's version adds the crime of highway robbery (29:29); the sūrah of Hūd records the town's assembly proposing to the guests (11:78–79); the Book's overall portrait is of a walled community whose walls were its main achievement. Verse 82 tells the reader what such a community does when something on the inside refuses to match the outside.

**The Book's Irony**

The Book does not need to comment; the reader supplies the irony. The inhabitants of a town whose practice has been called unprecedented, whose assemblies have been named in the Book of God, stand in the street and expel the only family that could have stayed in it on the terms God set. The sentence they pronounce — *they want to be pure* — will be quoted in Muslim liturgies for the rest of history as the model of mockery, and the Book of the sūrah's own chapter of the events states the answer in the following verse: a rain from the sky, and a morning that found the towns turned over.

That sequence is the sūrah's method in miniature. It reports speech, and then it reports consequence, and it refuses to editorialize between them, on the assumption that the reader's conscience will supply the verdict. The verdict in this case is available from the earlier verses of the same passage: Sodom's mockery happened in the world where the scale of 7:8 applies, and the deed it mocked was called, by its own messenger, the same purity the Book commends.

**A Town That Expelled What It Needed**

The final observation on the verse belongs to the Book's use of Sodom as a warning to the towns of its own audience. The sūrah has described the town's answer to the messenger as an expulsion — *akhrijūhum min qaryatikum* — and it is not incidental that the Qur'an's account of the migration that follows, in other sūrahs, makes Lot's departure from Sodom the one journey that saved his family (29:26; 51:35). What Sodom did to Lot, God arranged for his rescue.

The Book's narratives of the towns are full of such reversals, and the sūrah's audience in Makkah had its own version of them under way. The Prophet would be expelled from a city which the Book describes as a town of traders who could not tolerate sincere counsel, and the expulsion would be the beginning of a migration rather than an end. The verse of the Aʿrāf records the formula in the mouths of Sodom: *they are people who want to be pure*. The Book's silent response is that this is exactly why they were let go.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:83

> **So We saved him and his family, except for his wife; she was among those who lagged behind.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Family and the One Who Stayed**

*Fa-anjaynāhu wa-ahlahu illā imraʾatahu kānat mina al-ghābirīn* — so We saved him and his family, except his wife; she was among those who stayed behind. The rescue is stated in the sūrah's familiar form, and one clause alters the pattern that Noah's and ʿĀd's towns had established. In the earlier narratives, the remnant was defined by belief; here the household itself is named as the saved unit, and the exception is spelled out.

The Book's other accounts supply the drama the sūrah leaves out. Sūrat al-Ḥijr and Sūrat Hūd record the angels arriving at Lot's door and the instruction that he should travel by night with his family and that no one should look back (15:65; 11:81). Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt has them leave before dawn (51:35), and 11:76 records the negotiation over the guests. The wife's sympathy for the town's order is named only in the phrase *she was among those who stayed behind* — *al-ghābirīn*, those who remained. The word's root carries both the sense of remaining and of delay, and the Book uses it for those left inside a punishment when the righteous are extracted.

**The Doctrine of the Household**

The exception is one of the Book's clearest statements about the limits of kinship. The Qur'an's teaching that ties of blood do not transmit faith is not a side note; it is stated in several places, and this verse is one of its canonical instances: *“Indeed, among your wives and children are enemies to you, so beware of them”* (64:14); *“You will never find a people who ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day loyal to those who defy Allah and His Messenger, even if they were their parents, children, siblings, or extended family. For those ˹believers˺, Allah has instilled faith in …”* (58:22). Sūrat al-Taḥrīm makes the point with two examples and both of them are women married to prophets: *“Allah has set an example for those who disbelieve: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot; they were married to two of Our righteous servants, but they betrayed them, and they will both enter the Fire”* (66:10).

The Book's prophets are thus shown to be without a special power over their own households. Noah's son drowned (11:42–43); Abraham's father died in his error (9:114); the Prophet's uncle was condemned in a sūrah named for him (111:1–5). The sūrah's narrative keeps the record honest, and verse 83's exception — one woman in a rescued house — is the scripture's protection against treating a believing household as a charm.

**"She Was One of Those Who Stayed Behind"**

The sūrah's manner of stating the wife's fate is worth pausing over. She is not described as having committed any act; she is named by her position. The Book states no crime for her, and the phrase *was among those who remained* locates her with the town rather than with the family that was extracted. The Qur'an's moral accounts often use this form of description; its most economical examples are the statements about those who *have not believed* and *have turned away*, where the label sums up a life rather than a moment.

The sūrah's own later verse on the entry to Paradise makes the complementary point: the community of the Hereafter is built on the criterion of response, not of family (7:49's vicinity; cf. 7:38–39). The sūrah's narrative of the towns has been putting that principle on display for three verses running: families divided by belief inside the same house, a household saved entire except for one, a town wiped out around a single righteous man.

**The Rescue and Its Terms**

*Anjaynā* — the verb that has closed each narrative in the sūrah — appears again, and the Book's consistent account of the deliverances is that they are accomplished by mercy and by the faithful transmission of instructions. In the fuller versions, the rescued are given a deadline, a direction, and a prohibition against looking back; the Book's narrative interest is in the fact that the rescue was arranged in advance and announced as an event. Sūrat al-Ḥijr's version is the most vivid: the angels tell Lot that the town's term is the morning, *“We revealed to him this decree: ‘Those ˹sinners˺ will be uprooted in the morning.’”* (15:66 vicinity; cf. 11:81).

The saved household's smallness is part of the sūrah's persistent theme. The narrative does not rescue a city; it removes a handful of people under cover of night. The Book's account of the migration that follows places Lot's journey in the tradition of Abraham's departure and Joseph's and Moses's — the pattern of the righteous person operating without a community and trusting a promise. Verse 83 leaves the wife behind as the narrative's one fixed point: not everyone who shares a house shares a direction.

**What the Verse Leaves in the Reader's Hands**

The verse's last clause is stated without explanation, and the sūrah has already given the reader enough to understand it. The town's crime was a culture of the limitlessness; the wife's position was inside that culture; the rescue required a departure that she did not make. The Book's narratives refuse the consolation that family is the same as fate, which is why the sūrah's account of the towns has been so insistent that each community receives its own sentence. Lot's house and Lot's town were separated by a decision, and the Book records that decision under the plainest term: she remained.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:84

> **And We sent down a rain upon them; so behold how the guilty fared in the end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rain and the End**

*Wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhim maṭaran fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mujrimīn* — and We rained down upon them a rain; then see what was the end of the criminals. The sūrah's fourth town closes with the shortest and harshest of its endings, and the sequence of verbs is exact: the rain is sent, and the reader is addressed directly.

The Book's other accounts of the event supply the materials of that rain. Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt names the stones: *“We sent upon them stones of clay, marked by your Lord for the transgressors”* (51:33–34). Sūrat al-Ḥijr records the hour and the direction: *“So the blast seized them at sunrise, and We turned the city upside down and rained upon them stones of hard clay”* (15:73–74). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ and Sūrat al-Qamar describe the morning and the overturned place (26:172–173; 54:37–38); and the Book's characteristic evaluation of the rain is stated at 25:40: *“they have certainly passed by the town on which a rain of evil fell”*.

**"See What Was the End of the Criminals"**

*Fa-nẓur* — then look, consider, see. The imperative is directed at the sūrah's reader and it turns the narrative into evidence. This is the same move the sūrah made after Noah's rescue, where the blindness of the town was named as its own verdict, and it is the move the Book makes repeatedly in the Makkan sūrahs: the ruins of the prophetic age are presented as available, inspectable data. Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's account of the same history ends with the same address to the reader: *“They pass by them in the morning and by night — will you not then understand?”* (37:137–138).

The term for the town is *al-mujrimīn*: the criminals, from the root *j-r-m*, which carries the sense of cutting off and of a harvest of guilt. The sūrah has used it in its own verdicts before (7:40, 7:41) — the residents of the Fire and the wrongdoers. The reader is therefore being told exactly where Sodom's category sits in the sūrah's moral taxonomy: the town is not an exotic curiosity of the ancient world but an instance of the category the sūrah has been describing since the gates were closed on the arrogant.

**The Pattern Complete in Four Towns**

With verse 84 the sūrah's town sequence reaches the end of its first run, and the shape of the series can now be assessed. Each narrative contains a messenger, a message that is always the same, an elite who lead the rejection, a refusal that takes the form of mockery, a sign that is either a natural event or an animal or a resident's conscience, a rescue of a small group, and a destruction that fits the town's own account of its strengths. Four towns, four variations, and no exceptions to the rule.

This is why the sūrah gives the narratives so little documentary texture and so much doctrinal uniformity. The Book is not writing the history of the peninsula; it is running a comparative experiment and reporting the constant. And the constant is stated in the verse that closes each story: the end of the criminals is what the reader is invited to observe.

**Rain, Water, and the Sūrah's Own Imagery**

There is a final resonance in the word *maṭar* that ties the narrative to the sūrah's earlier verses. In 7:57 the sūrah described God driving clouds to a dead land and sending down water as mercy, and it drew the argument for the resurrection from the rain that brings a barren place to life. Here the same vocabulary is used for the punishment: a rain that kills rather than revives. The Book's imagery is deliberately capable of either direction, and what determines whether water is mercy or judgment is the same thing that determines whether Hūd's cloud is a season's hope or a wind of destruction.

The sūrah's parallels fill out what this verse compresses. Sūrat Hūd describes the instrument and its maker: *“When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay, marked by your Lord ˹O Prophet˺. And these stones are not far from the ˹pagan˺ wrongdoers!”* (11:82–83). Sūrat al-Ḥijr gives the hour and the deed: *“So the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them at sunrise. And We turned the cities upside down and rained upon them stones of baked clay”* (15:73–74). Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt states the same with the economy of this verse (51:33–34). And Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt records that the site was left as evidence: *“And We did leave ˹some of˺ its ruins as a clear lesson for people of understanding”* (29:35) — ruins on the route the sūrah's first audience travelled north on its trade journeys. The command *then see* is therefore not rhetorical: it points to a road the reader had walked and to remains that outlived the town's laughter.

The sūrah's reader is left with the comparison and the imperative. The rain fell on Sodom while the message was still in the town's streets; Lot's counsel was still audible in the ears of the men who mocked it. Verse 84's request — *then see* — is thus addressed to people who have the whole account in front of them and to their own conduct, and the sūrah has given them the interpretive tools in advance: the scale of verse 8, the parables of the land, the soil that answers the rain and the soil that does not.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:85

> **And unto Midian, their brother, Shuʿayb. He said, “O my people! Worship God! You have no god other than Him. There has come unto you a clear proof from your Lord. So observe fully the measure and the balance and diminish not people’s goods, nor work corruption upon the earth after it has been set aright. That is better for you, if you are believers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet of the Market**

*Wa-ilā Madyana akhāhum Shuʿayban* — and to Madyan, their brother Shuʿayb. The sūrah's last town before Moses is a trading city, and the crime it is charged with is the one its economy makes possible: *fa-awfū al-kayla wa-l-mīzān* — so give full measure and weight — *wa-lā tabkhasū al-nāsa ashyāʾahum* — and do not defraud people of their goods.

The Book's association of prophecy with commercial honesty is one of its most distinctive features, and this verse states the connection at its strongest: the messenger's first duty after the call to worship is the correction of the scales. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn opens the Qur'an's only sūrah named for a mercantile crime: *“Woe to those who give short measure, who, when they take from people, demand it in full, but when they measure or weigh for them, give less”* (83:1–3). Sūrat al-Isrāʾ lists the honest scale among the commandments of the Decalogue tradition: *“And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with an accurate balance. That is better and fairer in the end”* (17:35). And Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records Shuʿayb's version of this verse with an addition about the danger of the practice: *“and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land.”* (26:183).

**The Order of the Commandments**

The verse's arrangement repays attention. It begins with the sūrah's standard formula — worship the One God — and then moves immediately to a list of economic obligations: full measure, honest weight, no defrauding of what belongs to others. The sūrah does not treat the second as a mere corollary of the first; it presents them as one message, and the Book's prophets are consistently the defenders of both. Sūrat al-Nisāʾ turns the connection into a warning: *“O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent. And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.”* (4:29), and Sūrat al-Baqarah's catalogue of the people of the Book includes the crime of consuming wealth unjustly and of devouring the property of the weak (2:188).

The Madyan narrative in Sūrat Hūd records the town's own understanding of Shuʿayb's demand, and their objection is revealing: *“They asked ˹sarcastically˺, ‘O Shu’aib! Does your prayer command you that we should abandon what our forefathers worshipped or give up managing our wealth as we please? Indeed, you are such a tolerant, sensible man!’”* (11:87). The answer implicit in the verse is that the question marks the boundary the message crosses: the worship of God is inseparable from the use of the scales, and the town's assumption that trade is a zone of moral freedom is precisely what Shuʿayb was sent to deny.

**"Do Not Spread Corruption in the Land"**

*Wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍ mufsidīn* — and do not spread corruption in the land — this is the third time the sūrah has used the prohibition, after its general instruction at 7:56 and Ṣāliḥ's warning at 7:74, and in this town it acquires a sharp economic meaning. The verb *ʿathā* names destructive action, and its objects in the Book's usage are crops, property, and people. A market in which the buyer cannot trust the measure is not a private vice; it is the mechanism by which a whole town's wealth is transferred to its unscrupulous, and it corrupts the land in the precise sense that it makes the exchange of goods — the lifeblood of a trading city — unsafe.

The Qur'an's later treatment of Madyan states the outcome in the Book's own idiom: *“So they rejected him, and ˹so˺ were overtaken by the torment of the day of the ˹deadly˺ cloud. That was really a torment of a tremendous day.”* (26:189), and Sūrat Hūd records the cry and the quake that took them (11:94–95). The narrative's severity is proportionate to the crime's social reach: a town that cheats its customers is punishing the strangers who have no clan to defend them, and the Book's sentence on it is the same as its sentence on the towns that mocked the prophets.

**"That Is Better for You, If You Are Believers"**

*Dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum muʾminīn* — that is better for you, if you are believers. The clause is the sūrah's central practical claim, and it is worth noting that it is an argument from advantage rather than from fear. Shuʿayb does not say that the short measure will be paid for in the Fire; he says that honesty is better. The Book makes the same move in the story of Madyan's fuller version, where Shuʿayb urges his people with a reminder of God's own largesse: *“my provision comes from Him; He has given me good things”* (11:88).

The Book's economic doctrine will be stated in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's later verse: *“O believers, do not consume your wealth among yourselves in falsehood, but only in trade by mutual consent”* (4:29 in its own sūrah), and its view of the market is that a working economy is a form of the trust the sūrah has been describing since its first pages. The hadith the tradition received on the same point is among the shortest and sharpest in the corpus: the Prophet ﷺ passed a heap of grain, put his hand into it, found its lower part damp, and said, *“Whoever cheats is not one of us”* (Muslim 102, from Abū Hurayrah). Verse 85 is that hadith's doctrinal foundation, and the sūrah's last town is judged by whether it could be trusted with a scale.

**The Prophet Who Speaks First to the Merchants**

Madyan's position in the sūrah's sequence is deliberate. Noah's city, ʿĀd, and Thamūd were countries of tenants, shepherds, and builders; Sodom was a city of transgression; Madyan is the town of commerce, and its prophet is the only one in the sequence whose message begins with a lesson in accounting. The sūrah's arrangement thus covers the whole range of the peninsula's societies and gives each one a prophet whose specific charge matches its specific temptation. The reader is meant to see that no department of life stands outside the message — least of all the one that measures, weighs, and takes.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:86

> **And do not lie in wait on every path, threatening and turning away those who believe in Him from the way of God, and seeking to make it crooked. And remember when you were few, and He made you many. And behold how the workers of corruption fared in the end!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ambush on the Road**

*Wa-lā taqʿudū bi-kulli ṣirāṭin tūʿidūna wa-taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāh man āmana bihi wa-tabghūnahā ʿiwajan* — and do not sit in ambush on every road, threatening and hindering from the path of Allah those who believe in Him, and seeking to make it crooked. The verse is the sūrah's most concrete description of the crime it announced at 7:45: the definition of the *ẓālimūn* as those who turn others from the road of God and seek to make it crooked is here given a location, a method, and a target.

The location is *every road* — the trade routes of Madyan, the caravan tracks that made the town's economy and connected the peninsula. The method is intimidation: *tūʿidūna* — you threaten, issuing warnings to travelers, taxing, harassing, and turning back the ones who might have come to hear the message. The target is stated with an exactness that the sūrah's earlier verse left implicit: *man āmana bihi* — whoever believes in Him. The town's roadblocks were not set against strangers in general but against believers in particular, and the sūrah's phrasing suggests an organized policy rather than casual banditry.

**The Book's Interest in Roads**

The Qur'an's concern with the safety of the roads is not incidental; the roads are how revelation travels and how the community of believers grows. The Book's prohibition of highway robbery appears in its legal and moral codes (5:33; 29:29 in the Madyan context), and its description of a believing merchant's world is one where the movement of goods and persons is protected. The Prophet's own city would be built on a trade route, and the security of the roads would become one of the practical concerns of the Sharīʿah.

Madyan's crime is therefore the inverse of the sūrah's own opening command: where verse 3 told the Prophet's audience to follow what has been sent down and not to take other guardians, the town of Madyan physically stood in the way of anyone who tried. The narrative makes the town's judgment the most socially detailed of the five, and it is the only one whose crime is described as an ongoing public operation — people stationed on roads with threats.

**"Remember When You Were Few"**

*Wa-dhkurū idh kuntum qalīlan fa-kaththarakum* — and remember when you were few and He multiplied you. The verse turns from the crime to the town's history, and it uses the same argumentation as Ḥūd's address to ʿĀd and Ṣāliḥ's to Thamūd: the community's present standing is a gift with a history. Madyan had been small, marginal, and dependent; it had grown into a settled trading center; and the measure of its growth is also the measure of its obligation.

The Book's use of demographic history as a religious argument is consistent from its earliest sūrahs. Sūrat al-Takāthur mocks the city's pride in numbers (102:1–2); Sūrat al-Naḥl reminds the Makkans of their weakness and God's protection (16:106 vicinity; cf. 8:26: *“Remember when you were few and oppressed in the land”* — a verse addressed to the first Muslim community in Madinah). Verse 86's instruction to Madyan is thus part of a general pattern: the Book reads a community's success as a ledger entry, not as a trophy.

**"See What Was the End of the Corrupters"**

*Wa-nẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn* — and see what the end of the corrupters was. The closing clause returns to the second person plural and points the town at the fate of its predecessors, which the sūrah has just narrated. Noah's town, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Sodom are the visible record the verse is invoking; Madyan's merchants, whose caravans passed the ruins the sūrah's earlier verse 69 called to the memory of ʿĀd, are being asked to read the same landscape they traded through.

The term *mufsidūn* — corrupters — is the sūrah's own word for what the towns were (7:74, 7:85, 7:86), and the verse binds the whole sequence together: the crime of Madyan, in the Book's framework, is the same crime as the crime of the towns before it, committed in a different department of life. The cheated customer, the threatened believer, and the bribed official are the same phenomenon in the sūrah's moral grammar — a community treating what does not belong to it as its own.

**The Second Half of the Sūrah's Definition**

It is worth setting this verse beside 7:45, because the sūrah has now supplied the full portrait it announced there. That verse defined the wrongdoers as those who hinder others from the path, seek to make it crooked, and deny the Hereafter. Verse 86 supplies a working example of the first two, and the surrounding narrative supplies the third: Madyan's elite will reject Shuʿayb's message and threaten expulsion (7:88), and their confidence is built on the assumption that no account is being kept.

The sūrah's arrangement makes this the last of the towns because it is the most immediately recognizable to its own audience. Makkah was a trading city; its notables were merchants; its caravans passed Madyan's ruins on the road north; and the sūrah's readers were about to be asked, in their own streets, what a scale is worth and what a believer's road is for. The verse's imperative — *see* — is addressed as directly to them as it was to the town.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:87

> **If a group of you believe in that wherewith I have been sent, and a group of you believe not, then be patient till God shall judge between us, and He is the best of judges.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Be Patient Until Allah Judges"**

*Wa-in ṭāʾifatun minkum āmanū bi-lladhī ursiltu bihi wa-ṭāʾifatun lam yuʾminū fa-ṣbirū ḥattā yaḥkuma Allāhu baynanā* — and if a group among you has believed in what I have been sent with, and another group has not, then be patient until Allah judges between us; for He is the best of judges.

The verse is the sūrah's clearest statement of the prophetic method, and it arrives at the point in the Madyan narrative where the town's division has become visible. Shuʿayb does not call for the believers to separate, does not threaten the unbelievers with the community's authority, and does not offer the town a deadline. He proposes patience, and the reason he gives is that judgment belongs to God.

**The Argument Against Compulsion**

The Qur'an's most famous statement on coercion is Sūrat al-Baqarah's *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.”* (2:256), and verse 87 is one of the places where the principle is displayed in the conduct of a prophet. The Book's messengers do not enforce; they convey, warn, and leave the verdict to its owner. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the same logic to Noah: *“So judge between me and them decisively, and save me and the believers with me”* (26:118), and this sūrah gives Shuʿayb the prayer it prepares: *“Our Lord! Judge between us and our people with truth. You are the best of those who judge”* (7:89).

The doctrine is important in the sūrah's own historical setting, where the towns' elites enjoy the power of coercion and use it. The narrative's inversion is total: the group with the police is the group that rejects the message, and the messenger's only weapon is a wait. The Book's later legislative sūrahs will build the community on the same premise — belief cannot be produced by force — and the Prophet's own career would be a long education in patience with a hostile majority before any question of governance arose.

**The Community in Transit**

The verse also gives a picture of a community in the middle of a decision, and the picture is unsparing in its realism: some believe, some do not, and both groups are standing in the same marketplace, trading with one another, sharing the same wells. The Book does not describe the believers of Madyan as a separate enclave; the sūrah's earlier narrative of Ṣāliḥ showed the believers as the town's underclass (7:75), and Shuʿayb's town has its own version of the split.

That realism is the basis of the sūrah's instruction to patience. A community that must live beside its opponents for years needs a discipline that does not depend on the outcome of the argument. The Book names it in the sūrah of the Cave: *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening”* (18:28), and in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr's summary of salvation: *“except those who believe and do good and urge one another to truth and urge one another to patience”* (103:3). The verse of the Aʿrāf supplies the same discipline to a merchant town that has just been told its scales are crooked.

**"He Is the Best of Judges"**

*Wa-huwa khayru al-ḥākimīn.* The phrase is one of the Qur'an's recurring doxological formulas, and it appears at points where human judgment is being contrasted with divine: Sūrat Yūnus has the Prophet told to follow what is revealed and to be patient *until Allah judges, for He is the best of judges* (10:109); Sūrat Yūsuf puts the phrase in the mouth of Jacob's sons (12:80); Sūrat al-Tīn closes with the same question: *“Is not Allah the best of judges?”* (95:8).

In the sūrah's narrative, the phrase has a practical edge: the town has judges of its own — the elite who will shortly threaten Shuʿayb with expulsion (7:88) — and the verse is relocating the case to a higher court. The messenger's patience is not passivity; it is the transfer of a case that the local bench cannot try fairly. The Book's audiences have always read this verse as the charter of a community that does not take the law into its own hands, and the Madyan narrative remains its canonical exhibit.

**The Waiting as a Position**

The Book's readers have noticed that Shuʿayb's patience is not the patience of a man without an argument. He has just delivered the most detailed of the sūrah's messages: the call to worship, the scale, the prohibition of defrauding, the warning about the ambush on the roads. Verse 87 comes after that case is complete, and the sequence matters: the patience is prescribed to a community that has said what it had to say, and its function is to prevent the messenger's followers from thinking that the lack of an immediate verdict means the case has failed.

That is the sūrah's own lesson to its audience in Makkah, which was watching the same wait unfold in its own streets. The Book's instruction to its Prophet in the middle of the persecution uses the same verb and the same limit: *“So be patient, as the messengers of firm resolve were patient, and do not seek to hasten the punishment for them”* (46:35 in its vicinity). Madyan's verse is the model for that instruction, and it says what the Qur'an says everywhere: the delay is not the absence of the judgment but the mercy that precedes it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:88

> **The notables among his people who were arrogant said, “We shall surely expel you, O Shuʿayb, and those who believe along with you from our town, unless you revert to our creed.” He said, “What! Even though we are unwilling?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Threat of Expulsion**

*Qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna istakbarū min qawmihi la-nukhrijannaka yā Shuʿaybu wa-lladhīna āmanū maʿaka min qaryatinā aw la-taʿūdunna fī millatinā* — the arrogant chiefs of his people said: we will surely expel you, O Shuʿayb, and those who believe with you, from our town, unless you return to our religion.

The sentence is the sūrah's most explicit statement of the towns' ultimate argument. Sodom had said *expel them*; Madyan formalizes it as a policy, with a condition attached: return to our religion and you may stay. The Book's record of the exchange shows the towns moving from argument to coercion over the course of the sequence, and it makes the coercive threat the last position of a losing case. The elite of Madyan cannot answer Shuʿayb's case about the scales, so they answer it with the town's boundary.

**The Word Millah**

*Millah* is the Book's term for a community's religion regarded as a shared way of life — the word used of Abraham's way (2:130, 3:95, 16:123), of the religion of the idolaters in Joseph's prison (12:37), and of the faith of the Prophet in Sūrat al-Kāfirūn's context (109:6, *“to you your religion and to me my religion”*). That Shuʿayb's opponents call their system a *millah* shows what they are defending: not a doctrine but a community's inheritance, the package of customs that made Madyan Madyan. The verse's threat is therefore an identity claim: conform, or leave.

The Qur'an's own address to the Prophet would later use the same word in the opposite direction, commanding him to follow the *millah* of Abraham (16:123), which is the Book's way of acknowledging that communities do possess corporate identities — and of insisting that the identity be traceable to a prophet rather than to an ancestor's invention.

**"Even If We Detest It?"**

*Qāla a-wa-law kunnā kārihīn* — he said: even if we detest it? The reply is three words in Arabic and it dismantles the threat. The elite's proposal had treated religion as a matter of choice in principle; Shuʿayb's question reveals what the choice actually involves. They are asking him to profess a faith he regards as false, and the interrogative — *even if we hate it?* — forces them to say aloud what coercion means.

The Book has a consistent position on this demand, and it states it in several registers. Sūrat al-Baqarah forbids compulsion in religion (2:256); Sūrat al-Naḥl's command to the Prophet to invite to the way of the Lord *with wisdom and good counsel* (16:125) rules out the alternative; Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah tells the Prophet, in the same sūrah, that he is not a controller over them (88:22). And the Book's own account of the hypocrites in Madinah makes the same point from the other side: a profession produced by pressure has no spiritual content, and the Book treats it as worse than the honest rejection because it corrupts the community's integrity.

Shuʿayb's question remains one of the sharpest three-word arguments in the Book, and its application reaches any community tempted to measure loyalty by recitation rather than by conviction.

**The Expulsion and the Sūrah's History**

The threat of expulsion is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the sūrah's portrait of the towns' last instrument. The narrative will note that the punishment took the town before the threat could be executed — Sūrat Hūd records the cry and the earthquake that seized them (11:94–95) — and the Book's pattern in such cases is to let the persecuted community outlive its persecutors. The sūrah's Makkan audience, hearing the verse recited, had its own expulsion in view: the Prophet's plan to leave a city whose establishment had made the same calculation.

The verse's details are also worth noting for what they say about the believers of Madyan: the threat is addressed to *those who believe with you*, which means the community had grown visible enough to be a targeted group. The sūrah's earlier narratives had let the believers of the towns appear in a single sentence; here they are a party whose removal is the town's condition for peace.

**The Last Resort of a Bankrupt Case**

The sūrah's sequence has now given the towns an inventory of arguments: Noah's chiefs used a label, ʿĀd used a taunt, Thamūd killed a sign, Sodom expelled a neighbour. Madyan's elite produce the same instrument as Sodom's — the boundary and the exit — but the sūrah places it in the mouth of the town's leadership as a formal decision, which is the clearest statement the sequence offers about what is really at stake. The town's rulers did not fear Shuʿayb's God; they feared that his followers would remain in Madyan and prove that another way of life was possible there.

That fear is the sūrah's diagnosis of the towns' motives, and it is stated by the verse's own vocabulary: *alladhīna istakbarū*, those who had made themselves great. The expulsion order comes from men whose standing is threatened by a scale that measures honestly. The narrative's judgment on them is delivered a verse later, in the answer of the prophet they proposed to expel, and the answer is a refusal to trade the truth for a residence permit.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:89

> **We would be fabricating a lie against God were we to revert to your creed after God had delivered us from it. It is not for us to revert thereto unless God, our Lord, should will. Our Lord encompasses all things in knowledge. In God do we trust. Our Lord! Decide between us and our people in truth, and Thou art the best of deciders.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Then We Would Have Fabricated a Lie Against Allah"**

*Qad iftaraynā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban in ʿudnā fī millatikum baʿda idh najjānā Allāhu minhā* — we would have fabricated a lie against Allah if we returned to your religion after Allah saved us from it. The answer to the expulsion threat is the sūrah's clearest statement of the cost of a return, and it names the cost with the vocabulary the sūrah reserves for the gravest crime in its book: *iftirāʾ*, the fabrication of a lie against God.

The term has been in the sūrah since verse 37, where the interrogative formed its most solemn sentence: *who is more wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God?* For Shuʿayb to say that a return to Madyan's religion would make him one of those people is to accept the town's frame and reverse its polarity. The elite had argued in terms of public order; the messenger answers in terms of truth\. To profess a religion he knows to be false in order to keep a place in a town is not prudence but a claim about God that God has not made.

**"After Allah Has Saved Us From It"**

The clause *baʿda idh najjānā Allāhu minhā* adds a second layer: what the town offers as a homecoming, Shuʿayb describes as a rescue already accomplished. The believer's relationship to his former religion is not that of a person who has left a village but of someone who has been taken out of a ruin. The Book's vocabulary for conversion in several places uses this language of deliverance: Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own account of the followers of the messengers, Sūrat al-Māʾidah's promise that God will bring a people whom He loves, and Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's description of the one who says, *“I was among the heedless”*. The town's proposal is an offer to return to what the messenger regards as the disease from which he has been cured.

The Book's later sūrahs make the refusal the duty of the community and the point of its identity: *“Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment”* (16:106). The narrative places that absolute in the mouth of a prophet who is being offered a practical way out.

**"Unless Allah Wills"**

*Wa-mā yakūnu lanā an naʿūda fīhā illā an yashāʾa Allāhu rabbunā* — and it is not for us to return to it unless Allah our Lord wills. The exception is one of the Book's characteristic qualifications, and it is a statement about the limits of the human will rather than a loophole in the refusal. The Qur'an places the clause on the believer's tongue in matters of resolve: *“you do not will unless Allah wills”* (76:30; cf. 81:29), and it teaches the community to say *God willing* about its own plans.

The clause protects the refusal from the sin of claiming absolute autonomy. Shuʿayb does not say *we will never return even if God wills it*; he says that a return is not open to them as agents, because the human will is itself a created thing. The Book's doctrine of the will is stated most precisely in Sūrat al-Insān's closing verse: *“what you will is only what Allah wills”* (76:30, from His decree). The phrase is thus the deepest level of the verse's answer to the town: even the refusal to return is not a personal achievement.

**"Our Lord Encompasses All Things in Knowledge"**

*Wasiʿa rabbunā kulla shayʾin ʿilman* — our Lord encompasses all things in knowledge. The Book's omniscience verses typically appear at decisive moments, and this one is placed where the town's leaders are weighing a private calculation and the messenger is declaring that no calculation is private. Sūrat al-Talāq's famous verse uses the same concept: *“Allah is the One who created seven heavens... that you may know that Allah has power over all things and that Allah encompasses all things in knowledge”* (65:12). Sūrat al-Anʿām states it in a form that would make any conspiracy absurd: *“And with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them but He; and He knows whatever is on the land and in the sea; and not a leaf falls but He knows it”* (6:59).

**"In Allah We Put Our Trust"**

*ʿAlā Allāhi tawakkalnā* — in Allah we have placed our trust. The Book's *tawakkul* is the disposition of a person who has done what can be done and then released the outcome, and the sūrah of the family of ʿImrān explains the sequence: *“And when you have decided, then place your trust in Allah; surely Allah loves those who trust”* (3:159). Shuʿayb's community had delivered its case, refused the coercion, and been told to wait; the trust is the state in which the waiting is possible.

The declared trust is also the community's answer to the threat of expulsion. What Madyan controlled was a place in Madyan, and the verse's sentence says that the believers' security is held elsewhere. The Book's later account of the believers' answer to Pharaoh uses the same formula: *“Your rage towards us is only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us. Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting ˹to You˺.’”* (7:126 vicinity; 10:85).

**The Prayer for Judgment**

*Rabbanā iftaḥ baynanā wa-bayna qawminā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-anta khayru al-fātiḥīn* — our Lord, judge between us and our people with truth; You are the best of judges. The closing petition is the sūrah's own version of the prophets' final resource. ʿĀd's messenger was told to wait; Noah was given a century; Shuʿayb asks for the verdict. The verb *fataḥa* — to open, to decide, to give the opening judgment — is used for legal and military verdicts throughout the Book, and the psalm-like echo of the request in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's later narratives (26:118) shows how consistently the Qur'an's prophets place the dispute in the court of the One who cannot err.

The prayer is also the seed of the town's destruction; the Book records that Madyan's end arrived as the day of the shadow (26:189). The sūrah's narrative will report the outcome without comment. Its interest is in the sentence that precedes it, in which the messenger's case, the town's coercion, and the community's willingness to wait are all on the record — and in a prayer that reads, in retrospect, like the last correspondence of a doomed city with its Creator.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:90

> **The notables among his people who disbelieved said, “Verily if you follow Shuʿayb, you shall surely be the losers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"If You Follow Shuʿayb, You Will Be the Losers"**

*Wa-qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qawmihi la-in ittabaʿtum Shuʿayban innakum idhan la-khāsirūn* — and the disbelieving chiefs of his people said: if you follow Shuʿayb, you will then be the losers. The town's last argument is not about God, revelation, or the resurrection. It is about the price of conversion, and the sūrah reports it in the plainest commercial grammar Madyan knew.

The word *khāsirūn* — losers — belongs to the ledger that this town's narrative has been about since its first verse: short measure, honest weight, defrauded goods, ambushed caravans. The elite of Madyan spoke the vocabulary of the market, and the sentence they choose is a market assessment: a man who follows a reformer in a trading city forfeits his standing with the men who control the routes. Sūrat al-Zumar reports the same calculus in the mouth of the Makkan leadership: *“The chiefs of the disbelievers say to the believers: if you follow the path we are on, we will carry your sins”* (29:12 in its vicinity; cf. 39:15's verdict on the true losers).

**The Cost of Conversion in a Trading Town**

The sūrah's setting makes verse 90 a study in social pressure. Madyan's believers were not being asked to accept a doctrine in private; they were being told that the town's notables would treat adherence to Shuʿayb as a form of commercial suicide. The Book's portrait of such pressure appears throughout its Makkan material: the believers of the Prophet's town were confronted with the loss of family protection (the *ḥilf* system), the boycotts, and the economic leverage of the Quraysh.

Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt describes the mechanism as a test: *“Do people think once they say, ‘We believe,” that they will be left without being put to the test? … We certainly tested those before them. And ˹in this way˺ Allah will clearly distinguish between those who are truthful and those who are liars.’* (29:2–3). And Sūrat al-Baqarah's lines about the cost of fidelity are aimed precisely at merchants who feared the loss of their livelihood: *“Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, ‘When will …”* (2:214 in its wording; cf. 2:155). Madyan's elite were delivering the warning in advance, and the sūrah's reader is meant to hear in their sentence the voice of every establishment that has priced loyalty.

**The Book's Answer: a Different Ledger**

The Qur'an's rebuttal of the town's arithmetic is not that losing money does not matter; it is that the town's ledger omits its largest column. Sūrat al-Tawbah puts the whole sequence in commercial terms: *“O believers, shall I show you a trade that will save you from a painful punishment?”* — followed by faith, struggle, and the promise of forgiveness, gardens, and noble dwellings (61:10–13 in Sūrat al-Ṣaff; 9:111 uses the language of purchase outright: *“Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their wealth in exchange for Paradise”*).

The sūrah of al-Baqarah states the failure of the alternative with the same metaphor: *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (2:16). Against that, the Book's promise to those who pay the price of following is stated in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf's own later chapters and in Sūrat al-Naḥl: *“Whoever emigrates in the way of Allah will find on the earth many a refuge and abundance”* (4:100); *“Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely give them a good life”* (16:97).

**The Word That Comes Back**

The sūrah's sharpest composition is the word's return. Verse 90's townsmen call the believers *losers*; verse 92 — two verses later, after the earthquake and the city's erasure — declares, in the Book's own voice, that the ones who rejected Shuʿayb *were the true losers*. The Qur'an performs this reversal repeatedly: the mocking sentence of the disbelievers is quoted back as a verdict, sometimes with the same vocabulary, sometimes with the same syntax. Sūrat al-Anʿām's version puts the challenge and the answer in a single exchange: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O my people! Persist in your ways, for I ˹too˺ will persist in mine. You will soon know who will fare best in the end. Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed.’”* (6:135).

Madyan's elite had the accounting exactly backwards, and the sūrah lets its reader watch the correction arrive within two lines. The narrative's point is not that the believers would eventually be compensated; it is that the towns were already, at the moment of their taunt, inside a ledger whose balance they could not see. The Book's word for that condition is the one it uses in the verse about the sealed hearts a few lines later: a people who cannot hear what is being said about them (7:100).

**The Threat That Failed**

The verse's function in the narrative is to record the elite's last instrument before the punishment. The sūrah's towns have run through their repertoire — arguments from ancestry, demands for signs, mockery, expulsion orders — and Madyan's ending is the bluntest of them all: a warning about the cost of dissent. What the record shows is that the threat accomplished nothing. The believers did not recant; the messenger did not moderate; and the town was destroyed without its notables having gained a day.

The Book's later history would prove the same point at scale. The trade routes of Madyan's region passed to other cities; the Muslims who had been warned of loss inherited the commercial networks of the Near East within a century; and the verse's sentence survives as the sūrah's standard example of a ruling class mistaking the future for an extension of the present. The town bet on the permanence of its own arrangements, and the sūrah's next verse reports what happened to the bettors.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:91

> **So the earthquake seized them, and morning found them lying lifeless in their abode.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earthquake at Madyan**

*Fa-akhadhat-humu al-rajfatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn* — so the tremor seized them, and they became, in their homes, fallen on their knees. The sūrah closes Madyan with the exact formula it used for Thamūd at 7:78: same instrument, same posture, same morning. The towns have been presented to the reader as a series of variations on a theme, and the two identical endings bind Madyan and Thamūd into a single lesson.

The Qur'an's other accounts of Madyan's end preserve details the sūrah leaves out. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ describes the instrument as a shadow and a day: *“So they rejected him, and ˹so˺ were overtaken by the torment of the day of the ˹deadly˺ cloud. That was really a torment of a tremendous day.”* (26:189). Sūrat Hūd has the cry and the quake together and adds that the town's dwellings were left uninhabited: *“When Our command came, We saved Shu’aib and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us. And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes, … as if they had never lived there. So away with Midian as it was with …”* (11:94–95 in the vicinity). The Book's use of several terms for one event — cry, blast, quake, shadow — is characteristic: the destruction is described from the viewpoint of the people who experienced its different phases.

**The Word Rajfah**

*Rajfah* is the shudder, the violent trembling of the ground; the Book uses it for Thamūd (7:78, 15:83 in the sense of a cry and quake), for the punishment that seized the people of Shuʿayb here (7:91), and for the quake at the destruction of the town in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt (29:37). Its choice for a trading city is deliberate in a Book that matches penalties to settings: Madyan's wealth was stored in stone and mud-brick, its caravans were somewhere on the road, and its people were confident in the solidity of the ground they had built on.

The Qur'an's habit of selecting the punishment a town could not imagine is consistent through the sūrah. Noah's people, who travelled by land, were drowned; ʿĀd, who considered themselves unassailable, were destroyed by a wind; Thamūd, who carved their security from the rock, were shaken out of it; Sodom, whose walls were famous, were destroyed from above and below. The Book's narrations of these endings are short because the interest is never in the mechanics, but the choice of each instrument is a sentence on the town's own account of its strengths.

**"In Their Homes" and the Morning**

*Fa-aṣbaḥū* — and they became in the morning — is a favourite construction of the sūrah's narratives and the Book's warnings more generally. The ending of a people is typically reported at dawn: Sūrat al-Ḥijr gives Sodom the sunrise and the cry (15:73); Sūrat Hūd has Madyan's destruction in the early morning (11:94 vicinity); the sūrah's own 7:97 will ask the Makkans whether they feel secure from a punishment that arrives *by night while they sleep*, or *by day while they play*. The morning is the hour when a community is most confident of the day ahead; the Book's verdicts arrive there.

The clause *fī dārihim* — in their homes, in their own dwelling — has the same function as it did for Thamūd: it converts the town's private architecture into a display of the punishment. The sūrah's reader is invited to picture the merchants of Madyan at home, in the rooms built from the profits of the trade they defended against the messenger's warning.

**The Town Without a Legacy**

Madyan's disappearance in the Book is complete, and the sūrah states it in the next verse: the town became *as though it had never been lived in* (7:92). The Book's interest in the destruction is not archaeological; it is the erased record that matters. The town's practices, its measures, its caravans, its liturgies, and its arguments all vanished, while the message that its elite had priced as a threat to their standing continued to be recited in every Muslim city within a generation.

That contrast is the sūrah's own running theme, and the sequence of the towns has now completed its purpose. Five communities, five messengers, five refusals, and five endings compressed into the few verses the sūrah allots each — with one difference: Madyan's death is followed by the sūrah's fullest reflection on the meaning of the entire series, a passage that will generalize the pattern for every society left standing. The next verses are the sūrah's commentary on the ruins it has just described.

**What the Earthquake Confirms**

The town's threat in verse 90 was that believers would become losers. The quake in verse 91 answers the threat without a word of commentary, because the sentence that follows it will do the talking. The sūrah moves from the domestic image to the monetary verdict with the same unadorned grammar it used for the scale and the gates, and it leaves the reader with a picture rather than an argument: the men who owned the roads of Madyan, fallen in their own houses in the posture the Book keeps for the condemned at the Judgment.

The narrative's speed is part of its severity. The sūrah does not grant Madyan a farewell scene in which its elite reconsider, and it does not report a single conversion from the town's leadership after the threat. The Book's histories are written this way because they are warnings rather than biographies, and the warning to the sūrah's own city was direct: the same threat, the same commerce, and the same option were on the table in Makkah, which read Madyan's story on the same page as its own fate.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:92

> **Those who denied Shuʿayb, it was as though they had never dwelt there. Those who denied Shuʿayb, they themselves were the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The True Losers"**

*Alladhīna kadhdhabū Shuʿayban ka-an lam yaghnaw fīhā* — those who rejected Shuʿayb became as though they had never lived there — *alladhīna kadhdhabū Shuʿayban kānū humu al-khāsirīn* — those who rejected Shuʿayb were the true losers.

The verse answers the town's sentence of two verses earlier, and it answers it in the town's own currency. The elite had warned the townsmen that following Shuʿayb would make them losers; the sūrah assigns the word to the elite and to everyone who accepted their warning. The Book performs this reversal throughout its narratives — the accusers' vocabulary is quoted back as the verdict — and verse 92 is one of the cleanest examples of the technique, because the same Arabic word *khāsirūn* carries both sentences and only the subject has changed.

**"As Though They Had Never Lived There"**

The image is the sūrah's most complete statement of what the destruction of a society means. *Ka-an lam yaghnaw fīhā* — as if they had never been settled there, never prospered there, never been at home. The verb *ghaniya* carries dwelling and sufficiency at once: it is the word for being settled in a place and the word for being well off in it. Madyan's disappearance erases both at the same time.

The Book uses the phrase for other destroyed towns and gives it additional weight each time. Sūrat Hūd's account of Thamūd ends with the same formula — *as though they had never lived there* (11:68) — and its account of Madyan repeats it (11:95). In both cases the phrase follows the observation that the towns' messengers had delivered their warnings and that the towns had possessed every material reason to listen. The Book's choice of words makes the erasure total: not conquered, not merely defeated, but unwritten — their buildings, names, and generations absorbed into a landscape that no longer remembered them.

**Why the Book Erases**

Modern readings of the sūrah sometimes take the verse for a piece of poetry about history's indifference. The Book's own framing of the same image is theological and, in its idiom, exact. Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ states the principle that the erasure is a judgment: *“How many societies have We destroyed that had been spoiled by their comfortable living — those are their dwellings, hardly inhabited after them, and We were the inheritors”* (28:58). Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt's version adds the morning: *“So their homes are there, uninhabited after them”* (27:52 in the vicinity of the same theme; cf. 29:38).

The theological claim is that a community's existence is a tenure, and its continuity is the gift of the One who granted it. When the Book says a town became as if it had never prospered in it, the sentence is not about memory; it is about ownership. The land's real proprietor had reclaimed what He had lent, and the record of the tenants was closed without ceremony.

**The Sūrah's Answer to the Threat**

The verse also completes the town's transformation from the inside. In verse 90 the elite spoke for the town's future and offered its members a choice: the message or their livelihood. In verse 92 the same town is described in two clauses, both of which name it by its rejection — not Madyan, not the merchants, not the tribe, but *those who rejected Shuʿayb*. The Book's habit of naming its communities by their response rather than their lineage is one of the ways it keeps the sequence of towns from reading as an ethnic history. The subject of both clauses is defined by what it did with the message.

And the repetition of the verb *kadhdhabū* — they rejected, they called him a liar — is the sūrah's way of fixing responsibility. The disaster did not befall a town; it befell a rejection. Everything the town had — its position on the caravan routes, its water, its skilled labour, its generations — was available to it as long as it was, and the Book's verdict treats the rejection as the cause of its removal from the land.

**The Loss That the Word Names**

The final clause's construction — *humu al-khāsirūn*, they are the true losers — is emphatic and restrictive in Arabic: the pronoun separates the subject and the definite noun claims the category. The sūrah's scale has already provided the definition: whoever comes to the Day with a light scale has lost himself (7:9). The elite of Madyan spent their political capital trying to prove that devotion to a prophet would bankrupt their city; the sūrah's answer is that the city's actual bankruptcy was recorded in a different account, under a different entry, and on a day when its notables had nothing left to protect.

The verse's position — between the quake and the messenger's farewell — is also significant. The reader meets the town's erasure and its verdict before he hears Shuʿayb speak again, and when the messenger does speak, in the next verse, he will not gloat over the ruins. His sentence returns to the same vocabulary the sūrah's prophets have used throughout, and the reader is reminded that the one person who had the right to be satisfied with the outcome was the one who had spent his life trying to avoid it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:93

> **So he turned away from them and said, “O my people! I indeed delivered unto you the messages of my Lord, and advised you sincerely. So how can I grieve for a disbelieving people?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Shuʿayb's Farewell**

*Fa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā qawmi laqad ablaghtukum risālāti rabbī wa-naṣaḥtu lakum fa-kayfa āsā ʿalā qawmin kāfirīn* — so he turned away from them and said: O my people, I have delivered to you the messages of my Lord and given you sincere advice; so how could I grieve for a people who disbelieve?

The sūrah's fifth town closes with the third of its farewells, and the differences between them measure the sūrah's composition. Noah left a drowned world for an ark; Ṣāliḥ left a city prostrate and spoke to it of counsel that was disliked; Shuʿayb leaves a town erased from the ground, and his final sentence is not about the town's response but about his own grief, ending in a question he answers himself. The Book's account of Madyan in Sūrat Hūd closes with the same erasure: *“as if they had never lived there. So away with Midian as it was with Thamûd!”* (11:95). The Hūd narrative also gives Shuʿayb a last line that is not grief but a warning: *“And watch! I too am watching with you!”* (11:93).

**"I Have Delivered the Messages of My Lord"**

*Laqad ablaghtukum risālāti rabbī* — I have delivered to you the messages of my Lord. The verb is the one the sūrah's messengers have used in their opening speeches (7:62, 7:68) and it returns here as the last word of the sequence's commercial prophet. The claim is not that the town heard, or that it understood, or that anyone was persuaded; it is that the transmission is complete, and the Book treats the completion as the messenger's whole responsibility.

The point is one the Book returns to in its instruction to the Prophet: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. … You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22 in its vicinity); *“Respond to evil with what is best. We know well what they claim. … And say, ‘My Lord! I seek refuge in You from the temptations of the devils. … And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, that they ˹even˺ come near me.’”* (23:96–98's sequence). The sūrah's own narrative has now shown five messengers fulfilling a duty whose results were not their property, and the farewells are the sūrah's way of setting the record straight about what a prophetic career is measured by.

**"Sincere Advice" — the Fourth Occurrence**

*Wa-naṣaḥtu lakum* — and I gave you sincere advice. The word *nāṣiḥ* has now appeared in the sūrah's sequence for the fourth time, after Noah (7:62), Ḥūd (7:68), and Ṣāliḥ (7:79), and its repetition across five towns is the sūrah's deliberately monotonous answer to the towns' accusations of folly. The messengers were not rulers, miracle-workers, or tribal champions; the Book's one-word summary of their function is the term for the man who mends what is torn without leaving a seam.

The sūrah's arrangement has the word do double duty. In Ṣāliḥ's farewell it names what the town refused to love (7:79). In Shuʿayb's it names what the messenger did anyway. The Book's account of that persistence is one of its quietest themes: the counseling continued through the insults, the threats, the expulsion order, and the ambush on the roads, and it continued to the day when the town's options closed.

**"How Could I Grieve?"**

*Fa-kayfa āsā ʿalā qawmin kāfirīn.* The question is rhetorical and its subject is the messenger's own capacity for sorrow — the Arabic *āsā* carries grief with the sense of consoling oneself. Shuʿayb's sentence does not say that he is glad the town is gone. It says that grief has nowhere to stand, because grief over a people requires that they still might have turned, and the sūrah records the towns' destruction as the closure of a decision rather than an accident.

The Book teaches the same discipline to the Prophet repeatedly, and its most reassuring form is in Sūrat al-Kahf: *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message.”* (18:6), followed by the sūrah's answer that the message is not the messenger's to enforce. Sūrat Fāṭir's instruction is the direct counterpart to Shuʿayb's question: *“Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good ˹like those who are rightly guided˺? ˹It is˺ certainly Allah ˹Who˺ leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides whoever He wills. So do not grieve yourself to death over …”* (35:8). The sūrah's farewell is thus not coldness; it is the boundary between a prophet's service and a final outcome that belongs to God.

**The End of the Five Towns**

Verse 93 ends the sūrah's first narrative cycle, and the sequence's design can now be seen whole. Five towns, five messengers, five summary messages — worship the One God, live by His limits — five refusals expressed through the local vocabulary of power, and five endings reported in a verse apiece. Noah's people drowned because they could not be drawn out of a routine; ʿĀd's vanished into a wind from the direction of their own optimism; Thamūd's were shaken out of the stone they trusted; Sodom's were erased from above and below; Madyan's, the last and the most like the sūrah's own audience, became as though they had never lived there.

The sūrah now begins its general reflection on what those towns mean. The verses that follow are no longer narrative but law — a statement of the pattern that governed all five histories and that now applies to every society on earth, including the one being addressed. Shuʿayb's farewell is the hinge: after it, the sūrah stops describing particular towns and starts describing the rule.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:94

> **We sent no prophet to a town but that We seized its people with misfortune and hardship, that haply they would humble themselves.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Law of Adversity**

*Wa-mā arsalnā fī qaryatin min nabiyyin illā akhadhnā ahlahā bi-l-baʾsāʾi wa-l-ḍarrāʾi laʿallahum yaḍḍarraʿūn* — and We never sent a prophet to any town without seizing its people with suffering and hardship, so that they might humble themselves. With this verse the sūrah stops telling the stories of towns and begins to state the law that governed them all.

The two nouns are a fixed pair in the Qur'an's language of trial: *baʾsāʾ* is the hardship that touches property and circumstances — poverty, hunger, loss — and *ḍarrāʾ* is the hardship that touches the body and the self — illness, pain, injury. The Book uses them together whenever it describes the afflictions by which a message is brought home: *“Indeed, We have sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ to other people who We put through suffering and adversity ˹for their denial˺, so perhaps they would be humbled”* (6:42), and the sūrah's own verse states the same purpose in the same pair of nouns (7:94). Sūrat al-Baqarah applies the pair to the Prophet's community: *“They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken”* (2:214).

**The Purpose Stated in the Verse**

*Laʿallahum yaḍḍarraʿūn* — so that they might humble themselves — the clause is the verse's whole doctrine, and its verb is one of the Qur'an's most precise religious terms. *Taḍarruʿ* is the self-abasement of a creature who has run out of resources; it is the root the sūrah used for the manner of supplication in its instruction at 7:55: *call upon your Lord humbly and secretly*. The Book's teaching here is that the hardships that visit a society are not random and not only retributive; they are summonses, and their intended outcome is the posture of a person who has stopped relying on his own strength.

Sūrat al-Anʿām's parallel passage uses the same word with the same purpose and adds a lament: *“We seized them with hardship and adversity so that they might humble themselves. Why then, when Our punishment came to them, did they not humble themselves? Instead their hearts hardened, and Satan made attractive to them what they were doing”* (6:42–43). The sūrah's verse hides the rest of the story in its optative form — *so that they might humble themselves* — and the next verse will show what actually happened in the towns the sūrah has been describing.

**Hardship as a Language**

The Qur'an's doctrine of the trial of nations is unlike a simple theory of punishment, and the difference is the purpose clause. A penal system looks backward at an offense; the Book's afflictions look forward at a possible change. The distinction appears in the Book's own use of the term *ibtilaʾ*, testing: the trials of a person or a community are described as exposure rather than merely as payment, and the object of the exposure is a response that only the tested can give.

Neither is the doctrine a denial of suffering's severity; the Book describes the destitution of the tested communities with an unromantic precision and regards their relief as a mercy when it comes. Sūrat Yūnus states the response that saves: *“When We give people a taste of mercy after being afflicted with a hardship, they swiftly devise plots against Our revelations! Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Allah is swifter in devising ˹punishment˺. Surely Our messengerangels record whatever you devise.’”* (10:21 in the vicinity). And the Qur'an's own account of the Prophet's community at Badr has the same structure at the level of the individual: hardship, then an answer, and the answer decides what the hardship was for.

**Why the Verse Precedes the Diagnosis**

The placement of 7:94 is worth noting: it arrives after all five towns and before the sūrah's closing analysis of them. The reader has just watched the towns destroyed, and the sūrah now tells him that the destruction was not the first word but the last — the first word was a period of hardship quietly addressed to each community while its messenger was still among it. The Book repairs the historical record before the reader can protest, and it does so with a single sentence covering every town it has described.

The remaining verses of the sūrah will state the rest of the sequence: what happened when prosperity replaced the hardship (7:95), what the towns said, and how the seizure came. The sūrah's reader is being prepared for a diagnosis of the whole phenomenon, and verse 94's function is to establish the premise that the destruction was always preceded by an invitation.

**The Hudaybiyyah Hadith and the Personal Scale**

The Book's law for nations has its counterpart in the Prophet's teaching about individuals, and the tradition preserved the analogy in a well-known report: *“No fatigue, nor disease, nor sorrow, nor sadness, nor hurt, nor distress befalls a Muslim — not even the prick of a thorn — but that Allah expiates some of his sins for it”* (al-Bukhārī 5641; Muslim 2573). The two doctrines operate at different scales, and both teach the same thing about the meaning of pain in the Book's universe: hardship is not evidence of abandonment, and the community that reads its troubles as a summons is the community that escapes the sūrah's next verse.

The sūrah's attention now turns to the outcome that makes the whole passage necessary — the astonishing fact that the towns, given hardship and then given relief, drew not humility but a false conclusion. That is the subject of the next line, and it is the sūrah's most exact account of how people misread their own history.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:95

> **Then We replaced evil [circumstances] with good, till they multiplied and said, “Hardship and ease visited our fathers [as well].” Then We seized them suddenly, while they were unaware.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wrong Conclusion From Relief**

*Thumma baddalnā makāna al-sayyiʾati al-ḥasanata ḥattā ʿafaw wa-qālū qad massa ābāʾanā al-ḍarrāʾu wa-l-sarrāʾu* — then We replaced the bad condition with the good until they multiplied and said: our fathers too were touched by hardship and ease. The verse is the sūrah's account of what the towns did with the mercy that followed their trials, and its analysis is one of the most penetrating in the Book.

The sequence is exact. A community receives the message; hardship arrives, and its function was stated in the previous verse — to make the people humble. Then the hardship is lifted, the community prospers, and at the moment of prosperity it draws the conclusion that had been prepared for it: the alternation of hardship and ease is a natural cycle that all previous generations experienced without consequence. The Book reports the sentence as a piece of false reasoning that explains itself: if our fathers went through both and nothing happened to them, then nothing follows from either.

**"Our Fathers Had Both"**

The argument the towns construct is the argument from normalcy, and the Book names it as the last stage of the deception. Its Qur'anic parallel appears in Sūrat al-Anʿām, where the sequence is stated almost as a law: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44). The same pattern is repeated in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn, where the sūrah describes a community receiving mercy after hardship and using it as an argument for security (23:75–77 in the sequence).

The Book's diagnosis of the error is worth stating precisely. The men of the towns were not denying that history contains cycles of fortune; their error was to read the cycles as evidence that no one was managing them. The Qur'an's answer appears at 7:95's heels in the following verses: the alternation is itself the test, and the community that treats its prosperity as a licence has misread the ledger of the One who granted it.

**The Suddenness**

*Fa-akhadhnāhum baghtatan wa-hum lā yashʿurūn* — so We seized them by surprise while they were unaware. The adverb *baghtatan* — suddenly — is the Book's standard attribute of the seizure that ends a heedless generation, and the pairing with *lā yashʿurūn*, while they were not aware, is stated at several points in the Makkan sūrahs: Sūrat al-Aʿrāf will repeat the formulation in its account of the Hour (7:187); Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ has the same phrase for the swallowing of Qārūn's household (28:81 vicinity); Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt's version of the punishment of the towns uses the same logic (29:53 in the vicinity); and Sūrat al-Naḥl's series of questions states the doctrine plainly: *“Do those who devise evil feel secure that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them, or that the punishment will not come to them from where they do not perceive, or that He will not seize them in the midst of their going to and fro, so that they will not escape?”* (16:45–46).

The wording is not merely picturesque. The Book's teaching is that the timing of judgment is a deliberate concealment: the generation that has made its peace with a warning cannot see the hour of the settlement, and the warning itself becomes part of the ordinary furniture of its life. Sūrat Yūnus gives the reason the delay exists: *“If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they wish to hasten good, they would have certainly been doomed. But We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (10:11 in its vicinity; cf. 16:61; 35:45).

**Prosperity as the Heavier Test**

The verse's most important claim is that the good condition after the bad was the actual trial, and the sūrah's own earlier material supports it. The rain that revives a dead land was described at 7:57 as a sign; the good soil's yield at 7:58 was an argument; and the sūrah's address to ʿĀd at 7:69 named *the favours of Allah* as the thing to remember. The Book's position is that abundance carries a heavier obligation than scarcity, because abundance removes the pressure that produces humility.

Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ puts the same thought in the mouth of its warning: *“Do not let them deceive you”* — and Sūrat al-Anʿām's verse about the opening of the gates of everything is followed by the sūrah's own explanation: *“So the wrongdoers were utterly uprooted. And all praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.”* (6:45). The sentence is typical of the Book's manner: the destruction of a community is reported with the doxology, because the standard being vindicated is the one the community had been writing off as a cycle.

**The Sentence the Towns Never Said**

The verse's careful composition can be appreciated by noting what the towns did not say. They did not attribute the relief to God and thank Him; the sūrah's earlier verse on the good soil identified the faculty that learns from such things — *for a people who are thankful* (7:58) — and the towns' sentence shows the faculty atrophied. They did not ask whether their hardship had signalled anything. They did not notice that their messenger's presence tracked the pattern.

Instead they produced the one sentence that made the later seizure possible: our fathers had both, and nothing happened. The sūrah has now shown its reader how a community talks itself into destruction, and the mechanism is not vice or violence but a theory of history — the theory that the alternations of fortune are weather, and that a generation may therefore live in the calm with no obligation to ask who sends it. The Book's answer is stated in the next verse as a counterfactual that flips the towns' reasoning: the prosperity they treated as proof of impunity was the very thing that could have saved them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:96

> **Had the people of the towns believed and been reverent, We would surely have opened unto them blessings from Heaven and earth. But they denied, so We seized them for that which they used to earn.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counterfactual**

*Wa-law anna ahla al-qurā āmanū wa-ttaqaw la-fataḥnā ʿalayhim barakātin mina al-samāʾi wa-l-arḍ* — had the people of the towns believed and been mindful, We would have opened upon them blessings from heaven and the earth. The verse is the sūrah's answer to the theory of history that the towns had constructed in the previous verse, and it states the alternative in the most concrete terms the Book possesses: rain, harvest, security.

The phrase *barakāt mina al-samāʾi wa-l-arḍ* — blessings from the sky and the earth — is one the sūrah of al-Māʾidah also uses of the People of the Book: *“Had they observed the Torah and the Gospel and what was revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been nourished from above them and from beneath their feet”* (5:66). The Book's imagery of blessing is always agricultural and economic before it is spiritual, because the audience of the Qur'an measured well-being in crops, flocks, and caravans; the verse is telling the towns that their prosperity was not the neutral climate they assumed but a response, and that its size depended on their conduct.

**Faith and Mindfulness**

The condition the verse attaches to the promise is a pair of terms the sūrah has used throughout: *āmanū*, they believed — trust in the message and the messenger — and *ittaqaw*, they were mindful — the practical restraint that the sūrah's prophets kept demanding, the honest scale at Madyan, the untouched she-camel at Thamūd, the undefiled sign at Sodom. The Book's grammar of blessing is consistently conditional in this way, and its statements are equally consistently corporate: the covenant is with a community, and so is the consequence.

Sūrat al-Naḥl's parable of the town states the two conditions under a single image: *“And Allah sets forth the example of a society which was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from all directions. But its people met Allah’s favours with ingratitude, so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for …”* (16:112). The verse is the sūrah's own theology in narrative form, and its details — security, ample provision, the denial of favour, the garment of hunger and fear — are the exact reversal of verse 96's promise. Sūrat Sabaʾ's account of the people of Sheba makes the same point with garden imagery: *“There was a sign for them in their dwelling place: two gardens on the right and the left; eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him”* — until the town turned away and was given, in place of its gardens, a flood and bitter fruit (34:15–17).

**"But They Rejected"**

*Wa-lākin kadhdhabū fa-akhadhnāhum bimā kānū yaksibūn* — but they rejected, so We seized them for what they used to earn. The second half of the verse returns from the counterfactual to the record, and it repeats the vocabulary of the previous verses: the seizure, the earning, the rejection. The sūrah has now stated the towns' whole history in the form of a conditional sentence with one clause filled in and one left open — and the open clause is the historical fact, that the towns did not believe and were not mindful.

The verb *kasaba*, to earn, is the Book's commercial term for moral action, and its appearance here is pointed in a sūrah whose last town was judged by its scales. The seizure is not described as an act of caprice; it is a harvest of what the towns had been accumulating in the only ledger that outlasts a civilization. The sūrah's reader has watched five communities make exactly the same mistake, and the verse's final clause names the mechanism without embellishment.

**The Doctrine of Blessing in the Book**

The Qur'an's promise that faithfulness produces abundance is not a formula for material success, and the Book's own accounts of its prophets prevent that reading: Noah's century produced a handful of believers, and the sūrah's own messenger in Makkah was boycotted, stoned, and expelled. What the verse promises is a community's collective well-being in the world when it accepts the terms on which the world was made — the honest scale, the protected road, the undefiled sign, the remembered favour.

And the Book's warnings about the alternative are equally corporate. Sūrat al-Anʿām's verse about the opening of every gate and the sudden seizure (6:44) is followed by the sūrah's statement of the purpose: *“So the wrongdoers were utterly uprooted. And all praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.”* (6:45). The sūrah's own 7:96 states the terms of the exchange, and the next three verses will ask the reader the only question that remains: whether he has considered the alternative.

**What the Towns Lost in the Trade**

The verse's pairing of belief with mindfulness is also a claim about what the towns thought they were buying. Madyan's merchants had calculated that following Shuʿayb would cost them their standing and their profit, and verse 90 recorded the calculation. Verse 96 states the price of the other choice: not merely the loss of prosperity but the loss of the supplier. The blessings from the sky and the earth had been available; the towns had been living on credit; and the verse's conditional perfect tense — *We would have opened* — records what the world would have been for a community that accepted the terms, said in the voice of a narrator who knows what happened instead.

The sūrah will now make the question explicit. The next three verses consist of nothing but questions addressed to the sūrah's own readers and to the towns: did they feel secure in the night, in the day, in the plan itself?

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:97

> **Did the people of the towns feel secure from Our Might coming upon them by night, while they were sleeping?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Did They Feel Secure by Night?"**

*A-fa-aminu ahlu al-qurā an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā bayātan wa-hum nāʾimūn* — did the people of the towns feel secure that Our punishment would not come to them at night while they were asleep? The verse opens the sūrah's most direct address to its own audience, and it does so with a question whose answer is meant to be embarrassing.

The scene is precise: night, and a town asleep. The Book's choices of hour are never accidental, and its narratives of destruction have already prepared the reader for this one. Sodom was destroyed at dawn (15:66); Thamūd's punishment came in the morning (15:83); the sūrah's own accounts of the townships mostly arrive at the beginning of a day. The night question adds the condition of sleep — the hours when a community has no defences, no sentries, and no consciousness of anything beyond its own walls and its own rest.

The Qur'an's parallel questions make the same point with the same architecture. Sūrat al-Mulk's address to the secure is the purest instance: *“Do you feel secure that He who is in the heaven will not make the earth swallow you while it is trembling? Or do you feel secure that He who is in the heaven will not send upon you a storm of stones? Then you will know how true My warning was”* (67:16–17). Sūrat al-Naḥl's list of possibilities — swallowing, seizure from beneath, confusion, a wasting punishment, an enemy — is followed by its own warning: *“Your Lord is truly Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”* (16:47), which is the verse's way of saying the delay in all those scenarios is mercy, not immunity.

**The Sleep of the Secure**

*Wa-hum nāʾimūn* — while they were asleep. The phrase, like its counterpart in the next verse, describes a state of mind as much as a physical condition. The towns that the sūrah has destroyed were not destroyed in war or in preparation for a crisis; they were ended in the middle of ordinary life, and the Book's word for that condition is the vocabulary of sleep. The Qur'an's own assessment of such security appears in the verse's closing question at 7:99, in the phrase the Book will use for the feeling of safety itself.

The Book's accounts of its prophets' enemies often show them at their ease immediately before the judgment arrives. Sūrat al-Isrāʾ describes the towns destroyed at night while their people slept (17:16 vicinity); Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt's account of the destruction of certain towns comes while their owners are unaware (29:53). The Book's rhetoric is designed so that the reader experiences a small version of the shock: the punishment arrives at the hour when no one is watching, because the whole point of the pattern is that vigilance is not a human possession but a discipline of the faithful.

**Living by Nightfall**

The verse's question is directed at the sūrah's audience, and its subject is not an ancient town. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's Makkan readers walked past the empty sites of the towns their caravans traded with; the question that follows each description of the ruins is always the same, and the Book asks it with the same forms: *do you feel secure*. The imperative is addressed to people who had read the warning, heard the recitation, watched the Prophet's community persist through hardship, and continued to assume that the ordinary arrangements of their city would hold.

The Book's own answer to the feeling of security is stated in the next verse and completed in the third question of the series, whose phrase the sūrah has already used in another context: *none feels secure from the plan of Allah except the losers* (7:99). The whole sequence is a piece of spiritual instruction, and its object is the reader's own estimate of his position in the world.

**The Sense of the Hour**

There is one more layer in the verse's composition. The night and its sleep are the Book's standing symbols of heedlessness in this world — Sūrat al-Furqān describes the believing servants as those who *“spend the night in prostration and standing”* (25:64), Sūrat al-Sajdah commends those *“They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them.”* (32:16), and the sūrah of al-Muzzammil opens with the command to *“stand in prayer at night, except a little”* (73:2). The sūrah's question asks what the sleeping town thought was protecting it during the hours when the faithful are described as awake.

The contrast is between two ways of passing a night: the town that sleeps in the confidence of its walls, and the believer who rises in the confidence of his Lord. The Qur'an's instruction to the Prophet about the Day's nearness uses the same imagery of the night and its end: *“So be patient over what they say, and glorify your Lord before sunrise and before sunset”* (20:130), and the sūrah of al-ʿAṣr's warning about humanity in loss is preached against the background of time's march. Verse 97 is thus the sūrah's invitation to a discipline, in the form of a question that no city can answer for itself.

**The Question the Reader Must Answer**

The sūrah's sequence has now ended its narratives and turned, with three questions, to its audience. The reader is placed among the people of the towns, not as a spectator to their history but as a participant in the same condition: the message recited, the signs visible, the delay extended, the ordinary life proceeding. What the verse asks is not whether he expects the punishment today; it is whether he has arranged his life as though it cannot come at all.

The Book's characteristic way of closing such a passage is to give the reader not a threat but a practice, and the practice appears in the sūrah's own earlier instructions: call on your Lord humbly and secretly (7:55); do not corrupt the earth after it has been set right (7:56); remember your Lord within yourself, humbly and with fear, in the mornings and the evenings (7:205). The questions of verses 97 to 99 are the other side of those commands, and the Book's reader is expected to hear in the first the reason the second exist.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:98

> **Or did the people of the towns feel secure from Our Might coming upon them in broad daylight, while they were playing?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Or by Day, While They Play?"**

*Aw-aminū an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā ḍuḥan wa-hum yalʿabūn* — or did they feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them in the daytime while they were playing? The second question mirrors the first and changes two words. Night becomes day; sleeping becomes playing.

The substitution is the verse's whole argument, and it deserves the weight the Book gives it. The towns of the sūrah were destroyed in the middle of their ordinary occupations, and the Qur'an's choice of the word *play* places those occupations in the light of the sūrah's earlier verdict on the same vocabulary. At 7:51 the Book described the condemned as those who *took their religion as amusement and play*; the verse's question turns the word back on the security of the prosperous. What the towns called business, pleasure, and the normal conduct of life, the Book calls by the name they reserved for other people's amusements.

**The Daytime and Its Confidence**

The pairing of night with day means that the sūrah's question covers the whole human clock. There is no hour of the day or night at which a society is, by its own arrangements, safe; the two conditions the verses name — sleep, the absence of consciousness, and play, the absence of seriousness — are the two modes of heedlessness, and both are described as the normal activity of the well-off.

The Book uses this double question elsewhere, and its versions are worth setting side by side. Sūrat al-Naḥl's series of possibilities covers seizure from above, from below, in confusion, or by a slow wasting (16:45–47). Sūrat al-Mulk's questions come in a pair with the earth's swallowing and the storm of stones (67:16–17). And Sūrat al-Isrāʾ's account of the towns destroyed uses the night explicitly: *“Whenever We intend to destroy a society, We command its elite ˹to obey Allah˺ but they act rebelliously in it. So the decree ˹of punishment˺ is justified, and We destroy it utterly.”* (17:16). In every version, the point is the same: the destruction arrives in the middle of a routine.

**The Play of the Prosperous**

*Yalʿabūn* is the Book's word for what a community does when it confuses the interval of mercy with a permanent concession. The Qur'an's use of the term is not confined to recreation; it covers the serious business of societies that have decided that the questions the prophets raise can be deferred indefinitely. Sūrat al-Anʿām's confrontation with the townspeople who made a game of their religion (6:70's *lahw wa-laʿib*) and Sūrat al-Ḥadīd's description of the world's own texture — amusement, play, adornment, boasting, competition in wealth and children (57:20) — express the same judgment.

Verse 98's question thus belongs with the sūrah's earlier diagnosis of the towns. The elites of Madyan and ʿĀd and Thamūd were not frivolous people; they were serious about their trade, their monuments, and their standing. What the Book calls play is the seriousness of a person who has excluded the one serious question from the ledger. Sūrat al-Muʾminūn's summary of the contrast puts the two lives side by side: the believers who turn away from idle talk (23:3) and the heedless who are described by the sūrah's recurring image of play (21:1–3; 44:9; 52:12).

**Why the Questions Have No Answer**

The two questions of verses 97 and 98 are constructed so that no reader can supply the answer they expect. To say *no, we do not feel secure* is to indict one's own community; to say *yes* is to claim knowledge that no creature possesses. The Book's method here is to let the reader stand in the silence of his own possible answers, while the third question of the series supplies the doctrinal term for the position: *the plan of Allah*.

The sūrah is also, in these verses, completing the work it began at 7:94. That verse established that hardship was the summons; 7:95 showed the towns misreading relief; 7:96 stated the counterfactual; and 7:97–98 ask whether the survivors of history have understood the lesson of those who have not survived. The Book's addressee has now been given the law, the case, the evidence, and the question. The verses that follow close the argument with one sentence about the only disposition that can be called sane.

**The Blessedness of Not Feeling Secure**

Read in the context of the sūrah's earlier teachings, the questions are not an accusation aimed at the reader; they are an invitation to a state of mind the Book calls *taqwā*. The faithful are described elsewhere as those who *fear their Lord unseen* and as those whose hearts tremble when His name is mentioned (22:35; 8:2). The night and day questions put the reader in the same position as the sūrah's towns in order to produce the opposite result: not paralysis and not dread, but the alertness that makes a person's ordinary day an act of worship.

Sūrat al-Zumar leaves the door of that interval open in the widest terms: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins”* (39:53). The question of verse 98 is severe precisely because the same span of ordinary days is offered to the reader as time to turn.

The sūrah's remedy for the towns' condition has been present in it since verse 55: supplication, humility, honest dealing, the remembrance of blessings, and the conscious expectation of the meeting. The next verse names the alternative to all of them — a security that the Book regards as the deepest form of loss.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:99

> **Did they feel secure from God’s plotting? None feels secure from God’s plotting, save the people who are losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Did They Feel Secure From Allah's Plan?"**

*A-fa-aminū makra Allāh* — did they feel secure from the plan of Allah? The third question of the series introduces the sūrah's sharpest theological term, and its sharpness has troubled readers for centuries. *Makr* in Arabic is the word for a stratagem, a scheme, a plan worked out with cunning; the Qur'an applies it to human scheming when it reports the plots of the disbelievers, and it applies it to God when it describes the counter-plot by which their schemes are undone.

The Book's instances of the pairing are numerous and deliberate. *“And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners.”* (3:54). *“And ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile you. They planned, but Allah also planned. And Allah is the best of planners.”* (8:30). Sūrat al-Naml's account of the plot against Ṣāliḥ states the principle in the sūrah's own terms: *“And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware.”* (27:50). The term's use is not a description of God's character in the abstract; it is the Book's way of saying that the schemes of the powerful are never the last move in a situation, because the One whose plan they are ignoring has already answered them.

**The Security That Is the Real Danger**

The verse's target is a state of mind that the Qur'an regards as the terminal spiritual illness: the conviction that one's arrangements are beyond the reach of consequence. The sūrah's towns have been shown displaying that conviction in a dozen forms — the pillars of Iram, the carved cliffs, the profitable scales, the expulsion orders — and the Book's sentence on it is stated in the second half of the verse: *none feels secure from the plan of Allah except the losing people*.

The clause's construction is worth noting. It does not say that the secure are punished for their confidence; it says that their confidence is itself the loss. The word *khāsirūn* returns to the sūrah for the third time in the passage (7:90, 7:92, 7:99), and in each use it names a different party: the believers, as the towns calculated the cost of following Shuʿayb; the towns themselves, as the Book struck the balance; and now anyone who mistakes the interval for immunity. The sūrah is teaching its reader the Book's definition of a loser, and it does so by repetition.

**The Believer's Fear and the Prophet's Prayer**

The Book's positive doctrine of fear appears throughout the Makkan sūrahs and is stated most fully in the sūrah of the Cave and the sūrah of al-Raʿd: *“and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment.”* (13:21); *“and those who fear the punishment of their Lord—”* (70:27). The Prophet's own practice is recorded in the hadith, where fear and hope are held together in his instruction: the Companions heard him say, *“None of you should wish for death because of a calamity; but if you must, say: O Allah, keep me alive as long as life is good for me, and take me when death is better for me”* (al-Bukhārī 5671; Muslim 2680). The sūrah's own earlier instruction — call upon God *with hope and fear* (7:56) — is the positive form of the same discipline.

The verse's function in the sūrah's argument is to prevent the reader from resolving the two previous questions too comfortably. It is easy to say that one does not feel secure while continuing to live as though one were; the sūrah's term for the alternative is the one it used for supplication in verse 55, where the manner of prayer is *humble and quiet*, the posture of a person who has no claim to press.

**The Doctrine Stated and Restated**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's third question is not the Book's only statement of the principle, and its other formulations fill out what the verse intends. Sūrat al-Mulk's questions about the security of the heaven-dwellers are answered with the observation that gratitude and walking humbly are the alternatives (67:16–22). Sūrat al-Rūm's account of the cycles of fortune warns that the alternation is by design (30:34–36) and Sūrat Fāṭir's passages on the same theme conclude with the phrase that sums up the sūrah's theology: *“Say: have you considered your partners whom you invoke besides Allah?”* (35:40 in the vicinity). The message of all of it is the one the sūrah has been stating since the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf opened with the clear Book: the created order runs on terms that no community can alter, and the security of a heedless generation is not a refuge but a debt.

**The Passage Complete**

With verse 99 the sūrah's meditation on the towns is complete, and its shape can be seen whole: the law of adversity (7:94), the misread relief (7:95), the counterfactual (7:96), the two questions about night and day (7:97–98), and the question about the plan itself (7:99). The five towns of the earlier narrative are the evidence, and the passage's four general verses are the sūrah's doctrine of history.

What follows will apply the doctrine to the sūrah's own readers with two more questions about inheritance and the seal on the heart (7:100–101), then draw the moral of the nations (7:102) and turn the page toward Moses and Pharaoh (7:103). The sūrah's reader, having been asked whether he feels secure, is about to be asked whether he has understood what he is standing on.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:100

> **Does it not serve as guidance unto those who inherited the earth after its [earlier] inhabitants that, if We willed, We could smite them for their sins and set a seal upon their hearts such that they would not hear?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Do They Not See Who Inherits the Land?"**

*A-wa-lam yabīn li-lladhīna yarithūna al-arḍa min baʿdi ahlihā an law nashāʾu aṣabnāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-naṭbaʿu ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yasmaʿūn* — is it not clear to those who inherit the land after its former people that, if We willed, We could strike them for their sins and seal their hearts so that they would not hear?

The verse closes the sūrah's meditation on the towns by turning from the dead to the survivors. Its addressees are the people who moved into the abandoned houses, irrigated the abandoned fields, and took over the caravan stops whose former owners the Book has just described being shaken out of them. The Book's question to them is a question about evidence: the ruins are visible, the story is public, the mechanism is documented — is all of that not plain?

**The Book's Doctrine of Succession**

The Qur'an consistently treats the succession of peoples as an office and not an accident. *“Then We made you successors in the land after them, to see how you would behave”* (Yūnus 10:14). *“Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? … We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins …”* (Anʿām 6:6). The Book's formula — establishment, abundance, destruction, replacement — is the same sequence the sūrah has just narrated in five instalments, and 6:6 states it as a general law addressed to the Prophet's contemporaries.

The doctrine cuts the other way as well. Sūrat al-Nūr makes the succession a promise to the faithful — *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good deeds that He will surely make them successors in the earth as He made successors of those before them”* (24:55) — and Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ states the condition plainly: *“Surely, following the ˹heavenly˺ Record, We decreed in the Scriptures: ‘My righteous servants shall inherit the land.’”* (21:105). The verse's warning and the Book's promise are the two faces of one law: the land changes hands, and the tenants' conduct decides the tenancy.

**The Seal on the Heart**

*Wa-naṭbaʿu ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yasmaʿūn.* The verse's second threat is graver than the first, and it names the penalty that makes the whole meditation on the towns intelligible. The Book's vocabulary of the sealed heart appears in several forms — *khatama* (2:7), *ṭabaʿa* (7:101, 10:74, 40:35), *rān* (83:14), *ghulf* (4:155) — and in each place it is the settled result of a person's own persistence rather than a sentence imposed from outside. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn states the mechanism with the most precision: *“But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14).

The Prophet's teaching preserved the same image in one of the best-known explanations of that verse. Abū Hurayrah reported him saying: *“When the servant commits a sin, a black spot appears in his heart; if he repents and desists, his heart is polished clean; but if he returns, it increases until it covers his heart — and that is the covering (rān) which Allah mentions: 'No indeed, what they used to earn has covered their hearts.'”* (al-Tirmidhī 3334, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ).

**"So They Will Not Hear"**

The verse's consequence is stated in terms of hearing rather than punishment, and the choice is deliberate in a sūrah that has been about speech and its reception from its first page. The Book's own description of the loss appears at 7:179, where the condemned have eyes that do not see and ears that do not hear, and at 7:198, where the idols' claimants are called and do not hear the call. Sūrat Yā Sīn states the same condition in the form of a barrier: *“We have placed before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them, so they do not see”* (36:9), followed immediately by the Book's own summary — *“and whether you warn them or do not warn them, they will not believe”* (36:10).

Sūrat al-Anʿām gives the same loss a first-person form: *“We shall turn away their hearts and their sight as they did not believe in it the first time”* (6:110). The sūrah's 7:100 therefore states, in one conditional sentence, the whole arithmetic of the Book's warnings: the punishment a nation fears may take away its cities, but the punishment that ends its possibility is the closing of the organ by which the message is received.

**What the Ruins Are For**

Read plainly, the verse's question explains the purpose of the historical material that fills the sūrah's middle. The Book's recitation of the towns' ends is not an attempt to prove God's power in the abstract; it is a demonstration arranged for tenants who believe themselves permanent. Sūrat Ṭāhā's formulation is the closest parallel: *“Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment.”* (20:128). Sūrat al-Sajdah's version adds the Book's own lament: *“Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs. Will they not then listen?”* (32:26) — and the verse ends with the very faculty that 7:100's seal removes.

**The Warning the Sūrah Leaves Open**

The verse's grammar leaves the reader inside the condition: *if We willed* — the strike is available, the seal is available, and the only thing standing between the inheritors and the towns' fate is not an absence of precedent but the interval described at 7:97–99. The sūrah has already given the reader the remedy in the same passage: the towns would have been opened upon with blessings had they believed and been mindful. The verse extends the option to the new tenants: the sealed heart of 7:100 is stated as a possibility precisely because it can still be avoided.

The sūrah's next verse will explain that the seal has a history in every people the Book has addressed, and that the recounting of the towns is itself the instrument by which the door is kept open. Between the law of the ruins and the doctrine of the seal, the sūrah's reader is being taught to hear — the one verb the verses keep pressing on him.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:101

> **These are the towns whose stories We have recounted unto thee. Their messengers certainly brought them clear proofs, but they would not believe in what they had denied earlier. Thus does God set a seal upon the hearts of the disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"These Are the Towns We Relate to You"**

*Tilka al-qurā naquṣṣu ʿalayka min anbāʾihā* — those are the towns; We relate to you some of their tidings. The verse is the sūrah's own caption on the narrative block that has just concluded, and it states the purpose of the material: the account of the five communities has been transmitted to the Prophet as information, for him first of all.

The Book gives the same caption to its stories in several places, and the repeated formula deserves attention because it defines what kind of book the Qur'an claims to be. *“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart. And there has come to you in this ˹sûrah˺ the truth, a warning ˹to the disbelievers˺, and a reminder to the believers.”* (11:120). *“This is how We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ some of the stories of the past. And We have certainly granted you a Reminder from Us.”* (20:99). *“We relate to you the best of stories”* (12:3). *“We relate to you their story in truth”* (18:13). *“We narrate to you ˹O Prophet˺ part of the story of Moses and Pharaoh in truth for people who believe.”* (28:3). The word *anbāʾ*, tidings, is the Book's term for the reports of the communities that its audience could not have known — and it classifies its own narratives as news rather than literature, historical verdicts rather than legends.

**"Their Messengers Came With Clear Proofs"**

*Wa-laqad jāʾat-hum rusuluhum bi-l-bayyināt.* The clause gathers the towns' experience into a single formula. Each of the sūrah's five narratives contained a demand for proof and a response: Noah's people called him in error, ʿĀd's demanded wonders, Thamūd's pointed to their own supremacy, Sodom's wanted the threat carried out, Madyan's argued about the scale and the roads. What all of them received, in the sūrah's own account, were clear proofs — the messenger's signs, the mechanism of the natural world, the testimonies of their own history. The Book's verdict on the towns' objections is therefore terse: *their messengers came with proofs*.

**"They Were Not To Believe What They Had Already Denied"**

*Fa-mā kānū li-yuʾminū bimā kadhdhabū bihi min qabl.* This is the verse's hardest clause, and the Book repeats it in nearly identical form elsewhere: *“Then after them We sent Moses to Pharaoh and his chiefs, and they denied Our signs — so see how the end of the corrupters was”* (7:103); *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people.”* (7:133); and the clause that closes the same pattern in Sūrat Yūnus: *“Then We sent after him messengers to their peoples, and they came to them with clear proofs, but they were not to believe in what they had already denied. Thus We seal the hearts of the transgressors”* (10:74).

The clause states the Book's doctrine of settled disbelief. The refusal the towns were practicing at the moment of the messenger's arrival was not a fresh decision; it was the continuation of a posture already adopted, and Sūrat al-Anʿām identifies the process in God's own voice: *“We shall turn away their hearts and their sight as they did not believe in it the first time, and We shall leave them to wander blindly in their transgression”* (6:110). Sūrat al-Baqarah's opening states the same verdict in its starkest form: the disbelievers are those whom warning and its absence leave equally unmoved, hearts, hearing, and sight alike covered (2:6–7).

**"Thus Allah Seals the Hearts of the Disbelievers"**

*Kadhālika yaṭbaʿu Allāh ʿalā qulūbi al-kāfirīn.* The doctrine of the seal is not a blot on the Book's justice; it is the explanation of why the towns' stories repeat with such monotony. The Qur'an's own remedy for a heart that is hardening is stated repeatedly in the imperative: remember, turn back, seek forgiveness, recite. Sūrat al-Zumar leaves the door open in the widest terms: *“Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah; surely Allah forgives all sins”* (39:53), and Sūrat al-Furqān's verse on repentance promises the substitution of good deeds for bad: *“As for those who repent, believe, and do good deeds, they are the ones whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (25:70).

The Prophet's own practice shows what the seal's alternative looks like in a life. Umm Salamah reported that he used to say in his supplication: *“O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion”* (al-Tirmidhī 3522, graded ṣaḥīḥ; Aḥmad and others). The request is telling, because it treats firmness of heart as a gift continually asked for rather than a possession, and it is the exact opposite of the condition the sūrah's verse describes.

**Why the Sūrah Tells the Stories at All**

The verse's placement answers a question the reader of the sūrah may have been asking through five narratives: why is the Book's argument so heavily historical? The answer is the one Sūrat al-Ṭāhā gives, and it is the sūrah's own: *“So relate the stories, that they may reflect”* (7:176). The towns are gone, but the mechanism they illustrate is present, and the Book's claim is that the mechanism is knowable — by evidence, by warning, and by the public record of what happened to peoples who treated their prosperity as a permanent concession. The next verse will state what the survey of those peoples found.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:102

> **We did not find most of them [faithful to their] pact. Indeed, We found most of them to be iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Found Most of Them Faithless to the Covenant"**

*Wa-mā wajadnā li-aktharihim min ʿahdin wa-in wajadnā aktharahum la-fāsiqīn* — and We did not find most of them keeping any covenant; indeed We found most of them to be transgressors. The verse is the sūrah's summary verdict on the communities it has surveyed, and its two nouns — covenant and transgression — name the two halves of the Book's anthropology.

**Which Covenant?**

The Qur'an's language of *ʿahd* and *mīthāq* refers to more than the agreements made with particular prophets. The Book describes a covenant taken from every human being before their earthly life: *“When your Lord brought forth from the Children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them testify about themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we testify”* (7:172) — the verse belongs to this very sūrah, and it is the reason the sūrah's summaries can speak of a covenant that every people has broken. Sūrat Yā Sīn restates the same primordial undertaking: *“Did I not covenant with you, O Children of Adam, that you should not worship Satan — indeed he is a clear enemy to you — and that you should worship Me? This is a straight path”* (36:60–61).

The Book also speaks of covenants with the prophets (33:7), with the People of the Book (3:187, 5:12–14), and with the Prophet's community (48:10: *“Those who pledge allegiance to you pledge allegiance to Allah”*), and it gives the ordinance its general form in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ: *“Do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity. And honour ˹your˺ pledges, for you will surely be accountable for them.”* (17:34). The verse's verdict that *most* of the towns kept no covenant is therefore a statement about the species and not only about Madyan: the human person knows an obligation that his conduct routinely ignores.

**The Anatomy of Transgression**

*Fāsiqīn* is the Book's precise term for that condition, and its root meaning is instructive: *fisq* in Arabic describes a date emerging from its skin, the fruit leaving the husk that should have contained it. The Qur'an's own definitions show the term operating in the realm of conduct rather than doctrine. Sūrat al-Ḥashr defines the transgressors as those who forgot God and were consequently made to forget themselves: *“Do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget their own souls; those are the transgressors”* (59:19). Sūrat al-Nūr treats the slanderer's offense as transgression (24:4), and Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt warns that a careless report may be a transgression with consequences (49:6–7).

Most telling for this verse is Sūrat al-Baqarah's linkage of transgression to covenant-breaking in a single passage: the sūrah's parable of the gnat misleads by it *many*, and the ones it misleads are defined immediately as those who break the covenant of Allah after ratifying it, sever what Allah commanded to be joined, and spread corruption in the earth (2:26–27). The Book's own sequence — covenant, breach, corruption, loss — is the sūrah's sequence in miniature, and the towns of al-Aʿrāf have just enacted it five times.

**The Weight of "Most"**

The verse's word *aktharihim*, most of them, prevents two errors. It refutes the notion that the towns were uniformly wicked, since the Book elsewhere insists that in every community there remains a remnant of those who hold to the covenant: *“And had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below. Some among them are upright, yet many do nothing but evil.”* (5:66's vicinity), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own later passage promises that *“among those We created is a community that guides by the truth and establishes justice by it”* (7:181). And it refutes complacency in the sūrah's readers, because the statistic is a warning: the majority is not the measure of the truth, and the pattern the Book documents is that the majority of every people addressed by a messenger has broken faith.

The sūrah's earlier verse on the towns' accounting made the same point with different words: the blessings were available to the faithful and mindful, and the record says that most preferred otherwise. The Qur'an returns to the theme insistently — *“most of the people will not believe”* (12:103, 13:1 vicinity), *“most of them do not know”* (6:37 and elsewhere), *“We have indeed established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood. ˹Yet˺ you seldom give any thanks.”* (7:10) — because the reader's instinct is to infer truth from numbers, and the Book's histories are written to sever that inference.

**The Verdict and the Road Ahead**

Verse 102 closes the sūrah's account of the nations, and its phraseology recurs at the sūrah's opening address to the Prophet's own audience: the towns' failure was not a deficit of information but a failure of covenant-fidelity, and the Book's narrator reports it as a finding of fact. The Book states the same verdict about communities that have gone before and the generations that inherit them: *“That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done”* (2:134; the same sentence closes 2:141).

The five towns have now been surveyed, explained, and generalized; the law of adversity, the misread mercy, the seal on the heart, and the broken covenant are all stated. The sūrah now performs the transition it has been preparing since its opening: with verse 103 it leaves the ruins of Arabia and returns to the story of Moses, the longest narrative in the Book, and the model against which the towns' history has been arranged.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:103

> **Then after them We sent Moses with Our signs unto Pharaoh and his notables, but they treated them wrongfully; so behold how the workers of corruption fared in the end.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Then After Them We Sent Moses"**

*Thumma baʿathnā min baʿdihim Mūsā bi-āyātinā ilā Firʿawna wa-malaʾihi fa-ẓalamū bihā fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn* — then after them We sent Moses with Our signs to Pharaoh and his notables, and they wrongfully rejected them; so look how the end of the corrupters was.

The verse is the sūrah's hinge. Twenty-three verses have surveyed the towns of Arabia and the region; from here the sūrah enters the story of Moses, the longest single narrative in the Qur'an, which the Book develops across al-Baqarah, al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, Ṭāhā, al-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Qaṣaṣ, and al-Muʾmin in extended form and mentions in dozens of other places. The study of the word *Moses* in the Qur'an's translation yields the striking figure: the prophet named more often than any other, roughly one hundred and thirty times.

**The Chain of Prophecy**

*Min baʿdihim* — after them — places Moses in the succession the sūrah has been tracing. The Book's own sense of prophetic history is genealogical and continuous: *“We gave Moses the Scripture and sent after him messengers in succession”* (2:87); *“We have already sent messengers before you; of them are those We have told you about and of them those We have not told you about”* (40:78); *“Indeed, We have sent revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ as We sent revelation to Noah and the prophets after him”* (4:163). The sequence is not a series of isolated episodes but one message entrusted to a chain of bearers, and the sūrah's five towns have been its first five links of the Arab world's portion.

The sūrah's arrangement makes Moses the pattern that the earlier narratives have been preparing. Each town received a messenger from among its own; Israel's messenger will be sent to a foreign court. Each town's elite rejected the message with arguments about their standing; Pharaoh will reject it with an army. Each town was ended by a catastrophe of the kind its own confidence had ruled out; Pharaoh's confidence will be drowned in the sea he claimed to command.

**"They Wrongfully Rejected Them"**

*Fa-ẓalamū bihā* — they did injustice to the signs — the phrase is the Book's standing analysis of the psychopathology of denial. Its fullest form appears in Sūrat al-Naml: *“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly. See then what was the end of the corruptors!”* (27:14). The Qur'an's claim is thus not that the deniers lacked evidence; it is that they suppressed an evidence they possessed. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf states the same mechanism at 7:146, where the Book announces that it will turn away from its signs those who behave arrogantly on the earth without right, and even if they see every sign they will not believe it.

The verb also exposes what the denial costs. The signs Moses brought are described in Sūrat al-Naml as *nine* clear signs (27:12) and in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ as the same nine (17:101); the Book's account of the Egyptian court's response is that it called them *magic* and attributed them to the skills of a professional class. The injustice in *ẓalamū bihā* is therefore directed at the signs themselves — the court denied what it had seen — and the Book's narrative treats the lying interpretation as a further offense rather than a mere error of judgment.

**"The End of the Corrupters"**

*Fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn.* The closing command is the sūrah's refrain and the age of the audience is invited into it: the ruins of the Egyptian world were still visible in the Book's own account — *“And We caused the people who had been oppressed to inherit the eastern and western lands which We had blessed”* (7:137) — and the story's later outcomes were known to its listeners. The Qur'an's usage of the term *mufsid*, corrupter, is exact: it is the antonym of the reformer the Book commends, and it is linked to the taking of land, the severing of kin, and the squandering of the world's ordered state: *“Those who break Allah's covenant after ratifying it and sever what Allah commanded to be joined, and spread corruption in the earth — those are the losers”* (2:27).

Sūrat al-Rūm's statement of the consequences is one of the Book's most quoted: *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people’s hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return ˹to the Right Path˺.”* (30:41). Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ adds the Book's standard for the office of the land: *“That home of the Hereafter We assign to those who do not seek exaltation in the earth, nor corruption; and the outcome is for the God-fearing”* (28:83). The sūrah's verdict on Pharaoh is thus a verdict on a category, stated in verse 103 before the narrative has begun to describe his deeds — a technique the Book uses to tell the reader in advance what kind of story he is about to hear.

**The Page Turned**

The verse thus ends a narrative cycle and begins another at the same time, and its vocabulary is the bridge. The three themes of the towns — signs, injustice, corruption — are handed to the longest of the Book's histories, and the sūrah's reader is expected to recognize in Pharaoh's court the familiar machinery of the towns' elites, now operating at the scale of an empire. Moses will begin, in the next verse, with the same announcement every messenger in this sūrah has made: that he is sent by the Lord of all worlds, and that he has come with a proof.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:104

> **And Moses said, “O Pharaoh! I am truly a messenger from the Lord of the worlds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"O Pharaoh, I Am a Messenger of the Lord of All Worlds"**

*Wa-qāla Mūsā yā Firʿawnu innī rasūlun min rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — and Moses said: O Pharaoh, I am indeed a messenger from the Lord of the worlds. The verse opens the sūrah's Moses narrative, and its first words are a title, advanced into a court where titles were the local currency.

**The Claim Made in the Belly of the Beast**

The Qur'an's Moses stories vary in detail and share this opening: the messenger walks into the seat of supreme power and names the authority that sent him. In Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ the conversation expands into a dialogue between two claims of lordship: Moses says *Lord of the worlds* (26:23), the court calls for the other gods of Moses' ancestors, and Moses answers with the Lord of the east and the west and all that lies between (26:28). Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt reports the verse that explains the exchange: *“And he said: I am your lord, the most high”* (79:24); Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ gives the same claim in the words *“I know no god for you but myself”* (28:38). Pharaoh's politics was a claim of divinity, and Moses' first sentence in the sūrah is the direct refusal of it.

**"The Lord of All Worlds"**

The title Moses uses is the Book's most emphatic answer to every local claim of sovereignty, and its presence in this sūrah is deliberate. The sūrah's own discourse on creation uses the same name: *“Indeed your Lord is Allah, who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then established Himself above the Throne; He covers the day with the night, seeking it eagerly; and the sun, the moon, and the stars are subservient to His command. Unquestionably, His is the creation and the command; blessed is Allah, Lord of the worlds”* (7:54). The word *ʿālamīn* is the plural of *ʿālam*, and its breadth is the point: not the Lord of one nation, one house, or one river, but of the totality of the worlds He has made.

The Qur'an's uses of the title carry the same political charge wherever they appear. Sūrat al-Fātiḥah opens with it before making any other statement about God; Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's account of Abraham uses it in his confrontation with a people who took their kings' traditions as law (37:87 vicinity); and the sūrah's own structure sets 7:104 against 7:54 much as the prayer of the saved in the hereafter — *“Praise be to Allah, Lord of the worlds”* (7:43 vicinity) — answers every claim of lordship that preceded it.

**The Messenger and the Man**

The Book insists that Moses is also one of the earthly powerless, and the narrative will make much of it. He had been raised in Pharaoh's palace and had fled it (28:14–21), he is described as a man who killed accidentally and who returned speaking with a tongue that his brother's fluency must support (28:33–34), and the Book's account of his confrontation in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ has the court attempt to discredit him with the fact that he was raised among them (26:18–19). The verse's claim thus rests on the office rather than the person: the authority of the speaker comes entirely from *rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, a Lord whose claim over Egypt had nothing to do with Egypt's gods or its king's edicts.

**The Pattern of the Sūrah's Narratives**

Preached to the Prophet's Makkan congregation, the sentence had an obvious double edge. Their own interlocutors were men of standing and lineage who treated the traditions of their city as law, and the sūrah's Moses cycle is the Book's fullest dramatization of what happens when a message arrives at an established order and the order tries to outlast it. The Prophet's own practice in this regard is preserved in the tradition: when he came to Madīnah and found the Jews fasting on ʿĀshūrāʾ, he asked what the day meant, and when told that it was the day Allah saved Moses and drowned Pharaoh, he said, *“We have a greater right to Moses than you,”* and commanded the fast (al-Bukhārī 2004; Muslim 1130). The Book's use of Moses as the emblem of deliverance from a tyrannical power is thus continuous with the community's own memory.

**What Follows**

Verse 105 will complete Moses' opening speech: *“obliged to say nothing about Allah except the truth; I have brought you a clear proof from your Lord, so let the Children of Israel go with me.”* This is the Qur'an's characteristic ordering of the message — the truth about God, then the proof, then the demand for the concrete wrong to be undone — and the sūrah will develop the confrontation through signs, magicians, the plagues, the sea, and the deliverance of Israel, before returning once more to the law of the towns.

The title also sets the terms of the confrontation that the next verses will develop: every demand Moses is about to make — the release of Israel, the end of the persecution, the recognition of a lordship higher than the throne — rests on a claim that Egypt's state religion could not accommodate and that its king understood, immediately, as a political threat.

Verse 104 thus marks the beginning of the sūrah's second half. It opens a narrative that will occupy sixty verses and end with a nation rescued from a superpower; and it places the reader, who has just been asked whether he feels secure, in the position of the witness who already knows how the confrontation between a messenger and an empire is settled in the Book's histories.
