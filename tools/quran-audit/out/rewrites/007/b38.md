**The Sentence at the Gate**

The sūrah's court has reached the stage of execution. *Qāla dkhulū fī umamin qad khalat min qablikum mina al-jinni wa-l-ins* — enter the Fire among the communities of jinn and men that have passed away before you. The wording is careful in two respects. The condemned do not enter alone; they enter *among* communities, as members of a company. And the company is not a single nation but the whole accumulated population of the two kinds — jinn and human beings — that preceded them. The last generation is not judged in isolation; it is inserted into a procession.

The commentators read the verse as the answer to a specific hope, one the Makkan leadership entertained and which the Qur'an records elsewhere. Sūrah al-Aḥqāf has the leaders of the people saying, *“We found our forefathers following a particular way, and we are following in their footsteps”* (43:22), and Sūrat al-Zukhruf has the warners anticipate the objection (43:24). The verse before us closes that door at the last moment: the ancestors are not an alibi. They are the front of the line.

**"Every Time a Community Enters, It Curses Its Sister"**

*Kullamā dakhalat ummatun laʿanat ukhtahā.* The word *ukht* — sister — is doing something remarkable in this sentence. The Qur'an's ordinary vocabulary for a doomed nation is *ummah*, a group defined by its direction. Here the same groups are given a family relation: they are sisters, and the relation survives into the Fire. The curse that a community pronounces on the one before it is, in the Qur'an's image, the continuation of a quarrel between relatives.

That is the point of the clause, and it is one of the sharpest psychological observations in the Book. In this world, the relationships among the nations that reject revelation are presented as alliances: shared economy, shared ancestry, shared opposition. The verse shows what is underneath the alliance. Every newcomer to the Fire curses the one who preceded it — the generation that modelled the practice, the teachers who taught the doctrine, the fathers who made the religion of custom look like religion. The solidarities that held them together dissolve on contact with the consequence, and what is left is blame.

The tradition drew a warning from this verse that has become proverbial: the fear that one be the *mudill* — the one led away and, worse, the one who led others away. The Prophet ﷺ stated the accounting for it in the strongest terms: *“Whoever sets a bad precedent in Islam will bear its burden and the burden of whoever acts upon it after him, without their burdens being diminished in the least”* (Muslim 1017, from Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh). The verse of the Heights supplies the scene in which that hadith is enacted.

**The Plea of the Last for the First**

*Ḥattā idhā iddārakū fīhā jamīʿan qālat ukhrāhum li-ūlāhum* — until, when they have all overtaken one another therein, the last of them will say of the first: *Our Lord, these led us astray, so give them double punishment of the Fire.* The plea is precisely the one the Qur'an records in four other passages, in almost identical words. Sūrat al-Aḥzāb has the condemned in the Fire saying, *“Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray from the Right Way. Our Lord! Give them double our punishment, and condemn them tremendously”* (33:67–68). Sūrat Ṣād has the same petition (38:61). Sūrat Sabaʾ records the exchange in full (34:31–33). And Sūrat al-Baqarah states the outcome: those who misled others disown their followers, the bonds between them are cut, and the followers cry out for a second chance (2:166–167).

The pattern is so consistently repeated that it must be read as a deliberate teaching device. The Book is telling its readers, in advance, what the social architecture of religious error looks like when the scaffolding is removed: the influential and the dependent, the eloquent and the persuaded, the ones who commanded and the ones who obeyed — each standing where he placed himself, each blaming the other, and neither able to produce the other as a defense.

**"For Each Is Double"**

The divine answer is a single line: *qāla li-kullin diʿf* — He will say, for each is double, *wa-lākin lā taʿlamūn* — but you do not know. The answer does not accept the plea's allocation while granting its principle. It affirms that responsibility is real for every party — the leaders for the invitation, the followers for the acceptance — and it refuses to let either shift the burden.

The "double" is not a doubling of torture; it is the doubling that the Book attaches to whoever combines two offenses. The leader who sinned *and* caused others to sin carries both; the follower who sinned and who imitated another's authority carries what he earned. The same arithmetic is stated as a law of the Book: *“Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Surely it is an evil burden they bear”* (16:25). And it is balanced by the verse of the same Book that says every soul will receive only what it earned: *“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”* (35:18; 53:38).

**What the Verse Is For**

Read at the level of the sūrah's argument, the verse is a preemptive dismantling of the single most common alibi for inherited religion: that one was following one's betters. This is the same objection that verse 28 recorded in its polite form — *we found our fathers doing it* — now answered in its final form, when the fathers are in the same fire and the daughter-community has nothing to say except a curse.

The passage ends on a note that is easy to overrun: *but you do not know*. The clause is not mercy and not sarcasm; it is the closing of the epistemic door the sūrah has been walking through since verse 28. There are facts about the consequence of leading others astray that no human being can calculate — which is exactly why the Qur'an does not encourage the game of distributing blame and does encourage the single question the reader can answer: what am I teaching, by my words, my practice, and my silence? The cursed sister-communities of this verse never had a chance to discuss it afterwards.
