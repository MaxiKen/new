## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:17

> **Then I shall come upon them from in front of them and from behind them, and from their right and from their left. And Thou wilt not find most of them grateful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Approaches and One Road**

The vow begun in the previous verse is now diagrammed. Iblīs announces that he will come at humanity from four quarters: *min bayni aydīhim* — from in front of them; *wa-min khalfihim* — from behind; *wa-ʿan aymānihim* — from their right; *wa-ʿan shamāʾilihimm* — from their left. The picture is of encirclement. He is not promising a single temptation but a surrounding, an approach from whichever side the traveler leaves unguarded.

The verse therefore explains the practical shape of the trial that the previous verses set up. A test conducted by frontal assault would be easy to recognize and easy to repel; a test that arrives from four directions at once means that the danger is rarely where a person is looking. The sūrah's later remedy fits this image exactly — the refuge is not a fortification on one flank but a turning to God from any flank: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah”* (7:200).

**What the Commentators Read into the Directions**

The classical exegetes, beginning with reports traced to Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ṭabarī, treated the four directions as a map of the ways a person is assailed, and their readings are worth recording because they have worn well.

From *in front*, they said, comes the temptation of the future: doubt about what lies ahead, the promise that there will be time later, the fear of what one may lose by obeying now. From *behind* comes the weight of what is past: inherited custom, the practice of ancestors, the argument that no one in one's family ever did this, the guilt that says the record is already too spoiled to repair. From the *right* comes corruption of religion itself: excess, self-satisfaction, the transformation of worship into display, the confidence of the person who is certain he is among the saved. From the *left* comes the pull of appetite: the open sins, the small indulgences, the countless daily permissions.

The classifications differ between al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and Ibn Kathīr in their details, and the differences are useful. They show that the exegetes did not treat the four directions as a code to be decoded but as a comprehensive figure: the whole perimeter of a human life. The enemy's claim is total, and the commentators' fourfold reading is simply an acknowledgment that the claim is not obviously false.

**The Directions He Did Not Name**

A widely transmitted observation, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās in the commentary literature, notes that Iblīs named four directions and left out two: he did not say he would come from above or from below. The explanation offered is that from above comes mercy, and from below comes the humility of the servant in prostration — the two postures the rebel cannot occupy.

The point should be held with a light hand. It is an exegetical insight rather than a revealed datum; the verse says what it says, and its silence about two directions is suggestive rather than dogmatic. But the suggestion is consistent with everything else the sūrah has established. The arrogant creature was ruined by refusing to lower himself, and the remedy for those he attacks is the very thing he would not do: to bow. The unguarded quadrant of the human being is not a blind spot in space but a missing posture in the soul.

**The Siege and Its Psychology**

The Prophet ﷺ described the siege from the inside, as a condition of every human life: *“Each one of you has a devil-companion from the jinn over him.”* When the companions asked whether this included him, he answered: *“Even me, yet Allah helped me against him until he submitted; he does not order me to do anything but good.”* (Muslim 2814, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd).

Two lessons follow from that report, and both answer this verse. The first is that the presence of the whisperer is not a sign of divine abandonment; it is the human condition, the same condition under which the Prophet ﷺ lived. The second is that the companion can be reduced to a whisperer who no longer commands — *“he does not order me to do anything but good”* — by help that comes from God. The four-sided approach of Iblīs is real; so is the possibility that a man reaches a state in which the approaches no longer find an entrance.

**His Verdict on Humanity — and the Remnant**

The verse closes with the enemy's own summary of the human race: *“and You will not find most of them grateful.”* The clause is an echo, and the sūrah intends it. Verse 10 had already told humanity the same thing in God's own words — *Little do you give thanks!* — and there the diagnosis stood as a charge against the ungrateful. Here the same charge appears in the mouth of the accuser, which is the sharpest possible way of stating it: the ingratitude is the entry point he is counting on.

The Qur'an confirms the statistic without confirming his conclusion. *“Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful”* (10:60; cf. 27:73; 16:83) — and the same Book says of the divine bounty that it reaches the grateful and the ungrateful alike, since *“He has granted you all that you asked Him for”* (14:34). The word *most* is where the hope lives. The enemy's sentence leaves a remainder, and the remainder is the company he himself was forced to acknowledge and exclude from his reach: *“except Your chosen servants among them”* (15:40; 38:83). Gratitude, in this sūrah's architecture, is not a virtue added to faith. It is the door the ambush was designed to block, and the shield that shuts it is a single remembered favour at the right moment.
