**Three Garments in One Verse**

The verse that follows the fall turns immediately to clothing, and it does so with a wordplay only Arabic can carry. *Libās* is any covering worn on the body; *rīsh* is the plumage of a bird — the feathers that make a creature not merely covered but beautiful; and *libās al-taqwā* is a third garment, made of neither cloth nor skin. Human beings, in this verse, are given three layers: a cover for the body, an adornment for the eye, and a covering for the soul.

The sequence deserves to be read slowly, because the sūrah has just left a scene of leaves stitched together in panic (7:22). The first garment given to the first couple was God's; the leaves were their own attempt at replacement; and this verse announces that the original arrangement stands — covering itself is from God, *anzalnā*, We sent down.

**"Sent Down": Clothing as a Divine Gift**

The verb *anzalnā* is the same verb the Qur'an uses for rain, for the Book, and for iron (*“We sent down iron, in which is great might and benefits for people”*, 57:25). Clothing is placed in that company deliberately. The wool of sheep, the strength of flax, the growth of cotton, the shade of a tree — none of these were manufactured by the human being who wears them; what the human being contributes is knowledge of how to use what was supplied. The same point is made in Sūrah al-Naḥl, where garments and their purposes are traced to the Creator: *“He has also provided you with clothes protecting you from the heat ˹and cold˺, and armour shielding you in battle” (16:81)* (16:81). For the mercantile Makkans, whose cloth trade was a livelihood, the verse quietly relocates the origin of their merchandise: the wealth of their caravans was borrowed.

**rīsh: The Case for Beauty**

The second garment is not a concession; it is named with affection. *Rīsh* is plumage, and the commentators record two overlapping readings. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as the adornment of this world — the fine clothing and the beauty that makes a person pleasing to look at. Others, following a lexically attested sense of the root *r-y-sh* (prosperity, the means by which one lives at ease), read it as provision and sufficiency. The two readings converge: God is not embarrassed by beauty, and He supplies the conditions for it.

This is the verse that Muslim tradition cites against a spirituality of self-neglect. The Prophet ﷺ, when a man came to him looking disheveled, remarked that God is beautiful and loves beauty, and that the display of God's favour upon a servant is part of what is due to Him. The Qur'an's own term for the good things of life is *ṭayyibāt*, and it treats their enjoyment as worship when gratitude accompanies it (2:172; 7:32). *rīsh* is the licence for that enjoyment, written into the earliest instruction given to the human race after the fall.

**libās al-taqwā: The Garment That Cannot Be Bought**

Then the verse introduces its comparative: *wa-libāsu al-taqwā dhālika khayr* — and the raiment of reverence, that is best. The *khayr* is not a dismissal of the earlier garments; it is a ranking. Cloth covers a body that will decay; *taqwā* covers a soul that will be presented to God. The Qur'an elsewhere names *taqwā* as the thing that survives when all provisions are counted: *“And take provision, but the best provision is taqwā”* (2:197). The metaphor works because it shares the function of clothing exactly. Taqwā hides what ought not to be seen — the faults, the appetite that has not yet been disciplined — and it protects its wearer from exposure. In the sūrah's own story, the first thing sin produced was exposure; the remedy offered to the children of Adam is a covering that cannot be lost by taste or theft.

**A Sign to Be Remembered**

The verse closes by naming itself a sign: *that haply they may remember*. And the reason clothing functions as a sign is anthropological. Human beings are the only creatures in the world whose dignity depends on covering; a horse needs no garment, a bird is already dressed. The nakedness that shamed Adam and his wife was not a bodily defect but a moral fact — the loss of the covering that God had given. Every garment a person puts on is therefore a reminder, whether he notices or not, that he is a being with a shame to guard and a Lord who provided for it.

That is why the verse ends with memory rather than command. The argument is not that clothing is obligatory; it is that clothing is evidence. A person who sees the evidence will not spend the rest of his life treating his outer covering as though it were the whole story, or (as the next passage will warn) treating the loss of God's command as a small thing because the world is warm and there are leaves enough.

The verse's sequence is therefore itself an argument: the fall is mentioned, and the clothing is named immediately afterwards, as though the Book wanted the reader to see that the provision for the body and the provision for the soul were given together and never revoked.
