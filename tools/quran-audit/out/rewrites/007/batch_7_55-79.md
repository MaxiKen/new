# Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:55–7:79 — rewritten sections

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:55

> **Call upon your Lord humbly and in secret. Truly He loves not the transgressors.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Throne to the Supplication**

Verse 54 ended with the claim that the creation and the command belong to God, and the next two verses explain what a creature is supposed to do about that claim. The doctrine moves immediately into practice: the One who made the heavens and the earth is the One who is asked. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same proximity in its own way — *“When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me”* (2:186) — and Sūrat Ghāfir supplies the command with a warning attached: *“Your Lord has proclaimed: Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60).

**Teacharrruʿan wa-Khufyatan: The Two Adverbs**

The Qur'an does not only command supplication; it prescribes its manner. *Taḍarruʿan wa-khufyatan* — humbly and quietly. The first word names the posture of a person who is visibly in need: the root carries the sense of abasement, of showing one's weakness, and it is used of the suppliant who approaches without a claim. The second describes the voice: *khufya* is secrecy and silence, the opposite of performance. What the verse rules out on both ends is theatrical religion — the wailing of the mourners of the age and the loud bargaining of a man who thinks his voice is leverage.

The sūrah returns to the same instruction in its own later verse, in a line that is the practical commentary on this one: *“Remember your Lord inwardly with humility and reverence and in a moderate tone of voice, both morning and evening. And do not be one of the heedless.”* (7:205) — that is, the same two adverbs, applied to remembrance rather than request. Sūrat al-Isrāʾ takes the pulse of the regulation from the other side, in the middle of an argument with the pagans about the manner of their prayer: *“And do not say your prayer too loudly, nor silently, but seek a middle way between them”* (17:110).

The Qur'an's most complete portrait of a supplicant resembles this verse's two adverbs and adds a third detail: Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ describes the prophets as those who *“used to call upon Us with hope and fear, and they were humble before Us”* (21:90), and Sūrat al-Sajdah describes the believers by their nights: *“They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them.”* (32:16).

**"He Does Not Like the Transgressors"**

The prohibition's object — *al-muʿtadīn*, the transgressors — is the sūrah's key moral word, and its placement here tells the reader what kind of supplication exceeds the limit. The exegetes listed the forms: asking for what is forbidden, asking with an air of demand rather than need, invoking God by names He has not given Himself, and — in the general sense the sūrah prefers — the transgression that consists in turning worship into a display. The same root names the boundary-crossers of verse 55's legal context throughout the Book: *“O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder— a free man for a free man, a slave for a slave, and a female for a female. But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then bloodmoney should be decided fairly …”* (2:178), *“do not transgress; surely Allah does not like the transgressors”* (2:190).

The sentence therefore does double duty. It forbids the manner of supplication that treats God as a functionary — the loud, the convenient, the transactional — and it reminds the reader that the One being called is also the One who set the limits the suppliant is asking Him to work within. The Book's ritual instructions are frequently delivered in this key: the command and the constraint arrive together, because the Book is not teaching people to influence a power but to stand in the right relationship to their Maker.

**The Three Modes the Verse Excludes**

Putting the two adverbs beside the prohibition yields a short list of the modes of prayer this verse is correcting. First, the prayer of the claimant: the person who states a case, as if he were owed. Second, the prayer of the performer: the person whose devotion requires an audience, whether an earthly one or the self-satisfied one inside. Third, the prayer of the transgressor: the person who wants God's aid inside a project God has forbidden.

Against all three, the verse sets the two conditions that the tradition kept repeating in its instruction of children: *humbly* — without status, and *quietly* — without posturing. The Book's prayer is a private transaction between a creature and his Creator who is closer to him than the vein in his neck (50:16), and the silence the verse recommends is less an acoustic requirement than a statement about who is listening.

**A Practice With a Door**

The verses that follow continue the same practical thread — do not corrupt the earth, call with hope and fear — and the sūrah is arranging the life of its reader around the doctrine of verse 54. The God whose Throne is above the waters and who covers the night with the day is available to the quiet voice of a person who has nothing to offer and nowhere else to go. Sūrat al-Furqān puts the last piece in place: it is not the volume of the prayer or the standing of the suppliant that determines the answer, but the fact that the one calling is calling the One who responds — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘You ˹all˺ would not ˹even˺ matter to my Lord were it not for your faith ˹in Him˺. But now you ˹disbelievers˺ have denied ˹the truth˺, so the torment is bound to come.’”* (25:77).

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:56

> **And work not corruption upon the earth after it has been set aright, but call upon Him in fear and in hope. Surely the Mercy of God is ever nigh unto the virtuous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Repair What Is Set Right**

*Wa-lā tufsidū fī al-arḍ baʿda iṣlāḥihā* — and do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. The verse sits immediately after the command to call upon God humbly, and its placement makes a theological point that the Qur'an never leaves in doubt: the disorder that the Book calls *fasād* is not merely a civic nuisance. It is damage done inside a repair.

The word *iṣlāḥ* — the setting right — is the sūrah's own term for what the messengers came to do. Verse 35 described the saved as those who *reform*; the first command of the sūrah's opening address was to follow what has been sent down (7:3), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's prophets are consistently described as *nāṣiḥ* — sincere, repairing counsellors (7:62, 7:68, 7:79). *Fasād*, its opposite, covers the whole field of ruin: the destruction of crops and livestock (2:205), the breaking of treaties, the exploitation of the weak, and the corruption of religion, which the sūrah has been cataloguing since verse 28. The verse's premise is that the world arrived ordered — that the earth was established by its Maker in a condition suited to human life — and that the human being's job description is maintenance, not demolition.

**Corruption With a History**

The Book uses this verse's vocabulary consistently in its accounts of ruined nations. Sūrat al-Baqarah describes the mischief-maker who is told not to corrupt the earth and answers that he is merely a reformer: *“Behold! It is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it”* (2:11–12). Sūrat al-Rūm states the general law: *“Corruption has spread on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned, so that He may let them taste the consequence of some of what they did, so that they may return”* (30:41). And Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records Shuʿayb's prohibition in the same terms: *“And do not follow the command of the transgressors — who spread corruption throughout the land, never setting things right”* (26:151–152).

The sūrah's own use of the verb will arrive twice within a few verses: once in Hūd's reminder to ʿĀd (7:74) and once in Shuʿayb's to his people (7:85). Its presence here, before the first prophet appears, makes it a footnote under the whole succeeding narrative: the towns the sūrah is about to describe did not fall for a single crime, they fell for the systematic undoing of what had been set in place.

**"Call Upon Him With Hope and Fear"**

*Wa-dʿūhu khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan.* The verse returns to supplication and now gives it its emotional content. *Khawf* is fear of the consequence and the judgment; *ṭamaʿ* is the eager hope of recompense and forgiveness. The pairing is one of the Qur'an's favourite descriptions of the believer's inner register, and the Book rarely mentions one without the other. Sūrat al-Sajdah describes the believing worshippers as *“calling upon their Lord in fear and hope”* (32:16); Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ states that the prophets *“were quick in doing good, calling upon Us in hope and fear”* (21:90); Sūrat al-Zumar asks, with a touch of rebuke, whether the one who *“spends his hours prostrating and standing in worship, wary of the Hereafter and hoping for the mercy of his Lord”* is equal to the heedless (39:9), and Sūrat al-Isrāʾ places the same fear in the prayer of every sincere suppliant (17:57).

The doctrinal effect of the pair is a balanced religion. Fear without hope curdles into despair, and the Book condemns it explicitly (12:87; 39:53); hope without fear curdles into presumption, and the Book condemns that too — *“Did they feel secure against Allah’s planning? None would feel secure from Allah’s planning except the losers.”* (7:99). The one supplication that is forbidden by 7:55 is the one that has lost the fear; the one that is sterile is the one that has lost the hope.

**"Mercy Is Near"**

The verse closes: *inna raḥmata Allāhi qarībun mina al-muḥsinīn* — surely the mercy of Allah is close to those who do good. The predication has been noticed by every commentator: mercy is not called *close* in any limiting sense — it is not said that mercy is near a place or a moment — but *near*. Sūrat al-Baqarah's verse about the divine response says simply, *“I am near”* (2:186), and the Qur'an speaks of God's help in the same idiom when it reassures a besieged community: *“Do not despair of the mercy of Allah”* (39:53).

The qualifier concentrates the promise on conduct. *Muḥsinūn* — those who do good, who do a thing well — carries in the Book the sense of a life with quality in it: the Book's first mention of the term at 2:195 attaches it to generous spending, and 4:125 to the religion of Abraham. The connection being drawn is precise: the person who calls with hope and fear does not receive the mercy because the prayer was loud or the formalities correct, but because the caller's life has been aiming at the good. The verse therefore links the practical instruction at its beginning with the practical standard at its end: worship is not a substitute for integrity, and the sūrah will not let its reader think that the cultivation of the earth is a side issue in the religion of its Creator.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:57

> **He it is Who sends the winds as glad tidings ahead of His Mercy, so that when they bear heavy-laden clouds, We may drive them toward a land that is dead, and send down water upon it, and thereby bring forth every kind of fruit. Thus shall We bring forth the dead, that haply you may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Winds That Herald the Mercy**

*Huwa alladhī yursilu al-riyāḥa bushrā bayna yaday raḥmatihi* — He is the One Who sends the winds as good tidings ahead of His mercy. The sūrah has just asked its reader to call upon God with hope, and it now supplies one of the Book's most repeated arguments for hope: the ordinary weather. The winds go before the rain the way a messenger goes before a king, and the Book adopts the imagery in several places with the same term: *“He is the One Who sends the winds as good tidings ahead of His mercy, and We send down pure water from the sky”* (25:48); *“Among His signs is that He sends the winds as bearers of glad tidings, and to let you taste His mercy”* (30:46); *“It is He Who shows you the lightning, causing fear and hope, and produces the heavy clouds”* (13:12).

The vocabulary is deliberately administrative. *Bushrā* is the announcement given to a person awaiting news; *raḥma* here is specifically rain, the sustenance that keeps a desert population alive from one season to the next. The verse is telling its reader that the mercy he is asking for in hidden, quiet prayer is the same thing he watches arrive overhead every winter with a herder's anxiety — the Book is not introducing a new category of event, only naming the author of a familiar one.

**The Argument From the Dead Land**

*Ḥattā idhā aqallat saḥāban thiqālan suqnāhu li-baladin mayyitin* — until, when they carry heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land. The progression is careful: the winds, the clouds, the journey of the clouds by a movement the verse attributes to God (*suqnāhu*, We drove it), the rainfall, and then the fruit. What is emphasized is the destination. The clouds are not sent to the sea, where the rain would be wasted, or to the fertile mountains; they are driven to the *mayyit* — the corpse-land, the dust that has no green on it.

Then comes the application: *kadhālika nukhriju al-mawtā laʿallakum tadhakkarūn* — that is how We will bring forth the dead, so that you may remember. The comparison is drawn in the Book repeatedly and with a consistent structure: rain revives a dead land, and that revival is the closest thing in the experience of the Book's audience to the resurrection. *“And We send down blessed water from the sky, producing gardens and grain from it”* (50:9), followed a verse later by *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). Sūrat al-Rūm makes the inference explicit: *“Look at the effects of Allah's mercy: how He revives the earth after its death. Surely He is the One Who will revive the dead”* (30:50; cf. 16:65; 45:5; 22:5).

The argument works because it is inductive, and the sūrah trusts its audience to complete it. Nobody in the audience doubted that last year's rain produced last year's pasture; the only question the verse asks them to face is whether the power that did that is short of the power required for a different kind of spring.

**The Resurrection Argument in Its Own Register**

The Book's readers have often noted that its proofs of the resurrection are drawn overwhelmingly from agriculture and gestation rather than from philosophy. *“Consider the seed you sow: is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who cause it to grow?”* (56:63–64). *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—in order to …”* (22:5). *“It is Allah Who sends the winds, which raise the clouds, and We drive them to a dead land and revive the earth after its death — such is the resurrection”* (35:9).

The choice is a claim about accessibility. Arguments from first principles require learning, and the Book is addressed to shepherds and weavers as much as to the learned; its demonstration is a process every farmer performs annually without noticing that he is performing it. The dead land, the rain, the green shoot, and the fruit are, in the logic of these verses, a rehearsal of the Day performed in front of the whole human race on an annual basis — and the final phrase of verse 57, *laʿallakum tadhakkarūn*, is the Book's sigh at the fact that they still forget.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The verse's closing words matter: *so that you may remember*. The Qur'an's reminders are always aimed at a faculty rather than at an ignorance, and the here-and-now setting of the sūrah — its Makkan audience watching the winter rains and the caravans — makes verse 57 one of the most concrete verses in the chapter. The Book does not tell them that they have never encountered mercy; it tells them that they have encountered it every year under a different name and have not drawn the conclusion. The cultivation of the earth that verse 56 commanded and the rain that verse 57 describes are the same story told twice: the world is maintained by a mercy that keeps arriving, and the religion being preached in Makkah is the act of noticing where it comes from.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:58

> **As for the good land, its vegetation comes forth by the leave of its Lord. And as for the bad, it comes forth but sparsely. Thus do We vary the signs for a people who give thanks.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Parable of Two Soils**

*Wa-l-baladu al-ṭayyibu yakhruju nabātuhu bi-idhni rabbihi* — the good land brings forth its vegetation by the permission of its Lord — *wa-lladhī khabutha lā yakhruju illā nakidan* — and the land that is bad brings forth nothing but with difficulty. The verse is the application of the rain of verse 57, and it is stated as an observation of nature that any farmer in the audience could test. Two plots of ground receive the same rain. The good soil answers with a crop; the exhausted, salty or stony ground, though it receives the same water, gives back little.

The Qur'an builds a whole doctrine on this image, and the sūrah of Ibrāhīm has the most developed version: *“Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky, always yielding its fruit in every season by the will of its Lord”* — and immediately, the bad counterpart: *“And the example of an evil word is that of an evil tree, uprooted from the surface of the earth, having no stability”* (14:24–26). Sūrat al-Raʿd adds the element of foam: the torrent carries away the dross while the water that is useful stays for people, and the Book concludes the image with a sentence that reads like an exegetical note: *“This is how Allah sets forth parables of truth and falsehood”* (13:17).

**What the Soil Stands For**

The classical commentary is practically unanimous on the referent: the land is the human heart, and the rain is revelation. The good land gives its crop *by the permission of its Lord* — the phrase is important because it keeps the comparison from becoming a doctrine of self-cultivation. Even the productive soil does not manufacture the harvest; it responds, and the fruit comes by God's permission. The bad land's failure is described with a word — *nakid*, scant, bitter, hard-won — that sounds like the complaint of an exhausted farmer: the plot that requires enormous labour and returns almost nothing.

The image aligns with the sūrah's own anatomy of its audience. Verse 52 described the Book as a guidance and mercy for people who believe; verse 146 will describe God turning away from His signs those who behave with arrogance; and the sūrah's whole narrative section — the prophets and the towns — is an exhibit of two soils receiving identical messengers. Noah's town and the ark; Ḥūd's people and the remnant saved with him; Ṣāliḥ's lowly believers and the arrogant chiefs of verse 75: the same rain fell in each case and the same split appeared.

**"Thus We Vary the Signs"**

*Kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāt li-qawmin yashkurūn* — this is how We vary the signs for a people who are thankful. The verb *ṣarrafa* is the Book's word for turning a thing through its aspects: to put a subject in different lights so that each angle is exhausted, to rotate an argument. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly as a description of its own method: *“And so We vary our signs to the extent that they will say, ‘You have studied ˹previous scriptures˺,” and We make this ˹Quran˺ clear for people who know.’* (6:105); *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away. Other Gods?”* (17:41).

The Book's self-description is a claim about redundancy by design. The same lesson arrives as a prophet narrative, a courtroom scene, a weather report, a soil sample, and a scale; the reader who misses the agricultural version will meet a military version, and the reader who misses both will meet it in the fire of the Hereafter. What the variety presupposes is a reader who is still able to perceive — and the qualifying phrase of the verse, *a people who are thankful*, names the faculty. *Shukr* in the Qur'an is not politeness about goods received; it is the recognition of the giver that turns a recipient into a student.

**Why the Parable Matters Here**

It is worth noticing where the sūrah places this parable. It has just argued from rain and dead ground to the resurrection; it is about to begin the long sequence of prophets, in which the pattern of acceptance and rejection will repeat through five towns in the next forty verses. The soil verse stands at the seam and explains the mechanics of both: why the same message produces a garden in one heart and nothing in another, and why the Book keeps ploughing ground it has already sown.

The verse's theology also protects the sūrah's later judgment scenes. When the people of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Sodom, and Madyan are destroyed in the coming narratives, the reader has already been told the principle: the rain fell on all of them. The Book's retribution in the sūrah's narratives is consistently the consequence that follows the refusal of a mercy already given — which is why the whole section ends with the resurrection argument of verse 57 and the farmer's observation of verse 58. Nothing in it is arbitrary and nothing is secret.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:59

> **Indeed, We sent Noah unto his people, and he said, “O my people! Worship God! You have no god other than Him. Truly I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous day!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First of the Five Towns**

*Laqad arsalnā Nūḥan ilā qawmihi* — indeed We sent Noah to his people. With this verse the sūrah begins the longest sequence of prophetic narratives in the Qur'an: Noah (7:59–64), Ḥūd and ʿĀd (7:65–72), Ṣāliḥ and Thamūd (7:73–79), Lot (7:80–84), Shuʿayb and Madyan (7:85–93), followed by Moses and the children of Israel in the longest installment of all (7:103–171). Each of the five pre-Mosaic accounts follows a recognizable architecture: the calling, the accusation by the town's elite, the messenger's defense, the warning, and the end. The reader who has received the parable of the two soils in verse 58 is now shown the same rain falling five times on the same kind of ground.

**"Worship Allah; You Have No God Besides Him"**

The sūrah gives each prophet the same sentence, and the repetition is the point: *“O my people! Worship Allah — you have no other god except Him.”* The message of the entire sequence is thus placed under one heading. The towns being described were not atheistic; they had gods, sanctuaries, liturgies, and inherited customs, and the Qur'an never accuses them of doubting that a divine existed. What the messengers attack is the plurality: the assignment of divine prerogatives to created things — stone, star, ancestor, king — and, behind that assignment, the assumption that worship can be distributed the way power is distributed.

Sūrat al-Naḥl states the principle the messengers were enforcing: *“Allah has said: do not take two gods; surely He is only One God”* (16:51), and Sūrat al-Zumar puts it in the first person with a rhetorical question that dismantles the position: *“Say: is it other than Allah that you order me to worship, O ignorant ones?”* (39:64). The sūrah's own first narrative had already staged the same issue from the other direction: Iblīs's refusal was the refusal of a creature to accept the ordering God had given his kind. The five towns commit the same error at collective scale.

**"I Fear for You the Torment of a Tremendous Day"**

*Innī akhāfu ʿalaykum ʿadhāba yawmin ʿaẓīm* — I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous Day. The formula is the sūrah's own and its speakers' — Ḥūd and Ṣāliḥ will speak in the same register, and Sūrat al-Zumar's prophet uses the identical phrase (39:13). The *tremendous Day* is the Day of Judgment, and the sūrah has already defined it three times in its own voice (7:14, 7:51, 7:53). What is distinctive in Noah's sentence is the verb: *I fear for you*, not *I warn you of what is about to happen to you*. The messenger places himself inside the anxiety; the sūrah's prophets are not prosecutors but men burdened with an outcome they would prefer not to see.

That tone is worth noticing, because the sūrah's audience is about to meet it five times and it will always be followed by the same response. The pastoral vocabulary of the messengers — *my people*, *I fear for you*, *sincere advice* — is set against the vocabulary of the towns — *misguided*, *fool*, *liar* — and the sūrah's reader is invited to compare the two dictionaries at his leisure.

**Noah in the Book's Larger Portrait**

The Qur'an gives Noah the longest continuous treatment of any pre-Mosaic prophet, and the details of the other passages fill out what the sūrah assumes. Sūrat Nūḥ preserves his private reasoning with his Lord over the centuries of his calling: *“My Lord, I have called my people night and day, but my calling has only added to their flight”* (71:5–6). Sūrat Hūd records the elite's argument that he is merely a man like themselves and that only the lowest follow him (11:27), and Noah's response that he will not drive the believers away (11:29–30). Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt gives the duration: *“We certainly sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them a thousand years less fifty”* (29:14).

The significance of the narrative in this sūrah's arrangement is its function as the paradigm. Every subsequent messenger in the sequence will be accused as Noah was accused, will be rejected as Noah was rejected, and will see the rescue of a remnant as Noah saw it. The ark of verse 64 will become the sūrah's image of the one security available in a drowning world, and the towns that follow will keep failing to build one.

**The Great Day, Named Early**

Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt records the duration of that mission in a single clause — *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty”* (29:14) — and Sūrat Nūḥ preserves the messenger's own report of what the years produced: *“My Lord! I have surely called my people day and night, but my calls only made them run farther away”* (71:5–6). Verse 59's brevity compresses one of the longest ministries in the Book's history, and the compression is the sūrah's method: five towns, five speeches, one message, and a reader invited to notice that the outcome was never in doubt.

The sūrah has not yet told its reader what will happen to Noah's people, but the reader of the Book already knows from the other accounts: the flood, the ark, the people who called Noah a liar and drowned. Verse 59 works as an overture precisely because it says so little about the outcome and so much about the message. The reader who has followed the sūrah's first fifty-eight verses has already seen the courtroom, the scales, the gates, the wall, and the two crowds; he knows what a *tremendous Day* means, and he knows that the towns currently holding their breath in the narrative are walking toward it with their gods in their arms.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:60

> **The notables among his people said, “Truly we think that you are in manifest error.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict of the Elite**

*Qāla al-malaʾu min qawmihi innā la-narāka fī ḍalālin mubīn* — the chiefs of his people said: we surely see you in clear misguidance. The sentence is short and it does three things at once, and all three are the standard operating procedure of the sūrah's towns.

First, it identifies the speakers: *al-malaʾ*, the assembled notables — the men whose standing gives them the authority to pronounce. The Qur'an uses this term for the elite of every ruined city in this sūrah, and it uses it as a diagnosis. Noah's town (7:60), ʿĀd (7:66), the court of Pharaoh (7:103), Madyan's affluent leaders (7:88) — in each case the first voice raised against the messenger is the voice of established power. The ordinary people of the towns are not the ones who speak; the sūrah will later show them believing (7:75) and being silenced.

Second, the sentence is not an argument but a classification. Noah has just delivered a message about the worship of the One God and a warning about the Day; the response says nothing about either. What the chiefs supply instead is a label, *ḍalāl mubīn*, clear misguidance — one of the two verdicts the sūrah's narratives will trade in, the other being *liar*, which ʿĀd will use of Ḥūd (7:66) and Thamūd of Ṣāliḥ (7:77). The Qur'an's actors prefer the label because the label requires no reasoning: it moves the discussion from what was said to who is saying it.

Third, the plural is important. *We see you* — the claim is corporate. The chiefs are speaking for the town, and behind the grammar is the oldest argument in religious history: the weight of numbers and respectability. The verse's audience already knows how the Book answers that argument, because verse 48 placed a crowd of tyrants in the Fire being told that their multitude had availed them nothing.

**The Word Turned Around**

The label's irony is the sūrah's own. *Ḍalāl* — misguidance, straying — is the Qur'an's technical term for the condition of those who reject revelation, and the sūrah's earlier verse 30 had already used it in the divine voice: *“He has guided some, while others are destined to stray”* (7:30). From the chiefs' mouths, the word is a verdict on Noah; from the Book's, it is a verdict on them. Sūrat al-Naḥl records the same inversion at the Day of Judgment, where the followers of the misleaders say, *“For those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, We will add more punishment to their punishment for all the corruption they spread.”* (16:88 in its vicinity; cf. 33:67–68).

The Qur'an's habit of returning the accusers' vocabulary is not rhetorical flourish but the doctrine of requital in kind that the sūrah has been stating since the scale of verse 8. Every label the towns apply to the messengers in the following verses — misguided, fool, liar, sorcerer, bewitched — is used by the Book, later, of the towns.

**The Elite as a Category in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an's analysis of the *malaʾ* deserves attention because it is one of the Book's most consistent social observations. Sūrat al-Muʾminūn's version of Noah's narrative has the chiefs say, *“But the disbelieving chiefs of his people said ˹to the masses˺, ‘This is only a human like you, who wants to be superior to you. Had Allah willed, He could have easily sent down angels ˹instead˺. We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our …”* (23:24). Sūrat Hūd's has them argue that only *the lowliest* follow him (11:27). Sūrat Sabaʾ has the affluent of a town explain their superiority in wealth and children (34:35). Sūrat al-Zukhruf has the Makkans object that the Qur'an should have been sent to *a great man of the two cities* (43:31).

The common structure is the identification of truth with status. What makes the messengers unbearable to the elite is not usually the theology in the abstract but its consequence: if the message is true, the elders of the town have been wrong for a lifetime, and the poor who believed them first are ahead of them. The sūrah shows that reflex operating five times, and it does not let its reader forget that its own first audience, in Makkah, was reading the story of its own chiefs.

**What Noah Does Not Do**

The last thing worth noticing in verse 60 is what it does not contain. Noah is called clearly misguided by men with the power to ruin him, and the sūrah does not record that he answered with equivalent language. His response, in the next verse, begins with the address *O my people* and immediately denies the charge in the plainest terms while retaining the relationship, and then answers with the thing he actually is: a messenger conveying the messages of the Lord of all worlds. The Book's prophets are always more interested in the message than in their reputations.

The verse that follows the accusation is thus a small manual for anyone who has been publicly mislabelled for religious reasons: state the case, refuse the label, keep the address, and let the outcome be God's. The sūrah's coming narratives will supply the outcome five times over, and in each case the town's elite will have disappeared before the verdict, which is itself a comment on how much their consensus was worth.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:61

> **He said, “O my people! There is no error in me, but rather I am a messenger from the Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"You Are No Fool; You Are a Messenger"**

*Qāla yā qawmi laysa bī ḍalālatun wa-lākinnī rasūlun min rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — he said: O my people, I am not misguided; rather I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds. The reply is a model of economy, and its two halves correspond exactly to the two halves of the accusation.

Against the charge of misguidance, Noah states a bare negation: there is no error in me. He does not argue, does not produce proofs, and does not return the insult by calling the chiefs misguided, though the sūrah's reader knows they are. Instead he states the positive content of his position: *I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds*. The claim is the answer to the charge. A man whose commission comes from the Lord of all worlds cannot be at the same time a man in clear misguidance; the two descriptions are mutually exclusive, and the fact that the chiefs cannot see this is the real subject of the passage.

**"Lord of All Worlds"**

The title Noah uses is the one the sūrah used a few verses earlier in its own voice: *Tabāraka Allāh rabbu al-ʿālamīn* (7:54). The echo is deliberate. The chiefs of the town have just measured a man by the standards of their settlement; the man has just named the authority he represents as the Lord of every world. Makkah, for all its importance as a sanctuary, was one town among thousands; the message being proclaimed in it belonged to a jurisdiction that has no boundary.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ puts this contrast at the head of every one of its prophets' speeches: *“I am a trustworthy messenger to you, so fear Allah and obey me; I ask no reward of you, for my reward is only with the Lord of all worlds”* (26:107–109 and parallels). The phrase is the Qur'an's shorthand for the universality of the prophetic claim and the total absence of a local interest in it. Noah is not asking for the ḥimā of a clan or a share of the caravan traffic; he is announcing a message that has no patrons.

**"I Am Not Misguided" — Prophetic Self-Definition**

The Books of the prophets in the Qur'an show a consistent reticence: the messenger never defends his own spiritual attainment, and he never claims personal authority. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's Shuʿayb answers a similar charge by pointing to what is *with Allah* (7:89); Hūd says less than Noah does here (7:67); and Ṣāliḥ's rebuke of his people in verse 79 places the whole affair in the category of *counsel delivered*. The Book's only claim on behalf of its messengers is the message: *“Your fellow man is neither misguided nor astray. … Nor does he speak of his own whims.”* (53:2–3), and *“Say, ‘I am not the first messenger ever sent, nor do I know what will happen to me or you. I only follow what is revealed to me. And I am only sent with a clear warning.’”* (46:9).

The reticence is doctrinal, not modest. A messenger whose authority was personal would be replaceable; a messenger whose authority is the words given to him is not the point at all. Sūrat Fuṣṣilat states the same position in the Prophet's own voice: *“I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God”* (41:6).

**The Familiar Address**

The verse's opening words are worth keeping in view: *yā qawmi*, O my people. Noah has been pronounced misguided, and he answers by calling the accusers his own. The Book's prophets never sever the relationship while the argument continues, and the sūrah's five narratives show this address surviving every stage short of the destruction: Noah uses it here, Hūd in his replies (7:65, 7:68), Ṣāliḥ at the end of his town's history, when he turns away saying, *“O my people, I surely conveyed to you my Lord's message and gave you sincere advice”* (7:79), and Shuʿayb in the last speech of the sequence (7:89).

This vocabulary places the sūrah's prophets in the lineage of the Qur'an's pastoral language: a messenger is not an officer sent to a district, and his warnings are not a state's verdicts. The word *qawmī* — my people — carries an admission that the speaker belongs to the same community in every sense except the one that matters, which is precisely what makes the message credible and the rejection tragic. Noah's century of rain and ridicule, described elsewhere in the Book, happens inside that pronoun.

**The Pattern the Sūrah Is Building**

The answer in verse 61 establishes the template that the rest of the sequence will follow. Each prophet will be accused; each prophet will deny the charge with the same economy; each will then identify himself by his commission, and each will defend the towns' right to hear the truth even as the towns take away their right to speak. What changes from town to town is only the specific charge — ʿĀd prefers the word *fool*, Thamūd prefers *sorcerer* and *liar*, the people of Lot prefer *expulsion* — and the sūrah's reader is meant to notice that the dictionary of abuse is the towns' only real contribution. The messengers' vocabulary is the same in every case, and it is the vocabulary of the object being announced.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:62

> **I deliver unto you the messages of my Lord, and advise you sincerely, and I know from God what you know not.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Duties of a Messenger**

*Uballighukum risālāti rabbī wa-anṣaḥu lakum* — I convey to you the messages of my Lord, and I give you sincere counsel. The verse names the messenger's work under two headings, and the order is significant. The first is transmission: the message is not his, and his task is not interpretation or improvement. The second is advice: he is a *nāṣiḥ*, a sincere counsellor, and the word carries in Arabic a whole ethic of service without advantage.

The Qur'an's prophets all describe themselves in the same terms, and the sūrah will use *nāṣiḥ* again for Ḥūd (7:68) and twice at the end of Ṣāliḥ's story (7:79), where he repeats it as the summary of his mission. The word's root — *n-ṣ-ḥ* — carries the sense of purity and the sense of stitching: the adviser whose counsel is free of self-interest, and the tailor who mends without leaving a seam. The Book uses it for the man of Madyan who warned Moses of a plot against him (28:20) and for the brother of Yūsuf, and it is the standard term for the religious duty of giving good counsel to those who are open to it.

**"And I Know From Allah What You Do Not Know"**

*Wa-aʿlamu mina Allāh mā lā taʿlamūn* — and I know from Allah what you do not know. The sentence is the epistemological edge of the prophetic claim, and the Book's messengers keep it sharp. The knowledge in question is not information about the future or mastery of hidden things; Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own Ḥūd will declare no such possession, and Sūrat Hūd records Noah saying explicitly that he does not claim the treasures of God or knowledge of the unseen (11:31). What the messengers do possess, in the Book's consistent account, is something received: *“Nor does he speak of his own whims. … It is only a revelation sent down ˹to him˺.”* (53:3–4).

The verse's audience is being asked to entertain the possibility that a human being might have access to facts about the moral structure of reality that they have not been given — and that this access is not a rank but a burden. The Book ties the burden to the transmission in the same breath. Sūrat al-Anʿām puts it: *“Still your people ˹O Prophet˺ have rejected this ˹Quran˺, although it is the truth. Say, ‘I am not a keeper over you’”* (6:66; cf. 6:107). Knowledge of the kind Noah claims does not confer control; it confers responsibility to speak.

**The Two Ways of Hearing the Verse**

The classical commentators read the pair of duties as a division of labour that answers two very different impulses in the messenger's audience. Transmission speaks to the person who wants information: what does God require? The answer is the message, and it is delivered without alteration — the sūrah's Moses will receive the command *“We wrote for him on the Tablets ˹the fundamentals˺ of everything; commandments and explanations of all things. ˹We commanded,˺ ‘Hold to this firmly and ask your people to take the best of it. I will soon show ˹all of˺ you the home of the rebellious.”* (7:145), and the Book's final prophet is instructed simply to recite (96:1). Advice speaks to the person who wants a relationship with the messenger: a father, a guide, a man who wants his people's good. The Qur'an's messengers are not allowed to become authorities over conscience — *“You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:22), *“Had Allah willed, they would not have been polytheists. We have not appointed you as their keeper, nor are you their maintainer.”* (6:107) — but they are required to be sincere.

The combination protects both the message and the messenger's humanity. The message cannot be diluted on the grounds of pastoral cleverness, and the messenger cannot be turned into a machine. Noah's century of calling, in the Book's telling, is filled with both duties: he explains and he pleads (71:5–20).

**What the Chiefs Refuse to Hear**

Read against verse 60, the two headings identify what the chiefs' accusation made impossible. They called Noah misguided, which is a judgment about his person; they did not engage the message, which is a claim about God. The messenger's reply says, in effect: the question was never my competence. It is whether the message I convey came from the Lord of all worlds, and whether the advice I am giving you is sincere. Both of those questions are answered by testing the content, and the towns will not test it.

The other accounts supply what this verse leaves out, and none of them reports a reversal. *“And it was revealed to Noah, ‘None of your people will believe except those who already have. So do not be distressed by what they have been doing’”* (11:36) — the disclosure arrives after the centuries of argument and confirms what the messenger had learned from God rather than from the town. The claim *I know from Allah what you do not know* is therefore not a boast about private information; it is the statement of a man whose knowledge of the outcome came from the One who sent him, and who continued to call anyway.

The sūrah's arrangement will make that refusal the explicit charge against every city in the sequence. In the account of Noah to come, the town's answer to a century of speech is a decision to wait and see: *“they said: our Lord, if this is the truth from You, then rain down upon us stones from the sky”* (8:32 is Makkan rhetoric; Noah's own version is 71:23–24). The Book's presentation is that the refusal is not an intellectual position at all but a social one: the notables could not examine the message without examining themselves.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:63

> **Or do you marvel that a reminder from your Lord should come unto you by means of a man from among yourselves, so as to warn you, that you might be reverent, and that haply you may receive mercy?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Do You Wonder?"**

*A-wa-ʿajibtum an jāʾakum dhikrun min rabbikum ʿalā rajulin minkum* — do you find it astonishing that a reminder from your Lord should come through a man from among you? The question is the sūrah's answer to the towns' real objection, and it is stated with an incredulity that turns the tables. The chiefs find it *astonishing* that God would send a human being; the verse asks what else they expected.

The Qur'an records the objection in several forms and answers it in kind. *“And they say ˹mockingly˺, ‘What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner,”* (25:7). *“And what prevented people from believing when guidance came to them was only that they said: has Allah sent a human being as a messenger?”* (17:94). The Book's reply to the last one is the most elegant: *“Say: had there been angels walking the earth, settled and at ease, We would have sent them an angel from the sky as a messenger”* (17:95). The choice of messenger follows the nature of the addressee; revelation comes in the tongue of the people who must receive it, and the Qur'an says so directly elsewhere: *“We never sent a messenger except in the language of his people, so that he might make it clear to them”* (14:4).

**The Argument From Human Ordinariness**

Behind the objection sits an assumption the sūrah is dismantling: that divine speech requires a superhuman vehicle, that a creature who eats and sleeps and dies cannot be a conduit for something eternal. The Qur'an's counter-argument is that the supernatural vehicle would defeat the purpose. The messenger's ordinariness is the guarantee of the message's transferability: a man from *among you*, whose honesty you can audit, whose household you know, whose life before the commission you can inspect. Sūrat Yūnus records the Makkan audience being told exactly that: *“Say: if Allah had willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you. I had lived a lifetime among you before it — will you not then understand?”* (10:16).

The Book returns to this protection in its accounts of the messengers' humanity. They are men who eat food (25:20), who marry and have children (13:38), who grieve and get angry, who die. Sūrat al-Kahf instructs the Prophet to declare, *“I am only a human being like you, but it has been revealed to me that your God is one God”* (18:110). The humanity is not a compromise; it is the design.

**"A Reminder From Your Lord"**

*Dhikr* — the key word of the verse — is the Qur'an's own name for revelation: a reminder, not a new invention. The Book's self-presentation is that it restores what the human being already knows and has let slip. *“It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it.”* (15:9). *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind.”* (88:21). Noah's question to his town is therefore pointed: the thing arriving is not a foreign system but a recollection, and a recollection does not need a supernatural speaker to be credible. It needs a hearer who is willing to remember.

The sūrah's own audience had the same reminder in the same form: *“This is a blessed Book which We have revealed, confirming what came before it, so that you may warn the mother of cities and all around it”* (6:92), and its Prophet was told to say that his message and the earlier ones share a single source (41:43; 46:9). Verse 63's use of the word places Noah's preaching on the same continuum as the Book being recited in Makkah.

**The Purpose in the Last Clauses**

*Wa-lākinnī rasūlun... li-yundhirakum wa-laʿallakum turḥamūn* — the warning and the mercy. The verse ends by naming the two outcomes the reminder is designed to produce: *so that you may beware*, and *so that you may be shown mercy*. The order is the Book's standard soteriology. Awakening precedes mercy; fear is not the opposite of hope but its antechamber, and the sūrah has just told its readers, two verses before its first prophet opened his mouth, to call upon God *with hope and fear* (7:56).

The Book's frequent phrasing — *perhaps you may be shown mercy* — is not a statement of uncertainty about God's willingness but a condition placed on a human variable. The same construction appears throughout the sūrah's warnings: the towns may be shown mercy, and their conduct decides whether the mercy has a landing place. Sūrat al-Anʿām puts the principle in a single line: *“and ‘If Allah touches you with harm, none can undo it except Him. And if He intends good for you, none can withhold His bounty. He grants it to whoever He wills of His servants. And He is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.’”’* (10:107 in its own sūrah).

**The Wonder That Damns**

The one thing the verse leaves untouched is the chiefs' capacity for wonder. Their question is not *what does the reminder say* but *how did it get here*; their intelligence has been spent on the vehicle rather than the cargo. That reflex is the sūrah's definition of the elite of every town it describes, and it is the reflex the Book repeatedly judges by its fruit. Sūrat al-Mulk's question answers it across the whole world: *“Do they not look at the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them up except the Most Compassionate”* (67:19). Wonder at the mechanism is available to everyone; attention to the message is the harder discipline, and the sūrah's next verse shows the town declining to attempt it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:64

> **Yet they denied him. So We saved him and those who were with him in the Ark, and We drowned those who denied Our signs. Truly they were a blind people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ending of the First Town**

*Fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-anjaynāhu wa-lladhīna maʿahu fī al-fulki* — but they rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the ark. The sūrah has compressed the entire flood into a single sentence, and the compression is the point. What the town did is stated first and in one word, *kadhdhabūhu*: they declared him a liar. What God did follows immediately: the rescue and the drowning. Between the two clauses stands the whole of a civilization's history, and the sūrah gives it the length of a comma.

The reader of the Qur'an knows the details from the other accounts: the sign given to Noah about the oven (11:40), the pairs of every species loaded into the vessel (11:40), the ark riding on waves like mountains (11:42), the son who would not board (11:42–43), the call from the ship to a world that had mocked it for a century (11:42), the waters that rose until the vessel came to rest on al-Jūdī (11:44), and the command to the sky and the earth, *“O earth, swallow your water; O sky, cease”* (11:44). Sūrat al-Qamar gives the whole event four verses and a purpose: *“and We left it as a sign — is there any who will be mindful?”* (54:15).

**The Ark as the Sūrah's Image**

In the architecture of the sūrah, the ark carries more than its cargo. It is the first of the deliverances that will punctuate the sequence of towns, and it establishes the pattern of the Book's narrative logic: the warning is genuine, the delay is generous, and the rescue is a remnant. Ḥūd's people are saved by mercy (7:72), the believers in Thamūd are named as the lowly who spoke up (7:75), and Lot's family leaves in the night (7:83).

The vessel itself is described elsewhere in the Book with a vocabulary that makes it the prototype of the Hereafter's escape routes. *“And it was said: O earth, swallow your water, and O sky, withhold your rain. And the water subsided, and the matter was accomplished”* (11:44). Sūrat al-Qamar, as noted, preserves the ship as a permanent sign (54:15), and Sūrat al-Muʾminūn adds the doctrine of *taskhīr*: the ark rides by God's command (23:27). The Book's use of the flood as a model of the resurrection is explicit in Sūrat al-Qamar: *“We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain, and We caused the earth to gush forth with springs, so the waters met for a purpose already decreed”* (54:11–12).

**"A Blind People"**

*Innahum kānū qawman ʿamīn* — they were a blind people. The adjective is the sūrah's final diagnosis of Noah's town, and its choice is deliberate. The Qur'an's vocabulary of blindness is used consistently for the failure of perception rather than of eyesight — *“deaf, dumb, and blind; they will never return”* (2:18); *“those who were blind in this life will be blind in the Hereafter”* (17:72) — and the Book's most complete statement of the doctrine appears in this very sūrah, eleven verses later: *“They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear”* (7:179).

The word closes the account with a verdict that is not moral but perceptual. Noah's town was not condemned for being wicked in the abstract; it was condemned for standing in the sun for a century and refusing to concede that there was light. The rain of verses 57 and 58 had already described the difference between the fertile land and the barren one, and here the sūrah gives the barren land its name.

**The Bodily Waste of a Warning**

There is a heartbreak built into the grammar of the verse that the Book's audiences have always felt: the person saved is *him and those with him*, and the phrase *those with him* includes the tiny remnant attached to one family — the sons who believed, the wives who did not, and, in some traditions, the household members who fit in a boat. The messenger's century produced a fraction of one town.

The Book's other flood narratives fill in the details this verse withholds. Sūrat al-Qamar describes the vessel — *“We carried him on that ˹Ark made˺ of planks and nails”* (54:13) — and states its purpose: *“We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful?”* (54:15). Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah addresses the drowned generation directly: *“Indeed, when the floodwater had overflowed, We carried you in the floating Ark ˹with Noah˺, so that We may make this a reminder to you, and that attentive ears may grasp it”* (69:11–12). And Sūrat Hūd ends its account with the Book's command to the creation itself: *“O earth! Swallow up your water. And O sky! Withhold ˹your rain˺. The floodwater receded and the decree was carried out. The Ark rested on Mount Judi”* (11:44). Verse 64's single clause — *We saved him and those with him* — is the sūrah's whole account of that event, and the economy is the point: the interest lies in the verdict, not the spectacle.

The sūrah does not linger on the emotion, and this is characteristic of it. What it lingers on is the causal chain: they rejected, therefore He saved and drowned. The sequence is the same one the reader met in the Hereafter scenes: the refrain that began with the question *who is more unjust than the one who invents a lie about God?* (7:37) reaches its first historical instance here, and the next four towns will run the experiment again with different people, different gods, and identical results. The reader who has been paying attention to the pattern knows what will happen to ʿĀd before the sūrah tells him, and the Book's invitation is to notice which of the responses he recognizes as his own.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:65

> **And unto ʿĀd, their brother, Hūd. He said, “O my people! Worship God! You have no god other than Him. Will you not be reverent?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿĀd and Their Brother**

*Wa-ilā ʿĀdin akhāhum Hūdan* — and to ʿĀd, their brother Hūd. The sūrah's second city is introduced with a phrase that carries more weight than its brevity suggests. *Akhāhum* — their brother — is the Qur'an's way of locating the messenger inside the community he addresses. Hūd is not a stranger sent from elsewhere with a warrant; he is a man of ʿĀd, known to its families, sharing its water, its grazing, and its ancestors. The Book uses the same word for every pre-Mosaic messenger in this sequence: the brother of ʿĀd, the brother of Thamūd, the brother of Madyan (7:65, 7:73, 7:85).

The effect is an intensification of the towns' responsibility rather than a softening of the message. The Qur'an's classic statement of the point appears in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ, where the townsmen are told that their messenger's credentials are local: *“when their brother Hûd said to them, ‘Will you not fear Allah?’”* (26:124, with the same opening for Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ, and Shuʿayb at 26:142, 26:161, 26:177), and the Prophet's own community is reminded that there *“certainly has come to you a messenger from among yourselves”* (9:128). The Book's summons is never exotic. It arrives in the voice of a neighbour, and the towns' decision to reject it is a decision about someone they could have questioned any day of the week.

**The People of Iram**

ʿĀd is not a footnote in the Qur'an; it is the Book's standing example of a civilization destroyed by strength. Sūrat al-Fajr names their city with a grandeur the Book reserves almost exclusively for it: *“˹the people˺ of Iram—with ˹their˺ great stature, … unmatched in any other land;”* (89:7–8). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ lists their monuments and asks whether they built them for eternity: *“Do you build a landmark on every high place, amusing yourselves, and take for yourselves palaces as though you would live forever?”* (26:128–129). Sūrat Fuṣṣilat records the boast that the sūrah turns into their epitaph: *“As for ’Ȃd, they acted arrogantly throughout the land with no right, boasting, ‘Who is superior to us in might?” Did they not see that Allah ˹Himself˺, Who created them, was far superior to them in might? Still they persisted in denying Our signs.’* (41:15).

The Makkans who first heard this sūrah did not need the identification explained. ʿĀd's territory lay along the southern trade routes, and the ruined towers of the people the Qur'an calls *dhāti al-ʿimād* were the first thing a caravan saw on the road toward Yemen.

**"Worship Allah; You Have No God Besides Him"**

Hūd's message is Noah's message, word for word, and the sūrah does not vary it. This is a deliberate feature of the sequence and it makes a doctrinal claim about prophecy: the messengers were not religious innovators adapting a message to local taste. *“He has ordained for you the same religion He enjoined upon Noah, and that which We have revealed to you, and that which We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: establish the religion and do not divide over it”* (42:13). The towns' objections differ, their gods differ, their civilizations differ radically; the message does not.

The verse then adds the ending that the sūrah gives to Hūd alone: *a-fa-lā tattaqūn* — will you not then fear Him? The question form is the Book's most common rhetorical instrument, and it calls for a specific kind of answer. It does not ask whether the hearer has understood the argument; it asks whether the hearer is willing to live inside the consequence of a God who is real. The sūrah's own opening used the same construction to open the whole sūrah: *“˹This is˺ a Book sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺— do not let anxiety into your heart regarding it—so with it you may warn ˹the disbelievers˺, and as a reminder to the believers. … Follow what has been sent down to you from your Lord, and do not take …”* (7:2–3 context), and Sūrat al-Naḥl's affirmation of the divine oneness closes with a similar question (16:51–52).

**Why Ḥūd's Address Begins With Worship**

The verse places worship first and ethics nowhere, and a reader might wonder why the sūrah's messengers always begin with ritual. The answer given by the Book's own accounts of ʿĀd is that the town's problem was not confined to its liturgy; it was the confidence of power. Sūrat al-Aḥqāf records the advice they received and refused: they were reminded of the favours of God and answered with the argument of precedent — *“You have come to us to turn us away from our gods; then bring us what you threaten us with, if you are truthful”* (46:22). The worship of ʿĀd's gods was not devotion to statues in the abstract; it was the sanctification of their own order, the ritual expression of a society that answered to nobody.

Hūd's demand therefore cuts at the root of the town's self-understanding. If the One God is the only God, then the tribe's authority is delegated rather than inherent, the stronghold is a trust rather than a possession, and the pillars of Iram are neither gods nor monuments to one. The verse's single sentence dissolves the architecture of ʿĀd's political theology, and the sūrah's reader is meant to hear in it the same dissolution applied to the merchants and chiefs of Makkah.

**The Question the Town Never Answers**

The verse ends on a question, and the town's answer will come in the next verse as an insult. Between the two lies the whole distance between the sūrah's method and its opponents': Hūd asks a question about accountability, and ʿĀd replies with a description of Hūd's intelligence. The Book notes the exchange without comment, which is its own comment.

The pattern is the same one the reader saw with Noah, and it will repeat three more times before the passage ends. The sūrah's prophet sequence is thus less a set of biographies than a controlled experiment: the same message, the same audience categories, the same escalation, and the same outcome, presented consecutively so that no reader can attribute the result to a quirk of one town or one messenger. The next verse makes the result explicit, and its terms are the terms of the towns, not of the Book.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:66

> **The notables among his people who disbelieved said, “Truly we think that you are foolish, and we consider you to be among the liars.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Charges From the Chiefs**

*Qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qawmihi innā la-narāka fī safāhatin wa-innā la-naẓunnuka mina al-kādhibīn* — the disbelieving chiefs of his people said: we surely see you in foolishness, and we surely think you are one of the liars. The answer to Hūd's question about accountability is a two-part accusation, and both parts are worth examining because they are the two standard defenses of a threatened establishment.

The first is *safāhah*, folly — an assessment of capacity. The word covers lightness of judgment, imprudence, the mind that cannot weigh things properly. Against a prophet whose message is a claim about ultimate reality, the charge says: you are not wicked, you are not dangerous, you are simply not a serious person. The second is *kādhibīn*, liars — an assessment of character, and the two are mutually exclusive in any sane court. A fool cannot be a consistent fraud, and a deliberate liar is by definition not a fool. The chiefs assert both because their purpose is not to describe Hūd but to remove him from consideration, and any two labels will do the job.

**The Grammar of Social Defense**

The verse is careful about who is speaking: *al-malaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qawmihi* — the chiefs of his people who disbelieved. The qualifier is doing explicit work. The sūrah has already told its readers, in the account of Ṣāliḥ, that the elite and the ordinary believers were distinct groups (7:75–76); here the particle *alladhīna kafarū* marks off the notables who disbelieved from the town as a whole. The Book does not hold a people responsible for its leadership's unbelief unless the people follow them into it.

And the accusation comes from the establishment because only the establishment had something to lose. Hūd's message did not ask ʿĀd for money or territory; it asked ʿĀd to stop treating its own tribal order as sacred. What the chiefs heard was a threat not to their wealth but to their standing, and the response they produced is the response that the sūrah's readers will have seen in their own town: a decision to attack the messenger's competence rather than the message's content.

**The Qur'an's Reply to Both Charges**

The Book answers the two accusations throughout its narrative sections with weapons it never tires of using. To the charge of folly, it answers with the Criterion itself: a text whose coherence is offered as evidence, recitation whose like no human being has produced (2:23; 10:38; 11:13; 17:88), and the plain fact that the messengers' claims about the natural order — rain, dead land, resurrection — are the claims their audiences already accepted about their own agriculture. To the charge of lying, it answers with the messengers' biographies: the century of Noah's preaching, the decades of Hūd's presence among people who knew his honesty, the disinterestedness of their message. *“Say: I ask no reward of you for it — it is not for me to impose upon you. My reward is only with the Lord of all worlds”* (26:109 and parallels). A liar has a motive; the Book repeatedly points out that the messengers had none.

Sūrat Fuṣṣilat renders the whole exchange in a form that strips the charges of their respectability: *“The disbelievers advised ˹one another˺, ‘Do not listen to this Quran but drown it out so that you may prevail.’”* (41:26). That is the same defense in a cruder register, and the sūrah's version — fools and liars — is only the dressed-up form of the same tactic.

**What the Accusation Reveals About ʿĀd**

The chiefs' words also contain the sūrah's analysis of the town's religion. The men who called Hūd foolish were not, by any account in the Book, foolish themselves; they were the engineers of the pillars, the organizers of the caravan routes, the managers of a sophisticated society. What they lacked was a category for obedience. Their world was made up of things they could assess and use, and a summons to submit to an absent authority was, inside that world, indistinguishable from foolishness.

The sūrah's ʿĀd therefore becomes the Book's case study in the confidence of competence: a people so capable that the very idea of accountability looked like an insult, and so successful that the warning of a Day read like bad manners. Sūrat Fuṣṣilat records their boast — *who is stronger than us in might?* (41:15) — and answers it in the next clause: *did they not see that Allah, who created them, is stronger than them in might?* What ʿĀd possessed was real. The question the sūrah keeps putting to them, and to its own listeners, is whether strength is a warrant or a loan.

**The Second Refusal**

The verse ends the town's first exchange, and the sūrah's second cycle is now running exactly as the first did. Noah was told he was misguided; Hūd is told he is a fool and a liar. The escalation in the vocabulary is worth noting, because the sūrah's towns are progressively less courteous as the sequence unfolds. What does not change is the position of the messenger: Hūd will reply in the next verse with the same denial and the same commission, and the sūrah will continue to present the messengers' sentences side by side until their identity of content becomes impossible to miss, and impossible to attribute to invention.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:67

> **He said, “O my people! There is no foolishness in me, but rather I am a messenger from the Lord of the worlds.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Answer in the Same Words**

*Qāla yā qawmi laysa bī safāhatun wa-lākinnī rasūlun min rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — he said: O my people, there is no foolishness in me; rather I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds. Hūd's reply is Noah's reply transposed by one letter of vocabulary: Noah denied *ḍalālah*, misguidedness; Hūd denies *safāhah*, folly. Everything else is identical, down to the address *yā qawmī* and the title *rabb al-ʿālamīn*.

The repetition is one of the sūrah's boldest compositional decisions, and it is not a stylistic failure. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ, which tells the same five narratives at greater length, organizes itself around a refrain repeated verbatim by each prophet: *“I am a trustworthy messenger to you, so fear Allah and obey me. I ask no reward of you; my reward is only with the Lord of all worlds”* (26:107–109, 26:125–127, 26:143–145, 26:162–164, 26:178–180). The Book's audiences were meant to hear the cadence and to draw the inference: the message has one source, and the messengers are its successive carriers rather than its authors.

**The Prophet's Own Commission**

The title *rasūl min rabbi al-ʿālamīn* is also the title the sūrah applied to Muhammad in its opening verses, and the Book links the two missions explicitly. Sūrat al-Aḥqāf instructs the Prophet to say, *“I am not the first of the messengers, nor do I know what will be done with me or with you. I only follow what is revealed to me, and I am only a clear warner”* (46:9). Sūrat al-Shūrā grounds the unity more deeply: *“He has ordained for you the same religion He enjoined upon Noah, and what We revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus”* (42:13).

What the Makkan chiefs were hearing, therefore, when the Prophet recited Hūd's denial, was not a piece of ancient history. It was a mirror held up in their own assembly, in which their own accusations — *a poet, a soothsayer, a man possessed* (69:41–42; 52:29–30) — were shown to be the latest edition of a document that had been in use for millennia. The sūrah's repetition of the messengers' words is designed for precisely this recognition, and the Book has no interest in making the parallels subtle.

**"O My People": The Address as Evidence**

The consistent opening of the reply — *yā qawmī* — deserves its own attention, because it is the one element of the exchange that the towns never imitate. The chiefs refer to Hūd in the third person among themselves (*we see you… we think you…*); the messenger addresses them as his own. The Qur'an's prophets hold the community inside their speech right up to the last verse of their missions, and the sūrah will show Ṣāliḥ doing it after his town is dead: *“O my people, I surely conveyed to you my Lord's message and gave you sincere advice”* (7:79).

The Book's later account of the Prophet's own manner repeats the pattern. When his people drove him from al-Ṭāʾif, the tradition reports that he prayed for them rather than against them; and Sūrat al-Qalam, addressing the accusations of the Makkan chiefs point by point, ends with an instruction to patience and a reminder of the man of the whale, who called out in the darkness (68:48). The address *my people* is the sūrah's evidence that the messengers were not combatants in this dispute but kinsmen.

**What Repetition Teaches the Reader**

There is a pedagogical consequence to the sūrah's arrangement that is easy to miss and impossible to overstate. By the time the reader reaches Hūd's reply, he has heard Noah say the same sentence. By the time he reaches Ṣāliḥ, he has heard it three times; Shuʿayb will make it five. The reader's familiarity with the formula becomes, by the end of the passage, a form of knowledge about the shape of religious history: this argument has been conducted before, in the same terms, with the same objections, and the outcome is documented.

That is precisely what the sūrah has been preparing since verse 59, and it explains why the narratives are compressed rather than expanded: the detail is not in the town's collapse but in the messengers' sentences, and the reader is expected to memorize them.

**The Denial That Names the Charge**

The Book's other accounts of ʿĀd give the boast in the town's own words. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves it as a question: *“˹Why˺ do you build a landmark on every high place in vanity, and construct castles, as if you are going to live forever?”* (26:128–129). Sūrat Fuṣṣilat records the arrogance and the answer together: *“As for ʿĀd, they acted arrogantly throughout the land with no right, boasting, ‘Who is superior to us in might?’ Did they not see that Allah ˹Himself˺, Who created them, was far superior to them in might?”* (41:15). Hūd's denial of foolishness is thus spoken into a town that had already decided the only argument worth hearing was its own strength.

One detail distinguishes Hūd's denial from Noah's: the form. Noah says, *there is no misguidance in me*; Hūd says, *there is no foolishness in me*. Both negations take the preposition and the indefinite noun, both refuse the label without insulting the labellers, and both then supply the alternative account of the man speaking. The Book's messengers never respond to a personal charge with a personal countercharge. The refusal is total and the alternative is positive: not *I am wise*, but *I am a messenger*. The reader is invited to see that the exchange was never about the speaker's qualities at all, and that only one of the parties to it seems to know that.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:68

> **I deliver unto you the messages of my Lord, and truly I am a trustworthy adviser unto you.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counsel and the Commission**

*Uballeghukum rasālāti rabbī wa-anā lakum nāṣiḥun amīn* — I convey to you the messages of my Lord, and I am a sincere adviser to you. Hūd's sentence repeats Noah's double duty with one addition at the end: *amīn*, trustworthy. The word is the crown of the verse, and it belongs to the vocabulary of deposit and return. *Amīn* is the person entrusted with something — a caravan's goods, a household's stores, a secret — who returns it whole. The messenger is a trustee of the message, and the term claims for the transmission the reliability the towns were denying him in the previous verse.

The slogan is the same one that the Qur'an uses for the Prophet himself: *“I am a trustworthy messenger to you”* (26:107, 26:125, 26:143, 26:162, 26:178), and the sequence in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows it deliberately repeated by each prophet in the sūrah's own cadence. Trustworthiness is the one quality the Book insists on for its messengers: not brilliance, not eloquence, not miracles, but the faithful return of what was entrusted to them.

**What Trustworthiness Is For**

The Qur'an's arguments for revelation lean heavily on this attribute, and the way it uses it is nearly forensic. A message is only as good as its carrier; a messenger who alters the text to suit his audience destroys the evidence he is bringing. Hence the Book's repeated insistence that the Prophet recite without adjustment — *“Say: I do not say to you that the treasures of Allah are with me, or that I know the unseen, or that I am an angel; I only follow what is revealed to me”* (6:50) — and its equal insistence that the message be delivered whole, including the parts that are inconvenient to its own community: *“What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.’”* (38:70); *“Say, ‘I am not the first messenger ever sent, nor do I know what will happen to me or you. I only follow what is revealed to me. And I am only sent with a clear warning.’”* (46:9).

The makings of ʿĀd's refusal become visible under this instruction. The chiefs did not accuse their messenger of incompetence; they accused him of lying. The verse takes that charge and answers it with a claim about the messenger's reliability — which, for the reader, will be tested by whether the message survives the tongue of a man who was going to be killed for it.

**Sincerity Without Return**

*Nāṣiḥ* — the sincere adviser — reappears here, the second use of the word in the sūrah's sequence, after Noah's. It is worth noting what the messengers do not receive for it. Nowhere in the sūrah's narratives does the sincere adviser of a town profit in any way from his counsel; the Qur'an's prophets are poor, mocked, boycotted, and in most cases driven out. Hūd's audience will end the story by asking him to justify himself with a miracle and will then call for the destruction he warned them of (7:70). The Book's prophets are, by design, without leverage.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ' formula makes this explicit in the messenger's own voice: *“I ask no reward of you for it; my reward is only with the Lord of all worlds”* (26:127). The repeated phrase means that any account of religion as a profession of self-interest must fail against the Book's own description of its prophets, and ʿĀd's charges — fool and liar — collapse against a man with nothing to gain.

**The Sūrah's Doctrine of the Messenger**

This verse and its counterpart in Noah's account give the sūrah's fullest summary of what a messenger is, and everything else in the Book's account hangs on it. He does not rule, does not heal at will, does not know the unseen, and does not command obedience for its own sake — Sūrat al-Kahf instructs the Prophet to announce his servitude and mortality in one breath (18:110), and Sūrat al-Naḥl tells him *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. … You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22 in its sūrah). What he does is carry a text and give counsel.

The narrowness is the point. The Book is protecting the message from its carrier, and the towns in this sūrah are shown repeatedly trying to turn the argument into a referendum on the prophet's character — the accusation of folly, the charge of lying, the sneer at verse 75 that the believers are following a deluded man. The messenger's reply to all of it in these verses is the same: what he carries is not his, and he will not let the discussion migrate from the text to himself.

**The Two Words the Towns Never Use**

The sūrah's contrast can be stated precisely. The messengers call themselves *transmitters* and *counsellors*; the towns call them *fools*, *liars*, and *misguided*. In the five narratives of this sūrah, the elite never once accuse the messenger of altering the message or of wanting something from them — because the messengers' conduct makes those charges impossible. What remains for the towns to attack is the messenger's person, and the sūrah's readers, watching the exchange from the distance of a few thousand years and a few pages, are being trained to notice when the same substitution happens in their own arguments.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:69

> **Or do you marvel that a reminder from your Lord should come to you by means of a man from among yourselves, so as to warn you? Remember when He made you vicegerents after the people of Noah, and increased you amply in stature. So remember the boons of God, that haply you may prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Do You Wonder?" Again**

*A-wa-ʿajibtum an jāʾakum dhikrun min rabbikum ʿalā rajulin minkum* — do you find it astonishing that a reminder from your Lord should come to you through a man among you? Hūd asks Noah's question in Noah's words, and the sūrah's towns give the same non-answer. The Book's repetition is designed to break the objection's credibility: if the wonder at a human messenger is unreasonable in the first town, it is unreasonable in the second and the fifth, and the Qur'an has already supplied the reason — *“had there been angels walking the earth, settled and at ease, We would have sent them an angel from the sky as a messenger”* (17:95).

The additional purpose clause in Hūd's version is *li-yundhirakum* — so that he may warn you. The warning is the message's function, and the Book keeps attention on it because the towns keep trying to move the conversation elsewhere.

**The Argument From Succession: "He Made You Successors After the People of Noah"**

*Udkhurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi qawmi Nūḥ* — remember when He made you successors after the people of Noah. The verse turns from the messenger's credentials to the audience's history, and it uses a word that occurs at several key points in the sūrah: *khulafāʾ*, successors, deputies, the ones who come after. The Qur'an uses it for humanity's viceregency on the earth (2:30), for the generations that inherit a land after its inhabitants (7:74, 7:100, 10:14, 27:62), and for David's succession (38:26).

The claim being made to ʿĀd is that their civilization is a legacy. The town did not create the land it farms or the routes it trades; it occupied a place made empty by a judgment they themselves regarded as history. The verse is thus a direct assault on the false consciousness of the elite, and it corresponds to the sūrah's own earlier argument from the ruins: the towns of the peninsula passed each other's rubble on their caravan routes and drew no conclusions.

**"And Increased You Greatly in Stature"**

*Wa-zādakum fī al-khalqi basṭatan* — and He increased you in the creation, in stature. The phrase has been read as referring to physical size, and the tradition of commentary preserves reports about the height and strength of ʿĀd; it has also been read more broadly as increase in means — the stretch and abundance of their possessions, the grandeur of their buildings. Both readings fit the Book's account of the town, which elsewhere emphasizes their monuments and their boasts. Sūrat al-Fajr's description of the pillar-city, *the like of which was never created in the lands* (89:8), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's reproof about the palaces built as though their makers would live forever (26:129), support the wider sense.

The point either way is the same and it is the core of the verse's argument: the blessing and the test are one thing. The strength that made ʿĀd formidable made their accountability heavier. The sūrah's scale doctrine of verse 8 applies exactly — what is increased in capacity increases the range of what could be done with it, and the Book's consistent view is that capacity is a trust with a report attached.

**"So Remember the Favours of Allah"**

*Fa-dhkurū ālāʾa Allāh laʿallakum tufliḥūn* — so remember the favours of Allah, so that you may succeed. *Ālāʾ* is the Qur'an's word for the successive gifts of God, and the sūrah will use the same instruction in the next narrative, from Ṣāliḥ (7:74). The command to remember is not an invitation to sentimentality; in the Book's usage, remembrance is the act of tracing a good thing back to its source, which is why it is so closely bound up with gratitude — the faculty the sūrah named at the end of the parable of the soils (7:58).

The connection between remembering and success is stated here as a chain: the town's prosperity is a fact, the source of the prosperity is a knowable fact, and the outcome of acknowledging the second depends on what the town does with the first. The sūrah is offering ʿĀd an identity that does not depend on their strength: they are the recipients of gifts, not the authors of them. Whether or not they take the offer, the reader of the sūrah is invited to test the description against his own city and his own account of his own life.

**The Test of Blessing in the Book**

The Qur'an uses prosperity as a trap and as a mercy with a consistency that has always unsettled comfortable readers. Sūrat al-Anʿām describes a people showered with everything they desired until they became proud of what they were given and were cut off (6:44). Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ describes Qārūn, who said his wealth was due to knowledge he possessed, and was swallowed by the earth (28:78–81). And Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself will shortly tell of the towns whose security was taken from them *while they were sleeping* (7:97–98).

Verse 69 stands at the head of that theme in the sūrah. It is noteworthy that Hūd does not tell ʿĀd that their blessings will be withdrawn; he tells them that the blessings have a name. The disasters that follow in the sūrah's narratives arrive, in every case, at the hands of towns that had been given everything and could not say from whom.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:70

> **They said, “Have you come unto us so that we may worship God alone, and leave aside what our fathers worshipped? Then bring upon us that wherewith you have threatened us, if you are truthful.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand for the Punishment**

*Qālū a-jiʾtanā li-naʿbuda Allāha waḥdahu wa-nadhara mā kāna yaʿbudu ābāʾunā* — they said: have you come to us so that we worship Allah alone and give up what our fathers used to worship? The response is the sūrah's clearest statement of the towns' real objection, and it is not a theological one. The chiefs of ʿĀd are not denying that a single God might exist; they are objecting to a proposal that would require them to abandon the religion of their ancestors.

The objection is answered in the Book with a consistency that repays attention. Sūrat al-Zukhruf puts it in the words of every town in history: *“We found our fathers following a particular way, and we are following in their footsteps”* — and the Book supplies God's answer to all such claims: *“Even if I bring you better guidance than what you found your fathers following?” They said, “We surely reject what you have been sent with”* (43:22–24). Sūrat al-Baqarah's account of the same reflex is sharper still: when the people of Israel were told to follow what God had revealed, they answered, *“Rather, we will follow what we found our fathers doing”* — and they too were asked whether their fathers had understood anything at all (2:170).

The sūrah has been preparing for this verse since its beginning. Verse 28 recorded the townspeople's defense that *“we found our fathers doing so”*, and verse 70 shows the same sentence doing the only work it has ever been able to do. The sūrah's God answers it twice in the following verses, and the reader is meant to notice that the answer does not consist of disparaging the fathers; it consists of asking who authorized the practice they handed down.

**"Then Bring Us What You Threaten Us With"**

*Fa-ʾtinā bimā taʿidunā in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīn* — bring us what you threaten us with, if you are truthful. The challenge converts the argument into a test of strength, and the sūrah's towns use it repeatedly: Thamūd demands the punishment from Ṣāliḥ (7:77), the Makkans demand it of the Prophet (8:32, 29:29, 51:14 in the vicinity), and the Qur'an records the formula on the lips of the impatient in nearly every era: *“Our Lord, hasten our share of the punishment before the Day of Reckoning”* (38:16).

The Book's answer to the taunt is not a scheduled miracle but a statement about the purpose of delay. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand.’”* (27:72), and Sūrat Yūnus explains why the postponement exists: *“If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they wish to hasten good, they would have certainly been doomed. But We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (10:11 vicinity; cf. 16:61, 35:45). The towns treat the absence of the penalty as evidence of impunity; the Book treats the gap as the mercy that keeps them alive.

**The Fathers as an Argument**

It is worth isolating the precise role of the ancestors in this verse, because the sūrah's critique is often misunderstood as a rejection of tradition as such. The Book is not against inherited religion; it is against inherited religion held without examination — and it makes the distinction explicitly by counting itself among the things inherited. *“Follow the way of your fathers Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac”* is not the same argument as *“we found our fathers on a way”*, and the difference is whether the tradition has a warrant in revelation.

The sūrah's question at 7:71 will name that warrant with a single word — *sulṭān*, authority — and its absence is the whole case against the gods of ʿĀd. The Book does not say that the ancestors were liars; it says that the practice they handed down has no authorization from God, and that no community can transfer the responsibility for its worship to its forebears. Verse 38 of the sūrah has already staged the end of the argument in the Hereafter, where communities curse their predecessors as they enter the Fire, and where the excuse of following others is refused (7:39).

**The Ask That Would Have Settled It**

Hūd's opponents make a fair point in form: a messenger who warns of a disaster should be able to produce one. The Book's own accounts of the messengers show that this is not how the pattern is designed. The signs that authenticated the prophets appear on God's schedule and in God's kind — a she-camel for Thamūd, a sea for Moses, a flood for Noah, a fire for Ibrāhīm — and they are never delivered as answers to an audience's test paper. The Book states the principle when the Makkans demand signs: *“Say: the signs are only with Allah, and I am only a clear warner”* (29:50), and it points out that the demanders would not believe even after them (6:109; 2:145 in its context).

What ʿĀd asked for was not evidence but leverage; the challenge presumes that the messenger's claim depends on the audience's satisfaction rather than on God's authority. The sūrah's next verse records Hūd's answer, and it does not supply a miracle. It supplies a fact about the past tense.

**The Question Behind the Demand**

Read with the sūrah's earlier verses, the exchange is a drama about authority with an unusually complete inventory of positions. Noah's town had answered the message by labelling the messenger; ʿĀd's town answers by relocating the question to the ancestors and then to the schedule of punishment. What is missing from both replies is the one thing the messengers keep asking for: a decision about God.

The verse's form of the demand — *bring us what you threaten us with* — has a theological double edge that the sūrah's next verses bring out. The thing Hūd is threatening them with is not a personalized magic trick; it is the judgment of God, which the Book elsewhere describes as having been earned already. The challenge to produce it is therefore answered, in the sūrah's narrative, by the fact that the delay was the only thing standing between ʿĀd and a verdict that had been accumulating for a generation.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:71

> **He said, “Defilement and wrath have already come upon you from your Lord. Do you dispute with me over names that you have named — you and your fathers — for which God has sent down no authority? Then wait! Truly I am waiting along with you.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Wrath and Anger Have Already Fallen"**

*Qāla qad waqaʿa ʿalaykum min rabbikum rijzun wa-ghaḍab* — he said: wrath and anger from your Lord have already descended upon you. The tense is the verse's startling feature. Hūd does not say that punishment *will* come; he says it has *fallen* — a past-tense verdict pronounced on a town that is still standing, still trading, still strong, and still mocking. The sūrah's doctrinal point is that the judgment of a people begins before the sky changes colour: the decision has been made in the record, and ʿĀd's prosperity is the interval between the sentence and its execution.

*Rijz* is the Qur'an's word for the punishment that is also a pollution — the same term the Book uses for the plagues that fell on Egypt (7:133, 2:59), for the filth God casts on those who refuse to use their reason (10:100), and for the torment that follows the unjust. *Ghaḍab*, anger, is God's displeasure stated in the human vocabulary of a personal response; the Book uses it for those who have earned it (2:61, 5:60, 8:16 in its context). The combination names both the fact and the response: a penalty that is a defilement, and a judgment that is a relation.

**The Radical Critique of Idols: "Only Names"**

*A-tujādilūnanī fī asmāʾin sammaytumūhā antum wa-ābāʾukum mā nazzala Allāhu bihā min sulṭān* — do you dispute with me concerning names which you and your fathers have named, for which Allah has sent down no authority? The verse is the Qur'an's most complete statement of what idols are, and the Book repeats it in exactly these terms at two other points: *“Whatever ˹idols˺ you worship instead of Him are mere names which you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. It is only Allah Who decides. He has commanded that you worship none but Him. That is the upright faith …”* (12:40, spoken by Yūsuf in prison, and 53:23). The word *asmāʾ* — names — is the whole indictment. The gods of ʿĀd are not described as demons, or as false powers, or as frauds practised on the people; they are described as labels without referents.

The second term is *sulṭān*: authority, evidence, a warrant. The Book's argument against the town is not that the practice is old, or unpopular, or socially damaging, but that it can produce no authorization from the one whose prerogative it invades. The same word appears throughout the sūrah at the exact points where the towns' positions are tested — *“He said, ‘You will certainly be subjected to your Lord’s torment and wrath. Do you dispute with me regarding the so-called gods which you and your forefathers have made up —a practice Allah has never authorized? Then wait! I too am waiting with you.’”* (7:71 vicinity; 3:151; 10:68), *“they follow nothing but conjecture”* (6:116). In the Book's epistemology, a claim about God without a warrant from God is not a theology; it is a name.

**The Names People Give Their Gods**

The Qur'an's treatment of the pagan pantheon in Makkah follows the same logic. Sūrat al-Najm names al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt and dismisses them in a single clause: *“These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up — a practice Allah has never authorized”* (53:23). The same analysis applies to ʿĀd, and the sūrah's reader notices that what is condemned is not only the carved stone but the practice of assigning the divine to whatever a community names as sacred.

Verses 70 and 71 together thus form a compact critique of inherited religion and the substitution of vocabulary for authority. The fathers handed down the names; the children defend them; God has authorized neither. When the same Book turns to the older Abrahamic traditions and commands their preservation, it does so on the basis of revealed texts and prophetic precedents — which is precisely the basis that the gods of ʿĀd cannot produce.

**"Then Wait; I Am Waiting With You"**

*Fa-intaẓirū innī maʿakum mina al-muntazirīn* — then wait; I am waiting along with you. The sentence ends the exchange by accepting it. Hūd does not insist, does not threaten, and does not call for immediate action; he places himself on the same bench as his accusers and lets the record run. The Book's messengers use this formula when their arguments are exhausted — Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ's Prophet is told to say, *“Say: everyone is waiting; so wait, and you will know who is on the straight path”* (20:135 vicinity), and Sūrat al-Sāffāt records Noah's townsmen being told the same.

The phrase is a disciplined act of faith and a rebuke at once. It says that the outcome of the dispute is not up to either party in the room; it says that the messenger is not going to force the issue, because the arbitration belongs to a court neither side controls. The sūrah's rhetorical achievement in these verses is that the whole exchange — the challenge, the taunt, the answer — has been conducted with no violence on the messenger's side at all, and the destruction, when it arrives in the next verse, arrives from a direction nobody in the dialogue was managing.

**The Pattern of the Deferred Verdict**

The Book keeps this structure across its prophetic narratives because it is arguing about how God deals with the world. Noah was given a century; ʿĀd was given generations of prosperity and repeated warnings; Thamūd would be given a living sign and told to leave it alone. The summons precedes the sentence, and the delay is not indecision but opportunity: *“Be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say”* (38:17) is addressed to the last of the messengers, whose own people were being given exactly the interval the earlier towns had wasted.

Verse 71 therefore records a verdict from the messenger's perspective and abstains from the verdict itself. What Hūd knows is that the case has closed in heaven: the towns' gods have no authority, the towns' ancestors have no exceptional standing, and the warning has been delivered in a language they understood. The remaining question — when the sentence is executed — the verse places entirely in the hands of the One whose commands Hūd was sent to convey.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:72

> **So We saved him and those who were with him through a mercy from Us, and We cut off the last remnant of those who denied Our signs and were not believers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Saved by Mercy, Cut Off for Denial**

*Fa-anjaynāhu wa-lladhīna maʿahu bi-raḥmatin minnā* — so We saved him and those with him by a mercy from Us. The rescue is attributed, as in Noah's case, to a rescue rather than to merit: *bi-raḥmatin minnā*, by mercy from Us. The sūrah's narrative has thus far shown towns punished and remnants spared, and it takes care each time to name the mechanism. Ḥūd's people are not saved because they had proven their case or exceeded their accusers in virtue they could measure; they are saved because mercy reached them, and the mercy is the reason given.

The point carries weight in the sūrah's overall argument because it is the same doctrine the Book states in its treatment of the Hereafter. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own scene of the entry into Paradise has the saved confess that they would never have been guided had God not guided them (7:43), and the Prophet's hadith places the same fact in a single sentence: *none of you will be saved by his deeds* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816). The narratives of this sūrah and the doctrine of its Hereafter scenes are teaching one thing in two registers.

**"We Uprooted Those Who Denied Our Signs"**

*Wa-qaṭaʿnā dābira alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — and We severed the root of those who denied Our signs. The verb is one of the Book's grimmest images of destruction: *qaṭaʿa dābira* means to cut off the rear, to leave no successor behind, to end a line. The Qur'an uses it for nations (6:45, 8:7, 15:66, 7:72 here) and the image is agricultural in origin — the cutting of a stem so that nothing regrows from the base.

What makes the phrase pointed in ʿĀd's case is the Book's account of their civilization elsewhere. This is the town of the lofty pillars, of the buildings that imitated eternity (26:129), of the boast *who is stronger than us in might?* (41:15). Their destruction in the sūrah's narrative is the demolition of a legacy: not merely the death of persons but the end of a name that no one could rebuild. Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah's description of the wind makes the physical shape of the sentence explicit: *“a screaming wind which He imposed on them for seven nights and eight days consecutively, so that you would see the people fallen like hollow palm trunks”* (69:6–7). Sūrat al-Qamar adds the morning-after detail: *“Indeed, We sent against them ˹only˺ one ˹mighty˺ blast, leaving them like the twigs of fencebuilders.”* (54:31).

**The Irony of the Delivered Form**

The most remarkable detail of ʿĀd's destruction in the Book appears in Sūrat al-Aḥqāf, where the punishment arrives in the form of what they had been waiting for all along: *“Then, when they saw it as a dense cloud coming towards their valleys, they said, 'This is a cloud bringing us rain!' Rather, it is what you sought to hasten — a wind bearing a painful punishment, destroying everything by the command of its Lord. And they became such that nothing could be seen except their dwellings”* (46:24–25). The Book's composition has the towns killed by their own demand. They asked for what was threatened; what was threatened came dressed as mercy and turned out to be a storm.

That structure is why the sūrah ends the verse with a sentence that is not merely descriptive: *wa-mā kānū muʾminīn* — they were not believers. The clause secures the fairness of the account. What was destroyed was not a town of innocent people; it was a town that had lived through its own century-scale warning and rejected it, and the Book's reader is expected to keep that fact in view when he condemns the destruction as excessive.

**The Remnant and the Mercy**

The other half of the verse — *him and those with him* — returns to the pattern established in Noah's account, and the sūrah will keep it. In this sūrah's narratives, the rescue is always small, always named in the same breath as the town's destruction, and always attributed to mercy. The Book's later chapters add the detail of how the remnant was preserved; Sūrat al-Ḥūd records that they were saved *by a mercy from Us* from a punishment that came as a cry and an earthquake (11:58, 11:67 in the two accounts), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ has the town ask, at the last moment, whether there is any remnant to be spared (26:139 in its wording).

The narrative's shape is worth stating in a sentence: in this sūrah, the mercy of God and the judgment of God arrive together, and the difference between the two populations is not their circumstances but their response to a message. That is the sūrah's thesis about history, and it will now be demonstrated three more times with the same structure and different scenery.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

ʿĀd's story is the second of the five towns, and its closing sentence — *they were not believers* — completes a triplet that the reader has now heard twice and will hear four times: the messengers warn, the elite mock, the people disappear. The sūrah's towns have been given varying degrees of detail, and ʿĀd is the one granted a small masterpiece of irony by the Book's later chapters: an end that arrived in the shape of a promise fulfilled and a rain cloud that was something else entirely.

The reader who has just been told, in verse 57, that God drives clouds to a dead land to bring it to life, is now shown a cloud driven to a living town to bring it to death. The sūrah's imagery is deliberately reversable, and what determines the direction is never the weather.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:73

> **And unto Thamūd, their brother, Ṣāliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship God! You have no god other than Him. There has come unto you a clear proof from your Lord. This she-camel of God is a sign unto you. Leave her to graze freely on God’s earth, and cause her no harm, lest a painful punishment seize you.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thamūd and the She-Camel**

*Wa-ilā Thamūda akhāhum Ṣāliḥan* — and to Thamūd, their brother Ṣāliḥ. The third town enters the sequence with the same formula, and its messenger brings a new element: a sign that lives among the community and makes demands on it. *Hādhihi nāqatu Allāhi lakum āyatan* — this is the she-camel of Allah, a sign for you — *fa-dharūhā taʾkul fī arḍi Allāh wa-lā tamassūhā bi-sūʾin* — so leave her to eat in Allah's land and do not harm her, or a painful punishment will seize you.

The Book gives this sign more space than any other pre-Mosaic miracle, and the reason is its peculiar design. The she-camel is not a spectacle performed once and debated afterwards; it is an animal pastured on the town's land, drinking from the town's water, and its presence is the test. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ states the arrangement in the town's own hearing: *“Ṣâliḥ said, ‘Here is a camel. She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day. … And do not ever touch her with harm, or you will be overtaken by the torment of a tremendous day.’”* (26:155–156). Sūrat al-Qamar compresses the whole affair into four verses: the warning, the consultation, the hamstringing, and the cry (54:23–31).

**The Sign as a Neighbour**

What is theologically remarkable about the she-camel is that it turns a natural resource into a test of obedience. Thamūd's people did not need to accept an argument or assent to a doctrine in order to pass the test; they needed to share the water. The Book elsewhere names the sign as one of the reasons the town's destruction was inevitable, and it is worth noting the tradition's interest in this verse: classical exegetes discuss how the animal's daily access to the spring displaced the town's own use, and how the distribution was arranged. The details are less important than the principle, which the sūrah states in a single clause: *do not harm her*.

The Book's use of the sign also protects the messengers from the charge of magic. A wonder that appears at will and vanishes is a claim about the man; an animal that stays and requires restraint is a claim about the hearers. The Qur'an notes that the sign was *for them*, and the Book's language around it is transactional: a proof given, and a limit set.

**Ṣāliḥ's Address and Its Additions**

Ṣāliḥ's opening speech is the same as Noah's and Hūd's — the call to the worship of the One God — with the addition of the sign and the warning of the punishment to follow its violation. The sūrah gives him no new doctrine. What the account adds is the mechanism of the town's doom: not a philosophical error, not a failure of argument, but a collective decision to kill a particular animal in order to demonstrate that the messenger's God could not protect it.

Sūrat al-Shams tells the same story in its own way and supplies the reason the refusal became fatal: *“when the most wicked of them was roused ˹to kill the she-camel˺. … But the messenger of Allah warned them, ‘˹Do not disturb˺ Allah’s camel and her ˹turn to˺ drink!” … Still they defied him and slaughtered her. So their Lord crushed them for their …’* (91:12–14). The Book's analysis locates the sin in the act of hamstringing and in the rejection that made it deliberate. The town had been given a sign that was also a daily ordinance, and it chose to destroy the ordinance in public.

**The Geography of Thamūd**

The sūrah's next verse will describe the town's achievements, but the Book supplies its own portrait in several places, and the archaeological details are consistent: the carving of dwellings in the rock, and the trading of security for wealth. Sūrat al-Ḥijr records that the people *“They carved their homes in the mountains, feeling secure.”* (15:82), and Sūrat al-Fajr names Thamūd with the people who *hollowed out the rock in the valley* (89:9). Sūrat al-Shuʿayb's own memory of the tribe appears in his address in this sūrah's later verses (7:85 vicinity), and the Qur'an returns to the empty houses of Thamūd as a lesson: *“So their homes are there, uninhabited after them”* (27:52).

The picture is of a people who had solved the problem of nature — water, shelter, defence — and had begun to think of security as a property they possessed. The sign they were given was a living interruption in that sense of possession: an animal that drank their measured water and could not be touched. The Book's towns are consistently destroyed by the one thing they cannot incorporate into their systems, and Thamūd's system had been built precisely to minimize interruptions.

**The Sign the Town Chose to Kill**

The sūrah does not yet say what will happen, but its audience had heard the story in the markets and the Book has told it elsewhere: the she-camel was hamstrung, the town was given three days, and the cry took them (11:65, 54:31). Verse 73 closes with the warning attached to the sign — *or a painful punishment will seize you* — and the reader who knows what comes next reads the sentence as the last free warning the town ever received.

The arrangement of the sūrah's three towns so far is the same in each case: a message, an elite who object, a sign or an argument, and a deadline. Noah's town had a century; ʿĀd had generations; Thamūd was given an animal that any shepherd could see. The Book's escalation of proximity is deliberate, and the next verse shows the town at ease in the middle of it, boasting about its real estate.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:74

> **Remember when He made you vicegerents after ʿĀd and settled you on the earth: you build castles for yourselves on the open plain and hew dwellings in the mountains. So remember the boons of God, and behave not wickedly upon the earth, working corruption.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Established in the Land**

*Wa-dhkurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi ʿĀdin wa-bawwaʾakum fī al-arḍ* — and remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the land. The verse repeats the argument Hūd used against ʿĀd, addressed now to their heirs, and the repetition is itself the message. Thamūd occupies the territory ʿĀd lost, and their claim to it is the same claim their predecessors made: possession as evidence of favour.

The word *bawwaʾakum* — settled you, established you — is the vocabulary of placement by a host. The Book uses it for Israel in the promised land (10:93) and for the settlement of the believers after their trials (22:41 vicinity). The verb's grammar keeps Thamūd's tenancy in the gift: the town did not occupy an empty peninsula by its own strength; it was placed, in the same way that its predecessor had been displaced.

**Palaces on the Plains and Homes in the Mountains**

*Tabnūna bi-suhūlihā qaṣūran wa-tanḥitūna al-jibāla buyūtan* — you build palaces on its plains and carve the mountains into homes. The description is precise and it matches the Book's other accounts: Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ has Ṣāliḥ asking, *“˹Why˺ do you build a landmark on every high place in vanity, … and construct castles, as if you are going to live forever,”* (26:128–129), and Sūrat al-Ḥijr records the practice of carving dwellings from the rock *“feeling secure”* (15:82). The two halves of the verse's image — the tower on the plain and the chamber in the cliff — form a catalogue of defensive achievement, and the Book's readers have always recognized the picture: an architecture designed to make attack impossible and time irrelevant.

The Qur'an's treatment of this architecture is consistently theological. The stones are not criticized as luxury; the Book's objection is to what such building expresses about its builders' estimate of their own permanence. Sūrat al-Fajr's description of Iram's pillars is framed by the same thought — *the like of which was never created in the lands* — and immediately followed by the account of a people who thought their strength made them immune to judgment (89:6–13). What Thamūd had converted into self-confidence was, in the Book's account, a loan by which it was being tested.

**"So Remember the Favours of Allah"**

*Fa-dhkurū ālāʾa Allāh* — so remember the favours of Allah. The instruction is repeated verbatim from Hūd's address (7:69), and the sūrah's towns are shown to receive identical counsel from identical messengers. The word *ālāʾ* covers the whole inventory the verse has just listed: the succession, the settlement, the plains, the cliffs, and the skill to use them. Nothing in the list is described as a product of Thamūd's own creative power; the city is presented as a set of gifts that happens to be inhabited by people with names.

The Book's later verses about Thamūd make the same point from the ruins: *“So their homes are there, uninhabited after them”* (27:52). Whatever the town thought its architecture was for, the Book's epitaph is empty buildings — an inventory of materials with the tenants gone. The verse's instruction to remember is thus the only defence the sūrah offers against that outcome, and it is offered at the last moment before the town's refusal is sealed.

**"Do Not Spread Corruption in the Land"**

The verse ends with the prohibition that the sūrah placed at the head of its practical instructions at 7:56: *wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍ mufsidīn* — and do not go about the land spreading corruption. The verb *ʿathā* adds to *fasād* the sense of violent, ruinous action — the destruction that leaves wreckage behind — and the Book uses it for those whose conduct goes beyond mere disobedience into devastation (2:27, 2:60, 26:151–152, 29:36).

The placement of this clause in Ṣāliḥ's speech connects the town's coming crime to the sūrah's broader moral framework. Thamūd's destruction will not be triggered by an abstract dispute about the names of gods; it will be triggered by the deliberate killing of an animal placed under the town's protection as a sign. The sūrah's vocabulary makes that act a form of *fasād* in the literal sense: the ruin of a living thing in an ordered land, performed as a public gesture. And the Book's portraits of these towns are consistent in presenting their crimes as acts of vandalism against a world that had been set right (7:56) — a correspondence the sūrah's audience, watching their own sacred precinct put to tribal uses, was equipped to notice.

**The Third Warning and Its Heirs**

The verse's function inside the narrative is to lay the foundation for verse 75, where the town's elite will turn on the lowest of their own people. It has just reminded Thamūd of every advantage they possess, and it has named them the heirs of a drowned generation — and the two facts together are the sūrah's case for their accountability. They inherited the lesson and the land at the same time.

The reader of this sūrah has now watched three towns receive the same message and answer it in three dialects: Noah's town with a label, ʿĀd with a demand and a taunt, Thamūd with a living sign they were told not to touch. What remains to be seen, in the verses that follow, is the internal cost of the refusal — the split inside the town between those who believed and those who owned the plains and the cliff-houses. That split, and not the she-camel, will occupy the next several verses, and it is the sūrah's most exact diagnosis of how a community talks itself into destruction.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:75

> **The notables among his people who were arrogant said to those among them who believed and whom they deemed weak, “Do you know that Ṣāliḥ has been sent by his Lord?” They said, “Truly we believe in that wherewith he has been sent.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question the Elite Ask the Poor**

*Qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna istakbarū min qawmihi li-lladhīna istuḍʿifū li-man āmana minhum* — the arrogant chiefs of his people said to the weak ones among them who had believed. The sūrah has been building to this sentence since its first pages, and the sentence's structure contains its whole social analysis. The town is divided into two vocabularies: the *malaʾ* who are *mustakbirūn*, arrogant, and the *mustaḍʿafūn*, those treated as weak. The Book's terminology is not neutral descriptive sociology; it assigns responsibility. One group inflates itself; the other is made small, and the passive voice in *istuḍʿifū* says who did it.

The Qur'an uses this pair of terms across its narratives of the prophetic age, and it repeatedly describes the same alliance of the powerful and the same constituency of the message. Sūrat Sabaʾ records the exchange between the arrogant and the weak in the Hereafter and has the weak say to the arrogant, *“The disbelievers vow, ‘We will never believe in this Quran, nor in those ˹Scriptures˺ before it.” If only you could see when the wrongdoers will be detained before their Lord, throwing blame at each other! The lowly ˹followers˺ will say to the …’* (34:31–33 in the vicinity). Sūrat al-Furqān describes the objection of the disbelievers with the same social logic: *“And they say ˹mockingly˺, ‘What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner,”* (25:7) — the objection of those who expected the message to arrive with attendants.

**"Are You Certain That Ṣāliḥ Has Been Sent?"**

*A-taʿlamūna anna Ṣāliḥan mursalun min rabbihi* — are you certain that Ṣāliḥ has been sent by his Lord? The question is one of the finest pieces of villainy in the Book. It contains no argument, no counterclaim, and no denial of Ṣāliḥ's credentials; it is a probe. The elite of Thamūd do not ask the believers to defend their faith before God; they ask whether the believers are *sure*, and they address the question to people whose poverty had taught them to hedge every statement and whose standing in the town depended on those above them.

The wording the sūrah gives the question — *do you know* — is the language of certainty, and the answer it receives is expressed in the language of confidence: *we certainly believe in what he has been sent with*. The Book's care here is worth noting. The believers are not asked about their degree of assurance, and they do not claim to know everything about the messenger's mission. They testify to what they trust, and the two verbs — *we know* / *we believe* — are the sūrah's comment on the difference between the elite's epistemological posturing and the poor's actual faith.

**The Answer of the Lowly**

*Qālū innā bimā ursila bihi muʾminūn* — they said: we certainly believe in what he has been sent with. The sentence is a monument of the sūrah, and its composition deserves attention. The believers do not answer with a proposition about Ṣāliḥ; they answer with a proposition about the *message*. What they affirm is *bimā ursila bihi* — "what he has been sent with" — which is precisely the ground on which the elite hoped to unsettle them.

The reply has been quoted by Muslims in every generation facing official pressure, and the Book itself prepares the reader for it earlier in the sūrah: the reader has already heard the divine voice describe the saved of the end times as those who *“As for those who believe and do good—We never require of any soul more than what it can afford—it is they who will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever.”* (7:42), and has already watched the lowly appear on the sūrah's wall of separation while the tyrants stand in the Fire (7:48–49). The sūrah's structure is designed to let the reader see the answer of verse 75 from both directions at once: inside the town, where it looks like weakness; and inside the Hereafter, where the same people are the ones told to enter Paradise.

**The Poor as the Book's Constant Constituency**

The Qur'an's sociology of belief is one of its least fashionable and most consistent features. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version of the towns' objection is explicit: *“Shall we believe in you, when only the lowest follow you?”* (26:111; cf. 11:27, where the chiefs say that no one follows Noah except the lowliest, and note that Noah refuses to drive them away). The Book does not romanticize poverty; it says that power and wealth systematically make their holders less able to accept a message that costs them precedence.

Sūrat al-Anʿām's instruction to the Prophet is the doctrine in action: *“Do not dismiss those who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure”* (6:52), and Sūrat al-Kahf repeats it: *“Patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening…”* (18:28). Both verses were revealed in a Makkah where the Prophet was under pressure to send away the poor in order to attract the notables — the same pressure the sūrah's Thamūd applies from below.

**A Question About Others' Faith**

The elite's question has one more feature that deserves notice: it is about somebody else's belief. The chiefs do not ask whether *they* owe the messenger anything; they interrogate the poor about what the poor think. The Book's consistent portrait of the towns' leaders is of people who find it easier to manage the religious life of others than to examine their own, and the pattern repeats in the sūrah's account of the Hereafter — where the leaders of the world discover that the faith of the lowly, whose certainty they had mocked, was the one thing they themselves had never bothered to possess (7:49).

The verses that follow will give the elite their slogan and their deed, and the sūrah will report both without commentary beyond the facts. The reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening — the scales, the gates, the wall, the crowds — already knows how the two sentences in this exchange will be judged, and which of them will be quoted in the Garden.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:76

> **Those who were arrogant said, “Truly we believe not in that which you believe.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Reject What You Believe In"**

*Qāla al-ladhīna istakbarū innā bi-mā āmantum bihi kāfirūn* — the arrogant said: we reject what you have believed in. The reply is a sentence about the believers rather than about God, and its grammar reveals how religious rejection actually works in the sūrah's towns. The chiefs do not say *we disbelieve in the messenger* or *we deny the Lord of all worlds*; they say they are *kāfirūn* with respect to the faith of the lowly. The object of their denial is another group's confidence.

The Book records this structure repeatedly, and it is one of its sharpest psychological observations. Sūrat al-Baqarah's accounts of the prophets' audiences show the leaders' objections moving from the message to the messenger to the messenger's followers. Sūrat al-Muʾminūn's Noah narrative has the chiefs objecting that *only the lowest of the people follow him*, as though the composition of a community were evidence about its God (23:24 in the vicinity; cf. 26:111). And the Qur'an's description of the disbelievers' own logic at the Judgment places exactly this sentence in a larger diagnosis: *“And they will lament, ‘If only we had listened and reasoned, we would not be among the residents of the Blaze!’”* (67:10).

**The Maintenance of Rank**

The social mechanism behind the sentence is worth stating plainly. In a town structured by lineage and standing, the acceptance of a message involves more than a change of belief; it involves a change of position. If the poor of Thamūd are right, then the elite's ancestors were wrong, the town's sanctuaries were frauds, and the pillars and cliff-dwellings were paid for by the labour of people whose judgment the elite had treated as negligible. The Book's narratives repeatedly show that this is the objection that cannot be answered by argument, because it is not an argument. Verse 76 is the sūrah's cleanest specimen: the elite's theology is a function of their social position, and the sūrah states it in five Arabic words.

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own Hereafter scenes put the machinery on display for the reader's benefit. The arrogant and the lowly argue in the Fire (7:38–39); each side blames the other; the Book's verdict is that the leaders carry the teaching-load and the followers carry the imitation-load, and neither gets to transfer the bill. The exchange of verses 75 and 76 is the earthly version of that scene, and the sūrah's reader is meant to recognize that the two arguments are the same argument, staged twice with a lifetime in between.

**Rejection as Group Identity**

The Book's vocabulary adds a nuance that translations often lose. *Kāfirūn* in this sentence functions socially as much as doctrinally: to *kafara bi-shayʾ* is to cover, deny, and repudiate — and in the towns of the sūrah, the repudiation is an act of tribal self-definition. The chiefs are asserting what they are, not what is true.

Sūrat al-Baqarah's refutation of the same habit is aimed at a different community but makes the identical point: *“When it is said to them: believe as the people have believed, they say: should we believe as the fools have believed? Unquestionably, it is they who are the fools, but they do not know”* (2:13). The Book's reply is to take the towns' word for the believers and apply it to the mockers, and this sūrah has already done exactly that in its scale verses: the light scale belongs to those the towns laughed at, and the heavy one to those they trusted.

**What the Sentence Concedes**

It is worth noticing what the chiefs' sentence quietly grants. They do not dispute that the believers exist, nor that their belief is sincere, nor that Ṣāliḥ has been with them. Their objection is neither evidential nor moral; it is social. Sūrat al-Zukhruf catches the same concession at the highest level, where the Makkans, unable to attack the message, attack the recipient: *“And they exclaimed, ‘If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!’”* (43:31) — a sentence that presumes the Qur'an might be genuine and objects only to its address.

The Book's later sūrahs preserve the end of this argument at the Judgment, when the elite and their followers exchange recriminations. Sūrat Sabaʾ reports the followers' answer to their leaders — *“No! It was your plotting by day and night — when you ordered us to disbelieve in Allah and to set up equals with Him”* (34:33) — and their final plea is preserved in Sūrat al-Aḥzāb: *“Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray from the ˹Right˺ Way. Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment, and condemn them tremendously”* (33:67–68). Verse 76's sentence — *we surely disbelieve in what you believe in* — will therefore be spoken twice in the Book's history: once by Thamūd's elite in the town's assembly, and once on the Day when the order of speakers is reversed.

The sūrah's towns are not usually asked to deny that a revelation exists; they are asked whether they are willing to accept that it has arrived among people they had written off. The verse that follows shows what a community does when the answer is no, and the Book does not need to editorialize. The she-camel was a sign, and the town's elite had just announced — two verses before touching it — that the people who called it God's sign were people whose faith they would not share.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:77

> **So they hamstrung the she-camel and insolently defied the Command of their Lord. And they said, “O Ṣāli! Bring upon us that wherewith you have threatened us, if you are among those sent [by God].”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Argument to Slaughter**

*Fa-ʿaqarū al-nāqata wa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim* — so they hamstrung the she-camel and defied the command of their Lord. The sūrah's third town now crosses the line its predecessors approached, and the verb the Book chooses is significant: *ʿaqara* — to hamstring, to cut the tendon that makes a creature useless. It is not a description of a general massacre; it is the precise act of crippling an animal. The crime of Thamūd, in the Book's account, is an act of deliberate vandalism against a sign left among them.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves the town's own planning: *“They called their companion, and he took a sword and hamstrung her”* (54:29 vicinity; 26:157 in the same sūrah's telling has them plotting against Ṣāliḥ's household). Sūrat al-Shams names the demagogue: *“when the most wicked of them was roused ˹to kill the she-camel˺.”* (91:12). The Book's account of the events shows a division of responsibility that the sūrah's narrative has already prepared: a designated agent performs the deed, and the town consents — the Qur'an reports that they were all pleased with it — and so the punishment falls on all of them (91:14).

**"They Defied the Command of Their Lord"**

The second clause, *wa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim* — they turned away from the command of their Lord — states the theological content of the act. *ʿAtā* is to overstep the limit placed in front of a person; the phrase is used throughout the Book for the violation of an explicit divine prohibition (2:61, 5:78, 7:166). What makes Thamūd's act different from the pagan rites of their neighbours is that the command they defied was not a general moral law they had never heard; it was the specific instruction attached to the sign visible in their pasture: *do not touch her with harm*.

The Book's narratives consistently give each town exactly one decisive prohibition — a small, checkable, public command that tests the community's willingness to be governed by anything other than its own judgment. For Thamūd it was an animal; for Adam it was one tree; for the people of the sabbath it was the fishing net (7:163); for Abraham's people there was no such command but a fire. The sūrah's reader is being taught that the form of the test is not the point; the point is whether the hearers accept any limit at all, and Thamūd's answer was recorded in the architecture they left behind: unrestricted.

**"Bring Us What You Threaten Us With"**

*Wa-qālū yā Ṣāliḥu ʾtinā bimā taʿidunā in kunta mina al-mursalīn* — they said: O Ṣāliḥ, bring us what you threaten us with, if you are one of the messengers. The pattern of ʿĀd returns, now with the taunt sharpened by the deed: the town has just killed the sign, and it demands the consequence in the same speech. The sentence is a public demonstration of impunity — a community establishing, before its own members, that the messenger's warnings have no force.

The Book records the taunt at several points with a consistency that shows how central the challenge was to the prophetic experience: *“Our Lord, hasten for us our share before the Day of Reckoning”* (38:16); *“They said, ‘Have you come to us so that we would worship Allah alone and abandon what our forefathers used to worship? Then bring us what you threaten us with, if what you say is true!’”* (7:70); *“Then bring us what you threaten us with”* (46:22). And the Book's replies repeat the fact that the demanded item has already been scheduled: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.’”* (34:30 in its vicinity; cf. 71:4). The sūrah's audience, hearing verse 77 read aloud, already knows what follows in the next verse. So did Thamūd, in principle.

**The Silence of the She-Camel**

One element of the story is worth noticing because it belongs to the sūrah's rhetoric of signs: the she-camel does nothing. She is not a wonder that performs; she is an animal that nurses, grazes, and drinks. The Book's most durable sign is the one the community has become used to — the ordinary creature whose ordinary existence has been declared the possession of God. The town was not asked to accept a miracle; it was asked to accept a boundary around a living thing.

And the boundary is what it destroyed. The Qur'an's account of what follows places the hamstringing at the centre of the judgment: *“Still they defied him and slaughtered her. So their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground.”* (91:14); *“Indeed, We sent against them ˹only˺ one ˹mighty˺ blast, leaving them like the twigs of fencebuilders.”* (54:31). The sūrah's sequence closes with the town wiping out the last evidence of its own warning, and the Book's manner of reporting the collapse — one verse, in the next lines — is a comment on the difference between the years of patient speech and the instant of the answer.

**The Inversion the Sūrah Keeps**

The reader has now watched three towns move at increasing speed from argument to action. Noah's town needed a century of preaching; ʿĀd needed a taunt; Thamūd needed only the sight of a sign and the confidence of a ruling class. The Book's point is that the pattern accelerates: the more evidence a community has, the less patience it musters for the messenger who brings it.

Verse 77 closes with the town's challenge still hanging in the air, and the next verse supplies the fulfilment in a form they did not choose. The Book's narratives of the towns are, among other things, a lesson in the way the warned describe their own coming destruction — as an accusation to be tested rather than a fact to be prepared for. Thamūd's last words in this account are a dare, and the answer arrives before the sentence has had time to settle.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:78

> **So the earthquake seized them, and morning found them lying lifeless in their abode.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tremor**

*Fa-akhadhat-humu al-rajfatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn* — so the tremor seized them, and they became, in their homes, fallen on their knees. The sūrah's account of Thamūd's end is one sentence long, and every word of it is chosen to close an argument. The instrument of destruction is *al-rajfah*: the quake, the shudder — the punishment that arrives through the ground the town had built its palaces on.

The Book uses different instruments for different towns, and the variation is a teaching device. Noah's people drowned (7:64); ʿĀd's were carried off by a wind (69:6–7); Thamūd's were shaken; the people of Lot were overwhelmed by a rain of stones and a deafening cry (7:84, 15:73–74); Madyan's were seized by the quake and the day of the shadow (7:91, 26:189). The sūrah's list of penalties matches the towns' accounts of their own invulnerability: a maritime people would not have feared a quake, a mountain people would not have feared a flood. The Book gives each town a destruction that its own pride had ruled out.

**"In Their Homes"**

*Fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn* — and in the morning they were in their home, fallen. The details deserve the attention the sūrah gives them. The town of carved mountains is destroyed *in their home* — inside the buildings that were supposed to be shelter from exactly this kind of judgment. The Book's earlier verse has just listed their architecture as evidence of their blessings; the next sentence cancels it. The Qur'an repeats the formula for ʿĀd and Madyan (7:72 vicinity; 7:91) and uses it again in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt: *“So their homes are there, uninhabited after them”* (27:52), and in Sūrat Hūd: *“And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes,”* (11:67).

*Jāthimīn* is a precise posture-word: kneeling, fallen on the chest, crouched. The image the sūrah is drawing is not of a battlefield but of a settlement stopped in the middle of an ordinary morning — the corpse as a domestic object. The Book's Hereafter scenes use the same language in a different register when the towns stand before the Judge: *“you will see every community kneeling, each community called to its record”* (45:28). The sūrah's reader is meant to link the two: the posture that filled the morning in Thamūd's streets is the posture of the Day, and one of them happened without warning.

**The Speed of the Verdict**

The single sentence of verse 78 has another function: at the level of the narrative, it delays nothing. The reader of Sūrat al-Aʿrāf has watched the sūrah build its case for the justice of the Hereafter across seventy verses — the question, the record, the scale, the gates, the wall, the conversations. Here is the verdict in the world, and it arrives in the time it takes to recite a line of poetry.

The Book's speed in these narratives is deliberate, and its own explanation of the mechanism appears in the sūrah of Hūd: *“So when Our command came, We saved Ṣāliḥ and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us, and from the disgrace of that Day”* (11:66). The pattern of rescue is stated there with the same words used for the towns: a command arrives, the believers are taken out by mercy, and the place is emptied. Nothing in the sūrah's accounts of the towns is drawn out, because the interest is never in the destruction itself.

**What the Book Says About the Warnings**

The Book's repeated judgments on the towns are not merely narrative; they are evidence in the sūrah's argument about the Hereafter. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself will make this explicit in a few verses, in a passage about the towns of the peninsula whose ruins are visible: *“Did the people of those societies feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them by night while they were asleep? … Or did they feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them by day while they were at play?”* (7:97–98). The question is asked in the present tense, in the sūrah's own voice, and it is addressed to its audience.

Verse 78 supplies the display case for that question. Nothing the town possessed — the plains, the fortresses, the treasuries, the standing army, the legitimacy conferred by generations of possession — altered the outcome by a single day. The Book's narration of the destruction is not gloating; its tone is the tone of a man who has watched the ruins on the road, and its purpose is stated in its own vocabulary: *a reminder*.

**The Verse as a Turning Point**

Inside the sequence, Thamūd's destruction is the middle of the five towns, and the sūrah uses it differently from the earlier two. Noah's account ended with the ark and a blind people; ʿĀd's ended with a rescue and an uprooting. Verse 78's glimpse of the corpses in the houses sets up the most affecting verse of the whole sequence — the next one, in which Ṣāliḥ addresses the ruins.

The reader should notice the placement: the sūrah does not end the town's story with a moral until the messenger has had the last word over the bodies. That word, in verse 79, is not a condemnation but an account of a mission that has now ended badly, pronounced by the one man the town could have listened to. The Book's arrangement of the sequence gives the town its destruction and then immediately gives its messenger the elegy, and the sūrah is by that arrangement making a claim about who the narrative is for.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:79

> **So he turned away from them and said, “O my people! I indeed delivered unto you the message of my Lord, and advised you sincerely, but you love not sincere advisers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Eleventh Hour and the Eleventh Verse**

*Fa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā qawmi laqad ablaghtukum risālata rabbī wa-naṣaḥtu lakum wa-lākin lā tuḥibbūna al-nāṣiḥīn* — so he turned away from them and said: O my people, I have conveyed to you the message of my Lord and given you sincere advice, but you do not like sincere advisers.

The verse is the sūrah's elegy, and its structure is exact. The messenger turns his back — *tawallā* — and then speaks, which is to say that the speech is delivered to a town already emptied of hearers, or to the reader, or to the record. The Book's narratives use this farewell posture at the close of a mission in several places; Sūrat al-Muʾminūn gives Noah the same words, and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own Shuʿayb will turn away with an almost identical sentence a few verses later (7:93). What the sūrah is showing its reader is the shape of a completed argument: the messenger has no further speech, and the town has no reply.

**"I Have Conveyed the Message"**

*Laqad ablaghtukum risālata rabbī* — I have conveyed to you the message of my Lord. The verb *ablagha* is the strongest form of conveyance: to deliver something to its destination in full, to bring a thing all the way. It is the term the sūrah used for the messengers' duty in their opening speeches (7:62, 7:68) and the term the Book uses for the completion of the prophetic commission. The messenger's claim, made in the presence of a destroyed town, is not that the town refused to listen but that the terms of the delivery are complete: whatever comes next is not a failure of transmission.

That claim is important in the Book's moral economy. The sūrah's reader is repeatedly shown that the messengers are not blamed for the outcome of their preaching, and the Book's instruction to the last Prophet repeats the principle: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. … You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22 in its vicinity; cf. 42:48). Verse 79 teaches the same lesson with the town's corpse as the visual aid.

**"Sincere Advice" for the Third Time**

The word *naṣaḥtu* — I gave sincere advice — appears in the sūrah's sequence now for the third time, after Noah (7:62) and Ḥūd (7:68), and it is used again by Shuʿayb (7:93). The repetition is the sūrah's way of defining the prophetic office through the term that the towns most thoroughly misread. The messenger's counsel had been free, patient, uninterested in reward, and addressed to the town's own interest; the town experienced it as interference.

The sūrah's final clause states the reason the advice failed, and it is devastating in its modesty: *wa-lākin lā tuḥibbūna al-nāṣiḥīn* — but you do not love sincere advisers. The Book does not say that the town did not understand, or lacked evidence, or was deceived by a stronger power. It says the town did not love the counsel, and the word *love* shifts the whole explanation to the level of preference. Thamūd's problem was not epistemic but affective; they disliked being counselled, and the sūrah's last word on the town is about a taste.

**The Role of Reproach in the Book**

The sūrah's messenger does not curse the town in this verse and does not invoke punishment upon it; he reports his own conduct and the town's dislike. It is worth comparing the Book's other farewells: Shuʿayb's in the same sūrah ends with *“We would surely be fabricating a lie against Allah if we were to return to your faith after Allah has saved us from it. It does not befit us to return to it unless it is the Will of Allah, our Lord. Our Lord has encompassed everything in ˹His˺ …”* (7:89 area), and Noah's prayer over his people in Sūrat Nūḥ is recorded at length — *“My Lord, do not leave a single disbeliever on earth”* (71:26). The Qur'an's messengers are not uniformly gentle, and they are not uniformly severe; what the Book's portraits share is that none of them takes the town's answer personally. Their reproach is always directed at the refusal of the message, and their grief is always for the people, not for themselves.

Ṣāliḥ's sentence is the cleanest statement of the pattern in the entire sequence: the mission is complete, the counsel was given, and the failure is located in the hearers' appetite. If the sūrah had wanted the reader to pity the town, this verse would be the place; instead it hands the reader a mirror, because the Book's narratives of the towns are not mainly about the past. Its audience was told that these stories were *reminders*, and the reminder in verse 79 is that a community can spend its entire window of opportunity disliking the people who told it the truth.

**The Silence After**

The verse's last feature is what the sūrah does next: it begins the story of Lot. There is no pause for lament and no comment from the narrator, and the reader is left holding a scene of one man speaking to an empty street. The sūrah's sequence moves the way the Book's histories move — the town falls, the messenger speaks, the narrative advances — and the accumulation is the argument. Five towns will be laid end to end in this chapter, and the reader who arrives at the end of them will have heard three words repeated in the mouths of the messengers and never once answered: *worship Allah; you have no god besides Him*.
