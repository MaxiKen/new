## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:5

> **Their plea, when Our Might came upon them, was but to say, “Truly we were wrongdoers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Arrives Too Late**

The fourth verse of the sūrah left the destroyed towns asleep in their beds or resting at midday; this fifth verse reports what they said when the Might of God arrived. The Arabic is *fa-mā kāna daʿwāhum idh jāʾahum baʾsunā illā an qālū innā kunnā ẓālimīn*. The translation "plea" renders *daʿwā*, a noun from the root *d-ʿ-w*, to call out. Its lexical range is instructive: in the market and the courtroom of the Arabs the same word meant a *claim* — the assertion a man entered on his own behalf. What the verse records, then, is not a prayer. It is the last claim the townspeople filed before a court whose session had already ended.

The grammar carries the weight. *Mā kāna … illā* is a restrictive construction: the whole of their speech was *nothing but* that one clause. Not a defence, not a bargain, not an appeal to ancestry or custom — the entire content of their final hour collapsed into four words: *we were wrongdoers*.

**Why the Confession Cannot Help Them**

The word rendered "wrongdoers" is *ẓālimūn*, from *ẓ-l-m*. Its primary sense is not cruelty but displacement: to put a thing where it does not belong. A man is a *ẓālim* in this Qur'anic sense when he installs himself in a station that is not his — when he sits in judgment over what belongs to God, treats his own desire as the standard, and answers for an account that was never his to settle. The confession in the verse is therefore exact. The townspeople do not confess a list of crimes; they confess a mislocation, a life spent in a place that was not theirs.

The tragedy is that the words are true and the time is gone. The Qur'an draws the line between two confessions with unmistakable clarity. Repentance belongs to the living hour: *“repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, ‘Now I repent!’”* (4:18). Pharaoh made this confession with the water at his throat — *“I believe!”* — and the answer still echoes: *“Now you believe? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors”* (10:90–91). The dead cry out for a second term: *“My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind”* — and the door answers: *“Never!”* (23:99–100).

Across those scenes the pattern is one anatomy in three parts: the recognition is genuine, the repair is impossible, and the verdict is unchanged. The verse therefore does not describe penitence; it describes penitence's counterfeit — the moment when agreement with God's judgment costs nothing, because nothing can any longer be rescued by it.

**The Clock Inside the Verse**

Read this way, 7:5 stops being a report about vanished nations and becomes a measurement of the reader's own position. The sentence *we were wrongdoers* has two lives. Spoken inside the season of the open door, it is the beginning of the turning; spoken after the closing, it is the final entry in a ledger already sealed. The same words, one hour apart, are two different acts — and the two verses before this one established that no one controls the hour, since the torment came at night or at midday, unannounced.

The Qur'an places the cure beside the diagnosis. *“Whoever does wrong or sins and then seeks forgiveness of God will find God Forgiving and Merciful”* (4:110) is what the townspeople could have said and did not, in the years when saying it would have worked. The Prophet ﷺ described God's reception of such a turning in terms that make this verse's warning land: *“God is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one of you who finds his camel that he had lost in the desert.”* (Muslim 2747).

The destroyed town and the returning servant say the same sentence and end in opposite places. What separates them is not the sincerity of the tone but their position on the clock of mercy — and the reader of this verse stands, by definition, in the part of the hour where the sentence still works.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:6

> **Then We shall surely question those unto whom Our message was sent, and We shall surely question the messengers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Interrogations, One Order**

The previous verse left the nations with a confession; this one opens the court in which the confession is taken. The emphatic form is deliberate: *la-nasʾalanna* is an oath-future reinforced by *nūn al-tawkīd*, the strongest way Arabic has of saying *this will certainly happen*. The parties are named in a fixed order — first *those unto whom the message was sent*, then *the messengers* themselves.

The order is the argument. The community stands first because the question at issue is what it did with what reached it; the messengers stand second because their testimony closes the door on every plea of ignorance. No one will be able to say that the warning never came, because the same court will call the carrier who brought it.

**What the Messengers Are Asked**

The Qur'an describes the messengers' interrogation, and it is narrow: *“the Day Allah will gather the messengers and say, ‘What response did you receive?’”* (5:109). The question is not whether they were eloquent or successful — success was never theirs to engineer — but whether the message was delivered. The Book states their commission in the same short form again and again: *“your duty is only to deliver the message”* (13:40); *“then your duty is only to deliver the message clearly”* (16:82). A messenger's account is settled by the delivery, and the delivery is what leaves the community without an excuse.

**Why the People Are Questioned at All**

The classical commentators took seriously a difficulty that other verses create: some passages seem to say the guilty will not be questioned. *“On that Day there will be no need for any human or jinn to be asked about their sins”* (55:39). A witness will be brought from every faith-community (4:41; 16:84), the record will be laid open (39:69). How can there be interrogation if there is nothing to ask?

The answer the exegetes gave is the key to this verse: the questioning is not an investigation. God does not ask in order to learn; the account is already complete, and the very next verse says so — *“Then We shall recount unto them with knowledge.”* The questioning of 7:6 is the arraignment: the moment the evidence is put to the person and the person is made to stand inside it. That is why 16:84 ends with the disbelievers allowed neither to plead nor to appeal — the interrogation exposes the excuse; it does not discover the fact.

**The Interrogation That Has No Interpreter**

The Prophet ﷺ described the setting of that encounter with the precision of an eyewitness: *“There is none among you except that Allah will speak to him, with no interpreter between Him and him. He will look to his right and see nothing except what he has sent forth, and to his left nothing except what he has sent forth, and ahead of him nothing but the Fire — so protect yourselves from the Fire even with half a date.”* (al-Bukhārī 1413; Muslim 1016).

The detail that carries this verse's weight is the absence of intermediaries. Human power is screened: secretaries, advocates, patrons, spokesmen stand between the ruler and the ruled. The court of 7:6 has none. The question is put by the Lord directly to the servant, with the witnesses and the messengers present — and every layered defence a person spent a lifetime building (*my father did so*, *my people did so*, *no one told me*) has already been answered by the earlier session of the same court, in which the carriers were asked and the delivery was confirmed.

In two clauses, then, the verse gives the whole architecture of accountability: a message that arrived, carriers who delivered it, and a meeting in which both sides answer — the carriers for the delivery, the receivers for the response, and no one else to blame.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:7

> **Then We shall recount unto them with knowledge, for We were never absent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Recounting That Matches the Fact**

From the question the verse moves to the narration: *fa-la-naquṣṣanna ʿalayhim bi-ʿilm*. The verb is *qaṣṣa*, to follow a thing step by step — the root that also gives *qiṣṣah*, a story, because a story is a tracing of events in their order. To recount in the Arabic of the Qur'an is not to summarize; it is to reproduce the sequence so faithfully that the hearer recognizes his own life inside it. The instrument is named: *with knowledge* — not evidence reconstructed after the fact, not inference from testimony, but the knowledge that was present when the act itself occurred.

The final clause states the ground of that completeness: *wa-mā kunnā ghāʾibīn* — We were never absent. *Ghāʾib* is the one who is elsewhere; the negation removes not merely a lapse but the whole category of distance. The Qur'an expresses the same fact elsewhere in images the imagination can hold: *“We are closer to them than their jugular vein”* (50:16); *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything fresh or dry which is not in a clear record”* (6:59). The Book does not say that God was informed afterwards. It says that the record was never left unwritten.

**The Reading of the Record**

The sequence of this sūrah — question, recounting, weighing — matches the courtroom imagery the Qur'an uses for the final accounting. The record is brought out and opened: *“And the record will be laid open, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is written in it”* (18:49). Each person is handed his own book: *“We have bound every human's destiny to their neck, and on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each person a record which they will find laid open”* (17:13) — and is told: *“Read your record. You alone are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14). Before the prophets and witnesses are brought forward, the earth shines with the light of its Lord and judgment is passed (39:69).

The purpose of that architecture is that a person is not merely judged; he is made to read. The recounting of 7:7 is that reading, and *with knowledge* is why it cannot be contested. No one can plead that the file was mistaken or that a context was missing or that the intention was better than the deed — because the One who recounts was present at the intention as well.

**The Hours No One Saw**

The Prophet ﷺ gave this certainty a name and a set of questions. The feet of the servant will not move on the Day of Judgment, he said, until he is asked about his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he wore it out, his wealth and how he earned it and where he spent it, and his knowledge and what he did with it (al-Tirmidhī 2417, from Abū Barzah; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; a fourfold version is reported from Muʿādh in al-Dārimī 554). Not one of those four can be answered by reputation. All of them belong to the interior — to hours that no one but God observed.

That is the practical force of *We were never absent*. Human accountability is limited to what can be seen; the divine recounting is not — and this is a mercy before it is a dread. A person who did good that nobody ever learned of has lost nothing at all, and a person who kept an evil behind a locked door has hidden it from everyone except the One who is now doing the recounting. The verse makes secret-keeping as impossible as it already was, and makes integrity a matter of fact rather than of performance: the only kind that survives the reading of the record.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:8

> **And the weighing on that Day is true. So those whose balance is heavy, it is they who shall prosper.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day That Weighs**

This verse sets the third action of the sūrah's courtroom: after the question (v. 6) and the recounting (v. 7), the weighing. The syntax puts the verdict first: *wa-l-waznu yawmaʾidhin al-ḥaqq* — and the weighing on that Day is the truth. *Ḥaqq* does double duty here. It means the scales are real, not a metaphor that dissolves on inspection; and it means they are true — exact, uncorrupted, incapable of registering a false weight.

The choice of the scale as the instrument of judgment answers the audience of this Makkan sūrah. Al-Aʿrāf speaks to a mercantile city whose life ran on balances: measured grain, weighed silver, a handshake over a pan. The Qur'an takes the instrument of the market and moves it to the last Day, where it is operated by the One who cannot be bribed. The point is sharpened in another sūrah, where woe is pronounced on traders who demand full measure when they buy and short it when they sell (83:1–3). The market's scale is precisely where men learn to weigh for advantage — which is why it is a scale that is set up on the Day of Judgment, and why the verse insists that the measuring there is *ḥaqq*.

**The Scale That Weighs What Scales Cannot**

The Qur'an describes the operation of this balance with mechanical precision: *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth”* (21:47). It will register an atom's weight (4:40; 99:7–8), and its verdicts are given in the very phrasing used here: *“As for those whose scale is heavy, it is they who will be successful. But those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves”* (23:102–103); and in Sūrat al-Qāriʿah, *“So as for those whose scale is heavy, they will be in a life of bliss”* (101:6–7).

What, then, is weighed? The Prophet ﷺ answered with an image no arithmetic can hold. A man is brought before God with ninety-nine scrolls of sins, each stretching as far as the eye can see, and a single card bearing the witness *there is no god but God*. The scrolls are placed on one pan and the card on the other: the scrolls rise, the card descends — *“nothing is heavier than the Name of God”* (al-Tirmidhī 2639, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr; graded ḥasan; Ibn Mājah records it as well).

That image is this verse's commentary. The scales of the hereafter do not weigh quantity, so that whoever piles up the most acts wins; they weigh reality — the actual worth of what a life was aimed at. A mountain of activity performed for display can rise like chaff, while one sincere confession of the One God can descend with the weight of a lifetime. This is why the Day can be called *the truth*: it is the moment when things are measured as they are, and not as they were advertised.

**The Prosperity of the Heavy**

The verse closes with the outcome for those whose pan descends: *fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn*. *Mufliḥūn* comes from *f-l-ḥ*, the root of the farmer — *fallāḥ* — who splits the earth, plants, and waits. *Filāḥ*, prosperity, is the same word as cultivation, because in the Qur'an's vocabulary success is not what a man extracts from the world but what grows out of what he has planted in it. The Arabic quietly answers the mercantile image: the real trade is not the one that closes when the caravan departs, but the one whose harvest is delivered on the Day the scales are set.

Heaviness, in that grammar, is not a burden. It is the weight of the things that survive — a confession guarded in secret, a charity no one recorded, an injustice refused at the moment when it would have paid. All of it descends in the pan, in the sight of no one except the One who was never absent.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:9

> **And as for those whose balance is light, it is they who have ruined their souls by having treated Our signs wrongfully.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lightness as a Verdict**

The verse completes the pair begun in 7:8 with an exact mirror: the heavy scale and the light. What makes a scale light is not always the absence of deeds — the scrolls and the card have already shown that volume counts for nothing — but the absence of weight. The measure of the hereafter is not the number of acts but their worth, and the worth of an act is set by the aim to which it was offered.

The fate is then stated in commercial Arabic: *alladhīna khasirū anfusahum* — those who have lost themselves. *Khusrān* is the merchant's word for a loss, and the Qur'anic usage is exact: this is not the loss of profit but the loss of capital. The person in the verse leaves the market of his life having traded away the very self he was trading with. The same ledger appears in another sūrah: those who reject the meeting with their Lord have *“rendered their deeds void, so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day”* (18:105). A deed with no weight to give is a loss no accounting can repair.

**The Cause: Wronging the Signs**

The verse then names the cause, and it is not the one a reader might expect. Lightness is not diagnosed here as weakness or as ignorance. It is *an ẓalamū bi-āyātinā ẓulm* — that they wronged Our signs. The noun is the same one the townspeople used of themselves in 7:5, and the echo ties the sūrah's opening to its weighing: the destruction of the first verses and the light scale here are one offense at two moments of the story. Wronging a sign (*ẓulm*) is, in the Qur'an's usage, putting it out of its place — denying it outright, or acknowledging the words while treating them as though they carried no claim, or bending their meaning toward a preferred outcome. The imperfect *yaẓlimūn* adds what "having treated wrongfully" can only hint at: this was not a single lapse but a practice.

The seriousness follows from what a sign is. The signs — the revelation, the messengers, the world itself with its dawns and its marks of design — do not merely inform; they oblige. To meet one and shrug is not neutrality; it is a decision about the One who sent it. That is why the wronging of signs is counted among the gravest acts, and why the lightness of the scale is its natural consequence: a person who spends a lifetime refusing weight arrives at the judgment with none.

**The Exactness That Spares No One**

The verses around this one guard the verdict from despair. *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least”* (21:47). *“Indeed, Allah never wrongs anyone — even by an atom's weight”* (4:40). *“So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7–8). The lightness of this verse is therefore no accident of arithmetic, and no one will be condemned for a gram that was never his to supply: the same Book that weighs a mustard seed also declares that God does not require of any soul more than it can bear (2:286).

What follows in the sūrah — the settlement of humanity on the earth, its brief tenure, and the return (7:10 onward) — reads as the practical conclusion of this weighing. If the scale is real, then the days in which a pan may still be loaded with something of weight are exactly the days being lived now. Verse 9 closes the ledger with a warning whose force lies in its tense: those who *were* wronging Our signs made their own lightness, one deliberate and unremarkable moment at a time.
