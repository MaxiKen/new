**The Question That Indicts the Questioner**

*Man ḥarrama zīnata Allāh* — who has forbidden the adornment of God? The construction is a question, but its force is that of a challenge, because the answer it invites is silence. No one in the sūrah's audience could claim a divine warrant for the prohibitions they had invented, and the verse makes the absence of a warrant visible by asking for it in public.

The Qur'an is careful with what follows. The adornment is not described as a human possession; it is *Allāh's* adornment, *allatī akhraja li-ʿibādihi* — which He brought out for His servants. The possessive construction repeats in two directions: the beauty belongs to God, and its purpose is the welfare of the people who serve Him. To forbid it is not austerity but expropriation — the removal of a gift from the beneficiaries God Himself designated.

**The Long History of Pious Prohibition**

The verse is addressing a pattern, not a single practice. The commentators attach it to several: the Arabs who set aside certain livestock and crops as *baḥīrah*, *sāʾibah*, and *waṣīlah*, declaring them forbidden by religious vow (5:103; 6:138–139); the People of the Book who forbade themselves foods that God had never forbidden (3:93; 4:160; 6:146); and the ascetics, referred to in the Qur'an's own words about monasticism: *“As for monasticism, they made it up — We never ordained it for them — only seeking to please Allah…” (57:27)* (57:27).

The Book's attitude to all of them is consistent. Self-denial is not a virtue in itself, and legislation that God did not issue is not worship but a claim upon His authority. The Prophet ﷺ made the principle explicit when he heard that some of his companions had resolved to fast continuously, to stand all night in prayer, and to abstain from marriage: he refused to have anything to do with such a discipline and said, *“But I fast and break the fast, I pray and sleep, and I marry — so whoever turns away from my practice is not of me”* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401). The response is not a rebuke of devotion; it is the rejection of devotion conducted by a law that God did not write.

**"For the Believers in the Life of This World, and for Them Alone in the Hereafter"**

The verse's second half is a statement about ownership, and it answers a question the first audience disputed. Some had argued that the good things of the world were destined for the believers alone, or that non-Muslims enjoyed them illegitimately; others held the opposite, that the Hereafter offered something thinner than the pleasures of the present life. The verse settles both: *khāliṣatan lahum yawma al-qiyāmah* — on the Day of Resurrection they are exclusively for them.

Al-Ṭabarī records the readings. The dominant one is that in this world the good things are shared — the rain falls on everyone, the fields of the ungrateful yield as well as those of the grateful — and in the Hereafter the good things will be pure, reserved, and without the mixing that characterizes a world in which the righteous and the wicked live side by side. This is what the Book means elsewhere when it says of the Garden that it contains *“what no eye has seen, no ear has heard”* and that its people *“will have whatever they desire”* (41:31; 50:35). The promise is not that the believer will be repaid in kind; it is that the enjoyment of the good will finally be untroubled.

The verse closes: *Thus do We expound the signs for a people who know.* The phrase is not decorative. The dispute about permitted and forbidden is, in the Qur'an's analysis, an epistemic dispute: it arises where knowledge has been replaced by custom, fear, or self-importance. Al-Rāzī makes the point sharply: the question of what God has permitted is answered by revelation, and where revelation has spoken, no further authority may add to it.

**Two Pieties the Verse Rejects by Name**

The question *who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought out for His servants?* answers two opposite errors, and the Book answers each of them elsewhere in its own terms. The first is the piety that treats the good things of the world as inherently defiling: Sūrat al-Māʾidah commands, *“Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors”* (5:87), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself had laid the foundation two verses earlier: *“Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful”* (7:31). The second error is the licence that reads the verse as permission for excess, and the same clause rules it out: the lawful is *ṭayyib*, good, and the Book's command is to eat it with gratitude (2:172; 16:114), not to gorge on it.

The Prophet ﷺ settled the first error with a scene that has become the standard commentary on this verse. Anas ibn Mālik reports that three men came to the houses of the Prophet's wives asking about his worship; when it was described to them, they treated it as insufficient and each vowed an extreme: prayer all night, fasting without a break, and celibacy. The Prophet came out and said, *“Are you the ones who said such and such? By Allah, I am the most God-fearing and pious among you, yet I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me”* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401).

The sūrah's case is doctrinal rather than merely disciplinary, and its final clause states it: the good things of God are *for those who believe during the life of this world, exclusively for them on the Day of Resurrection*. The believer's enjoyment of what God permits is not a concession to weakness, and it is not shared with the Hereafter, where it becomes his alone — a reallocation that quietly reverses the whole economy of Makkah, whose notables had assumed that the pleasant things of God were theirs in this world and would remain theirs in the next.
