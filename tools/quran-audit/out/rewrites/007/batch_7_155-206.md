## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:155

> **And Moses chose seventy men from his people for Our meeting. And when the earthquake seized them, he said, “My Lord! Hadst Thou willed, Thou wouldst have destroyed them and me beforehand. Wilt Thou destroy us for that which the fools among us have done? It is naught but Thy trial, whereby Thou leadest astray whomsoever Thou wilt, and guidest whomsoever Thou wilt. Thou art our Protector, so forgive us and have mercy upon us, and Thou art the best of forgivers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Seventy Men for Our Appointment"**

*Wa-khtāra Mūsā qawmahu sabʿīna rajulan li-mīqātinā fa-lammā akhadhathumu al-rajfatu qāla rabbi law shiʾta ahlaktahum min qablu wa-iyyāya a-tuhlikunā bimā faʿala al-sufahāʾu minnā* — Moses chose seventy men from among his people for Our appointment and, when they were seized by an earthquake, he cried: "My Lord! Had You willed, You could have destroyed them long ago, and me as well. Will You destroy us for what the foolish among us have done?"

**The Delegation**

The verse opens with a selection: seventy men, drawn from the community, chosen for the appointment Moses himself had already attended. The sūrah's narrative of the mountain had ended with the law recovered and the community's repentance accepted (7:153–154); the delegation belongs to the community's effort to approach what its prophet had been granted.

**The Same Seventy in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Baqarah records the demand that produced the earthquake, and it is the raw claim of people who had watched the sea open: *“And ˹remember˺ when you said, ‘O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes,’ so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on”* (2:55). The sūrah's seventy are the representatives of that demand, and their seizure is the answer to it.

**The Earthquake and the Plea**

*Al-rajfah* — the quaking — takes them, and the prophet's first response is not a defense of his own standing but a plea for his people. Moses' prayer is placed in the mouth of a leader whose community had just made a calf, and he does not disown it: the phrase he uses is *among us*.

**The Question of Collective Punishment**

*A-tuhlikunā bimā faʿala al-sufahāʾu minnā* — will You destroy us for what the foolish among us have done? The Book answers that question elsewhere as a principle: *“No one will reap except what they sow. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”* (6:164). The punishment of a community in the Qur'an is never arbitrary, and the sūrah's narrator will shortly say so in its own words.

**What Moses Does Not Claim**

The prayer does not claim that the community is innocent. The men seized had demanded the vision; the calf had been made in the camp; and Moses himself had asked to see God at the same mountain days earlier (7:143). His plea is therefore not a protest of innocence but an appeal to mercy, and the sūrah's readers are shown a leader who identifies with the people he asks God to spare.

**The Confession of the Test**

*In hiya illā fitnatuk* — this is only a test from You, by which You allow whoever You will to stray and guide whoever You will. The sentence relocates the event inside the sūrah's consistent doctrine of testing: the sea, the manna, the calf, the mountain, and the earthquake are all trials, and the community's standing is measured by its response rather than by its comfort.

**The Guardian and the Request**

The prayer closes with two names and two requests: *You are our Guardian. So forgive us and have mercy on us. You are the best forgiver*. The request's terms are the Book's standard petition for a community under judgment, and the sūrah's previous chapter had ended with the same pair (7:153).

**The Delegation's Place in the Narrative**

Read in sequence, the verse supplies what the sūrah's Israel narrative had been missing since the calf: an act of the community's own religious initiative. The seventy are chosen by the people's prophet for a meeting with God, and the Book reports the choice without comment, which is its own verdict on the delegation's intent.

**The Death and the Restoration**

Sūrat al-Baqarah gives the sequel to the thunderbolt: *“Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful”* (2:56). The sūrah's readers know from that account that the delegation was restored, and the sūrah's own narrative continues in the next verse with the mercy that encompasses everything — a clause that answers the earthquake and the calf at once.

**The Prophet as Intercessor**

The scene belongs to the Book's pattern in which a messenger pleads for his people at the moment of their worst failure. Moses' prayer here follows his prayer at the calf (7:151) and precedes his prayer before the drowning; the Qur'an's prophets are consistently described as intercessors inside their communities rather than as prosecutors of them.

**Why the Seventy Were Chosen**

The number matters in the Book's memory: the tradition of Israel's leaders is twelve, and the Qur'an's account of the covenant with the Children of Israel names twelve chiefs (5:12). The seventy here are a delegation rather than an institution, and the sūrah treats them as representatives — which is why their failure is the community's failure and their restoration is the community's mercy.

**The Sūrah's Second Mountain**

The sūrah has now described two gatherings at the mountain: the appointment of Moses alone, where he asked for the vision and the mountain was levelled (7:143), and the appointment of the seventy, where an earthquake seized the representatives. The same lesson is given in two registers — the nearer a community comes to the divine presence, the smaller its claim to self-sufficiency becomes.

**What the Next Verse Answers**

The prayer for mercy continues into the next verse's request for good in this life and the next, and God's answer to it states the widest form of mercy in the Qur'an. The sūrah's reader is given the earthquake, the plea, and the answer within three verses, and the passage as a whole is the sūrah's own summary of how a community recovers.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:156

> **And prescribe good for us in the life of this world, and in the Hereafter; truly we have turned unto Thee.” He said, “I cause My Punishment to smite whomsoever I will, though My Mercy encompasses all things. I shall prescribe it for those who are reverent, and give alms, and those who believe in Our signs,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"My Mercy Encompasses Everything"**

*Wa-ktub lanā fī hādhihi al-dunyā ḥasanatan wa-fī al-ākhirati innā hudnā ilayka qāla ʿadhābī uṣību bihi man ashāʾu wa-raḥmatī wasiʿat kulla shayʾin fa-sa-aktubuhā li-lladhīna yattaqūna wa-yuʾtūna al-zakāta wa-lladhīna hum bi-āyātinā yuʾminūn* — "Ordain for us what is good in this life and the next. Indeed, we have turned to You ˹in repentance˺." Allah replied: "I will inflict My torment on whoever I will. But My mercy encompasses everything. I will ordain mercy for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations."

**The Prayer for the Two Abodes**

*Wa-ktub lanā* — decree for us, write for us — asks for good in this world and the next in a single sentence. The Book's prayers characteristically join the two, and the Qur'an states the contrast: *“Yet there are others who say, ‘Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire’”* (2:201), against the petition of those who want only this life (2:200). Moses' sentence asks for both.

**The Return to God**

*Innā hudnā ilayka* — we have turned to You in repentance — closes the prayer with the word the sūrah used for Israel's remorse at the calf (7:149). The Book's repentance formulas make the turning the condition of the request, and the sūrah's placement of the clause after the plea for mercy keeps that order intact.

**The Answer's First Clause**

*ʿAdhābī uṣību bihi man ashāʾu* — I will inflict My torment on whoever I will. The Book's statements about punishment consistently attach it to a decision rather than a mechanism, and the sūrah's readers have just seen the mechanism at work in Egypt and in the wilderness. The clause is not a threat aimed at Israel; it is the declaration that mercy and severity both belong to the One being addressed.

**The Widest Sentence in the Qur'an**

*Wa-raḥmatī wasiʿat kulla shayʾ* — My mercy encompasses everything. The sentence is the Book's most expansive statement of divine mercy, and the Qur'an states its logic elsewhere: *“Our Lord! You encompass everything in ˹Your˺ mercy and knowledge”* (40:7). Here the phrase answers the interrogation of a people whose representatives had just been struck down.

**The Condition Attached**

The verse does not leave the mercy as an abstraction. *Fa-sa-aktubuhā li-lladhīna yattaqūna wa-yuʾtūna al-zakāta wa-lladhīna hum bi-āyātinā yuʾminūn* — I will ordain it for those who shun evil, pay alms-tax, and believe in Our revelations. Three marks are named, and the second is a financial obligation given to a community that had been complaining about its food in the desert.

**The Three Marks in the Qur'an's Law**

The triple — God-consciousness, alms, and faith in the signs — describes the Qur'an's standard of the believer's life: the inner disposition, the social duty, and the doctrinal commitment. Sūrat al-Baqarah's definition of righteousness makes the same move from ritual to substance (2:177), and the sūrah's own later verse about abiding by the Scripture and establishing prayer repeats the structure (7:170).

**The Mercy and the Law Together**

The answer joins the widest mercy in the Book to a specific list of obligations, and the sūrah's readers are meant to hold both. Sūrat al-Ḥijr states the pairing as a command to the Prophet: *“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and that My torment is indeed the most painful”* (15:49–50). Verse 156 is the same pairing in the form of an answer to prayer.

**What the Community Was Asking For**

The seventy had asked for good in two worlds and had confessed their turning; the answer grants the request conditionally and extends the mercy to everything that exists. The sūrah's narrative then continues in the next verse with a description of "those who follow the Messenger" — the Book's way of extending the condition forward in time.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Argument**

The sūrah's central argument since the towns has been that signs and messengers do not compel, and that a community's fate is decided by its response. Verse 156 supplies the theological frame for that argument: mercy is universal in its reach and specific in its recipients, and the recipients are identified by conduct rather than by lineage.

**The Book's Wider Use of the Sentence**

The phrase has functioned in Islamic theology as the Qur'an's charter of hope, cited against despair and against the restriction of mercy to a party. The sūrah's own context keeps it from becoming a slogan: the sentence is spoken to a prophet interceding for representatives who had demanded to see God, and it is followed immediately by the conditions.

**The Note on Which the Answer Ends**

The answer's final words name belief in the revelations as the third mark, and the sūrah's next verse describes the community that receives the unlettered Prophet and believes in the light sent down with him. The reader is carried from the widest mercy in the Book to the newest revelation in a single movement, and the two belong together in the sūrah's design.

**The Prayer's Continued Life**

The words of verse 156 have remained part of Muslim supplication because of their shape: a request for good in both worlds, a confession of turning, and an acknowledgment of mercy that precedes the request. The sūrah gives them to a delegation whose members had just been seized by an earthquake, which is the Book's way of saying that the prayer of a shaken community is still heard.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:157

> **those who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whom they find inscribed in the Torah and the Gospel that is with them, who enjoins upon them what is right, and forbids them what is wrong, and makes good things lawful for them, and forbids them bad things, and relieves them of their burden and the shackles that were upon them. Thus those who believe in him, honor him, help him, and follow the light that has been sent down with him; it is they who shall prosper.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The Description They Find in Their Torah and Gospel"**

*Alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-rasūla al-nabiyya al-ummī alladhī yajidūnahu maktūban ʿindahum fī al-tawrāti wa-l-injīl* — ˹they are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel. He commands them to do good and forbids them from evil, permits for them what is lawful and forbids to them what is impure, and relieves them from their burdens and the shackles that bound them.

**The Verse's Subject**

The sentence continues God's answer in the previous verse: the mercy ordained for those who shun evil, pay alms, and believe in the revelations is the mercy ordained for those who follow the Messenger described here. The sūrah's reader is carried from Israel's delegation to the Prophet of the Qur'an, and the Book's claim is one of continuity rather than replacement.

**"The Unlettered Prophet"**

*Al-ummī* is the Book's designation, used in the Qur'an for the Prophet and for the people to whom he was sent. Its sense is that he neither wrote nor studied a book, which is the point the Book makes about the revelation's source: the message's authority rests on its being given, not on its messenger's learning.

**"Whose Description They Find in Their Torah and Gospel"**

The verse claims that the earlier scriptures name him. The Qur'an makes the claim elsewhere: *“And ˹remember˺ when Jesus, son of Mary, said, ‘O children of Israel! I am truly Allah's messenger to you, confirming the Torah which came before me, and giving good news of a messenger after me whose name will be Aḥmad’”* (61:6), and Sūrat Āl ʿImrān's covenant with the prophets states the same pattern (3:81). The sūrah's readers are told that the new revelation fulfils a text already in their hands.

**"He Commands Them to Do Good and Forbids Them from Evil"**

The formula *al-maʿrūf* and *al-munkar* — the recognized good and the repudiated evil — becomes one of the Book's standard descriptions of prophetic office and of the Muslim community's public duty: *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil — it is they who will be successful”* (3:104). The sūrah presents it as a continuation rather than an innovation.

**"What Is Lawful" and "What Is Impure"**

*Wa-yuḥillu lahum al-ṭayyibāti wa-yuḥarrimu ʿalayhim al-khabāʾith* — he permits the good things and forbids the foul. The Qur'an's moral vocabulary distinguishes things by their quality rather than by arbitrary decree, and the sūrah's readers have just been told about the adornments of God and the question of who forbade them (7:32). The verse states the prophetic office as the removal of such prohibitions.

**"Relieves Them from Their Burdens and the Shackles"**

*Wa-yaḍaʿu ʿanhum iṣrahum wa-l-aghlāl allatī kānat ʿalayhim* — the burdens and the fetters are the accumulated obligations the Book associates with the handling of the earlier covenant. The verse presents the new message as a lightening, and the Book calls the Prophet's law a mercy in the same breath.

**Who Succeeds**

The verse's close names four responses: belief in him, honouring him, supporting him, and following the light sent down with him. The Book's descriptions of the believers are built on such lists, and the sequence is significant — the community's relation to the messenger is not admiration but support, and its relation to the revelation is not possession but following.

**The Sūrah's Audience and the Claim**

For the Prophet's community in Makkah, the assertion that the earlier scriptures had described their messenger was a claim about the Book's unity; for the People of the Book addressed by the sūrah, it was a challenge to read their own texts. The sūrah's later chapters address that audience directly, and this verse states the grounds on which the address is made.

**The Prophet's Description in the Book's Own Words**

The Qur'an describes its messenger in several verses as a mercy, a warner, and a light, and elsewhere as the seal of the prophets (33:40). Verse 157 belongs to that series, and its distinctive note is the claim about the earlier books: the community's prophet is not a stranger to revelation but its continuation.

**What the Verse Relieves and What It Confirms**

The sūrah's charges against the earlier communities had been about concealment, distortion, and the abandoning of the Book (7:162, 7:169). The verse's answer is not a severance from them but a restoration of what they had been given — the good things made lawful, the impurities forbidden, and the burdens lifted. The Book's self-description here is a claim about recovery.

**The Conditions of Success**

The verse's final clause makes success conditional on the four responses, and the sūrah's readers hear the condition in a passage that has just described Israel's failures. The Book's promises attach to conduct throughout, and the sūrah's earlier verse about Israel's favoured position (7:140) shows how the Book uses such claims.

**The Passage's Continuation**

The next verse addresses humanity directly with the Prophet's declaration that he is God's Messenger to all of them, extending the audience from the People of the Book to the whole of humanity. The sūrah thus moves from the mountain of Sinai to the universal address in two verses, and the Book presents the movement as a single messenger with a single message.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:158

> **Say, “O mankind! Truly I am the Messenger of God unto you all—Him to Whom belongs Sovereignty over the heavens and the earth. There is no god but He. He gives life and causes death. So believe in God and His Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, who believes in God and His Words; and follow him, that haply you may be guided.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"I Am Allah's Messenger to You All"**

*Qul yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innī rasūlu Allāhi ilaykum jamīʿan alladhī lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi lā ilāha illā huwa yuḥyī wa-yumīt* — Say, ˹O Prophet˺: "O humanity! I am Allah's Messenger to you all. To Him ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He gives life and causes death."

**The Address's Scope**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nās* — O humanity — is the Qur'an's widest vocative, and the sūrah places it at the end of a narrative about one people and their mountain. The verse's claim, *to you all*, is the Book's answer to the assumption that a messenger belongs to a single lineage, and the sūrah's readers in Makkah are told that the message they are hearing is addressed to everyone.

**The Universal Messenger in the Book**

The Qur'an states the same universality in several sūrahs: *“We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity, but most people do not know”* (34:28), and *“We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world”* (21:107). The sūrah's verse belongs to that series, and its placement after the Sinai narrative gives the universality a historical argument.

**The Authentication of the Message**

The verse's next clause grounds the messenger's authority in his sender: *to Him ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth*. The Book's messengers argue from the sovereignty of the One who sent them, and the sūrah has already used the same reasoning in Moses' opening speech to the court of Egypt (7:104) and in the magicians' confession (7:121).

**The Testimony of the Book**

Sūrat al-Anʿām records the Prophet's instruction to state that God is witness between him and his people, and that the Qur'an was revealed to him so that he might warn with it (6:19). Verse 158's declaration is the same testimony in the form of a public announcement, and its content — one God, one messenger, one humanity — is the sūrah's summary of its own message.

**"He Gives Life and Causes Death"**

The clause is the Book's compact statement of God's exclusive power over existence, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly as the ground of resurrection and accountability. The sūrah's readers have just been shown the sea opening for one people and closing over another; the sentence is the doctrine that governs both events, and it is put in the messenger's mouth as a claim about the God he serves.

**The Command to Believe and Follow**

*Fa-āminū bi-llāhi wa-rasūlihi al-nabiyyi al-ummī alladhī yuʾminu bi-llāhi wa-kalimātihi wa-ttabiʿūhu laʿallakum tahtadūn* — so believe in Allah and His Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, who believes in Allah and His revelations. And follow him, so you may be ˹rightly˺ guided. The verse asks for two things — belief and following — and it identifies the messenger by his own belief, which is a detail the sūrah's readers are meant to notice: the Prophet is presented as a believer before he is obeyed.

**The Unlettered Prophet Again**

The designation reappears from the previous verse, and its repetition in consecutive verses gives the passage a refrain. The Qur'an's argument about the Prophet's illiteracy is not a claim about ignorance but about the message's origin: what he brought could not have been assembled by him, and the sūrah's readers are being asked to accept the consequence.

**Belief in "His Revelations"**

The phrase *kalimātihi* — His words — is used in the Book for God's decrees and for the revelation given to His messengers, and the Qur'an's instruction to the believers elsewhere joins belief in God, His angels, His books, and His messengers (2:285). The verse's requirement is therefore not a personal loyalty but the acceptance of a message that has come through a chain of divine speech.

**The Sūrah's Movement Completed**

With this verse the sūrah's address turns from the history of the earlier communities to the community being addressed, and the rest of the sūrah — the Sabbath-breakers, the covenant of the descendants of Adam, the parable of the dog, the names of God, and the closing instructions — is spoken to that audience. The verse is the hinge on which the sūrah's second half turns.

**What the Verse Does Not Claim**

The verse does not claim that the earlier messengers are obsolete, and it does not claim that the Prophet's message revises the content of their scriptures in any way other than restoration. The sūrah's charge against the People of the Book is about what they did with what they had, not about what they had been given.

**The Closing Clause's Condition**

*Laʿallakum tahtadūn* — so that you may be guided — attaches the guidance to the following rather than to the hearing. The sūrah's narratives have repeatedly shown people who heard messengers and were not guided, and the Book's conditional grammar keeps the verse's promise inside the reader's response.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Structure**

Read with the previous two verses, the passage moves from the widest mercy in the Book, through the description of the messenger who relieves burdens, to a universal summons. The sūrah's reader is given the theology, the mission, and the invitation in three sentences, and the next verse returns to the history of Israel with a statement about the righteous among them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:159

> **And among the people of Moses is a community that guides by the truth and does justice thereby.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Some Among the People of Moses Who Guide With the Truth"**

*Wa-min qawmi Mūsā ummatun yahdūna bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-bihi yaʿdilūn* — there are some among the people of Moses who guide with the truth and establish justice accordingly.

**The Verse's Function**

The sūrah has just finished a long account of Israel's failures — the calf, the demand for vision, the delegation struck down, the Sabbath-breakers to come — and it now states that the community is not uniform. The sentence is the Book's refusal of collective condemnation, and it is placed precisely where a reader might have concluded that the whole people had been written off.

**The Same Sentence for the Prophet's Community**

The sūrah repeats the formula almost verbatim later, and the repetition is deliberate: *“And among those We created is a group that guides with the truth and establishes justice accordingly”* (7:181). The two sentences answer each other across the sūrah's second half: Israel has such a group, and so does humanity as a whole, and the criterion in both is identical.

**Guidance with the Truth**

*Yahdūna bi-l-ḥaqq* — they guide by the truth — names the standard rather than the community. The Book's definition of a guided group is not its lineage, its history, or its claims, but the truth by which it guides others, and the same word *al-ḥaqq* has been the sūrah's term for what prevailed in Egypt's courtyard (7:118).

**"And by It They Establish Justice"**

*Wa-bihi yaʿdilūn* — the second clause converts guidance into administration. The Qur'an's righteous communities are described as those who establish justice, and the sūrah's earlier narratives measured the destroyed towns by the same standard: Madyan's fraud, ʿĀd's oppression, and Sodom's violence were failures of justice rather than of ritual.

**The Righteous Among the People of the Book**

The Qur'an states the same doctrine about the earlier communities in several places. *“Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah's revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺. They believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good”* (3:113–114), and the verses that follow promise that their good will not be denied (3:115).

**The Verse's Comfort for the Sūrah's Prophet**

Addressed to the Prophet at the end of a narrative about Israel's failures, the sentence is also an encouragement. The Qur'an's narratives of rejection are consistently accompanied by statements that the rejection is not universal: Sūrat al-Anʿām's instruction to follow the guidance given to the earlier messengers (6:90) rests on the same idea, and Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ's promise of a believing remnant follows a similar pattern.

**The Criterion and Its Recipients**

The verse does not say that the group is numerous, and the Book's statements about the righteous are consistently quantitative-minimal: *“except for a few”* (11:40's phrasing), *“˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful”* (34:13). The point of the sentence is not the size of the remnant but the possibility of guidance within a community that has otherwise failed.

**The Book's Rejection of Two Errors**

Read with the sūrah's surrounding verses, the sentence refuses two symmetrical errors. It denies that a community's failures justify a blanket verdict on every individual in it, and it denies that membership in a favoured lineage substitutes for conduct. The Book's doctrine of responsibility, stated earlier in the sūrah (6:164) and in its covenant passages, is applied here to a people with the longest record of signs.

**Justice as the Test of Guidance**

The pairing of guidance with justice in the verse is not accidental. The sūrah's narratives about the towns had ended with the destruction of communities whose religion had become a screen for fraud, and its accounts of Israel's failures had included the unlawful gains of the generations who inherited the Scripture (7:169). The test of a guided group in this sūrah is therefore whether its guidance reaches its scales.

**The Group in the Book's Later History**

The Qur'an's address to the People of the Book elsewhere commands the community to compete in good and notes that among them are believers (5:48's command, 3:199's statement about those who truly believe in what was revealed to them and to the Muslims). The sūrah's verse is the first statement of that theme in this sūrah, and the later verses of the Qur'an develop it.

**What the Verse Contributes to the Sūrah's Address**

The sūrah has been arguing that the earlier communities' failures do not entitle their descendants to anything, and the new community's claim is not lineage either. The verse supplies the pattern the Qur'an applies to its own audience: the criterion is guidance and justice, and the group that meets it exists within a people rather than in place of one.

**The Next Verse's Turn**

The verse is followed immediately by the account of the division into twelve tribes, the water from the rock, and the manna — a reminder of the favours God had given to the people whose remnant is now being acknowledged. The sūrah's arrangement offers the reader both facts at once: the record of failure and the existence of a righteous group, and it declines to resolve them by either denial.

The verse's placement also answers a question the sūrah's readers may carry from its earlier narratives: if the towns were destroyed for their refusal, what of the individuals inside them who did not refuse? The Book's answer is given here in a clause about the people of Moses, and it is repeated later in the sūrah for humanity as a whole (7:181).

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:160

> **And We divided them into twelve tribes, communities. And We revealed to Moses, when his people asked him for water, “Strike the rock with thy staff.” Then twelve springs gushed forth from it: all the people knew their drinking place. And We shaded them with clouds, and sent down manna and quails upon them, “Eat of the good things We have provided you.” And they wronged Us not, but themselves did they wrong.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Twelve Tribes, Twelve Springs"**

*Wa-qaṭṭaʿnāhum ithnatay ʿashrata asbāṭan umaman wa-awḥaynā ilā Mūsā idhi stasqāhu qawmuhu ani iḍrib bi-ʿaṣāka al-ḥajara fa-nbajasat minhu ithnatā ʿashrata ʿaynan* — We divided them into twelve tribes — each as a community. And We revealed to Moses, when his people asked for water, "Strike the rock with your staff." Then twelve springs gushed out. Each tribe knew its drinking place.

**The Division Into Tribes**

The verse treats Israel's social organization as a divine arrangement: *We divided them into twelve tribes, each as a community*. The Qur'an's account of the covenant with the Children of Israel names twelve leaders appointed from among them (5:12), and the Book's administrative vocabulary here matches the structure of the event at the rock in which each group received its own water.

**The Identical Account in Al-Baqarah**

Sūrat al-Baqarah preserves the same scene in nearly the same words: *“And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, ‘Strike the rock with your staff.’ Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place”* (2:60). The Book's repetition of the scene at the head of its longest sūrah and again here, in the middle of its narrative of the wilderness, marks it as one of the founding signs of Israel's formation.

**The Rock and the Staff**

The provision comes through the staff which had earlier become a serpent and then split the sea, and the Book's readers have followed that implement through the whole narrative. The sūrah's lesson is consistent: what Moses is asked to do is to use what he holds, and what follows the act is the divine provision rather than the wood's own property.

**Water as the Foundational Gift**

The Qur'an's wilderness narrative supplies the community with water before it supplies anything else, and the Book's address to the believers uses water as the image of life and provision in several places — the rain, the rivers of the gardens, and the springs of the Hereafter. The sūrah's readers are shown a community whose ongoing survival was a standing sign.

**The Manna and the Quails**

*Wa-ẓallalnā ʿalayhimu al-ghamāma wa-anzalnā ʿalayhimu al-manna wa-l-salwā* — We shaded them with clouds and sent down to them manna and quails. The verse's pair of provisions — covering and food — answer the desert's two dangers, and Sūrat al-Baqarah reports the same gifts in Israel's own memory: *“And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails”* (2:57).

**"Eat from the Good Things We Have Provided for You"**

The command attached to the provision is the Book's standard shape for a gift: the supply comes first, and the instruction follows. Sūrat Ṭāhā's account of the same incident adds a warning — *“Eat from the good things We have provided for you, but do not transgress in them, or My wrath will befall you”* (20:81) — and the Book's pairing of provision with accountability is the sūrah's own doctrine.

**"They Did Not Wrong Us, but Wronged Themselves"**

The verse's final clause is one of the Book's most quoted statements of the logic of wrongdoing: *wa-mā ẓalamūnā wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn*. The Qur'an states the principle in several forms — *“Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss”* (41:46) — and its function in this narrative is to strip the community's complaints of any claim against God.

**The Complaint About the Food**

The sūrah's readers have already heard what the community said about the provision, in the account of the demand for a change of diet: *“O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺”* (2:61). The Book reports the complaint in the same breath as the gift, and verse 160's closing clause is its verdict on the exchange.

**Favours as a Test**

The wilderness provisions belong to the sūrah's doctrine of testing: the same gifting that forms a community can expose its dispositions, and the Book's narratives about manna include the gathering on the Sabbath and the community's discontent. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the pattern in its own account of the same years (2:57–61), and the sūrah's readers are meant to read the gifts and the complaints together.

**The Continuity With the Sūrah's Warning**

The verse sits between the acknowledgement of a righteous remnant among the people of Moses (7:159) and the account of the city whose inhabitants changed what they had been told (7:161–162). The Book's arrangement sets the gifts and the rebellion side by side, and the sūrah's reader is given the juxtaposition as the narrative's permanent structure.

**The Book's Argument About Ingratitude**

The Qur'an comments on ingratitude as a disposition rather than an act, and its formal statement appears repeatedly: *“Whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him ˹alone˺ you cry ˹for help˺”* (16:53). The sūrah's wilderness narrative supplies the concrete version of that verse: a people fed from the sky and reminded that their complaint was against themselves.

**What the Verse's Verb Claims**

*Wa-qaṭṭaʿnāhum* — We divided them — attributes the community's structure to God's act, and the Book's narratives of peoples consistently present their composition as part of the divine arrangement rather than as an accident. The sūrah's readers in Makkah were being given a picture of a community deliberately constituted, generously provisioned, and still capable of failure.

**The Next Chapter of the Narrative**

The sūrah now turns to the city the Israelites were commanded to enter with humility, and the account of what they substituted for the words they had been given. The provision at the rock and the rebellion at the gate follow one another in the Book's sequence, and the sūrah's placement of them together is the comment it makes on the community's record.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:161

> **And when it was said unto them, “Settle in this town, and eat of that which is therein wheresoever you will, and say, ‘Remove the burden!’ And enter the gate prostrating, that We may forgive you your iniquities. We shall increase the virtuous.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Enter This City and Eat Freely"**

*Wa-idh qīla lahumu skunū hādhihi al-qaryata fa-kulū minhā ḥaythu shiʾtum wa-qūlū ḥiṭṭatun wa-dkhulū al-bāba sujjadan naghfir lakum khaṭīʾātikum sa-nazīdu al-muḥsinīn* — and ˹remember˺ when it was said to them: "Enter this city ˹of Jerusalem˺ and eat from wherever you please. Say, 'Absolve us,' and enter the gate with humility. We will forgive your sins, ˹and˺ We will multiply the reward for the good-doers."

**The Command in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Baqarah reports the same instruction almost word for word: *“And ˹remember˺ when We said, ‘Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, “Absolve us.” We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers’”* (2:58). The Book's repetition of the command fixes it as one of Israel's defining moments, and the sūrah's readers meet it here at the end of the wilderness narrative.

**The City and the Promised Land**

The entry the verse commands is the entry into the land the Qur'an's Moses had announced: *“O my people! Enter the Holy Land which Allah has destined for you ˹to enter˺. And do not turn back or else you will become losers”* (5:21). The sūrah's readers have just been given the twelve tribes and the provision in the desert; the command to enter belongs to the same sequence of gifts, and it is the moment the community had been prepared for.

**"Eat From Wherever You Please"**

The permission is unrestricted, and its generosity is the point. The Book's gift narratives consistently grant more than the recipient needs — the twelve springs, the manna and quails, and now a land whose produce is open to them — and the sūrah's readers are meant to measure the community's subsequent conduct against the scale of what it had been given.

**"Say: Absolve Us"**

*Ḥiṭṭatun* is a single word of petition, and the Book preserves it as a word rather than a prayer. The Qur'an's narratives of ritual frequently attach a specific formula to an act — the greeting, the testimony, the takbīr of the pilgrimage — and the sūrah's readers are being shown that the entry into a land was to be made with a sentence on the lips rather than with a claim of entitlement.

**"Enter the Gate With Humility"**

*Sujjadan* — bowing, prostrating — describes the posture of entry, and the Book's accounts of the same command elsewhere repeat the requirement: *“We raised the Mount over them ˹as a warning˺ for ˹breaking˺ their covenant and said, ‘Enter the gate ˹of Jerusalem˺ with humility’”* (4:154). A people who had crossed a sea on dry ground and been fed from the sky were to enter their inheritance on their knees.

**The Promise Attached**

*Naghfir lakum khaṭīʾātikum* — We will forgive your sins — and *sa-nazīdu al-muḥsinīn* — We will multiply the reward for the good-doers. The Book's promises of forgiveness are attached to acts of obedience, and the second clause attaches an increase to the quality of the act: the community is not merely pardoned but rewarded for the manner of its entry.

**The Formula the Sūrah's Readers Recognize**

The verse's instruction has been preserved in Islamic tradition as part of the vocabulary of repentance, and the Book's use of a specific word for the entry mirrors its practice elsewhere of giving the community a language for its turning. The Qur'an's repentance vocabulary — *istighfār*, *tawbah*, and here *ḥiṭṭah* — is consistently formulaic in this way.

**The Command and the Coming Rebellion**

The sūrah's readers have been told what the community actually did, and the next verse states it: the wrongdoers among them changed the words they had been commanded to say (7:162). The Book's arrangement of command and disobedience in consecutive verses is its standard method for showing how a community loses a gift, and the sūrah's narrative continues in this pattern.

**The Book's Geography of the Gift**

The city's identity has been discussed by the commentators, and the Qur'an's reference to a city the Israelites were commanded to enter with humility is connected in the tradition to the settlement in the Holy Land. What the sūrah emphasizes is not the location but the terms: a gift given, a posture required, and a word to be spoken.

**The Reward for Good-Doing Multiplied**

The verse's final clause introduces the doctrine the Qur'an states elsewhere in precise terms: *“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold”* (6:160). The sūrah's readers are being told that the entry into the land was not simply a political event but an opportunity for reward, and its conditions were a request for pardon and a bowed head.

**The Same Command for the Prophet's Community**

The Book's address to its own community uses the vocabulary of covenant and entry in its own way, and the command to remember the favour and the covenant when the believers said *“We hear and obey”* (5:7) is the parallel formula. The sūrah's narrative is thus instructive rather than merely historical: the terms on which a community receives a gift have not changed.

**What the Verse Assumes About the Community**

The command presupposes that the community can enter humbly — that its members are capable of the posture and the sentence. The Book's moral teaching consistently assumes that what God commands is within the servant's power: *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286). The failure that follows in the next verse is therefore a choice rather than a limitation.

**The Scene's Place in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The sūrah has moved from the sea to the mountain, from the calf to the delegation, from the tribes and the springs to the gate of the city. Each scene gives the community a gift and a command, and each is followed by a report of what the community did. Verse 161 is the last of the gifts before the final rebellion of this stretch of narrative, and the Book gives it the same patient detail as the others.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:162

> **Then those among them who did wrong substituted a word other than that which had been said unto them. So We sent down upon them a torment from heaven for the wrong they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Changed the Words They Were Commanded to Say"**

*Fa-baddala alladhīna ẓalamū minhum qawlan ghayra alladhī qīla lahum fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim rijzan mina al-samāʾi bimā kānū yaẓlimūn* — but the wrongdoers among them changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their wrongdoing.

**The Substitution**

The verse's verb, *baddala*, is the substitution of one thing for another, and the Book uses it for the alteration of revelation itself. The wrongdoers did not merely fail to say the formula of the previous verse; they replaced it with a different speech, and the sūrah names the act as the offense for which the punishment came down.

**The Charge Against the Earlier Communities**

The Qur'an's accusations against the People of the Book concentrate on this kind of alteration. *“Some Jews take words out of context and say, ‘We listen and we disobey’”* (4:46); *“They distorted the words of the Scripture and neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold”* (5:13); *“There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book — but it is not what the Book says”* (3:78). The sūrah's verse is the first entry in that dossier.

**The Formula They Were Given**

The word the community had been told to say was a request for pardon (7:161), and the tradition's reports of what was substituted describe a mocking parody of the phrase. The Book does not repeat the substitution, and its silence is a form of censure: the offense is named, the content of the offense is left to the reader's understanding of what happens when a community loses reverence.

**The Distinction Among the People**

The verse's phrase *the wrongdoers among them* repeats the sūrah's persistent care not to condemn a whole community for the acts of its members. The sentence immediately before it had reported a righteous group within the people of Moses (7:159) and a multiplied reward for the good-doers (7:161), and the Book's verdict here falls on the specific agents of the change.

**The Punishment From the Heavens**

*Rijz mina al-samāʾ* — a punishment from the heaven — is the Book's term for the affliction that came upon the Israelites in the wilderness, and the same word appears in the sūrah's account of the plagues of Egypt (7:134's *al-rijz*). The Qur'an's narratives of Israel and Egypt share the vocabulary because they share the diagnosis: a community that alters what it has been given is answered from above.

**The Word for Their Wrongdoing**

*Bimā kānū yaẓlimūn* — for the wrongdoing they persisted in — the imperfect verb marks a practice rather than an incident. The sūrah's earlier verse about the wilderness generation had said the same thing in almost the same words (7:160's *they wronged themselves*), and the Book's reading of the community's history is that the failures were habitual.

**Al-Baqarah's Version of the Same Scene**

Sūrat al-Baqarah reports the substitution and its consequence in one verse: *“But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness”* (2:59). The two sūrahs give the same sentence, which is the Book's way of fixing the event in the community's memory as a warning rather than a curiosity.

**The Violence Done by Speech**

The Book's treatment of the offense is worth noting: what provoked the punishment was the misuse of a word. The Qur'an attributes enormous weight to language — the Qur'an itself is a book of speech, the covenant is made in words, and the community's confession is a sentence — and the sūrah's narrative treats the alteration of a sacred formula as an act of rebellion rather than a lapse of taste.

**The Alteration and the Sūrah's Frame**

The sūrah's opening verses had described the revelation as a book whose signs are clear and whose verses are detailed; its second half has been documenting what communities do with clear signs. Verse 162 is the sharpest instance: the community was not confused about the words, it deliberately substituted others, and the substitution was measured against the instruction.

**The Consequence and the Mercy**

The verse's punishment is followed in the sūrah's narrative by further failures and by further mercy: the city by the sea, the Sabbath-breakers, the generations who inherited the Scripture and seized unlawful gains (7:169), and the warning of dispersion (7:168). The Book's account of Israel is a long record of substitutions and returns, and the punishment of verse 162 is one entry in a pattern that the sūrah declines to end.

**The Warning for the Sūrah's Readers**

The Prophet's community receives the same caution through the story: a formula that the community recites must be recited as given, and the Book's own insistence on precise transmission — the care with which the Qur'an's verses are established, the warning against altering its words — is the positive form of the sūrah's censure. The offense of verse 162 has an exact counterpart in the duty of the community receiving this recitation.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say the community abandoned the rite, the city, or the covenant. It says they changed the words, and the Book treats the change as sufficient to warrant the punishment. The sūrah's readers are shown a people still worshipping, still entering, and still belonging — and still under judgment, because the words were not theirs to alter.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:163

> **And ask them about the town that was by the sea, when they transgressed the Sabbath. Their fish would come to them, surfacing on the day of their Sabbath, but on the day when they did not observe the Sabbath, they would not come to them. Thus did We try them for their having been iniquitous.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Ask Them About the Town by the Sea"**

*Wa-sʾalhum ʿani al-qaryati allatī kānat ḥāḍirata al-baḥri idh yaʿdūna fī al-sabti idh taʾtīhim ḥītānuhum yawma sabtihim shurraʿan wa-yawma lā yasbitūna lā taʾtīhim kadhālika nablūhum bimā kānū yafsuqūn* — ask them ˹O Prophet˺ about ˹the people of˺ the town which was by the sea, who broke the Sabbath. During the Sabbath, ˹abundant˺ fish would come to them clearly visible, but on other days the fish were never seen. In this way We tested them for their rebelliousness.

**The Command to Ask**

*Wa-sʾalhum* — ask them — addresses the Prophet and turns the sūrah's narrative into an interrogation of the People of the Book standing in front of him. The Qur'an uses the same device elsewhere when it asks the Prophet to consult the People of the Book about what their scriptures contain, and the sūrah's readers are being shown that the story about to be told is one the addressed community knows.

**The Town by the Sea**

The Qur'an leaves the town unnamed, and the commentators identify it with a settlement on the shore whose people were tested by the Sabbath. The Book's narratives of the towns in this sūrah have been occupied with their crimes and their fates; this account's distinguishing feature is that the crime is a manipulation of the law rather than a rejection of it.

**The Fish That Came on the Sabbath**

*Taʾtīhim ḥītānuhum yawma sabtihim shurraʿan* — their fish came to them on their Sabbath day, plainly visible, and on the other days they did not appear. The sūrah presents the abundance as a test rather than a providence, and the Book's narratives frequently use provision as an examination: the manna in the desert and the plagues of Egypt were both gifts and trials.

**The Test Named Explicitly**

*Kadhālika nablūhum bimā kānū yafsuqūn* — in this way We tested them for their rebelliousness. The sūrah's readers have met the vocabulary of testing throughout: the covenant's terms, the calf, the delegation, the entry into the city. Here the Book states the mechanism in its plainest form — the abundance of the fish on the appointed day was the test itself, and the community's response to it revealed what it was.

**The Sabbath in the Qur'an**

The Book's teaching about the Sabbath appears in several places: it was a sign for a people who disputed, and the Qur'an's own verse in Sūrat al-Naḥl says *“˹Honouring˺ the Sabbath was ordained only for those who disputed about Abraham”* (16:124). Israel's obligations here are treated as a specific charge laid on a specific community, and the sūrah's account of its violation is presented as an affair between that community and its Lord.

**The Charge in the Other Accounts**

Sūrat al-Baqarah reports the episode and its sentence: *“You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, ‘Be disgraced apes!’”* (2:65), and Sūrat al-Nisāʾ includes the Sabbath breach in its account of the covenant's violation (4:154–155). The sūrah's readers are therefore hearing a story their own scriptures and the Qur'an share.

**The Manipulation of the Law**

The fish arrived on the day when fishing was forbidden and vanished on the days when it was permitted, which put the community's obedience on a permanent knife-edge. The Book's presentation of the test exposes the character of legalistic evasion: the community had the letter of a prohibition and immediately looked for a way around its intent, and the sūrah's narrative treats the innovation as rebellion rather than cleverness.

**The Sūrah's Vocabulary of Transgression**

*Yafsuqūn* — the same word the sūrah has used for those who broke their covenants (7:102) and for the wicked of Egypt (7:133) — closes the verse. The Book's moral vocabulary is consistent across its narratives: transgression is a departure from a commitment, and the sabbath-breakers had a covenant that made their cleverness a violation.

**The Warning Inside the Story**

For the Prophet's community, the account of a people who turned provision into a scheme is a standing caution about ritual obedience. The Book's legal material elsewhere works out the principle — the prohibition of legal fictions and the insistence on the intent of a law — and the sūrah's narrative supplies the founding illustration of the principle's opposite.

**The Scene's Economy**

The verse gives the setting, the test, and the verdict in a single sentence, and it leaves the town's fate to the following verses. The Book's narrator consistently spends fewer words on the mechanics of a sin than on its consequence, and this passage is a clear instance: three clauses for the offense, and the next stretch of narrative for the community's own arguments about it.

**What the Town Represents**

The sūrah's sequence of destroyed communities now includes a town whose offense was religious rather than political: no messenger was rejected, no guest was harmed, no scale was falsified. The community had a law, a provision, and a temptation, and it chose the temptation. The sūrah's readers are shown that the pattern of judgment extends to those who were inside the covenant rather than outside it.

**The Verse's Place in the Address**

The command to ask the People of the Book about this town follows the verse about the substitution of the words at the gate, and the two scenes answer each other: a community that altered holy speech and a community that outwitted a holy day. The sūrah's narrative of Israel is being assembled as a series of warnings with successive illustrations, and the town by the sea is the next installment.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:164

> **And when a community among them said, “Why do you admonish a people whom God is about to destroy or punish with a severe punishment?” They said, “As an excuse before your Lord, and that haply they may be reverent.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Why Do You Warn a People Whom Allah Will Destroy?"**

*Wa-idh qālat ummatun minhum lima taʿiẓūna qawman Allāhu muhlikuhum aw muʿadhdhibuhum ʿadhāban shadīdan qālū maʿdhiratan ilā rabbikum wa-laʿallahum yattaqūn* — when some of ˹the righteous among˺ them questioned ˹their fellow Sabbath-keepers˺: "Why do you ˹bother to˺ warn those ˹Sabbath-breakers˺ who will either be destroyed or severely punished by Allah?" They replied: "Just to be free from your Lord's blame, and so perhaps they may abstain."

**The Conversation Inside the Town**

The verse reports an argument among the townspeople, and it is the sūrah's most intimate scene of community life: some of them had been warning the offenders, and others questioned the point of the warnings. The Book's narratives rarely show the inner deliberation of a condemned town, and its decision to record this exchange makes the passage a study in collective responsibility.

**The Question's Resignation**

The questioners' premise is that the offenders are beyond reach: *Allāhu muhlikuhum aw muʿadhdhibuhum* — God will destroy them or punish them severely. Their position is not sympathy for the sin but doubt about the usefulness of speech, and the Book records it without rebuking them, which leaves the reader to weigh the argument.

**The Warners' Answer**

*Maʿdhiratan ilā rabbikum* — an excuse, a discharge of obligation, before your Lord. The warners state their ground plainly and without illusions: they do not expect conversion, they expect to be able to answer for their silence. The Book's institution of *al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf* — commanding good and forbidding evil — has this verse among its foundations, and the sūrah states the duty's limit as well as its basis.

**"So Perhaps They May Abstain"**

*Wa-laʿallahum yattaqūn* — and perhaps they may become mindful. The clause preserves hope as a possibility rather than a prediction, and the Book's grammar of *laʿalla* appears in such contexts throughout: the divine address itself uses the same particle when it states the purpose of signs and warnings (7:130, 7:168). The warners' position combines duty with a modest expectation, and the sūrah treats it as the correct one.

**The Qur'an's Insistence on Warning**

The Book repeatedly makes warning a communal obligation. *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil — it is they who will be successful”* (3:104); the believers are described as those who “encourage good and forbid evil” (9:112, 3:114); and the Qur'an's criticism of the People of the Book includes the failure to forbid one another: *“They did not forbid one another from doing evil. Evil indeed was what they did!”* (5:79).

**The Counter-Text in Al-Baqarah**

Sūrat al-Baqarah states the duty's inner contradiction in an address to the community: *“Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?”* (2:44), and Sūrat al-Ṣaff's warning is even sharper: *“O believers! Why do you say what you do not do? How despicable it is in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do!”* (61:2–3). The sūrah's warners are the model of the opposite posture — they speak and they are not themselves the offenders.

**The Command's Place in Islamic Law**

The tradition drew from this verse and its companions the doctrine that forbidding evil is obligatory where it can be done, with the warning that the one who forbids must be qualified to speak and must expect either amendment or at least the discharge of the duty. The Book's formulation here — excuse before God — is the basis of the reasoning that the obligation attaches to the act of warning rather than to its outcome.

**The Sūrah's Structure at This Point**

The verse explains why the warning had been given at all, and the next verse reports what happened when the warning was ignored. The sūrah's narrative therefore contains a complete anatomy of a community's collapse: a law, a temptation, an offense, a warning, a debate about the warning, and a judgment. The Book's readers are shown each stage, and the judgment is reserved for the end.

**The Warning That Was Not Heeded**

The verse's questioners were right about one thing: the offenders would not stop. The next verse states that the warnings were ignored and that the community was overtaken. The Book's characteristic honesty about the limits of preaching is on display — the warners did their duty, and the outcome was what it was, and the sūrah treats both facts without sentimentality.

**Who the "Ummah" Were**

The translation's *some of them* renders *ummatun minhum* — a group within the town — and the Book's narrations of Israel consistently distinguish parties within the people: the righteous remnant of verse 159, the wrongdoers of verse 162, and here the warners and their questioners. The sūrah's account of Israel is social rather than monolithic, and the Book's readers are meant to see a community making choices inside itself.

**The Verse's Instruction for the Prophet's Community**

The Prophet's community receives the same charge in the Qur'an's own words: the community is to enjoin the good and forbid the evil, and its failure in that duty is treated as a mark of the earlier communities' decline. The sūrah's scene supplies the pattern and the caution: warning is owed even when it will not be accepted, and the one who warns is not responsible for the response.

**The Two Positions in the Verse**

The Book gives both sides their sentences and declines to referee between them, which is a striking narrative choice. The questioners do not endorse the sin, and the warners do not guarantee the result; the argument is about whether speech is worth making when the outcome is fixed. The sūrah's answer is contained in the sequel: the warners were saved and the offenders were not (7:165).

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:165

> **When those who were violating the Sabbath forgot that whereof they had been reminded by those who admonished them, God punished the violators and saved those who forbade evil—that is, by exhorting their fellow Israelites to observe the Sabbath.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Rescued Those Who Warned Against Evil"**

*Fa-lammā nasū mā dhukkirū bihi anjaynā alladhīna yanhawna ʿani al-sūʾi wa-akhadhnā alladhīna ẓalamū bi-ʿadhābin baʾīsin bimā kānū yafsuqūn* — when they ignored the warning they were given, We rescued those who used to warn against evil and overtook the wrongdoers with a dreadful punishment for their rebelliousness.

**The Two Outcomes in One Verse**

The Book's habit of pairing rescue and punishment in a single sentence appears here in its plainest form. The verse distinguishes the two groups by their conduct: those who warned, and those who wronged. The sūrah's readers had watched the argument of the previous verse about whether warning was worth the effort, and the answer is now given as a verdict.

**"When They Ignored the Warning"**

*Nasū mā dhukkirū bihi* — the verb the sūrah uses is forgetting, and the Book's use of it for the rejection of reminders is a consistent theme: the sūrah's own charge against the Sabbath-breakers is *being heedless of them* (7:136's clause about the Egyptians), and the Qur'an's warnings about forgetting the reminder recur throughout. The verse's grammar marks a settled refusal rather than a single lapse.

**The Rescue of the Warners**

*Anjaynā alladhīna yanhawna ʿani al-sūʾ* — We rescued those who used to forbid evil. The Book's narratives of destroyed towns frequently report a saved party, and the sūrah's readers have already met the pattern in the account of the towns whose righteous were brought out (11:81's account of Lot's household) and in the sūrah's own statement that there was a group among the people of Moses who guided with truth (7:159).

**The Punishment's Word**

*Bi-ʿadhābin baʾīs* — a dreadful, miserable punishment — and the closing clause, *bimā kānū yafsuqūn*, for their rebelliousness. The sūrah's diagnosis has not changed since the town was introduced: the offense is transgression against a covenant, and the punishment is named as the consequence of the same conduct the fish were sent to test (7:163).

**The Warners' Reward**

The verse does not describe the warners' reward beyond rescue, and the Book's restraint here is characteristic. Its narratives of the saved frequently leave their future undescribed, and the Qur'an's promises for those who enjoin good are stated elsewhere: the community that calls to goodness is described as successful (3:104), and the sūrah's own promise of multiplied reward for the good-doers (7:161) covers the same ground.

**The Warning's Vindication**

The argument of verse 164 is now settled: the questioners had doubted whether warning could prevent anything, and the verse shows that the warning did not prevent the punishment but did distinguish its speakers. The Book's doctrine is thus not that preaching saves a community but that it marks a person's position within it, and the sūrah treats the marking as decisive.

**The Book's Pattern of Selective Rescue**

The Qur'an frequently describes a community's destruction in terms that exempt a party: Noah's family and the believers with him, Lot's household, the people of Jonah, and here the warners of the town by the sea. The pattern is one of the Book's most consistent narrative features, and it serves the doctrine that judgment in the Qur'an is individual even when it arrives collectively.

**The Sentence's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

The sūrah has been building a case about the relation between signs, warnings, and outcomes since its account of the towns in the first half. Verse 165 supplies the conclusion of that case at the level of a single community: the signs were given, the warnings were given, the offenders persisted, and the outcome was exact. The Book's readers are given the anatomy in miniature before the sūrah moves on to the dispersion of the people in the next chapters of their history.

**What the Verse Tells Its Readers About Speech**

The verse is among the Qur'an's most direct statements that the act of enjoining good is not wasted even when it fails to persuade. The saved party in this narrative is identified solely by the fact that it had warned, and the Book's later instruction to the believing community is built on that identification. What a community says, in the sūrah's account, is part of what it is.

**The Judgment and the Sūrah's Restraint**

The sūrah reports the community's destruction in a clause and does not describe the punishment's form; the accounts of the tradition supply what the reader might imagine. The Book's narratives of destroyed towns in this sūrah have been similarly spare, and the restraint keeps the reader's attention on the moral sequence rather than on the mechanics of the penalty.

**The Verse's Continued Instruction**

Muslim scholars used this passage, with the surrounding verses, to establish the obligation of forbidding wrong and to describe its conditions: the duty is on those able to perform it, the offender may not be beyond reach, and the aim is amendment rather than the discharge of anger. The sūrah's own narrative supplies both the duty and its ethos in two verses, and the tradition read them accordingly.

**The Turn to Judgment**

The next verse reports the sentence pronounced on the offenders, and it is the Book's shortest form of a verdict on the community: three words and a decree. The sūrah's readers have been given the town's setting, its test, its argument, and its rescue, and the final step in the sequence is the judgment itself, stated in the imperative of the next verse.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:166

> **Be you apes, outcast,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Be Disgraced Apes"**

*Fa-lammā ʿataw ʿan mā nuhū ʿanhu qulnā lahum kūnū qiradatan khāsiʾīn* — but when they stubbornly persisted in violation, We said to them: "Be disgraced apes!"

**The Verdict's Form**

The punishment is announced as a command rather than a description: *be*. The Book's uses of the imperative for transformation echo its language of creation — *kun fa-yakūn*, be, and it is — and the effect is to present the metamorphosis as an instantaneous act of divine authority rather than a process. The sūrah's readers are shown a decree whose brevity matches its finality.

**The Same Sentence in the Other Accounts**

Sūrat al-Baqarah reports it identically: *“You are already aware of those of you who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, ‘Be disgraced apes!’”* (2:65). Sūrat al-Māʾidah's catalogue of the cursed among the Children of Israel names those who incurred condemnation and were turned into apes and pigs (5:60), and Sūrat al-Baqarah's next verse states the purpose: *“So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing”* (2:66).

**The Word for Disgrace**

*Khāsiʾīn* — driven away, abased — describes the state of an animal that is cursed and expelled, and the Book attaches the same participle to Iblīs's expulsion earlier in this sūrah: *madhʾūman madḥūran* in the account of his sentence (7:13), and the Qur'an's vocabulary of abasement is used consistently for those who overstep. The punishment thus matches the offense in the sūrah's own lexicon.

**What the Metamorphosis Means**

The Book presents the transformation as a divine sign rather than a natural event, and the tradition understood the apes here to be a literal punishment whose recipients did not reproduce. The sūrah's interest is not in the biology but in the verdict: a community whose conduct had become bestial was answered in kind, and the Book's narratives of punishment repeatedly take this form.

**The Charge of Persistence**

*Fa-lammā ʿataw ʿan mā nuhū ʿanhu* — when they persisted in what they were forbidden. The Book's word *ʿataw* is obstinacy, and the verse's opening clause establishes that every stage before the verdict had been completed: the law, the temptation, the warnings, the argument, and the refusal. The punishment is not the response to a surprise offense but to a settled course.

**The Example for Later Generations**

Sūrat al-Baqarah's commentary names the purpose of the punishment in words the sūrah's readers are meant to carry: an example to those present and those after them, and a lesson to the God-fearing (2:66). The Book's narratives of destroyed towns all serve that function in the sūrah, and the metamorphosis is its most vivid instance.

**The Meaning of the Curse**

The Qur'an's other reference to the transformation places it inside a catalogue of the condemned: *“Say, ˹O Prophet˺, ‘Shall I inform you of those who deserve a worse punishment from Allah ˹than the rebellious˺? It is those who earned Allah's condemnation and displeasure—some being reduced to apes and pigs and worshippers of false gods. These are far worse in rank and farther astray from the Right Way.’”* (5:60). The Book's formulation there and here treats the punishment as the visible form of a condition the community had already chosen.

**The Sūrah's Diagnosis Repeated**

The verse closes the account of the town by the sea with the same word the sūrah has used for the wicked of Egypt (7:133's *mujrimīn*) and for the offenders at the gate (7:162's *yaẓlimūn*). The Book's moral vocabulary in this sūrah is deliberately repetitive, and the sabbath-breakers are filed in the same drawer as the other communities whose conduct had made judgment necessary.

**The Community and Its Parties**

The verse's subject is the offenders, and the sūrah's readers know from the previous verse that the warners were rescued. The Book's narrative thus ends the episode with the town divided by the verdict: a group rescued for its speech and a group transformed for its conduct. The distinction is one the Qur'an applies in every one of its accounts of destroyed peoples.

**What the Verse Says About the Law**

The punishment's severity for a crime of legal evasion is the sūrah's statement about the gravity of tampering with divine commandments. The Book's narratives and its legal material alike treat the alteration of what God has established as an offense against the community's own foundation, and the verse's brief decree carries the weight of that doctrine.

**The Contrast With Mercy**

The sūrah's closing verses return to the widest language of mercy — the names of God, the group that guides with truth (7:180–181), and the call to seek refuge (7:200) — and the metamorphosis of verse 166 stands inside that context. The Book's arrangement of judgment and mercy in a single sūrah is its standing method, and the reader of this passage is given both accounts of the same community.

**The Verse's Instruction for the Prophet's Community**

The Prophet's community hears the story as a caution about its own law: the same Book that reports the punishment of the sabbath-breakers commands its readers to observe what has been given and warns them against the devices that turn obligation into evasion. The sūrah's account of the town by the sea is thus not a curiosity of Israel's history but a statement of the terms on which every community holds its covenant.

The metamorphosis is also the sūrah's comment on what a community becomes when its cleverness replaces its obedience: the Book names the result with an image rather than an argument, and it leaves the reader to draw the parallel between the shape of the punishment and the shape of the conduct.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:167

> **and Thy Lord proclaimed that He will send against them, until the Day of Resurrection, evil announcers. He will pour His Wrath over their heads; He will make them apes and pigs, and cast them into error. And he changed those who had wronged themselves, and He changed not that whereof they used to be.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He Would Send Against Them Others Until the Day of Judgment"**

*Wa-idh taʾadhana rabbuka la-yabʿathanna ʿalayhim ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati man yasūmuhum sūʾa al-ʿadhābi inna rabbaka la-sarīʿu al-ʿiqābi wa-innahu la-ghafūrun raḥīm* — and ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when your Lord declared that He would send against them others who would make them suffer terribly until the Day of Judgment. Indeed, your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**The Declaration**

*Taʾadhana* — the Book's verb for a solemn announcement — opens a sentence that runs to the end of history. The verse is the sūrah's account of a decree binding on the people of Israel until the Day of Judgment, and its placement after the punishment of the town by the sea extends the narrative's scope from a single community to a people's political fate.

**The Same Declaration in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ**

Sūrat al-Isrāʾ gives the decree its historical form: *“And We warned the Children of Israel in the Scripture, ‘You will certainly cause corruption in the land twice, and you will become extremely arrogant’”* (17:4), followed by the account of the two visitations — the servants of great might who ravaged their homes (17:5), the restoration of their power after repentance (17:6), and the second warning with its consequences (17:7). The sūrah's verse is the same decree stated as a permanent condition.

**Dispersion as the Decree's Shape**

The next verse reports the consequence: *“We dispersed them through the land in groups”* (7:168). The tradition read the two verses together as the Qur'an's account of Israel's exile and continuing subjection, and the Book's own narrative of the people from this point is a narrative of communities scattered among nations rather than a kingdom with a capital.

**The Qur'an's Earlier Statements About Their Fate**

The sūrah's readers have met the theme before: the Qur'an says of the Children of Israel that they were struck with disgrace and drew wrath upon themselves (2:61), and Sūrat Āl ʿImrān's account of their condition — *“They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people”* (3:112) — states the same law and adds its exception.

**Swift in Punishment, Forgiving and Merciful**

The verse closes with the Book's paired names, and the pairing is the sūrah's own refrain for the destruction narratives: *swift in punishment* and *All-Forgiving, Most Merciful*. The Qur'an places the two attributes side by side in this way repeatedly — *“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and that My torment is indeed the most painful”* (15:49–50) — and the sūrah declines to leave either attribute alone with the reader.

**The Decree and Human Agency**

The verse's sentence attributes the persecution that will befall the community to God's declaration, and the narrative's other accounts fill in the human actors. The Qur'an's theology of history consistently holds both: the decree is God's, and the agents are nations whose rise and fall the Book reports in the same breath. The sūrah's readers are meant to see the two levels as one event.

**The Pattern for the Prophet's Community**

The Book's address to its own community uses the same framework for its own fortunes, and the sūrah's later verses state the general law: a community's condition changes with its conduct, and the change is not a matter of chance. The verse's decree about Israel is thus the specific case of a rule the sūrah has already applied to Egypt and to the towns.

**Why the Sūrah Records the Decree**

The verse's function in the passage is twofold. It answers the reader's natural question about what happened to the people whose record has just been recited, and it removes the temptation to read their history as a series of accidents. The Book's answer is a decree that runs to the Day of Judgment, with mercy named in the same sentence.

**The Hope in the Second Half**

The verse's final clause prevents the decree from functioning as a sentence of despair. The sūrah's following verses report the generations who inherited the Scripture, seized unlawful gains, and claimed forgiveness (7:169), and they also report those who firmly abide by the Book (7:170). The sūrah's account of Israel ends, as it has throughout, with both a warning and an opening.

**The Book's Language of Duration**

*Ilā yawmi al-qiyāmah* — until the Day of Judgment — is the Book's most extended temporal formula, and it is used for the conditions the Qur'an describes as permanent within history. The sūrah's readers are being told that the decree they are hearing is not a phase but a standing feature of the world they live in, and the narrative's later verses continue inside that frame.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Address to the People of the Book**

The Prophet's contemporaries among the People of the Book are the audience of the sūrah's later chapters, and the verse supplies the framework for that address: a people under a decree who nevertheless retain the Book, the covenant, and the possibility of return. The Qur'an's engagement with them is consistently conducted on those terms.

**The Two Names and the Sūrah's Balance**

The names at the verse's end belong to the sūrah's recurring pairs, and their presence here — in a sentence about persecution until the end of time — is the Book's characteristic refusal to state a judgment without also stating the mercy that surrounds it. The next verse reports the dispersion, and the one after it reports the community's response.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:168

> **And We divided them in this land into communities. Among them are the righteous, and among them are otherwise. And We tried them with good things and evil things, that perhaps they might turn [to Us].**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Dispersed Them Through the Land in Groups"**

*Wa-qaṭṭaʿnāhum fī al-arḍi umaman minhumu al-ṣāliḥūna wa-minhum dūna dhālika wa-balawnāhum bi-l-ḥasanāti wa-l-sayyiʾāti laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — We dispersed them through the land in groups — some were righteous, others were less so. We tested them with prosperity and adversity, so perhaps they would return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**The Dispersion**

*Qaṭṭaʿnāhum fī al-arḍi umaman* — the verb is the same one the sūrah used for the division into tribes (7:160), and its repetition here marks a change of scale: the community is now scattered among nations rather than settled as a single people. The Qur'an's account of Israel's history after the kingdom is a history of communities, and the verse states it as the consequence of the decree in the previous sentence.

**The Two Kinds of People**

*Minhumu al-ṣāliḥūna wa-minhum dūna dhālika* — some righteous, others less so. The Book's insistence on the mixed character of Israel appears here again, and it has accompanied every stage of the narrative: the group that guides with truth (7:159), the wrongdoers who changed the words (7:162), the warners and their questioners (7:164), and now the dispersed communities.

**Testing as the Divine Method**

*Wa-balawnāhum bi-l-ḥasanāti wa-l-sayyiʾāt* — We tested them with good and evil. The sūrah has stated this law in general terms earlier in the same narrative — *“We afflicted its ˹disbelieving˺ people with suffering and adversity, so perhaps they would be humbled”* (7:94), and the exchange of hardship for prosperity that followed (7:95) — and the verse applies it to the scattered people. The Book's doctrine of history is a doctrine of examination.

**The Purpose Clause**

*Laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — so perhaps they would return. The sūrah's uses of the same clause cluster around its narratives of punishment and provision (7:130, 7:174), and the Book's grammar keeps the outcome open. The verse does not say the test succeeded or failed; it states the intent, and the sūrah's following verses supply the record.

**Prosperity and Adversity in the Book's Vocabulary**

*Al-ḥasanāt* and *al-sayyiʾāt* here mean good circumstances and bad, not good and evil deeds, and the Qur'an's use of the pair for both senses is a deliberate economy: a community's conditions are described in the moral vocabulary the Book applies to actions. The point is that circumstances, like deeds, come from the same hand and serve the same purpose.

**The Doctrine of Nations in the Qur'an**

The Book states the laws of communal rise and fall in several sūrahs: *“Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺”* (13:11); *“Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed. And He pardons much”* (42:30); *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done”* (30:41). The sūrah's verse is that doctrine in narrative form.

**Succession and Its Conditions**

The Qur'an's statements about the inheritance of the earth attach it to conduct: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you”* (6:165); *“Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act”* (10:14); *“My righteous servants shall inherit the land”* (21:105). The sūrah's readers are told that Israel's dispersion is the reverse image of the same law.

**The Sūrah's Refusal of Ethnic History**

The verse treats the people of Israel as a case within a general pattern rather than as a unique subject. The Book's histories of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Madyan, and Egypt were told for the same reason, and the sūrah's own addresses to the Prophet's community repeat the warning. What the Book offers its readers is a law of communities, and Israel is one of its clearest demonstrations.

**The Remnant Within the Dispersed People**

The verse's opening clause about the righteous among the dispersed answers the sūrah's earlier statement that there are some among the people of Moses who guide with the truth (7:159). The two verses frame the narrative of verse 161–166: the community has a righteous group even when it is scattered, and the Book's addresses to it elsewhere in the Qur'an are addressed to that group and to the covenant it carries.

**The Test's Instruments**

The Book's use of prosperity as a test is as prominent as its use of adversity, and the sūrah's readers have already watched Egypt misread its good fortune as a verdict on its own merit (7:131). The dispersion's test comprises both conditions, and the Qur'an's statements about wealth and ease as dangers belong to the same teaching.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

The verse gathers the sūrah's themes: the scattering of a people, the mixture of righteous and otherwise within it, the testing by circumstances, and the hope of return. Its position between the decree of the previous verse and the account of the generations who inherited the Scripture (7:169) gives the passage its shape: judgment, dispersion, test, and then the record of what the generations did with what they had.

**The Clause That Keeps the Door Open**

*Laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* is the verse's final word, and it is a statement of intention rather than prediction. The sūrah's account of Israel is a long record of returns and relapses, and the Book's habit of stating the purpose of its tests keeps the narrative from closing. The readers of the sūrah, receiving the history in their own recitation, are addressed by the same clause.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:169

> **Then We succeeded them with another generation. And the generation who succeeded them grasped the ephemeralities of this lower world and said, “We shall surely be forgiven.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Generations Who Inherited the Scripture"**

*Fa-khalafa min baʿdihim khalfun warithū al-kitāba yaʾkhudhūna ʿaraḍa hādhā al-adnā wa-yaqūlūna sa-yughfaru lanā wa-in yaʾtihim ʿaraḍun mithluhu yaʾkhudhūhu a-lam yuʾkhadh ʿalayhim mīthāqu al-kitābi an lā yaqūlū ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa wa-darasū mā fīhi wa-l-dāru al-ākhiratu khayrun li-lladhīna yattaqūn* — then they were succeeded by other generations who inherited the Scripture. They indulged in unlawful gains, claiming: "We will be forgiven." And if similar gain came their way, they would seize it. Was a covenant not taken from them in the Scripture that they would not say anything about Allah except the truth? And they were already well-versed in its teachings. But the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful ˹of Allah˺.

**The Succession of Generations**

*Khalafa min baʿdihim khalfun warithū al-kitāba* — the Book's word for a successor generation is used elsewhere for the heirs of a land (7:100) and for the generations that came after the messengers. The verse's subject is therefore not a break in transmission but a decay within it: the Scripture was inherited, read, and taught, and the life around it was arranged differently.

**The Unlawful Gains**

*Yaʾkhudhūna ʿaraḍa hādhā al-adnā* — they take the passing goods of this lower world. The Qur'an uses the phrase for what the earlier communities traded their covenants for, and the Book's accusations against them are consistently financial in this sūrah: Madyan's scales (7:85), the town's fish (7:163), and now the inherited Book's revenue.

**The Claim of Automatic Forgiveness**

*Sa-yughfaru lanā* — we will be forgiven — is the sentence the sūrah puts at the center of its charge. The Book's condemnations of the People of the Book include this expectation in almost the same words: *“˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, ‘The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days’”* (2:80), and *“This is because they say, ‘The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.’ They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying”* (3:24).

**The Second Seizure**

*Wa-in yaʾtihim ʿaraḍun mithluhu yaʾkhudhūhu* — and if a similar gain comes their way, they take it again. The clause exposes the claim's function: the expectation of forgiveness was not a hope but a license, and the verse's repetition of the taking shows it operating as one. The Book's analysis of such communities is that their religion had become insurance rather than obligation.

**The Covenant About Speech**

*An lā yaqūlū ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa* — that they would not say about Allah anything but the truth. The Book's covenant passages include this clause among their terms, and the sūrah's readers have already met the offense in its narrative form: the substitution of the words at the gate (7:162) and the distortion of the Scripture with the tongue (3:78). The verse's charge is that the inherited Book was used to authorize what it forbade.

**"Well-Versed in Its Teachings"**

*Wa-darasū mā fīhi* — they studied what was in it. The clause removes the excuse of ignorance, and it is the Book's standard technique in its accusations: the offense is named and the knowledge is established in the same sentence. Sūrat al-Baqarah's reproach to those who preach righteousness and do not practice it uses the same move (2:44).

**The Woe Pronounced Elsewhere**

The Qur'an's condemnation of this commerce is stated in its sharpest form in Sūrat al-Baqarah: *“So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, ‘This is from Allah’ — seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written and woe to them for what they have earned”* (2:79). The sūrah's verse is the same charge in narrative dress, and it names the market that the Book's scribes had entered.

**The Book's Threat to the Same Offense**

Sūrat al-Baqarah states the consequence with the same vocabulary: *“Indeed, those who hide Allah's revelations, trading them for a fleeting gain consume nothing but fire into their bellies. Allah will neither speak to them on the Day of Judgment, nor will He purify them; and they will suffer a painful punishment”* (2:174). The sūrah's clause about the Hereafter being better for the mindful is the positive form of the same contrast.

**The Home of the Hereafter**

*Wa-l-dāru al-ākhiratu khayrun li-lladhīna yattaqūn* — the sentence closes the verse by shifting the calculation. The generations had been weighing this world's gains against the Hereafter's, and the sūrah states the true exchange in a clause. The Book's use of the comparison is consistent, and its rhetoric is aimed at the scale of values rather than at the wealth itself.

**The Rulers Who Looked Away**

The sūrah's account is completed by Sūrat al-Māʾidah's parallel charge, which names the scholars' silence: *“You see many of them racing towards sin, transgression, and consumption of forbidden gain. Evil indeed are their actions! Why do their rabbis and scholars not forbid them from saying what is sinful and consuming what is unlawful? Evil indeed is their inaction!”* (5:62–63). The inherited Scripture in this account is not merely the Book in the hands of the unworthy; it is the Book in the hands of teachers who did not speak.

**The Verse's Continuing Address**

The sūrah's verse ends with *will you not then understand?* — a question to the addressed community — and its audience includes the Prophet's own followers as much as the People of the Book. The Book's warnings about the sale of religion are among its most repeated themes, and the Muslim community's own tradition read this passage as a caution about its own scholars and markets.

**The Turn to the Faithful**

The next verse answers the record of the generations with a statement about those who hold fast to the Book: *“As for those who firmly abide by the Scripture and establish prayer — surely We never discount the reward of those acting righteously”* (7:170). The sūrah's method in this stretch is to state both the failure and the exception, and its reader is given the two together.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:170

> **As for those who cling to the Book and perform the prayer—surely We neglect not the reward of the workers of righteousness.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Those Who Firmly Abide by the Scripture"**

*Wa-alladhīna yumassikūna bi-l-kitābi wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta innā lā nuḍīʿu ajra al-muṣliḥīn* — as for those who firmly abide by the Scripture and establish prayer — surely We never discount the reward of those acting righteously.

**The Verse's Function**

The sūrah has just described the generations who inherited the Scripture and used it as a cover for unlawful gain (7:169). The verse that follows names the exception, and its position is the Book's familiar arrangement: an indictment of a class, followed by the statement that the class is not the whole. The sūrah's readers have met the same move in its acknowledgement of the righteous group among the people of Moses (7:159).

**"Firmly Abide"**

*Yumassikūna* — the verb's form intensifies the holding, and the Book uses the root for grasping a covenant and for clinging to revelation. The Qur'an's description of those who truly believe in the Book is stated with the same idea: *“Those We have given the Book follow it as it should be followed. It is they who ˹truly˺ believe in it”* (2:121). The sūrah's readers are being told that the inherited Book can be held as a trust rather than used as a possession.

**The Second Clause: Establishing Prayer**

*Wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta* — and they establish the prayer. The Qur'an's standard pairing of scripture with prayer appears throughout its descriptions of the righteous, and its use here is deliberate: the test of the community that inherited a book is not its mastery of texts but the worship that the texts require. The Book's vocabulary of *iqāmah* — establishing rather than merely performing — adds the note of regularity and institutional care.

**The Parallel in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān**

The Qur'an's fullest description of the righteous among the People of the Book makes the same pairing and adds the community's public duties: *“Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah's revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺. They believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good”* (3:113–114). The verses that follow state the promise: *“They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done”* (3:115).

**"We Never Discount the Reward"**

*Innā lā nuḍīʿu ajra al-muṣliḥīn* — the sentence is a divine guarantee in the form of a negative, and the Book states it repeatedly: *“As for those who believe and do good, We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds”* (18:30). The sūrah's readers are being told that the qualifications of lineage and history do not enter the accounting, and that the reward follows the conduct.

**The Word for "Reformers"**

The verse's final noun, *al-muṣliḥīn* — those who act rightly, who put things right — echoes the sūrah's earlier use of the same root in Moses' charge to Aaron: *do what is right, and do not follow the way of the corruptors* (7:142). The Book's vocabulary pairs reform with corruption throughout, and the verse identifies the faithful among the People of the Book with the first of the two.

**The Reward's Universality**

The Qur'an's statements of reward are stated in terms of deeds rather than identity: *“But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord”* (2:112); *“But those who do good — whether male or female — and have faith will enter Paradise and will never be wronged ˹even as much as˺ the speck on a date stone”* (4:124); *“Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (2:277). The sūrah's verse belongs to that set.

**The Sūrah's Refusal of Two Errors**

Read with the preceding verse, the passage refuses the conclusion that a corrupted tradition disqualifies everyone inside it, and it refuses the conclusion that inheritance guarantees anyone anything. The Book's criterion is stated in the verse as conduct, and the sūrah's readers are left with a standard that applies to their own community as directly as to the people of Moses.

**What the Muslim Community Heard in the Verse**

The passage was read by the tradition as a warning and a comfort at once: the Book is inherited by generations, and the reward belongs to those who hold it and pray. The Qur'an's address to its own community uses the same warning about the sale of revelation and the same promise about holding it, and the sūrah's readers were meant to see themselves in the verse's description.

**The Book's Positive Account of Piety**

The verse's brevity gives it the force of a definition: to hold the Book and to establish prayer is the whole of the life the sūrah is describing. The Qur'an's other statements of the same content add the social duties — alms, good conduct, forbidding evil — and the sūrah's own earlier answer to the prayer of Moses had listed three marks of the recipients of mercy: shunning evil, paying alms, and believing in the revelations (7:156).

**The Contrast With the Generations**

The verse answers the previous one point by point: where the earlier generations took unlawful gain, these hold fast; where the others neglected what they had studied, these establish prayer; where the others claimed forgiveness without warrant, these are promised a reward that is not discounted. The sūrah's editor arranged the two verses as a diptych, and the reader is given the pair.

**The Verse's Continuing Address**

The sūrah's next verse returns to a scene from Israel's history — the mountain raised over the people at the covenant — and the placement is significant: the promise to the faithful is followed immediately by the account of what the covenant required. The Book's order here, as throughout the passage, is mercy and obligation together, and the reader of the sūrah is given both in consecutive sentences.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:171

> **And when We made the Mount rise above them as a threat, and We said unto them, “Take hold of that which We have given you with strength, and remember it that perhaps you may become reverent.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Raised the Mountain Over Them"**

*Wa-idh nataqnā al-jabala fawqahum ka-annahu ẓullatun wa-ẓannū annahu wāqiʿun bihim khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwatin wa-dhkurū mā fīhi laʿallakum tattaqūn* — and ˹remember˺ when We raised the mountain over them as if it were a cloud and they thought it would fall on them. ˹We said,˺ "Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺."

**The Scene's Imagery**

The mountain is described as a *ẓullah* — a canopy, a shade — and the people's assumption is that it is about to fall. The Book's narrative of the covenant at Sinai uses the image of the mountain's suspension in several places, and Sūrat al-Baqarah's account is the most compact: *“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, ‘Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you’”* (2:63).

**The Same Scene in the Other Covenant Accounts**

Sūrat al-Baqarah's second account adds the community's answer: *“they answered, ‘We hear and disobey’”* (2:93), and Sūrat al-Nisāʾ reports the same event as a warning attached to the breaking of the covenant: *“We raised the Mount over them ˹as a warning˺ for ˹breaking˺ their covenant and said, ‘Enter the gate ˹of Jerusalem˺ with humility’”* (4:154). The sūrah's readers are therefore hearing a scene their own Book reports three times, each with a different emphasis.

**What the Mountain Signified**

The Book does not present the raised mountain as a threat for its own sake; it presents it as the visible form of the covenant's weight. The sūrah's earlier account of the same mountain had described Moses' request to see God and the leveling of the peak to dust (7:143), and the Qur'an's use of the mountain as an image of awe runs through its revelation theology — *“Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah”* (59:21).

**The Command Repeated**

*Khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwatin* — hold firmly to what We have given you — the same charge given to Moses on the mountain (7:144–145) is now given to the community. The Book's language of reception is consistent: what is revealed is to be grasped, and the grasping is a matter of strength rather than sentiment.

**"Observe Its Teachings"**

*Wa-dhkurū mā fīhi* — remember what is in it — the command the sūrah's Israel has been failing since the narrative began. The Book's repeated charge to the People of the Book about remembering the covenant appears in Sūrat al-Māʾidah with the community's own formula of acceptance: *“Remember Allah's favour upon you and the covenant He made with you when you said, ‘We hear and obey’”* (5:7).

**The Purpose Clause Again**

*Laʿallakum tattaqūn* — so that you may become mindful. The sūrah has used the same clause for the towns' testing (7:94's *humbled*), for the plagues of Egypt (7:130), and for the dispersion of Israel (7:168). Its repetition in this verse closes the sūrah's account of the covenant with the same stated purpose: the entire sequence of signs, gifts, warnings, and punishments exists so that a community might become mindful.

**The Covenant's Solemnity**

The verse's detail — the mountain suspended, the people expecting it to fall — gives the covenant a physical solemnity that no verbal formula could supply. The Book's narratives of covenant frequently attach a sign to the undertaking, and the sūrah's readers are being shown that the agreement they are being reminded of was made under conditions they could not mistake.

**The Community That Received It**

The Israelites at the mountain had already seen the sea split, the water from the rock, and the manna; the covenant was not given to strangers. The Book's account of their failure is therefore an account of the failure of a formed community, and the sūrah's readers — who have followed the same signs in the same narrative — are being addressed by the same charge.

**The Book's Theology of Awe**

The Qur'an's treatment of awe (*taqwā*, *khashyah*) is not fear of arbitrary power but the response of a creature to its Creator's magnitude, and the raised mountain is the Book's visual statement of the proportion. The verse's command to hold and remember follows directly from the scene, which is why the sūrah places the admonition inside the description rather than after it.

**What the Verse Adds to the Sūrah's Case**

The sūrah has been arguing that the covenant's terms were clear, the signs abundant, and the community's failures deliberate. The raised mountain is the final element of that case: the terms were accepted under conditions that left no room for misunderstanding. The Quran's readers, receiving the story, are warned about the equivalent conditions in their own lives.

**The Following Verse's Change of Subject**

The sūrah moves from the mountain to the primordial covenant, and the two scenes are placed together because they are the Book's two accounts of the same fact: the human being's acknowledgment of God's lordship. The verse about the descendants of Adam (7:172) follows immediately, and the passage reads as a single argument about responsibility.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:172

> **And when your Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them bear witness of themselves—“Am I not your Lord?” They said, “Yes, we testify”—so that you may not say on the Day of Resurrection, “Indeed, we were unaware of this.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Am I Not Your Lord?"**

*Wa-idh akhadha rabbuka min banī Ādama min ẓuhūrihim dhurriyyatahum wa-ashhadahum ʿalā anfusihim a-lastu bi-rabbikum qālū balā shahidnā an taqūlū yawma al-qiyāmati innā kunnā ʿan hādhā ghāfilīn* — and ˹remember˺ when your Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and had them testify regarding themselves. ˹Allah asked,˺ "Am I not your Lord?" They replied: "Yes, You are! We testify." ˹He cautioned,˺ "Now you have no right to say on Judgment Day, 'We were not aware of this.'"

**The Primordial Testimony**

The verse describes an event before history: every human being brought forth and made to testify to God's lordship. The Quran's doctrine of the covenant of *alast* — named after the question's opening — has been one of the most discussed passages in Islamic theology, and its function in the sūrah is to establish that the knowledge of God is not an external imposition on a creature that would otherwise be without it.

**The Self as Witness**

*Wa-ashhadahum ʿalā anfusihim* — He made them testify against themselves. The Book's technique in this passage is to make the witness interior: the testimony is not extracted by an external sign or a messenger but by the creature's own existence. The Qur'an uses the same structure in Sūrat al-Rūm: *“the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people”* (30:30).

**The Question and the Answer**

*A-lastu bi-rabbikum* — am I not your Lord? — and *balā shahidnā* — yes, we testify. The Book's dialogues with humanity in the Qur'an are few, and this is the most fundamental: a question whose answer is a formula the Qur'an's readers repeat in prayer. The verse records the exchange as a completed event, and the testimony is presented as universal.

**The Purpose Clause**

*An taqūlū yawma al-qiyāmati innā kunnā ʿan hādhā ghāfilīn* — so that you cannot say on the Day of Judgment: we were unaware of this. The clause states the doctrine's function, and the sūrah's next verse completes it with the second excuse to be prevented: that the ancestors had associated others with God and the descendants merely followed (7:173). The Book closes both doors in advance.

**The Argument Against Inherited Excuse**

The verses form a pair whose target is the plea of inherited tradition. The Qur'an's refusal of the argument from ancestors appears throughout the sūrah's narratives — the community's *we found our forefathers doing the same* (26:74's phrasing in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ) — and the covenant of the loins is the Book's answer at the deepest level: the individual's own testimony precedes the tradition he inherits.

**The Nature of the Event**

The tradition discussed at length whether the testimony was a literal assembly of all souls or a statement about the constitution of human nature, and both readings agree on the doctrine the passage is establishing. The sūrah's placement of the verse after the raised mountain suggests that the Book is describing an acknowledgment at least as fundamental as the covenant of Israel.

**The Book's Appeal to Universal Knowledge**

The Qur'an appeals to the same knowledge elsewhere: *“Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy, but to worship Me ˹alone˺? This is the Straight Path”* (36:60–61), and the Book's address to an ungrateful humanity assumes a knowledge it should not need to be told again. The verse in this sūrah is the doctrinal ground for that assumption.

**The Testimony and Accountability**

The Book's whole doctrine of accountability rests on capacity, and this verse establishes the capacity at the most basic level. The Qur'an's statements that no soul is burdened beyond its strength (2:286) and that no one will be wronged (4:40) belong with the primordial testimony: the creature cannot plead that it was never given the means to know.

**Satan's Testimony to the Same Effect**

The Book's account of Iblīs's refusal states the principle from the opposite side when the devil admits that he will not be able to mislead God's chosen servants (15:39–40). The Qur'an's narratives consistently present the human being as capable of both directions, and the covenant of the loins is the Book's statement that the capacity for the first is native.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Structure**

The sūrah's narrative has moved from the creation of Adam and the refusal of Iblīs (7:11–18) through the history of the messengers, the towns, Egypt, and Israel. The covenant of verse 172 returns to the human being at the root, and the sūrah's later verses — the parable of the dog, the names of God, and the closing instructions about the Qur'an and remembrance — address the community on that foundation.

**The Recited Witness**

The words *bala shahidnā* have entered the vocabulary of Muslim prayer and reflection, and the verse's place in the sūrah is the reason: it is the Qur'an's statement that faith is a recognition rather than an imposition, and that the recognition has already happened. The sūrah's readers are asked, in effect, to act in accordance with what they have already said.

**The Next Verse's Second Excuse**

The verse's caution is completed by the next sentence, which blocks the appeal to ancestry, and the two together form the sūrah's account of why humanity has no defense of ignorance. The Book's narratives of the messengers then resume, and the reader continues with the knowledge that the ground of accountability has been established before the story began.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:173

> **Or lest you should say, “[It is] only that our fathers ascribed partners unto God beforehand, and we were their progeny after them. Wilt Thou destroy us for that which the falsifiers have done?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"It Was Our Forefathers Who Associated Others"**

*Aw taqūlū innamā ashraka ābāʾunā min qablu wa-kunnā dhurriyyatan min baʿdihim a-fatuhlikunā bimā faʿala al-mubṭilūn* — nor say: "It was our forefathers who had associated others ˹with Allah in worship˺ and we, as their descendants, followed in their footsteps. Will you then destroy us for the falsehood they invented?"

**The Second Excuse Blocked**

The verse completes the caution of the previous one by naming the argument it prevents. The first plea — that a person was unaware — is answered by the primordial testimony (7:172); the second — that the error was inherited — is answered here, and the Book's reply is that the tradition of the fathers does not transfer responsibility from the one who follows it.

**The Argument the Qur'an Meets Everywhere**

The plea of inherited practice is the Qur'an's most frequently answered objection. *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing’”* (2:170); *“What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us”* (5:104); *“We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps”* (43:22). The sūrah's verse states the argument in its most vulnerable form: the plea of people who admit the error and plead the precedent.

**The Book's Answer to the Ancestors**

The Qur'an's most sustained demolition of the argument is placed on Abraham's lips: *“Have you ˹really˺ considered what you have been worshipping — you and your ancestors? They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds”* (26:75–77), and his summary verdict on the people and their forefathers: *“Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray”* (21:54). The sūrah's readers have met the same logic in the catalogue of the patriarchs' faith.

**The Descendants and Their Own Acts**

The Book's doctrine of individual responsibility is stated in the sūrah's own earlier narratives — no soul bears another's burden (6:164's clause) — and in the Qur'an's address to the descendants of Israel: *“That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done”* (2:134). The verse's implicit answer to *will You destroy us for what they invented* is that the descendants are not being destroyed for their fathers' deeds but for their own.

**The Word for the Fabricators**

*Al-mubṭilūn* — those who make falsehood, who render things void — is the Book's term for the inventors of religious claims, and the sūrah's readers have met the same root in the account of the calf (7:139's *bāṭil*) and in the Egyptian court's performance. The plea of the descendants describes their fathers accurately, and the Book's answer is that accuracy in describing a crime is not an exemption from its consequences.

**The Covenant's Precedence**

The verse's position immediately after the covenant of the loins gives the Book's answer its sharpest form. If every human being has testified to God's lordship in his own person, then the inheritance of a contrary practice cannot be pleaded as ignorance of the alternative. The sūrah has built the argument in that order, and the reader is meant to see the two verses as a single statement.

**The Ancestors in the Book's Narratives**

The Qur'an's treatment of ancestral tradition is not a rejection of history but of its authority over conscience. The Book repeatedly honors the righteous ancestors — the patriarchs, the prophets, the people of the Book who believed — and it repeatedly refuses to let an unrighteous inheritance function as an excuse. The sūrah's earlier account of Israel's pious predecessors and its later generations makes the distinction clear.

**The Same Plea in the Prophet's City**

The Makkan elite's defense of its religion was substantially this argument, and the Qur'an's response to them runs through the Makkan sūrahs in the passages quoted above. The sūrah's readers are therefore being given the answer to an objection they had made or heard, and the Book states it in the form of a hypothetical future plea.

**The Book's Respect for the Individually Accountable**

The verse assumes a human being capable of evaluating what he inherits, and the Qur'an's whole call to reflection rests on that assumption: *“Have they not ever given it a thought?”* (7:184) follows a few verses later, and the sūrah's closing instructions address the individual's remembrance of God. The Book's anthropology is that of a creature who can be addressed rather than merely programmed.

**The Two Verses as a Single Argument**

The covenant of the loins establishes that the knowledge of God is native; the blocking of the ancestral plea establishes that its corruption cannot be blamed on inheritance. Together they state the sūrah's account of why humanity is answerable, and they are placed precisely where the sūrah's readers have just watched a community blame its past for its present.

**The Purpose Clause and the Signs**

The next verse closes the unit with the statement that the signs are made clear so that people might return, and the sequence — testimony, excuse blocked, signs clarified — is the sūrah's complete account of divine communication. The Book's readers are given the mechanism in three movements, and the narrative then resumes with a parable about a man who had the signs and let them go.

The verse's reasoning also answers a modern form of the same plea, and the Book's readers in every generation have had recourse to it: the argument that one's formation leaves no room for judgment is not an argument the Qur'an entertains, and its refusal is grounded in the testimony the verse presumes. The sūrah's readers are given the responsibility together with the capacity.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:174

> **Thus do We expound the signs, that haply they may return.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"This Is How We Make Our Signs Clear"**

*Wa-kadhālika nufaṣṣilu al-āyāti wa-laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — this is how We make our signs clear, so perhaps they will return ˹to the Right Path˺.

**The Verb for Detail**

*Nufaṣṣilu* — the Book's word for setting out in detail, distinguishing one thing from another — appears in the sūrah's account of the tablets as well: *commandments and explanations of all things* (7:145). The Qur'an's self-description is that of a text that separates and clarifies rather than one that leaves matters ambiguous, and the verse's use of the verb follows a passage that has distinguished the covenant of the loins from the excuse of ancestry.

**The Same Formula in the Qur'an**

The Book uses the formula repeatedly at the close of argumentative passages: *“This is how We make Our signs clear, so the way of the wicked may become distinct”* (6:55); *“That is your Lord's Path — perfectly straight. We have already made the signs clear to those who are mindful”* (6:126); *“We have certainly brought them a Book which We explained with knowledge — a guide and mercy for those who believe”* (7:52). The sūrah's own earlier verses used the same idiom, and the reader is meant to recognize it as a cadence.

**The Purpose: That They May Return**

*Laʿallahum yarjiʿūn* — so that they may return. The sūrah has used the same clause for the plagues of Egypt (7:130) and for the dispersion of Israel (7:168), and the Book's stated purpose for its signs is consistently remedial rather than merely informational. The Qur'an's God is described as making things clear in order to bring people back, not in order to convict them.

**The Word for Return**

*Rajʿah* — returning — is the sūrah's term of choice in these passages, and its meaning is broad enough to cover the return to a land, to a covenant, and to God. The Book's vocabulary of repentance is rich, and this verse's use of the general verb keeps the invitation open: what is asked is not a specific ritual but a change of direction.

**The Sign as a Category**

The term *āyāt* in this sūrah has covered the created world, the revealed verses, and the historical punishments, and the verse's claim is that all of them are made clear in the same way. The Qur'an's argument from signs is cumulative in its method, and the sūrah has been assembling that argument since its first verse.

**The Book's Technique of Repetition**

The formula appears at intervals in the sūrah, and its recurrence teaches the reader to recognize the ends of units: the catalogue of the covenant's terms, the account of the ancestors, and the parable that follows. The Qur'an's structure works partly by such markers, and the sūrah's readers are given them as a way of following the argument.

**What the Verse Means for the Address**

The sentence is spoken by God in the middle of the sūrah's address, and its function is to make the reader's position explicit: what he has been given is clarity, and the response that clarity invites is return. The Book's rhetoric in this passage is thus not merely descriptive but directive, and the sūrah's next unit applies the point with an example.

**The Example That Follows**

The parable of the man who was given the signs and abandoned them (7:175–176) is placed directly after this verse, and the arrangement is a further statement of the same purpose clause: the sign was clear, the man had it, and the choice was his. The sūrah's method in this stretch is to state the principle and then illustrate it, and the reader is carried from doctrine to story.

**The Doctrine of Clarity**

The Qur'an's claims about its own clarity belong to its account of divine justice: a command that could not be understood would be an imposition, and the sūrah's readers are told that the signs are detailed precisely so that no plea of confusion will stand. The theme runs through the sūrah's narratives of communities that claimed they had been given nothing.

**The Verse's Pastoral Function**

For the community receiving the recitation, the sentence is a reminder that the Book's function is remedial. The sūrah has reported the destruction of towns and the punishment of generations, and it interrupts that record to state the purpose of the whole recitation. The reader is given the option of return at the moment he might be tempted to read the history as merely a verdict on others.

**The Turn to the Parable**

The next verse introduces the story with a command to the Prophet: *relate to them the story*. The sūrah moves from a statement about its own method to a narrative, and the shift is characteristic of the Book's teaching: a doctrine stated, then a case study, then a conclusion. The reader of the sūrah is now invited to watch the principle operate in a life.

The Book's idiom of making signs clear is not confined to this sūrah, and its recurrence is a feature of the Qur'an's self-presentation: the reader is told, at the close of arguments about the earlier communities, that what he has been hearing is a detailed explanation rather than a riddle. The sūrah's own previous cadence — *We have certainly brought them a Book which We explained with knowledge* (7:52) — uses the same verb of detailing, and the two verses together describe the Qur'an's method as one of specification.

The audience of the sentence is the community in Makkah as much as the People of the Book, and the invitation to return is addressed to readers who have just been told that their own forefathers' practice cannot serve as a plea. The verse closes the unit by naming the purpose for which all of the sūrah's distinctions have been drawn, and the parable that follows shows what turning away looks like in a single life.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:175

> **And recite unto them the account of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he cast them off. So Satan made him his follower, and he became one of the deviant.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The One to Whom We Gave Our Signs"**

*Wa-tlu ʿalayhim nabaʾa alladhī ātaynāhu āyātinā fa-nsalakha minhā fa-atbaʿahu al-shayṭānu fa-kāna mina al-ghāwīn* — and relate to them ˹O Prophet˺ the story of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he abandoned them, so Satan took hold of him, and he became a deviant.

**The Command to Tell**

*Wa-tlu ʿalayhim* — recite to them — addresses the Prophet with the Book's verb for recitation, and the verse's status as a parable is stated in its opening word: *nabaʾ*, a report, a piece of news. The sūrah has been conducting an argument about signs and their recipients; the parable is its case study, and the Book presents it without naming the man because the type is the point.

**"He Abandoned Them"**

*Fa-nsalakha minhā* — the verb describes a snake sloughing its skin; the man stripped the signs off as one discards a garment. The sūrah's imagery of clothing had appeared earlier in the narrative of Adam's fall (7:26–27), where the garment of God-consciousness was named; here a man divests himself of revelation, and the Book's choice of the verb supplies the image.

**The Commentators' Identification**

The tradition discussed the identity of the man, and the reports mention a figure of Israel's history who was given knowledge and let it go, with some naming him as Balʿam, the man whose knowledge of God's covenant was famous among his people. The sūrah's silence about the name is deliberate, and the moral of the passage does not depend on the identification.

**"Satan Took Hold of Him"**

*Fa-atbaʿahu al-shayṭānu* — the phrase describes pursuit and attachment, and the Book's accounts of the devil's work use the same vocabulary: whoever turns away from the Reminder finds a companion set for him (43:36). The verse presents the sequence in its actual order: the abandonment came first, and the attachment followed. The sūrah's narrative is careful about where agency lies.

**The Consequence: Among the Deviants**

*Fa-kāna mina al-ghāwīn* — and he became of those who went astray. The word carries the additional sense of being undone, and the Book uses it for those who have wandered past the point of return; the sūrah's later verse about the sealed hearts belongs to the same family of terms. What the verse reports is the outcome of a process, not a moment of weakness.

**The Signs as a Trust**

The verse treats the signs as a possession that can be given and abandoned, and the Qur'an's instruction about revelation elsewhere is that it must be held firmly and conveyed (7:145, 7:171). The parable's man failed in both directions at once: he had the signs and neither kept them for himself nor delivered them to others.

**The Knowledge Without Practice**

The sūrah is full of figures who had knowledge and no practice: the generation who inherited the Scripture and traded it for gain (7:169), the scholars who did not forbid wrong (5:63), the inheritors of the Book who changed its words (7:162). The parable's man is the individual form of that collective portrait, and the Book tells his story to a community already familiar with the type.

**Why the Prophet Is Told to Recite It**

The command to relate the story addresses a man whose own community was being invited to accept the signs, and the parable serves him as a warning as much as his audience. The Qur'an's instruction to the Prophet elsewhere includes the caution about the messenger's own reliance on the revelation, and the Book's narratives do not exempt the bearers of revelation from the danger of losing it.

**The Word for the Devil's Role**

The Book's account of Satan in this verse is the account of an opportunist rather than a conqueror: the man abandoned the signs, and the devil followed. The Qur'an's other descriptions of the process use the same structure — the human choice followed by the attachment — and the sūrah's readers are meant to see the sequence as a warning about small departures.

**The Book's Parables of the Individual**

The Qur'an's parables of individuals are relatively few — the man of the two gardens (18:32–44), the owner of the vineyard (68:17–33), and this figure — and each of them concerns the same thing: the use of a gift in a life. The sūrah's readers are given one of the Book's most compact statements of that theme, and its outcome is stated in the next verse.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Address**

The verse's introduction follows the statement that the signs are made clear so that people may return (7:174), and the parable is that statement's illustration. The sūrah's argument is thus: the signs are clear, the covenant has been made, the excuse has been closed, and here is what happens to a man who has it all and lets it go.

**The Man's Example and the Next Verse**

The next verse reports God's will in the man's case — he could have been elevated by the signs — and then supplies the simile of the dog. The sūrah's reader is given the man's decision, its motive, and its image in three verses, and the passage as a whole is one of the Book's most complete portraits of the failure of knowledge.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:176

> **Had We willed, We would surely have elevated him thereby, but he inclined toward the earth and followed his caprice. Thus his parable is that of a dog: if you attack him, he lolls out his tongue, and if you leave him alone, he lolls out his tongue. That is the likeness of the people who deny Our signs. So recount the stories, that haply they may reflect.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"His Example Is That of a Dog"**

*Wa-law shiʾnā la-rafaʿnāhu bihā wa-lākinnahu akhlada ilā al-arḍi wa-ttabaʿa hawāhu fa-mathaluhu ka-mathali al-kalbi in taḥmil ʿalayhi yalhath aw tatrukhu yalhath dhālika mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā fa-qṣuṣi al-qaṣaṣa laʿallahum yatafakkarūn* — if We had willed, We would have elevated him with Our signs, but he clung to this life, following his evil desires. His example is that of a dog: if you chase it away, it pants, and if you leave it, it ˹still˺ pants. This is the example of the people who deny Our signs. So narrate ˹to them˺ stories ˹of the past˺, so perhaps they will reflect.

**The Ability That Was Not Used**

*Wa-law shiʾnā la-rafaʿnāhu bihā* — the verse states what could have happened and did not. The Book's parallel constructions typically name God's capacity rather than a withheld decree, and the Qur'an's teaching about guidance is stated in the same register elsewhere: *“It is not for any soul to believe except by Allah's leave”* (10:100). The man's failure is not attributed to a deficiency in what he was given.

**"He Clung to This Life"**

*Akhlada ilā al-arḍ* — the verb is a leaning, a settling, a taking root in the ground; the man's weight was in the world. The Qur'an's diagnosis of this disposition runs through the sūrah's narratives of the towns, and the Book's word for the opposite disposition is detachment of purpose. The verse is thus a statement about what the man loved rather than about what he knew.

**"Following His Evil Desires"**

*Wa-ttabaʿa hawāhu* — the Book's term for desire as a ruling principle appears in sūrah after sūrah, and the Qur'an's warning about it is among its most frequent: *“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly”* (45:23). The verse places the man in that category, and the sūrah's readers are shown a knowledgeable person whose appetite set his course.

**The Dog and Its Panting**

The simile's point is persistence without aim: the animal pants whether driven off or left alone, which is to say that its condition is unaffected by the treatment it receives. The Book uses animals as measures of human condition in several places — the donkey carrying books (62:5), the cattle that do not understand (25:44), and the dog in this verse — and each of them makes the same point about an incapacity that no external change can remedy.

**"The Example of the People Who Deny Our Signs"**

*Dhālika mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — the verse generalizes explicitly, and the sūrah's readers are told that the individual case is an image of a class. The Book's parables frequently end with such a statement, and the transition from the man to the people connects this passage to the sūrah's account of the towns and of the generations who inherited the Scripture.

**The Command to Narrate**

*Fa-qṣuṣi al-qaṣaṣa laʿallahum yatafakkarūn* — so recount the stories, so that they may reflect. The verse instructs the Prophet in the method the sūrah itself is using, and the Book's approval of narration as a form of argument appears throughout the Qur'an: the stories of the earlier communities are told, the Book says, so that people may use their reason.

**The Book's Attitude to Reflection**

The verb *yatafakkarūn* belongs to the Qur'an's moral anthropology: human beings are addressed as creatures capable of thinking their way to conclusions, and the Book's reproaches are aimed at those who have stopped. The sūrah's earlier verse about eyes that do not see and ears that do not hear (7:179, soon following) states the failure from the other side.

**The Two Kinds of Failure**

The parable's man had the signs and lost them; the sūrah's previous narratives showed people who never had them or who traded them for gain. The distinction matters for the sūrah's argument, and it is why the Book places the man's story at this point: the warning is addressed to those who have something to lose.

**The Relationship Between Knowledge and Conduct**

The Book's treatment of this man is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements that knowledge alone does not save, and the sūrah's readers are meant to apply it to themselves. The Qur'an's rebuke of the earlier communities for knowing and not acting uses the same logic, and the sūrah's later verse about the parables of the mosquito and the gnat (2:26's parallel) states the general principle that the Book's teachings sort people rather than inform them.

**The Verse's Practical Warning**

For the community receiving the recitation, the dog's panting is a warning about a religion that continues its motions without its purpose. The sūrah's narratives of the towns included communities that maintained their rites; the parable shows the endpoint of a life whose motions are unaffected by the signs it carries.

**The Parable's Structure**

The verse gives the man's choice, God's statement about what might have been, the simile, its application, and the instruction to narrate — all in one sentence. The compression is characteristic of the Book's parables, and the sūrah's reader is given the whole of the case in a passage he can memorize.

**The Next Verse's Verdict**

The verse that follows states the verdict on those who deny the signs — *what an evil example... they only wronged their own souls* — and the passage closes by returning the harm to its source. The sūrah's argument thus ends where its earlier narratives ended: the punishment is not an external injury but the fruit of the conduct itself.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:177

> **Evil is the parable of the people who denied Our signs and wronged themselves.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"What an Evil Example"**

*Sāʾa mathalan al-qawmu alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-anfusahum kānū yaẓlimūn* — what an evil example of those who denied Our signs! They ˹only˺ wronged their own souls.

**The Verdict's Form**

The sentence pronounces judgment on the example itself: the people who deny the signs are a bad model, and the verse gives the reason in the same breath. The Book's evaluations of human conduct frequently take this form — an exclamation, then an explanation — and the sūrah's readers are given the two in a single verse.

**The Word for the Example**

*Mathal* in the verse echoes the parable of the dog in the previous sentence, and the Qur'an's use of the term covers comparisons, proverbs, and the illustrative cases the Book sets before its readers. The verse is saying that the deniers have become a byword, which is also the fate the sūrah assigned to the destroyed towns.

**The Self-Harm Stated**

*Wa-anfusahum kānū yaẓlimūn* — they wronged their own souls, and the imperfect verb marks a continuing practice. The sūrah had closed its account of the wilderness generation with the same clause — *they did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves* (7:160) — and the Book's doctrine of wrongdoing is consistent across its narratives: the injury is always returned to the one who commits it.

**The Book's Sentences on the Deniers**

The Qur'an describes the condition of those who deny the signs in several registers: they are the losers whose works are void (18:103–105), they trade guidance for misguidance (2:16), and they will find no helper (3:177). The verse's formulation is the most compact of these and it belongs to the sūrah's own vocabulary of debt and loss.

**Why the Denial Harms the Denier**

The Book's reasoning about the self-inflicted nature of wrongdoing is grounded in its doctrine of God's self-sufficiency: *“Your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, Full of Mercy”* (6:133), and the Qur'an states explicitly that neither obedience nor disobedience adds anything to Him. The sūrah's readers are thus told that the whole register of profit and loss in the Book operates on the human side of the transaction.

**The Connection to the Parable**

The verdict follows immediately after the command to narrate the parable, which means that the sūrah's readers are meant to read the case of the man who abandoned the signs as an instance of the same self-harm. The Book's parables in this stretch are diagnostic: they describe a condition so that the reader can recognize it, and the verdict states the cost.

**The Vocabulary of Loss in the Sūrah**

The theme of loss runs through the passage and the sūrah: the elites of the towns called their prophet's followers the losers (7:90), the calf-worshippers feared being among the losers (7:149), and the sūrah's later verse states the law that whoever God leaves to stray are the true losers (7:178). The verse's ending belongs to that chain, and its placement prepares the reader for the next verse's statement about guidance.

**The Book's Refusal of Vindictiveness**

The verse's verdict is not framed as an injury done to God, and the sūrah's narratives consistently avoid presenting the divine judgment as a wounded reaction. The Book's God is described as not wronging anyone, and the human being is described as wronging himself; the doctrine removes both the possibility of a cosmic grievance and the excuse of a disproportionate penalty.

**The Example's Function in the Address**

The Prophet's audience is shown the example so that it will not become the example: the sūrah's method is to name the type, state its cost, and leave the reader at the point of decision. The command to narrate in the previous verse and the verdict in this one together constitute the sūrah's pedagogy, and the next verse completes the lesson with a statement about guidance.

**The Sūrah's Refusal of Despair**

The verse's severity is bounded by the sūrah's repeated statements that the door remains open: the signs are detailed so that people may return (7:174), the stories are told so that they may reflect (7:176), and the guidance of whoever God guides follows immediately (7:178). The Book's arrangement keeps judgment and possibility in view at once.

**The Verse in the Sequence**

The passage now moves from the individual's failure to the general law of guidance and straying, and the transition is one of the sūrah's most important hinges: the parable has shown a man who lost what he had, and the reader is now told who holds the outcome. The next verse states it in a sentence the sūrah's readers have met before in other words.

The Book's use of the term *mathal* for a person or a people is worth pausing over: the Qur'an says that the deniers have become an example, which is to say that their case has been made public for others to learn from. The sūrah's narratives of destroyed towns served the same purpose, and the Book states the function explicitly elsewhere — *So We made their fate an example to present and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing* (2:66).

The verse's closing clause also completes the sūrah's teaching about the nature of punishment: what the deniers suffer is described as their own doing rather than as an external sentence imposed from outside the moral order. The Book's other statements of the doctrine use the same grammar — *Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss* (41:46) — and the sūrah's readers are meant to hear the two clauses of verse 177 as a single verdict.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:178

> **Whomsoever God guides, he is rightly guided; and whomsoever He leads astray, it is they who are the losers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Whoever Allah Guides Is Truly Guided"**

*Man yahdi Allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadī wa-man yuḍlil fa-ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūn* — whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, they are the ˹true˺ losers.

**The Law Stated Plainly**

The verse presents the sūrah's doctrine of guidance in a single pair of clauses, and it does so immediately after the parable of the man who lost the signs. The arrangement is the Book's standard method in this stretch: a case, then the principle that explains it, and the reader is meant to see the man's fate as an instance of the second half of the sentence.

**The Same Sentence Elsewhere in the Sūrah**

The sūrah states the law again in almost the same words a few verses later — *“Whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance”* (7:186) — and the repetition frames the passage about the signs and the names of God. The Qur'an's use of such refrains is a feature of its teaching method: the reader is given the same principle at intervals until it becomes a lens for reading the rest.

**The Parallel Statements in the Qur'an**

The Book states the same doctrine in several sūrahs: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, you will find no guardians for them besides Him”* (17:97); *“And whoever Allah guides, none can lead astray”* (39:37); *“And whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide after Him”* (42:44). The Qur'an's repeated insistence on the point makes it one of the load-bearing sentences of its theology.

**The Misreading the Verse Prevents**

The sentence does not remove human responsibility, and the sūrah's surrounding verses are the evidence: the same passage commands the Prophet to narrate the stories, warns the deniers, and instructs the Prophet to seek refuge with God (7:200). The Book's doctrine of divine guidance is stated in a text that addresses people as agents, and the two levels of the account are held together throughout the Qur'an.

**The Vocabulary of Loss**

*Al-khāsirūn* — the losers — is the sūrah's recurring term for those whose end is ruin, and its use here closes a sequence: the towns' elites called the believers losers (7:90), Madyan's people used the same word for Shuʿayb's followers (7:92's account), the calf-worshippers feared that they would be among the losers (7:149), and the verse now states who those losers actually are. The Book's irony in this sūrah is structural, and the term's migration is its clearest example.

**The Guidance That Is Given**

The Qur'an's accounts of guidance present it as a gift that follows a person's own orientation: *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first”* (6:110), and the sūrah's earlier verse about turning away the arrogant (7:146) states the same law. The verse's first clause is thus a promise rather than an arbitrary distribution.

**The Book's Instruction to Ask**

The Qur'an teaches its readers to request guidance in the words of the Fātiḥah, and the sūrah's doctrine here gives the request its context: the guidance sought is the one that the verse says cannot be substituted. The Book's practice is to state the doctrine and to teach the prayer that follows from it, and the sūrah's readers receive both.

**The Verse's Pastoral Weight**

Read in the sūrah's sequence, the sentence is addressed to a community that has watched a man with signs lose everything and a people with a scripture trade it for gain. The verse answers the question such stories raise — whether anything can be done — and its answer is that guidance is in God's hand, and that the hand is not closed to the one who asks.

**The Contrast With the Parable**

The man of the parable clung to the earth and followed his desire; the verse states the alternative in a pair of clauses without describing it. The Book's rhetorical economy here is deliberate: the reader who has followed the argument supplies the picture of the guided person from the sūrah's earlier portraits — the warners who were rescued, the group who guided with truth, and the generations who held fast to the Book.

**The Verse's Use in the Community's Life**

The sentence became one of the Qur'an's most cited statements of reliance on God, and its position in this sūrah — between a parable of loss and a catalogue of the divine names — gives it its pastoral force. The Book's teaching here is that the outcome is not in the reader's control, and that this fact is a ground of humility rather than of resignation.

**The Turn to the Signs of the Heedless**

The next verses describe those who have hearts that do not understand and eyes that do not see (7:179) and then name the remedy in the Beautiful Names of God (7:180). The verse's law is thus immediately illustrated from the human side, and the reader is carried from the doctrine of guidance to its visible effects and then to the invocation that belongs with it.

The verse's two clauses also supply the sūrah's answer to the objection that its histories of destruction are unjust: if guidance and straying are matters of God's disposal, the reader is told, then the outcome belongs to the One who knows the hearts, and the human being's part is the response the sūrah has been describing throughout. The next verses state the visible marks of that response on both sides.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:179

> **We have indeed created for Hell many among jinn and men: they have hearts with which they understand not; they have eyes with which they see not; and they have ears with which they hear not. Such as these are like cattle. Nay, they are even further astray. It is they who are heedless.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Have Hearts They Do Not Understand With"**

*Wa-laqad dharaʾnā li-jahannama kathīran mina al-jinni wa-l-insi lahum qulūbun lā yafqahūna bihā wa-lahum aʿyunun lā yubṣirūna bihā wa-lahum ādhānun lā yasmaʿūna bihā ulāʾika ka-l-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu ulāʾika humu al-ghāfilūn* — indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided! Such ˹people˺ are ˹entirely˺ heedless.

**The Instruments Named and Unused**

The verse lists the three faculties of knowledge — the heart as the seat of understanding, the eye, and the ear — and describes them as present and idle. The Book's anatomy of the human being is consistent across the Qur'an: it is the same triple that the sūrah's covenant passage assumed when it established that the creature has been given the means to know (7:172). What the verse reports is not a defect in the equipment but a failure in its use.

**The Same Triple Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses the formula in sūrah after sūrah: *“They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺”* (2:18); *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered”* (2:7); and the account of those who listen to the recitation with veils over their hearts (6:25). The sūrah's readers have already met the theme of the sealed heart (7:100–101, 7:146), and the verse states it as a description of a class.

**"Destined for Hell"**

*Dharaʾnā li-jahannama* — the Book's statement of the destination follows the same logic as its statements about guidance: the instrument is given, the use of it is what fixes the end, and the Qur'an describes the outcome as the purpose for which such people exist. The Book's other formulations of the doctrine use the same register — the sūrah's own statement that God guides whom He wills and leaves to stray whom He wills (7:178).

**"Like Cattle, Even Less Guided"**

*Ka-l-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu* — the comparison to animals is one the Qur'an uses repeatedly: *“They are only like cattle — no, more than that, they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way!”* (25:44). The point is not a contempt for animals but a contrast between an animal's perfect conformity to its nature and a human being's failure to conform to his. The animal cannot be anything other than what it is; the human being is the creature capable of turning away.

**The Word for Heedlessness**

*Al-ghāfilūn* — the heedless — closes the verse, and the sūrah has used the same term for the Egyptians who drowned (7:136) and for the audience of the signs. The Qur'an's diagnosis of heedlessness is not that people lack information but that they do not attend; the verse's list of idle faculties is the same charge in three parts.

**The Jinn Included**

The verse names both jinn and humans, and the Book's inclusion of the jinn in its moral accounting recurs throughout the Qur'an. The sūrah's readers are being told that the categories of knowledge and responsibility are not restricted to one order of creation, and the same pair appears in the Book's statement of the purpose of creation.

**The Doctrines of Predestination and Responsibility**

The verse's opening phrase about those destined for Hell has been read by Islamic theology alongside the Qur'an's many statements of human accountability, and the tradition's resolution is that the Book's statements of the divine decree do not remove the creature's responsibility for the deeds by which the decree is known. The sūrah's readers are shown the outcome and the diagnosis in one sentence, with the description of the faculties placed between them.

**The Verse's Rhetorical Force**

The list of unused faculties is the Book's most compact argument against the excuse of ignorance. The reader is told that the instruments are there, that they were given by the One now speaking, and that the failure lies in the refusal to employ them. The sūrah's earlier narratives of communities that demanded signs and then dismissed them supply the illustration, and the verse supplies the diagnosis.

**The Command That Follows**

The sūrah's next verse turns from the heedless to the divine names, and the arrangement is deliberate: the reader who has just been shown an unused heart is offered the invocation by which hearts are used. The Book's method in this stretch is to state the disease and immediately name the remedy, and the two verses are meant to be read together.

**What the Verse Tells the Prophet's Community**

The passage is addressed to people who have the revelation recited among them, and its warning concerns the same faculties in their own case. The sūrah's later instruction to listen attentively when the Qur'an is recited (7:204) is the positive form of this verse, and the community's tradition read the two passages as a pair: the faculties are for listening, and listening is what they are for.

**The Sūrah's Portrait of Unbelief**

The verse gathers the sūrah's vocabulary of refusal into one portrait: the sealed heart, the covered eye, the stopped ear, the heedlessness, and the descent below the animals. The Book's readers are given the complete picture before they are given the remedy, and the sequence of the sūrah's closing verses — the names of God, the listening, and the remembrance — is built on the diagnosis stated here.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:180

> **Unto God belong the Most Beautiful Names; so call Him by them, and leave those who deviate with regard to His Names. Soon they shall be recompensed for that which they used to do.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"To Him Belong the Most Beautiful Names"**

*Wa-li-llāhi al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā fa-dʿūhu bihā wa-dharū alladhīna yulḥidūna fī asmāʾihi sa-yujzawna mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — Allah has the Most Beautiful Names. So call upon Him by them, and keep away from those who abuse His Names. They will be punished for what they used to do.

**The Names and the Invocation**

The verse joins a statement about God's names to a command to use them in prayer, and the Book's other formulations of the same doctrine add the instruction to call upon Him by any of them: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate — whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names’”* (17:110). The sūrah's readers are told that the names are not a subject for speculation but the vocabulary of supplication.

**The Names in the Qur'an's Theology**

The Qur'an's references to the divine names are concentrated in a few passages: *“Allah — there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He has the Most Beautiful Names”* (20:8), and the sūrah of al-Ḥashr's closing catalogue, which names the Creator, the Inventor, and the Shaper and ends with the same formula (59:24). The sūrah's verse belongs to that set, and its placement after the account of the heedless gives it its force: the remedy for a dead heart is the invocation of God by His names.

**The Hadith of the Ninety-Nine Names**

The Prophet's statement about the names is preserved in the two most reliable collections: al-Bukhārī 2736 and Muslim 2677, from Abū Hurayrah — *Allah has ninety-nine names; whoever commits them to memory will enter Paradise*. The tradition built its literature of the names on this hadith and on the Qur'an's command to call upon God by them, and the sūrah's verse is the Book's own instruction in the practice.

**"Those Who Abuse His Names"**

*Yulḥidūna fī asmāʾihi* — the verb describes deviation, distortion, a turning aside within a thing — is the Book's term for the misuse of the names, and the tradition understood it to cover several practices: the derivation of false claims about God from His attributes, the assignment of the names to idols, and the alteration of their sense. The sūrah's readers are told to keep away from those who do it, which is an instruction about company as well as about doctrine.

**The Threat at the End**

*Sa-yujzawna mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — they will be repaid for what they were doing — closes the verse with the imperfect verb of habitual practice. The sūrah's warnings consistently attach the consequence to persistence rather than to a single act, and the punishment of those who abuse the names is stated in the same form as the punishment of the heedless in the previous verse.

**The Names as a Subject of Reverence**

The Book's treatment of the names is characterized by restraint: the Qur'an names God by His attributes and does not permit speculation about their number, their derivation, or their application to creatures. The sūrah's verse states the command and the prohibition in a single sentence, and the reader is given both the practice and its boundary.

**The Muslim Tradition's Development**

The Islamic tradition produced its lists of the ninety-nine names from the hadith's statement and from the Qur'an's vocabulary, and the theological literature developed its discussion of the names as the primary locus of reflection on God's attributes. The sūrah's verse supplied both the authorization and the caution, and the tradition's careful distinction between the names and what they name derives from the same concern.

**The Verse's Pastoral Function**

Read in the sūrah's sequence, the verse is the answer to the paralysed heart of the previous verse. The reader who has just been told that his faculties are idle is told what to do with his voice, and the Book's method here is characteristic: the diagnosis is followed immediately by the practice. The Qur'an's remedy for heedlessness is repeatedly an act of speech, and the names are its vocabulary.

**The Abuse of the Names in the Book's Polemic**

The Qur'an's disputes with the Makkan elite involved the names of God directly, and the Book's insistence on calling upon Him by them was a statement about the unity of the divine attributes against the multiplication of divine beings. The sūrah's verse thus has a polemical edge, and the tradition read it as a defence of monotheism's vocabulary as well as an instruction in prayer.

**The Command's Universality**

The verse addresses all its readers, and the Book's invitation to call upon God by His names is one of the Qur'an's most accessible instructions: it requires no intermediary, no lineage, and no special knowledge. The sūrah's readers who have followed the narrative of the towns, Egypt, Israel, and the parable of the man with the signs are given a practice in three words.

**The Next Verse's Group**

The verse that follows names a group among those God created who guide with the truth and establish justice (7:181), and the placement returns the reader to the sūrah's theme of communities. The passage now moves from the individual's faculties and the names of God to the visible sign of a guided people, and the sūrah's closing chapters continue in that register.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:181

> **And among those We have created, there is a community that guides by the truth and does justice thereby.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"A Group That Guides With the Truth"**

*Wa-mimman khalaqnā ummatun yahdūna bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-bihi yaʿdilūn* — and among those We created is a group that guides with the truth and establishes justice accordingly.

**The Sentence and Its Earlier Twin**

The sūrah had already used this formula for the people of Moses: *there are some among the people of Moses who guide with the truth and establish justice accordingly* (7:159). The verse repeats it for humanity as a whole, and the Book's repetition is the argument: whatever the record of a community, the existence of a group that guides and does justice is stated as a standing fact about the creation.

**The Group's Identity**

The tradition read the verse as a reference to the community of the Prophet, and the Qur'an's own address to the believers elsewhere supplies the vocabulary: the community that calls to goodness, encourages what is good, and forbids evil is described as the successful (3:104), and the description of the righteous among the People of the Book adds the same elements (3:113–114). The verse is thus a statement about the continuity of a function across communities rather than a claim about one group's privilege.

**"Among Those We Created"**

The phrase enlarges the statement beyond any single people, and the Book's theology of creation in the sūrah supplies the frame: God created humanity from a single soul (7:189) and made the descendants testify to His lordship (7:172). The group that guides with truth is thus presented as an element within the created order rather than as an exception to it.

**The Standard: Truth and Justice**

The verse names two marks — guidance by the truth and the establishment of justice — and the sūrah's own narratives have measured the destroyed towns by the same standard. Madyan's scales, ʿĀd's arrogance, Thamūd's violence, and Egypt's enslavement were failures of justice; the group described here is the visible alternative, and the Book's readers are told that its existence is not a hope but a fact.

**The Verb of Establishment**

*Yahdūna* and *yaʿdilūn* are both imperfect verbs, which marks continuous practice rather than a completed achievement. The Book's descriptions of the righteous use the same verbal form throughout, and the sūrah's readers are given a portrait of a function rather than a list of qualifications.

**The Parallel in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān**

The Qur'an's address to the Muslim community uses the same note: *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity — you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah”* (3:110), and its description of the upright among the People of the Book names the same activities (3:113–114). The sūrah's verse avoids the language of supremacy and uses that of function: what the group does is guide and establish justice.

**The Verse's Answer to Despair**

Placed after the description of the heedless and the abuse of the names, the verse answers a question the reader may carry from the sūrah's long record of failure: whether the earth is left to the heedless. The Book's answer is that a group persists, and that its mark is not its size but its activity. The same answer appears in the sūrah's earlier acknowledgement of the righteous remnant.

**The Quran's Doctrine of the Remnant**

The Book's narratives of destroyed communities frequently preserve a saving element — Lot's household, the believers with Noah, the warners of the town by the sea — and the verse states the doctrine in its general form. The sūrah's readers are meant to understand that the destruction of the towns never meant the extinction of guidance.

**What the Verse Requires of Its Readers**

The verse's description is also an implicit charge: the reader who has been told that such a group exists is being asked where he stands in relation to it. The sūrah's closing instructions about listening to the recitation, remembering God, and seeking refuge answer that question in practical terms, and the verse supplies the frame within which those instructions are to be received.

**The Two Communities and the One Criterion**

The repetition of the formula for the people of Moses and for humanity shows the Book's refusal to make the criterion anything but conduct. Israel has a group that guides with truth; humanity has one; and the marks are identical. The sūrah's readers are given the standard rather than a claim, and the Book's addresses to its own community elsewhere apply the same test.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Closing Argument**

The sūrah's last movement runs from the heedless and the names of God to the guiding group, the gradual destruction of the deniers, the Hour, the idols, and the final instructions about the Qur'an and remembrance. The verse about the group belongs to that movement as its positive pole, and the reader is carried from it into the warnings that follow with the standard in hand.

The verse's function in the sūrah is also to keep the reader from misreading the histories it has just told: the destruction of the towns and the dispersion of Israel were never accounts of the extinction of guidance. The Book states the same fact about the community of the Prophet in its own address — the community that calls to goodness and forbids evil is described as successful (3:104) — and the two statements together give the sūrah's readers a standard by which to measure their own place among the guiding group.

The verse's placement beside the account of the heedless gives the sūrah its balance: the page that describes hearts, eyes, and ears unused is followed by the statement that among the created there are those who use them. The Book's readers are thus not left with a portrait of humanity in decline but with a description of the two conditions, and the choice between them is left where the sūrah has placed it throughout.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:182

> **And as for those who deny Our signs, We shall lead them on little by little, whence they know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Will Gradually Draw Them to Destruction"**

*Wa-alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn* — as for those who deny Our signs, We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend.

**The Verb of Gradual Ruin**

*Nastadrijuhum* — the Book's term for drawing a person down step by step — describes a ruin that arrives through the very conditions a man takes for comfort. The Qur'an uses the same verb in the same sentence in Sūrat al-Qalam: *“So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend”* (68:44), followed by the declaration that the planning is flawless (68:45).

**The Mechanism Stated Elsewhere**

The sūrah's readers have already been given the mechanism in its narrative form: Egypt read its prosperity as a verdict on its own merit (7:131), and the towns were seized after their prosperity had been granted (7:95). The Book states the law of the pattern in Sūrat al-Anʿām: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44).

**The Same Warning in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān**

The Qur'an addresses the same illusion about extended prosperity in a verse aimed at the deniers: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment”* (3:178). The sūrah's phrase *in ways they cannot comprehend* adds the note of blindness: the ruin is not recognized as such while it is happening.

**The Sūrah's Own Earlier Statement**

This is not a new law in the sūrah: the towns were warned, and their prosperity was described as a lure rather than a reprieve; the destruction came upon them *while they were unaware* (7:95's clause). The verse states the rule in its general form, and the reader is expected to connect it with the narratives he has just read.

**The Illusion of Increase**

The Book's analysis of the delusion is stated in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn's question and answer: *“Do they think, since We provide them with wealth and children, that We hasten to ˹honour˺ them ˹with˺ all kinds of good? No! They are not aware”* (23:55–56). The sūrah's verse belongs to the same teaching, and its placement after the account of the heedless gives it a diagnostic function.

**The Word for the Deniers**

*Kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — the sūrah's recurring phrase for the communities whose stories fill the narrative returns here, and the reader is meant to hear the whole catalogue behind it. The verse is not about a new class of people but about the class whose record the sūrah has assembled from Noah's town to the generations who inherited the Scripture.

**The Mercy That Looks Like Time**

The verse's phrase *in ways they cannot comprehend* implies that what the deniers experience as delay is itself the instrument. The Qur'an's doctrine of respite is stated with the same caution elsewhere — *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (68:45) — and the sūrah's next verse completes the statement in the divine voice.

**The Divine Voice in the Two Verses**

The following verse shifts to the first person: *I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless* (7:183). The two verses thus present the same fact from two angles — the act described in the third person and the intention stated in the first — and the sūrah's readers are given both the mechanism and its author.

**The Sūrah's Doctrine of Respite**

The Book's teaching about the delay granted to the wicked appears throughout the Qur'an, and its purpose is stated in several places: the respite is for repentance and for the completion of the record. The sūrah's readers have seen it operate in Egypt's plagues, where the relief came and the promise was broken (7:134–135), and the verse states the general law.

**What the Verse Denies**

The verse denies that advantage and security are evidence of divine approval, which is the conclusion the sūrah's Egyptian narrative had already refuted in its own way. The Book's readers are told explicitly that the sign of God's favour is not the abundance of goods, and the sūrah's later instructions about remembrance and supplication supply the positive account of what the sign is.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Address**

The verse follows the statement about the guiding group (7:181) and precedes the divine declaration of delay, and the arrangement is a complete statement about the two ways: a group that guides with truth and establishes justice, and a class that is drawn down gradually while believing itself secure. The sūrah's closing verses then address the Prophet's own task within that contrast.

The tradition's commentators placed the verse's warning beside the sūrah's own historical narratives and noted that the pattern of gradual ruin had been displayed three times in the passage already: in Egypt's plagues, in the towns that were seized after their prosperity, and in the generation that inherited the Scripture and was given respite. The verse's contribution is the statement of intention that the stories had shown in operation.

The classical commentators treated the verse's verb as one of the Qur'an's most precise descriptions of divine dealing with the ungrateful, and their discussions collected the instances the Book itself records: the gardens and the harvests of Sūrat al-Qalam, the generations of ʿĀd and Thamūd, and the towns whose ruins the sūrah has just recited. The verse supplies the general law for all of those cases, and the reader is invited to see the pattern rather than the particulars.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:183

> **And I will grant them respite; truly My scheme is firm.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"My Planning Is Flawless"**

*Wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīn* — I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.

**The Voice and the Claim**

The verse speaks in the first person, and its two clauses state a purpose and a character: the delay is granted, and the plan behind it cannot be defeated. The Book's use of the first person for these declarations in the later verses of sūrahs is a feature of its closing movements, and the phrase echoes the same declaration in Sūrat al-Qalam (68:45).

**The Word for Delay**

*Imlāʾ* is the granting of an extension, and the Book uses it for the time given to the offenders of every narrative: the respite of the towns, the years of Egypt's plagues, and the generations who inherited the Scripture. The Qur'an's treatment of time is consistently that extended opportunity is a test rather than a favour, and the verse states the doctrine in the divine voice.

**The Word for the Plan**

*Kayd* — plan, stratagem — is used by the Book for the schemes of human beings and for the divine response to them, and the verse's choice of the term in the first person is deliberate: the opponents' plotting is answered by a plan of which they are not aware. The sūrah's readers have watched exactly that in the story of Egypt, where the court's mobilization and the magicians' showmanship were answered by a staff.

**The Adjective of Firmness**

*Matīn* — firm, unbreakable, of a rope that will not fray — completes the sentence and answers the sense of security the previous verse described. The Book's use of the term belongs to its language of strength and construction, and its appearance here is a reminder that the created order's durability belongs to its Maker rather than to the powers within it.

**The Delay and the Record**

The Book's teaching about the interval granted to wrongdoers includes its purpose: the record is completed and the opportunity for repentance is given. The sūrah's own narratives of the towns report messengers sent and warnings repeated before destruction, and its account of Egypt's plagues is a sequence of respites used for further refusal. The verse states the pattern as the divine intention.

**The Respite and the Deniers' Error**

The Qur'an's frequent reproach to those who reason from their prosperity to their standing is given in several sūrahs, and the verse's placement after the account of the heedless makes the same point in the form of a statement. The Book's readers are told that the delay is the plan itself, which is why it looks like ordinary time.

**The Sūrah's Closing Use of the Doctrine**

The remaining verses of the sūrah turn from the deniers' ruin to the Prophet's own instructions: the thought that is asked of his audience (7:184), the reflection on the heavens and the earth (7:185), the Hour (7:187), and the final commands about recitation and remembrance. The verse about the flawless plan stands at the head of that movement, and it supplies the security the messenger and his community are to carry.

**The Commentary on the Sūrah's Pattern**

The tradition's commentators connected the verse with the sūrah's earlier statements about the towns' sudden seizure and noted that the Book's accounts of destruction consistently involve an interval. The verse's contribution to the sūrah is the explicit statement of intention that the narratives had shown in action.

**The Verse's Pastoral Weight**

For the community suffering pressure in Makkah, the sentence about the flawless plan was a assurance that the delay was not indifference. The Qur'an's address to the Prophet elsewhere states the same theme in the imperative — the command to be patient with the decree — and the sūrah's closing verses apply it to the community's own practice of recitation and remembrance.

**The Two Verses Together**

Read with the previous verse, the passage gives the reader the mechanism and its guarantee: a gradual drawing down that the offenders cannot perceive, and a plan that cannot be defeated. The Book's habit of pairing a description with a declaration in the divine voice is one of its means of reassurance, and this pair is among its most direct.

The verse's brevity also carries a warning about the reading of history: a community that measures God's favour by its own prosperity will misread every event it experiences, and the sūrah's narratives have supplied three examples of exactly that error. The Book's counsel to its readers is to measure by the revelation rather than by circumstance, and the verse's statement of the flawless plan is the ground of that counsel.

The passage's two verses, moreover, are addressed to a messenger facing opposition, and their function is a reassurance about the outcome rather than a description of the deniers' psychology. The Book's narratives of the earlier messengers show the same pattern of delay and sudden resolution, and the sūrah's readers are given the pattern in general form before the sūrah turns to its final instructions.

The Muslim tradition's teaching about divine planning has drawn on this verse and its companions for the doctrine that the apparent success of the wicked is not evidence about God's purpose, and that the resolution of every account, individual and communal, is reserved to Him who stated His plan here.

The Book's language of firmness is applied to several of its own instruments — the mountains, the covenant, the revelation — and its use here for the divine plan gathers all of them into one guarantee. The sūrah's readers who have followed the narratives of communities that seemed immovable in their power are given the statement of what actually holds, and the statement is made in the voice of the One who holds it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:184

> **Have they not reflected? There is no madness in their companion. He is naught but a clear warner.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Have They Not Given It a Thought?"**

*A-wa-lam yatafakkarū mā bi-ṣāḥibihim min jinnatin in huwa illā nadhīrun mubīn* — have they not ever given it a thought? Their fellow man is not insane. He is only sent with a clear warning.

**The Appeal to Reflection**

*Awalam yatafakkarū* — the Book's opening question in this verse belongs to the Qur'an's most characteristic mode of address: an appeal to the reader to consider what he already knows. The sūrah's previous command to narrate the stories *so that they may reflect* (7:176) used the same verb, and the verse returns to the reader with the demand that the reflection be applied to the messenger himself.

**"Their Fellow Man"**

*Ṣāḥibihim* — their companion, the one who lives among them — is the Qur'an's argument from intimacy: the Makkan elite knew the Prophet's life, and the Book repeatedly appeals to that knowledge. The same argument appears in Sūrat Sabaʾ: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I advise you to do ˹only˺ one thing: stand up for ˹the sake of˺ Allah — individually or in pairs — then reflect. Your fellow man is not insane. He is only a warner to you before ˹the coming of˺ a severe punishment’”* (34:46).

**The Charge Answered in Three Sūrahs**

The Qur'an answers the accusation of madness against its messenger in several places, and each formulation adds its own emphasis: Sūrat al-Qalam's *“By the grace of your Lord, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not insane”* (68:2) follows the oath of the pen; Sūrat al-Takwīr's *“And your fellow man is not insane”* (81:22) follows the account of the revelation's transmission; and the sūrah's verse answers the charge in the setting of the sūrah's own historical narratives.

**Why the Accusation Was Made**

The Book's account of the Makkan response includes several explanations for the charge: an unfamiliar claim, a change in the community's standing, and the inconvenience of the message. The sūrah's readers are shown the accusation's content and the Book's reply, which is a statement of fact about a life they knew: the man who brought the message had spent a lifetime among them before it came.

**The Messenger's Humanity**

The Qur'an's insistence on the Prophet's humanity is a fixed feature of its message — *“I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God”* (18:110), and *“I do not say to you that I possess Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel”* (6:50). The accusation of madness and the claim of divinity are both answered by the same fact, and the sūrah's verse presents the ordinary, well-known life as evidence.

**"Only a Clear Warner"**

*Nadhīrun mubīn* — the Book's self-description of its messenger is consistent: a warner, a bearer of good news, a mercy, and a light. The sūrah's readers have watched the messengers of the earlier narratives perform exactly this function, and the verse identifies the Prophet with that office rather than with the claims his opponents feared.

**The Verse's Logic**

The verse's argument is that the message should be judged by its content and its messenger's record rather than by its inconvenience. The Book's readers are asked to think rather than to react, and the sūrah's placement of the question in its closing movement gives it the tone of a final appeal before the account of the Hour.

**The Sūrah's Pattern of Appeal**

The sūrah has addressed its readers as rational agents throughout: the sūrah's questions about the ruins of the towns, the parable's instruction to narrate so that people may reflect, and the present appeal. The Book's doctrine of revelation consistently rests on the premise that the message can be evaluated, and that the evaluation is the hearer's responsibility.

**The Next Verse's Reflection**

The verse that follows extends the appeal to the created order: *have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created, and that perhaps their end is near?* (7:185). The sūrah's reader is carried from the messenger to the cosmos, and the two together supply the grounds of the summons.

**The Book's Use of Familiarity**

The Qur'an's strongest argument in the Makkan sūrahs is often the audience's familiarity with the messenger, and the verse's phrase *their fellow man* is its most economical statement of that argument. The Book's readers are asked to compare the message with the man they know, and the sūrah trusts the comparison to yield its own conclusion.

**The Verse's Address to the Sūrah's Readers**

Read in the community's own life, the verse teaches that a messenger's ordinary life is part of his evidence, and the Book's traditions about the Prophet's conduct belong to the same conception. The sūrah's closing verses about listening, remembrance, and prostration describe the community's response to the message, and the verse's question is the door through which they enter.

The passage also supplies the criterion by which the Makkan charge is to be tested, and it is a criterion the sūrah's readers can apply to any claim about a messenger: the message is checked against the life, the life against the message, and the verdict of the community's own knowledge of its neighbor is left standing. The Book's appeal is not to a private experience but to a public record.

The verse's position among the closing appeals of the sūrah gives it the weight of a summary: the messenger's sanity is defended not by a miracle but by the ordinary evidence of his life among the people, and the Book's readers are asked to reason about what they already know. The verses that follow turn the same appeal to the created world, and the pair of arguments — the man and the world — closes the sūrah's long case against the deniers.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:185

> **Or have they not contemplated the dominion of the heavens and the earth, and what things God has created, and that their term may already have drawn nigh? So in what discourse after this will they believe?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Have They Reflected on the Heavens and the Earth?"**

*A-wa-lam yanẓurū fī malakūti al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-mā khalaqa Allāhu min shayʾin wa-an ʿasā an yakūna qadi iqtaraba ajaluhum fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn* — have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created, and that perhaps their end is near? So what message after this ˹Quran˺ will they believe in?

**The Command to Look**

*Awalam yanẓurū* — the Book's verb is looking, watching, the sustained observation of a thing. The sūrah's previous verse had asked its audience to think about the messenger (7:184); this verse sends the same audience to the created order, and the two appeals together constitute the sūrah's closing argument: the man and the world he lives in both testify to the same message.

**The Kingdom of the Heavens and the Earth**

*Malakūt al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — the Book's word for the realm of God's rule appears in the Qur'an's other appeals to reflection, and its use here frames the created order as a domain rather than a display. The Qur'an's readers are asked not merely to notice the world but to recognize whose it is, and the sūrah's narratives of the towns have all turned on that recognition.

**"Everything Allah Has Created"**

The verse's third object of reflection is the totality of the creation: the phrase leaves nothing outside the summons. The Book's correlative statement in Sūrat al-Anʿām describes the created order as a set of communities: *“All living beings roaming the earth and winged birds soaring in the sky are communities like yourselves. We have left nothing out of the Record”* (6:38). The sūrah's readers are told that the world's contents are not neutral material but signs.

**The Cosmological Argument in the Qur'an**

The Book states the argument from creation in its fullest form in two famous verses: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason”* (3:190), and its expansion in Sūrat al-Baqarah's catalogue of the ships, the rain, the winds, and the clouds (2:164). The sūrah's verse belongs to that family of passages, and its brevity assumes the reader's familiarity with them.

**Reflection on One's Own Being**

The Qur'an also directs the same reflection inward: *“Have they not reflected upon their own being? Allah only created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term”* (30:8). The sūrah's readers are given the created world and their own existence as the same evidence, and the Book's method is to move between the two without distinguishing them.

**"Perhaps Their End Is Near"**

The second half of the verse supplies the motive for the reflection: *ʿasā an yakūna qadi iqtaraba ajaluhum* — perhaps their term has drawn near. The Qur'an states the proximity of the Hour in its own opening verses and in the sūrahs of the final revelation: *“˹The time of˺ people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away”* (21:1); *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺”* (54:1); and the Book's counsel that the knowledge of the hour belongs to God alone with the observation that perhaps it is near (33:63).

**The Suddenness of the Hour**

The Qur'an's warnings about the Hour emphasize that it comes without warning: *“Are they waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺?”* (43:66). The sūrah's own account of the towns had used the same note — the punishment came upon them *while they were unaware* (7:95) — and the verse attaches the created order's testimony to the possibility of an imminent end.

**The Question at the End**

*Fa-bi-ayyi ḥadīthin baʿdahu yuʾminūn* — so in what message after this will they believe? The Book poses the question in similar form in Sūrat al-Jāthiyah: *“These are Allah's revelations which We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. So what message will they believe in after ˹denying˺ Allah and His revelations?”* (45:6), and Sūrat al-Mursalāt closes with the same challenge (77:50). The verse is the sūrah's final statement of the futility of refusing this recitation.

**The Refusal of Wonder**

The verse implies that the failure being addressed is not an absence of evidence but an absence of attention, and the sūrah's portrait of the heedless (7:179) had already named the faculties that were idle. The Book's appeal to the heavens and the earth thus has a diagnostic function: what a person looks at and what he refuses to look at reveal which faculties he uses.

**The Sūrah's Argument Assembled**

The sūrah's closing verses now read as a summary of its method: the messenger is vindicated by his own life (7:184), the creation is invoked as evidence (7:185), the doctrine of guidance is restated (7:186), and the Hour is named (7:187). The sūrah's readers are given the grounds of the summons after the long narrative of the communities that refused it.

**The Created World and the Sūrah's Readers**

For the community reciting the sūrah, the verse makes the created order a permanent text. The Qur'an's teaching about nature is inseparable from its teaching about revelation — the same hand is behind both — and the Book repeatedly treats the two as a single address. The sūrah's readers are told to read the world in the same manner in which they read the verses.

**The Verse's Rhetorical Shape**

The sentence proceeds from question to evidence to urgency to a final rhetorical question, and its structure is the Book's standard form of closing argument. The sūrah's readers are carried from what they can see to what they cannot know, and the verse leaves them with the choice the whole sūrah has been describing.

**The Answer Withheld**

The verse asks who will believe in what message after this one, and the Book does not answer, which is its way of leaving the question with the reader. The sūrah's own earlier statement about the guided and the straying (7:178, 7:186) supplies the answer's terms, and the reader is given the doctrine he needs to respond.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:186

> **Whomsoever God leads astray, no guide has he. And He leaves them to wander confused in their rebellion.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"None Can Guide Whom Allah Leaves to Stray"**

*Man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-lā hādiya lahu wa-yadharuhum fī ṭughyānihim yaʿmahūn* — whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.

**The Refrain Repeated**

The sūrah had stated the same law two verses earlier in other words (7:178), and its repetition here frames the passage about the signs of the creation and the Hour. The Book's use of such refrains gives its teaching a rhythm: the reader meets the same principle at intervals until it becomes the lens through which the sūrah's narratives are read.

**The Parallel Statements Elsewhere**

The Qur'an states the doctrine in several sūrahs, each with its own emphasis: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, you will find no guardians for them besides Him”* (17:97); *“And whoever Allah guides, none can lead astray. Is Allah not Almighty, capable of punishment?”* (39:37); and Sūrat al-Shūrā's *“And whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide after Him”* (42:44). The sūrah's verse is the Book's most compact statement of the first half of the pair.

**"Wandering Blindly"**

*Yaʿmahūn* — the verb describes the blind wandering of one who cannot tell the road from the field, and the Book uses the same image in Sūrat al-Baqarah: *“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance”* (2:15). The verse's picture is not of a person who has chosen a wrong direction but of one who can no longer judge directions.

**The Word for Defiance**

*Ṭughyān* — the Book's term for the overstepping that comes of prosperity, the arrogance of a creature that has forgotten the limits of its place. The sūrah uses the same root for the generation of Noah — *indeed they were a people who had gone too far* (7:64's clause at the close of that narrative) — and for the Egyptians (7:127). The blindness of the verse is the final stage of the condition the word describes.

**The Formula's Divine Voice**

The verse is stated in the third person, and its companion at verse 178 is stated in the same way; the two together place the doctrine of guidance firmly in the sūrah's account of God's disposal while the surrounding verses continue to address the reader as an agent. The Book's readers are given both levels in the same passage, and the sūrah's closing instructions to listen and remember belong to the second.

**The Verse and the Messenger**

The verse is spoken to console and to direct the messenger: the refusal he encounters is not evidence of a deficiency in his delivery. The Book states the same thing in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ and in the sūrah's own commission — the messenger's duty is to deliver the message — and the verse supplies the doctrine that relieves him of responsibility for the outcome.

**The Refusal of Coercion**

The Qur'an's statement that none can guide those whom God leaves to stray is also a statement about the impossibility of forced belief: no power besides God can produce the orientation the verse is describing. The Book's instructions about the community's conduct consistently follow from this, and its addresses to the Prophet's opponents rest on persuasion rather than compulsion.

**The Sūrah's Two Sentences and the Reader**

Read together, verses 178 and 186 bracket the passage on signs, parables, and the created world, and they state the same fact from the side of the outcome. The sūrah's reader is not told which side of the pair he occupies; he is told what the two outcomes are, and the closing verses of the sūrah tell him what to do about it.

**The Guidance in the Book's Vocabularies**

The Qur'an's vocabulary of guidance includes the showing of the way, the directing of the heart, and the granting of success, and the sūrah's readers are given the word in a range of senses across its narratives. The verse's denial is of the last of these to any power besides God, and the Book's other statements of the doctrine — the appeal to ask for guidance — belong with it.

**What the Verse Implies About the Straying**

The verse describes a condition that is active rather than passive: the blind wandering is the continuation of a course the person has taken. The Book's accounts of the deniers consistently present their error as a settled direction rather than a single misstep, and the sūrah's narratives of the towns, the calf, and the man of the signs illustrate the same point.

**The Verse's Pastoral Force**

For a community reading the sūrah, the verse teaches reliance on God rather than on method: the persuasive resources of the Book, its narratives and its reasons, are spent in the service of an outcome that belongs to God. The sūrah's closing verses about remembrance and reading implement that teaching, and the reader ends the sūrah in the posture the verse describes.

**The Turn to the Hour**

The next verse reports the question put to the Prophet about the Hour, and the sūrah's final movement continues with the things people cannot know. The doctrine stated here — that direction belongs to God — belongs with the sūrah's treatment of the hidden: the Hour, the unseen, and the terms of every community are alike reserved.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:187

> **They question thee about the Hour, when it will set in. Say, “Knowledge thereof lies only with my Lord. None save He shall manifest it at its proper time. Heavy shall it weigh upon the heavens and the earth. It shall not come upon you but suddenly.” They question thee as if thou knew it well. Say, “Knowledge thereof lies only with God, but most of mankind know not.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Ask You About the Hour"**

*Yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī lā yujallīhā li-waqtihā illā huwa thaqulat fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi lā taʾtīkum illā baghtatan yasʾalūnaka ka-annaka ḥafiyyun ʿanhā qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Allāhi wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn* — they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour — when will it be? Say: its knowledge is only with my Lord. None will reveal its time except Him. It weighs heavily on the heavens and the earth; it will come upon you all of a sudden. They ask you as if you are well informed about it. Say: its knowledge is only with Allah, but most people do not know.

**The Question and Its Answer**

The verse gives the question and the answer twice, which is the Book's way of fixing the matter. The Qur'an addresses the same question in Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt: *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, ‘When will it be?’ But it is not for you to tell its time. That knowledge rests with your Lord ˹alone˺”* (79:42–44), and in Sūrat al-Aḥzāb the counsel that perhaps the Hour is near follows the same reservation (33:63).

**"Its Knowledge Is Only With My Lord"**

The answer is not a refusal of the questioner so much as a statement of the boundary between the messenger and his Lord. The Book's instructions to the Prophet about what he may and may not claim cluster in the sūrahs, and the verse's first clause belongs with them: the messenger speaks what he is given, and the timing of the end is not among what he was given.

**"None Will Reveal Its Time Except Him"**

*Lā yujallīhā li-waqtihā illā huwa* — the Book's verb for unveiling is used for the disclosure of what is covered, and the verse states that the unveiling belongs to God alone. The Qur'an's doctrine of the hidden is stated in the same terms elsewhere — *“The Hour is sure to come. My Will is to keep it hidden, so that every soul may be rewarded according to their efforts”* (20:15) — and the withholding is given a purpose in the same verse.

**"It Weighs Heavily on the Heavens and the Earth"**

The image is of a burden that the created order itself cannot sustain, and the Book's language about the Day of Judgment uses the same register: the heavens will be folded and the earth changed. The verse's readers are told that the event they are asking about is not a date in a calendar but a transformation of everything, and the question's confidence is quietly rebuked.

**"It Will Come Upon You All of a Sudden"**

*Lā taʾtīkum illā baghtatan* — the suddenness is stated as the event's manner, and the Book consistently pairs the warning of the Hour with the impossibility of preparation at the last moment. The sūrah's own account of the towns had reported punishment arriving *while they were unaware* (7:95) and the destruction of a community in a single night, and the verse's readers are meant to hear the historical pattern as a foretaste.

**"As If You Were Well Informed About It"**

The questioners' manner is described in the verse: they press the Prophet as though the date were his to disclose. The Book's portrayal of the demand for signs and dates in the Makkan sūrahs is unflattering, and the sūrah's readers are shown the questioners' assumption being dismantled in the answer that follows.

**The Knowledge Reserved**

The Qur'an's statements about what is withheld from creatures are numerous and consistent: *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs”* (31:34); the Book's counsel that every community has an appointed term (10:49); and the Prophet's declaration that he does not possess God's treasuries or know the unseen (6:50). The verse's answer is one member of a series.

**The Tradition's Reading**

The commentators noted that the verse was revealed in response to the Makkans' repeated questioning, and that its closing clause — *but most people do not know* — explains why the question is asked at all. The tradition also drew from the passage the principle that speculating about the date of the Hour is not a legitimate occupation of the believing community, and the sūrah's other verses about the hidden support the same conclusion.

**The Verse's Placement**

The verse follows the appeal to reflect on the heavens and the earth and precedes the command to the Prophet to state his own limits (7:188), and the sequence is a single argument about the boundaries of knowledge: creation testifies, the end is hidden, and the messenger knows only what he was given. The sūrah's reader is thus given the shape of a religious mind at the close of the sūrah's narratives.

**The Two Sayings in the One Verse**

The verse's answer is given twice, once with *my Lord* and once with *Allah*, and the second is addressed to the questioners more directly than the first. The Book's use of the two forms is deliberate, and the sūrah's readers are shown the messenger speaking in his own person and then in his office.

**What the Verse Asks of Its Readers**

The question about the Hour is answered by a statement of ignorance, and the reader is invited to share the posture: the Book's teaching is that the appointed time is a matter for the One who appointed it, and the community's part is to live in readiness. The sūrah's closing instructions about listening, remembrance, and patience follow in that spirit.

**The Hour and the Sūrah's Narratives**

The sūrah's long account of destroyed communities has been a series of small hours, each arriving at its own appointed time, and the verse's topic is the same fact at the scale of the whole. The reader who has followed the towns, Egypt, and Israel to their endings is prepared for the sūrah's final statement that the last of the appointments is also fixed and also hidden.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:188

> **Say, “I have no power over what benefit or harm may come to me, save as God wills. Had I knowledge of the unseen, I would have acquired much good, and no evil would have touched me. I am naught save a warner and a bearer of glad tidings unto a people who believe.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Say: I Have No Power to Benefit or Protect Myself"**

*Qul lā amliku li-nafsī nafʿan wa-lā ḍarran illā mā shāʾa Allāhu wa-law kuntu aʿlamu al-ghayba la-stakthartu mina al-khayri wa-mā massaniya al-sūʾu in anā illā nadhīrun wa-bashīrun li-qawmin yuʾminūn* — say, "I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah. If I had known the unknown, I would have benefited myself enormously, and no harm would have ever touched me. I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe."

**The Command to Say**

The verse opens with the Book's verb of declaration, and what follows is a statement of the messenger's own limits. The Qur'an puts such declarations on the Prophet's tongue repeatedly, and their function is to define the office: the messenger delivers what he is given and makes no claim to the powers his audience attributed to their gods or their soothsayers.

**"No Power to Benefit or Protect Myself"**

The Book states the same confession in Sūrat al-Jinn: *“Say, ‘It is not in my power to harm or benefit you’”* (72:21), and in Sūrat Yūnus with reference to the community's own appointed term (10:49). The sūrah's readers are told that the Prophet is a creature among creatures, and that the benefit and harm people seek from him are not his to dispense.

**"Except by the Will of Allah"**

The exception is the Book's standard qualification, and it preserves the messenger's humanity without setting him outside the realm of God's action. The Qur'an's declarations of human inability are consistently followed by this clause, and the sūrah's readers are given the correct boundary: the prophet is a servant through whom God may act, not a partner in the act.

**"If I Had Known the Unknown"**

The second sentence addresses the Makkans' assumption that a prophet is a source of hidden knowledge: if the Prophet had it, he says, he would have used it for good and avoided every harm. The Qur'an states the same denial elsewhere — *“I do not say to you that I possess Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel”* (6:50), and the Book's instruction in Sūrat al-Māʾidah that the messenger's duty is only to deliver the message (5:99).

**The Prophet's Humanity in the Qur'an**

The Book's insistence on the humanity of its messengers recurs throughout the Qur'an, and its most concise form is the command to the Prophet to say that he is a man like other men: *“I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God”* (18:110). The sūrah's verse belongs to that set, and its readers are given a portrait of the messenger stripped of the supernatural claims made for holy men elsewhere.

**The Office of Warner and Deliverer of Good News**

*Nadhīrun wa-bashīrun* — the sūrah's earlier verse had described the Prophet as a clear warner (7:184), and the pairing of warning with good news is the Qur'an's standard description of the prophetic function. The Book's readers are told what a messenger actually does: he states the consequences of both courses and leaves the outcome to his hearers and to God.

**"For Those Who Believe"**

The verse's last words attach the good news to a condition: the announcement is for a people who believe. The Qur'an attaches similar qualifiers to its promises throughout, and the sūrah's readers are shown the economy of the message: the warning is universal and the good news is received by those who respond.

**The Refusal of Divination**

The verse's second sentence is the Book's rejection of the whole apparatus of soothsaying and divination in a single clause. The Qur'an's readers are told that knowledge of the future is not a commodity available to holy men, and the sūrah's earlier treatment of the question about the Hour (7:187) belongs with the same doctrine.

**What the Verse Confirms**

The verse does not diminish the messenger; it defines him. The Book's portraits of the prophets consistently present them as men whose authority lies in what they were given rather than in what they control, and the sūrah's readers are given the contrast with the diviners and patrons of the Makkan religious marketplace.

**The Sūrah's Two Questions Answered**

The sūrah had reported the demand for the Hour's date (7:187) and the slander of madness (7:184); the verse answers both at once. The messenger knows neither the date nor the hidden things, and his sanity is attested by the teaching he delivers rather than by claims to power.

**The Pastoral Weight of the Verse**

Read in the community's own life, the verse teaches that reliance belongs to God and not to the messenger, and the sūrah's closing instructions about remembrance and recitation follow from the same principle. The Book's readers are given a religion in which the human bearer of the message is honored precisely because he does not claim what belongs to God.

**The Turn to the Creation**

The next verse returns to the theme of the created order and to the human being made from a single soul (7:189), and the sūrah's final movement continues with the signs in humanity itself. The verse's statement of the messenger's limits is thus followed by the Book's account of what every human being is, and the reader is carried from the office to the nature.

The verse's closing sentence also prepares the sūrah's final turn to the human being himself: the messenger disclaims knowledge and power, and the next verse describes what every person is — a creature made from a single soul and given a spouse and children. The Book's readers are carried from the limits of the prophetic office to the facts of their own nature, and the passage ends where every person can verify it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:189

> **He it is Who created you from a single soul, and made from it its mate, that he might find rest in her. Then, when he covered her, she bore a light burden, and carried it about. But when she had grown heavy, they called upon God, their Lord, “If Thou giveth us a healthy child, we shall surely be among the thankful.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Created You From a Single Soul"**

*Huwa alladhī khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin wa-jaʿala minhā zawjahā li-yaskuna ilayhā fa-lammā taghashshāhā ḥamalat ḥamlan khafīfan fa-marrat bihi fa-lammā athqalat daʿawā Allāha rabbahumā la-in ātaytanā ṣāliḥan la-nakūnanna mina al-shākirīn* — He is the One Who created you from a single soul, then from it made its spouse so he may find comfort in her. After he had been united with her, she carried a light burden and went about with it. Then when it grew heavy, they both prayed to Allah, their Lord: "If You grant us a good child, we will surely be grateful."

**The Single Soul**

The Book's account of human origins begins with the one soul and the pair made from it, and the sūrah states the same fact in the same words as Sūrat al-Nisāʾ (4:1) and Sūrat al-Zumar (39:6). The phrase's function in the sūrah is to establish the unity of the human family before the narrative turns to the family's own prayers and, in the next verse, to its failures.

**"So He May Find Comfort in Her"**

The verse gives the purpose of the pairing in a single word: *li-yaskuna ilayhā*, so that he may find rest in her. The Qur'an's statements about marriage use the vocabulary of tranquility and companionship — *“He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them”* (30:21) — and the sūrah's readers are shown the institution as a provision rather than a transaction.

**The Spouses as a Sign**

The Qur'an's treatment of the pair belongs to its catalogues of signs: Sūrat al-Rūm names the creation of spouses among the signs and adds the compassion and mercy placed between them (30:21), and Sūrat al-Naḥl lists spouses, children, and grandchildren among God's gifts (16:72). The sūrah's verse is the same teaching in narrative form, and its placement in the sūrah's closing movement gives it the weight of a summary.

**The Light Burden**

The verse's description of the pregnancy is deliberately gentle: a light burden that the woman carries about without difficulty. The Book's narratives of human life attend to the ordinary course of things, and the sūrah's readers are shown the beginning of a family in the plainest terms before the verse reports the prayer that came with its completion.

**When the Burden Grew Heavy**

*Fa-lammā athqalat* — when she grew heavy — the verse marks the point at which the couple's dependence becomes conscious, and their prayer follows. The Qur'an's presentation of human need is that it becomes articulate in extremity, and the sūrah's readers are given the moment of prayer in the natural sequence of a pregnancy.

**"They Both Prayed to Allah, Their Lord"**

The verse's *daʿawā Allāha rabbahumā* — the dual verb — makes the prayer a joint act, and the appeal to God as *their Lord* states the relationship on which the petition rests. The Book's prayers are consistently addressed by its own vocabulary, and the sūrah's readers are shown a couple beginning their parenthood with a supplication rather than with an assumption.

**"If You Grant Us a Good Child"**

*La-in ātaytanā ṣāliḥan* — if You grant us a good, righteous child — the condition is stated as the content of the request, and the promise that follows is gratitude. The Qur'an's prayers for offspring recur in its narratives — Zakariyyā's plea, Abraham's supplication, and the servants of the Most Compassionate who ask for comfort from spouses and children (25:74) — and the sūrah's verse belongs to that family of passages.

**The Gratitude Promised**

The couple's promise is to be among the grateful, and the Book's readers have already met the theme in the sūrah's warnings against the nation that was not grateful (7:130's provision misread, and 34:13's few grateful servants). The verse thus states the right response to a gift before the next verse reports the response that was actually made.

**The Book's Family Theology**

The Qur'an treats the family as the primary school of faith and the primary field of its testing, and the sūrah's verse stands near the beginning of that treatment. The Book's readers are shown a couple whose first act as parents is a prayer, and the tradition drew from the passage its teaching about supplication for children and about the gratitude owed for them.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The sūrah has moved from the created order and the Hour to the human being's own origin and family, and the movement continues into the verses about the idols that follow. The Book's arrangement is characteristic: the reader is taken from the widest frame to the most intimate, and the charge that follows is addressed to the intimacy he has just been shown.

**The Shadow of the Next Verse**

The verse ends with the couple's promise, and the next verse reports what their descendants did with the gift: *“But when He granted their descendants good offspring, they associated false gods in what He has given them”* (7:190). The sūrah's readers are thus given the prayer and its outcome in consecutive sentences, and the passage becomes a study of how a gift is received.

The Book's narratives of the patriarchs are full of such prayers for children — Zakariyyā's plea behind the sanctuary, Abraham's supplication for righteous offspring, and the believers described in Sūrat al-Furqān who ask for comfort in their spouses and offspring (25:74) — and the sūrah's verse gives the same petition in its simplest form: a good child and the gratitude that should follow.

The tradition read the verse's couple as the beginning of the human family and drew from the passage its teaching that the first response to a child should be a prayer and a promise rather than an assumption of possession. The Book's readers are shown the gift being asked for and the vow attached to it, and the next verse shows what happened when the gift was given. Nothing in the passage suggests that the couple's gratitude was universal in their descendants, and the sūrah's critique falls on those who received without returning thanks.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:190

> **Then, when He gave them a healthy child, they ascribed partners unto Him with regard to that which He had given them. Exalted is God above the partners they ascribe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Associated False Gods in What He Has Given Them"**

*Fa-lammā ātāhumā ṣāliḥan jaʿalā lahu shurakāʾa fīmā ātāhumā fa-taʿālā Allāhu ʿammā yushrikūn* — but when He granted their descendants good offspring, they associated false gods in what He has given them. Exalted is Allah above what they associate ˹with Him˺!

**The Verse's Antecedent**

The sentence completes the couple's prayer from the previous verse: they had asked for a good child and promised gratitude (7:189), and the report now follows. The verse's subject is disputed among the commentators, and the translation's *they* keeps the ambiguity of the Qur'an's own pronoun, which is the Book's usual practice in such passages.

**The Commentators' Discussions**

The tradition's exegetes read the pair either as Adam and Eve and their descendants, or as a generic pair whose story serves as the type of every household. The second reading is supported by the verse's plural verbs and by the presence of the same charge in the sūrah's other narratives of Israel, whose ancestors had made the calf. What the passage establishes in either reading is the pattern: a gift is given, and the giver is joined with others.

**"In What He Has Given Them"**

The phrase names the offense precisely: the association was made with respect to the very gift that had been asked of God. The Qur'an's charge against ingratitude is consistently framed this way — the creature uses the gift as a claim against the giver — and the sūrah's readers have already met the pattern in Egypt's prosperity and in Israel's abundance in the wilderness.

**The Book's Catalogue of the Same Offense**

The Qur'an states the charge against the polytheists in several registers: *“Yet they associate the jinn with Allah ˹in worship˺, even though He created them”* (6:100); the claim of the Makkans that their gods were merely intercessors (10:18); and the declaration of Sūrat al-Zumar that sincere devotion belongs to God alone while others take lords besides Him to bring them closer to Him (39:3). The sūrah's verse names the offense at the level of the family.

**"Exalted Is Allah Above What They Associate With Him"**

The Book's closing formula in the verse is its standard response to association, and its placement here is significant: the sūrah does not argue the case again but states the divine transcendence and moves on to the idols themselves. The verses that follow subject the associate-gods to a systematic examination, and the formula of verse 190 marks the transition.

**The Family and the Gift in the Qur'an**

The Book's treatment of children as provisions rather than possessions appears throughout the Qur'an, and its warnings about them are stated with the same vocabulary: the Qur'an describes children as a trial and wealth as well as a gift. The sūrah's readers are shown a family that received what it asked for, and the verse's charge is that the gift became the occasion of the offense.

**The Doctrine of Gratitude**

The Qur'an's teaching about gratitude is a doctrine about the recognition of the source of things, and its opposite is not merely forgetfulness but the assignment of the gift's benefits to another power. The sūrah's readers have met the same teaching in the story of the people who were given the land and the produce, and the verse in this passage states it at the level of the household.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The verse stands between the creation of the human pair and the long examination of the idols that occupies the sūrah's final verses, and its function is to explain how the examination became necessary. The Book's method here is the same as in its narratives of the towns: the offense is named first, and the argument against it follows.

**What the Verse Establishes About the Human Being**

Read with the previous verse, the passage presents the human being as the creature who prays in need and forgets in sufficiency. The Book's anthropology is consistent on this point, and the sūrah's readers are given the diagnosis in the two verses together rather than in a treatise.

**The Warning Inside the Passage**

The passage's warning is addressed to the community receiving the recitation as much as to the people of the story: the sūrah has been a long record of communities that were given gifts and turned aside, and the couple's prayer and its outcome make the same point at the smallest possible scale. The Book's readers are asked to recognize the pattern in their own household.

**The Tradition's Use of the Verse**

The commentators drew from the passage a caution about the pride of parents in their children, and the Book's readers were reminded that the child is a trust whose coming should deepen gratitude rather than complacency. The verse's charge is not against the desire for children but against the disposition that treats them as possessions.

**The Turn to the Idols**

The next verse begins the sūrah's systematic examination of the associate-gods: whether they can create, whether they can help, whether they respond, and whether they possess any of the attributes their worshippers ascribed to them. The passage's readers are carried from the story of the family to the argument about the gods, and the two are connected by the same question of gratitude.

The sūrah's manner of introducing the subject is also part of its teaching: the idols are not discussed at first as an abstract question of doctrine but as the sequel to a family's prayer. The Book's readers meet the theology of worship inside the story of gratitude, and the arrangement makes the later polemic the explication of a human failure rather than a dispute about ideas.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:191

> **Do they ascribe as partners those who created naught and are themselves created?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Those Which Cannot Create Anything"**

*A-yushrikūna mā lā yakhluqu shayʾan wa-hum yukhlaqūn* — do they associate ˹with Allah˺ those ˹idols˺ which cannot create anything, but are in fact created;

**The Question's Form**

The verse opens the sūrah's final examination of the idols with a question, which is the Book's method in its polemics: the reader is invited to test the proposition rather than to receive a verdict. The question's content is the distinction on which the whole Qur'anic argument about divinity rests — the creator and the created — and the sūrah's readers are asked where the associate-gods stand.

**"Cannot Create Anything"**

The Qur'an's argument against the idols begins with the one act that defines divinity in the Book's vocabulary: creation from nothing, the bringing of a thing into being. The idols not only fail to create; the verse's second clause states the sharper fact, that they are themselves created. The Book's readers are thus given an argument in a single sentence that no appeal to tradition or intercession can answer.

**The Same Argument in Sūrat al-Naḥl**

The Book states the case in almost identical words elsewhere: *“But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created. They are dead, not alive—not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected”* (16:20–21). The sūrah's verse is the Book's doctrine in summary form, and the two passages belong to the same argumentative tradition within the Qur'an.

**The Formulation in Sūrat al-Furqān**

Sūrat al-Furqān's version of the same charge adds the idols' inability to benefit themselves: *“Yet they have taken besides Him gods who cannot create anything but are themselves created. Nor can they protect or benefit themselves. Nor can they control life, death, or resurrection”* (25:3). The sūrah's verse and its companion in Sūrat al-Furqān together supply the full catalogue of what the associate-gods lack.

**The Book's Catalogue of the Idols' Limits**

The Qur'an returns to the theme in passage after passage: the idols possess not even an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth (34:22); they can never create so much as a fly even if all assembled for the attempt (22:73); and they are aware of nothing their worshippers say (46:5). The sūrah's readers are given a cumulative dossier rather than a single argument, and its consistency is the point.

**The Sūrah's Own Calf Narrative**

The sūrah had already applied the test to the golden calf, in the account of Israel's worship in Moses' absence: *“Did they not see that it could neither speak to them nor guide them to the ˹Right˺ Path? Still they took it as a god and were wrongdoers”* (7:148), and Sūrat Ṭāhā's version states the same test in a question (20:89). The sūrah's verse now generalizes the argument to all the associate-gods of Arabia.

**The Rhetoric of the Question**

The verse's question is addressed to the idolaters as a demand for explanation, and its form is the Book's characteristic way of stating the obvious: the reader who has any notion of what divinity means is expected to see the impossibility. The Qur'an's polemic avoids invective in these verses and relies on the mismatch between the claim and the object.

**The Argument's Logic**

The distinction the verse draws is not between powerful and weak creatures but between the creator and the created order as such, and the Book's readers are shown that no amount of honor or antiquity can move an object from one category to the other. The sūrah's narratives of the nations that worshipped what they had made with their own hands supply the illustration, and the verse supplies the rule.

**What the Worshippers Claimed**

The Book's accounts of Arab idolatry attribute to the worshipers the belief that their gods were intercessors and that they brought them closer to God (10:18, 39:3), and the verse's argument answers that claim at its base. An intercessor at least exists and hears; the sūrah's readers are asked to consider what the object in front of them can do.

**The Qur'an's Positive Statement of the Same Fact**

The Book's arguments against the idols are always accompanied by the statement of what God alone possesses, and the sūrah's next verses supply it in the Prophet's own declaration that his Protector is the One who revealed the Book (7:196). The sūrah's readers are given the negative and the positive statements in the same passage.

**The Reader's Position**

The verse's question is put to the sūrah's audience in every generation, and its relevance is not confined to stone or metal: the Book's argument concerns anything that is treated as a source of what only God provides. The sūrah's readers are asked to test whatever they rely on against the same two clauses.

**The Next Verse's Addition**

The next verse adds the second test — whether the associate-gods can help their worshippers or themselves (7:192) — and the examination proceeds by accumulation. The sūrah's reader is given a series of questions, each of which isolates one attribute of divinity, and the passage's conclusion is left to be drawn by the reader himself.

The Book's argument also presupposes something about its hearers that is worth noticing: it assumes they can distinguish between a thing made and a maker, and it expects the distinction to settle the question. The sūrah's readers are addressed as people whose own experience of making things — the sūrah's idolaters had fashioned their gods with their hands — supplies the answer before the argument is finished.

The sūrah's readers in the community of the Prophet were reminded by these verses of the distance between the Creator and the created, and the tradition's creeds took shape around exactly this distinction. The verses that examine creation, help, response, and anatomy are thus not a set of separate arguments but variations on one: whatever is made is not God.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:192

> **Those who can neither help them, nor help themselves?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Which Cannot Help Them, or Even Help Themselves"**

*Wa-lā yastaṭīʿūna lahum naṣran wa-lā anfusahum yanṣurūn* — which cannot help them, or even help themselves?

**The Second Test**

The verse adds the test of help to the test of creation, and the order is the Book's: existence first, then the capacity to be of use. The idols fail both, and the verse's second clause is the more damaging, since it removes even the self-interest that a worshipper might attribute to his god.

**The Book's Repeated Pair**

The Qur'an states the two failures together in Sūrat al-Furqān — the gods who can neither protect nor benefit themselves (25:3) — and in Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ: *“Or do they have gods—other than Us—that can protect them? They cannot ˹even˺ protect themselves, nor will they be aided against Us”* (21:43). The sūrah's verse is one member of a set of formulations that the Book repeats until the point is unavoidable.

**The Vocabulary of Help**

The Qur'an's word *naṣr* — help, victory, support — belongs to its language of reliance, and the Book uses it for the aid that God gives His messengers and the believers. Its use here in the negative is deliberate: the sūrah's readers are told that the beings they invoke cannot supply the one thing the word means.

**The Test of Self-Sufficiency**

The verse's second clause, that the idols cannot help even themselves, is aimed at the assumption that a god might at least be useful to its own interests. The Book's readers are shown the test in its weakest form and the idol still failing it, and the sūrah's calf narrative had made the same point: the calf could not speak, guide, or defend itself.

**What the Verse Implies About the Worshippers**

The sūrah's argument implies that the idolaters had transferred to their gods a capacity they themselves possessed in part: a human being can at least help his neighbor. The Book's satire in such passages is severe because the exchange described is a descent — the worshipper credits a stone with less than he himself can do.

**The Book's Polemic and Its Economy**

The Qur'an's arguments against the idols are consistently brief in their premises and vast in their application, and this verse is an example: two clauses dismantle the claim of an entire cult. The Book's method is to assume that a hearer who knows what divinity means will draw the conclusion, and the sūrah's readers are trusted with the inference.

**The Continuation in the Same Sūrah**

The sūrah returns to the same argument in verse 197, where the same sentence is repeated almost verbatim, and the repetition frames the passage about the Prophet's Protector (7:196). The Book's readers are thus given the idols' incapacity on either side of the declaration of God's protection, and the arrangement is not accidental.

**The Idols' Silence in the Book's Narratives**

The Qur'an's accounts of the Judgment include a scene in which the associate-gods themselves renounce their worshippers, and the sūrah's readers are reminded that the beings invoked will not even acknowledge the invocation (10:28–29, 16:86). The present verse's silence is thus a foretaste of the final rejection, and the two belong to the same discourse.

**The Next Verse's Test**

The following verse turns from capacity to response: whether the idols answer those who call on them for guidance (7:193). The sūrah's examination of the associate-gods thus proceeds through creation, help, self-help, and speech before it reaches the challenge of verse 194, and the accumulation gives the passage its rhetorical force.

**The Believers' Corresponding Certainty**

The Book's argument against the idols serves its positive teaching about reliance, and the sūrah's next verses declare the Prophet's own protector (7:196) and the Book that was revealed to him. The sūrah's readers are shown what a worshipper's trust looks like when its object is the One who creates, helps, and protects.

**The Reader Addressed**

The verse's question is directed at those who associate and at every reader after them, and the Book's readers are invited to consider what their own reliance is placed on. The sūrah's narratives of the towns and the nations have already shown the cost of the mistake, and the verse states its anatomy in a line.

**The Argument's Place in the Sūrah's Close**

The sūrah's final movement has gathered the created world, the messenger's limits, the human family, and the idols, and the examination of the idols is the last of its cases. The Book's argumentative sequence ends with the question of what a person relies on, and the sūrah's closing instructions about recitation, remembrance, and prostration supply the answer in practice.

The verse's brevity also puts the reader on notice about the form the sūrah's argument will take from this point: the rest of the passage is a sequence of short tests, and the reader is expected to hold the results together. The Book's rhetoric here is cumulative rather than demonstrative, and its force depends on the reader's willingness to follow it to the end.

The sūrah's earlier narratives had shown what happens when a community relies on what cannot help it, and the verse's two clauses are the doctrine those narratives illustrated: the sea, the manna, and the deliverance came from the power the towns had forgotten. The sūrah's readers are asked to see the test of the idols as a test of their own reliance.

The Book's readers are also given the measure of what help means in the sūrah's vocabulary: the next verses name the Protector who helps the righteous (7:196) and identify Him as the One who revealed the Book. The verse's negative statement and its positive counterpart are thus placed within a few lines of each other, and the reader is left to make the exchange.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:193

> **And if you call them to guidance, they follow you not. It is the same for you whether you call them or whether you remain silent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"If You Call Upon Them for Guidance, They Cannot Respond"**

*Wa-in tadʿūhum ilā al-hudā lā yattabiʿūkum sawāʾun ʿalaykum a-daʿawtumūhum am antum ṣāmitūn* — and if you ˹idolaters˺ call upon them for guidance, they cannot respond to you. It is all the same whether you call them or remain silent.

**The Third Test: Response**

The verse moves from capacity to communication, and the test is the one that matters most to a worshipper: whether the being he addresses can hear and answer. The Qur'an's argument in these verses proceeds by eliminating each attribute in turn, and the present verse removes hearing, response, and even the difference between speech and silence.

**The Book's Accounts of the Idols' Failure to Hear**

The Qur'an states the same fact in several places: the idols are unaware of the calls of those who invoke them (46:5); they cannot hear; and the Book's account of the Judgment has them disowning their worshippers outright (10:28–29). The sūrah's readers are thus given the hidden form of the scene in this verse and its final form on the Day of Judgment.

**"It Is All the Same Whether You Call Them or Remain Silent"**

The clause is a device for closing the last possibility: if a being does not hear, the difference between calling and silence disappears. The Book's rhetoric in these verses works by exhausting hypotheticals, and the sūrah's readers are shown a series of tests each of which ends in the same result.

**The Idolaters' Own Knowledge**

The Book's charge against the idolaters includes the observation that they knew the objects of their worship were inanimate, and the sūrah's calf narrative had reported that the people could see their idol could not speak (7:148). The verse's argument is thus addressed to a knowledge the hearers already had, and the Book's readers are shown that the error was not intellectual but moral.

**The Book's Dialogue With the Idols at the Judgment**

Sūrat al-Naḥl reports the exchange that will take place when the polytheists see their associate-gods and address them, and the gods' answer is not an intercession but a rebuttal (16:86). The sūrah's readers are told that the silence of the idols in this life will become speech against them in the next, and the two scenes are meant to be read together.

**The Argument From Experience**

The verse's premise is one that any hearer could test, and the Book's polemic in this passage relies on immediate experience rather than on revelation for its first step. The sūrah's readers are invited to consider what they have observed, and the conclusion follows without the need for further argument.

**The Book's Refusal of Compromise**

The Qur'an's monotheism does not permit the retention of the idols as intermediaries, and its arguments are framed to make the position untenable on its own terms. The sūrah's readers are shown the Book meeting the polytheists' claim at the point where they themselves located it — the usefulness of the gods — and dismantling it there.

**The Next Verse's Challenge**

The next verse states that the idols are created beings like their worshippers and challenges the idolaters to call upon them and see (7:194). The sūrah's examination thus moves from the statement of incapacity to a direct invitation, and the readers are given the argument's final form.

**The Believers' Corresponding Practice**

The Book's positive teaching about supplication is placed beside its critique of the idols, and the sūrah's later instructions about calling upon God by His names (7:180) and seeking refuge with Him (7:200) supply it. The sūrah's readers are told whom to call on in the same passage in which they are told whom not to call on.

**What the Verse Denies**

The verse denies the possibility of guidance from any source other than God, and its argument is thus inseparable from the sūrah's doctrine of guidance stated earlier in the same sequence (7:178, 7:186). The idols cannot respond; the sūrah's readers are told that the One who can has already spoken and continues to speak through the recitation.

**The Chapter's Cumulative Effect**

The verses 191–193 leave the associate-gods without creation, help, self-help, speech, or response, and the accumulation is designed to make the reader consider what remains. The Book's method is to remove every attribute in turn until the object's status is evident, and the passage's terseness gives it its rhetorical power.

**The Reader's Own Test**

The sūrah's question is addressed to each reader in the shape of his own reliance, and the Book's stories of the nations that trusted in what they had made are the standing illustration. The sūrah's final verses about the Qur'an's recitation and about the angels' prostration state the alternative in practice, and the reader is carried from the critique to the worship.

The verse's statement about the futility of calling is also a statement about the nature of guidance, and it belongs with the sūrah's earlier declarations that guidance and straying are in God's hand (7:178, 7:186). The sūrah's readers are told that no created thing can supply what only God bestows, and the critique of the idols is the negative form of that doctrine.

The Book's account of the idols' inability to respond is coupled throughout the Qur'an with the account of God's responsiveness, and the sūrah's readers are given the contrast in the same movement: the idols cannot hear, and the Book's own instruction is that God hears the suppliant (7:200's closing names). The sūrah's theology is stated by contrast as much as by assertion.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:194

> **Truly those whom they call upon apart from God are servants like you. So call upon them! Let them answer you, if you are truthful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Created Beings Like Yourselves"**

*Inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāhi ʿibādun amthālukum fa-dʿūhum fa-l-yastajībū lakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah are created beings like yourselves. So call upon them and see if they will answer you, if your claims are true!

**The Sentence's Sharpest Form**

The verse states that the objects of worship are servants like their worshippers, and the Qur'an's argument reaches its most compressed form here. The Book's use of the word for servants rather than for created things alone is deliberate: the idols belong to the same category as the human beings who bow to them, and both stand in need of God.

**The Same Idea in Sūrat Fāṭir**

The Qur'an's statement of universal need is made in a verse whose audience is all of humanity: *“O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”* (35:15). The sūrah's verse places the idols inside the same sentence, and the readers are shown the leveling effect of the Book's doctrine on every object of worship.

**The Challenge Issued**

*Fa-dʿūhum fa-l-yastajībū lakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — the invitation to call upon the idols and await an answer is a challenge to the worshippers' own claim. The Book uses this device elsewhere, as in its invitation to those who claim intercessors to produce their evidence, and the sūrah's readers are shown the argument being handed to the other side for testing.

**The Book's Challenges to the Associators**

The Qur'an states its challenges in several forms: the command to call upon those claimed besides Allah with the observation that they possess not an atom's weight (34:22), and the famous comparison of the idols' inability to create even a fly (22:73). The sūrah's readers are given a series of such invitations, each of which asks the idolaters to demonstrate their claim.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Calf Scene**

The sūrah had already applied the test to Israel's idol in Moses' absence: the people could see that the calf made no reply and offered no guidance (7:148). The verse's challenge generalizes that scene, and the sūrah's readers are shown the same argument operating in Israel's history and in the Arab present.

**The Sarcasm of the Invitation**

The verse's closing condition — if your claims are true — is a rhetorical device the Book uses for claims it regards as untenable, and the sūrah's readers are invited to hear the invitation's tone. The Qur'an's polemic in these verses is confident to the point of offering the demonstration to its opponents, and the confidence is grounded in the test's outcome.

**The Origin of Idolatry in the Book's Account**

The Qur'an explains the worship of the associate-gods as an inheritance from ancestors, as a claim about intercession, and as an attachment to objects made by human hands. The sūrah's readers are given the account in Sūrat al-Zumar's report of the pagans' own words — that they worship the idols only so they may bring them closer to God (39:3) — and the verse's argument dismantles the claim at its root.

**The Intercessors' Claim and Its Refutation**

The Book's refutation of intercession is not a denial of all mediation but a denial that the idols can perform it, and the verses that follow state the positive alternative in the Prophet's declaration of God's protection (7:196). The sūrah's readers are shown what may be relied on and what may not.

**The Argument's Cumulative Force**

The sūrah's examination now includes creation (7:191), help (7:192), response (7:193), and now the standing of the idols as servants. Each verse removes one more claim, and the reader who follows the sequence finds himself without a case for the associate-gods at all.

**The Verse's Implication for the Worshippers**

If the idols are servants like their worshippers, then the worship directed to them is addressed to equals, and the sūrah's readers are asked to consider what such an address implies about the worshipper's own standing. The Book's doctrine is that the human being was created for the worship of God alone, and the verse shows what happens when the object of that capacity is misplaced.

**The Next Verse's Anatomy**

The following verse examines the idols' bodies: feet, hands, eyes, and ears (7:195), and the sūrah's readers are shown the physical form of the argument. The Book's critique of idolatry includes this almost clinical inventory, and its effect is to make the object of worship visible as an object.

**The Reader's Own Position**

The verse's test is available to every reader, and the Book's readers are invited to apply it to whatever they invoke besides God. The sūrah's closing instructions about recalling God inwardly and reciting the Qur'an (7:204–205) describe the practice that follows from the verse's conclusion, and the passage ends where the sūrah means it to.

The verse's phrase about the idols as servants places them in the same category as the angels, who are described in the sūrah's final verse as worshipers who are not too proud to serve (7:206), and the comparison is instructive: the worshipped beings of the Qur'an are worshipers themselves. The sūrah's readers are given the company the idols keep, and the company is not the one their worshippers imagined.

The verse's challenge also belongs to the sūrah's treatment of the demand for signs, which occupies the passage's final verses: the idolaters who asked the Prophet to produce a miracle are invited to obtain one from their own gods first. The Book's readers are shown the demand being returned to its makers, and the sūrah's answer to the request follows soon afterwards (7:203).

The verse's tone is not anger but assurance, and the Book's polemics in its Makkan sūrahs frequently sound this way: the argument is made, the challenge is offered, and the messenger continues his work. **What the Worshippers' Gods Lack**

The inventory's items are not random: walking, grasping, seeing, and hearing are the capacities by which a being engages the world, and the idols have none of them. The sūrah's readers are told that the objects of the cult cannot act, cannot show themselves, and above all cannot hear the prayers addressed to them.

The sūrah's accounts of the towns and of Israel had repeatedly shown communities relying on what could not save them — the calf, the court of Egypt, the numbers of a tribe — and the verse now states the anatomy of that reliance. **The Reader's Own Test**

The verse's questions are directed at every reader who has ever placed his confidence in something other than God, and the inventory can be applied to whatever that thing is. **The Passage's Movement**

The sūrah's final treatment of the idols now proceeds from their creation (7:191) through their help (7:192), their response (7:193), their servitude (7:194), and their bodies (7:195), and the next verses repeat and complete the examination. The Book's method in the passage is exhaustive on purpose, and the reader is meant to reach the end of the list without finding a case.

The sūrah's readers are shown a messenger who can invite conspiracy against himself and then state that his Protector is the One who revealed the Book. The Book's teaching about reliance is thus delivered in the form of a life rather than a proposition, and the passage's closure is a declaration of trust.

The verse's inventory also serves the sūrah's closing contrast, since the next verses name the Protector who hears and acts, and the final verse of the sūrah describes the angels who worship and prostrate. The Book's readers are given three orders of being in a few lines: the idle image, the protected messenger, and the angers who serve — and the passage's argument is complete when the three are compared.

The commentators took the verse as a statement of the Prophet's reliance and as a promise to the believers, and the tradition's literature of trust in God drew on it and on its companions. The verse's identification of the Protector with the giver of the Book also tells the sūrah's readers something about how the messenger's authority and his safety are connected: the same act that made him a messenger made him a dependent, and the Book's readers are shown that the office carries the protection of the One who conferred it. The passage's readers are given the ground of the Prophet's confidence in a single sentence.

The verse's readers are also shown the difference between the messenger's dependence and the idolaters' reliance: both groups look outside themselves, and the Book's argument is about what is there when they look. The sūrah's closing instruction to seek refuge with God (7:200) applies the same reasoning to the ordinary moments of a believer's day, and the passage's doctrinal statement thus becomes a practice.

**Why the Qur'an Repeats**

The Qur'an's repetitions are not redundancies but instruments of emphasis and structure, and the sūrah's readers have met the technique in its repeated statements about guidance (7:178, 7:186) and in its repeated formulas about the signs. The Book's teaching is delivered by re-statement, and the passage at the sūrah's close is an example.

**The Worshippers' Position Stated Again**

The verse addresses the idolaters directly as those who call upon others besides God, and the Book's readers are shown the charge in the second person rather than as a description. The sūrah's polemic thus closes as it began, with an address to the people who invoke the associate-gods.

**The Idols' Double Failure**

The two clauses of the verse — no help for the worshippers and none for themselves — recapitulate the passage's argument, and the second clause is the more decisive. The Book's readers are shown that the beings invoked cannot even serve their own interests, and the conclusion about their worship follows.

**The Judgment Scene Recalled**

The sūrah's readers have been told how the associate-gods will behave on the Day of Judgment: they will disown their worshippers and testify that they were unaware of the worship offered them (10:29), and Sūrat al-Naḥl reports their rebuttal of the polytheists' appeal (16:86). The verse's statement about help in this world is completed by the Book's account of the next.

**The Argument's Conclusion**

The passage that began with the question about creation (7:191) now ends with the statement that the idols cannot help, and the reader is left with no attribute of divinity that the idols retain. The Book's method is to close a case with a restatement rather than with a new premise, and the sūrah's readers are given the conclusion in the form of a verdict.

**The Next Verse's Change of Subject**

The following verse turns from the idols' incapacity to their deafness and their sightless appearance (7:198), and the passage's argument is extended by one more clause. The sūrah's examination of the associate-gods is thus complete, and the reader is carried to the sūrah's final instructions for the community.

**The Doctrine of Reliance Stated Positively**

The sūrah's contrast between the idols and the Protector of the righteous (7:196) is the passage's positive teaching, and the Book's readers are given a doctrine of reliance in which the object of trust is able to act. The sūrah's closing verses about seeking refuge with God (7:200) and remembering Him (7:205) apply that doctrine to the community's daily practice.

**The Pastoral Force of the Repetition**

For a community surrounded by the claims of the Makkan cults, the repetition of the idols' incapacity was a steady reminder rather than a novelty, and the sūrah's readers were given a formula they could carry. The Book's repetitions in its Makkan sūrahs served the community's formation as much as its instruction.

**The Verse's Address to Every Reader**

The verse's second person addresses the sūrah's audience directly, and the Book's readers are asked to consider what they call upon besides God. The question's relevance is not confined to a particular cult, and the sūrah's teaching about reliance is meant to be applied in the reader's own circumstances.

**The Sequence Completed**

The sūrah's treatment of the associate-gods now includes their creation (7:191), their help (7:192), their response (7:193), their servitude (7:194), their bodies (7:195), and their final restatement (7:197), with the Prophet's declaration (7:196) at the center. The Book's readers are given a complete dossier, and the sūrah moves on to its closing instructions.

**The Turn to the Community**

The remaining verses of the sūrah address the Prophet and the believers: the command to be gracious and to turn from the ignorant (7:199), the instruction to seek refuge (7:200), the description of the mindful (7:201), the account of the devils' persistence (7:202), the answer about signs (7:203), the command to listen to the recitation (7:204), the remembrance morning and evening (7:205), and the prostration of the angels (7:206). The passage about the idols ends, and the sūrah's practice begins.

The verse's repetition of the idols' failure also prepares the reader for the sūrah's final command to seek refuge: the Book's readers are told that nothing besides God can help, and then instructed where help is to be sought. The sūrah's closing verses are thus a practical conclusion drawn from the argument of the preceding passage, and the transition is one of the sūrah's most deliberate.

The verse's wording also closes the door on the possibility that the associate-gods might act on the worshippers' behalf at some future point, since the verb addresses the present and permanent condition of the objects invoked. The sūrah's readers are told that nothing about the idols will change, and the Book's statements about the Day of Judgment depict the same incapacity in its final form.

The Book's readers are told that the idols are not merely powerless but inanimate, and the Qur'an's account of the calf had made the point in Israel's history: the golden image could not speak or guide (7:148). The verse leaves the worshippers to their own sight: they can see the idols, and the idols cannot see them. The sūrah's next verse turns to the Prophet with an instruction about conduct: to be gracious, to enjoin what is right, and to turn away from the ignorant (7:199). The verse's phrase about calling the idols to guidance repeats the sūrah's concern with the theme, and the Book's readers are reminded that guidance is God's to give. The verse's second person addresses both the idolaters and the sūrah's own community, and the Book's readers are invited to consider where they look for guidance. The examination of the associate-gods is complete with this verse, and the sūrah's last eight verses address the community's conduct, refuge, recitation, and worship. The Book's arrangement places the sūrah's practice after its argument, and the reader is given the doctrine and the discipline in the order the Book chose.

The sūrah's readers are about to be told to be gracious to those who wrong them, to seek refuge in God, and to listen to the Qur'an in silence — instructions that presuppose everything the sūrah has taught. The final verse's angels, who are not too proud to worship and who prostrate, complete the passage's contrast: the idols have no faces to turn and cannot see, and the nearest to God bow before Him.

The verse's clause about the idols facing the worshipper also carries a warning about the appearance of religion: the Book's readers are shown that a cult can have all the marks of devotion — a fixed direction, a gaze, a posture — and none of the reality. The sūrah's readers are asked to consider what distinguishes the worship of the living God from the service of an image, and the sūrah's final instructions supply the answer: hearing, remembrance, and prostration before the Lord.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:199

The verse gives the Prophet three instructions in a single line, and the tradition treated them as a summary of the sūrah's ethic. The command to be gracious comes first, then the charge to enjoin what is right, then the direction to turn away from the ignorant, and the order moves from the Prophet's own disposition to his public duty to his handling of opposition.

**"Be Gracious"**

*Khudhi al-ʿafwa* — the Book's phrase for taking what is easy, for accepting the surplus, for pardoning. The Qur'an's teaching about pardon appears throughout: *“The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah”* (42:40); the description of the believers as those who control their anger (3:134); and the instruction to the Prophet's community to pardon and bear with the People of the Book's hostility (2:109). The verse gathers that teaching into an imperative.

**"Enjoin What Is Right"**

*Wa-mur bi-l-ʿurf* — the Book's term for what is recognized as good, the customary right that reason and revelation both acknowledge. The sūrah's own description of the Prophet includes the same charge — he commands what is good and forbids what is evil (7:157) — and the Qur'an's address to the believers names the duty as a mark of the community (3:104). The verse is thus the Prophet's commission in miniature.

**"Turn Away From Those Who Act Ignorantly"**

*Wa-aʿriḍ ʿani al-jāhilīn* — the instruction is about conduct rather than about debate, and the Book's ethic of restraint is stated in similar terms elsewhere: the servants of the Most Compassionate respond to the foolish with peace (25:63), and the Book's counsel to answer evil with what is best (23:96, 41:34) belongs to the same teaching. **The Verse and the Prophet's Conduct**

The Book's accounts of the Prophet's dealings with his opponents show the instruction being carried out, and the Qur'an's own address in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān attributes the Prophet's leniency to God's mercy (3:159). The sūrah's readers are shown that the ethic of verse 199 is not an abstraction but the description of a life the community had observed.

The Qur'an's teaching about forbearance is not passivity: the same Book commands the establishment of justice, the defense of the wronged, and the correction of abuses. What the verse rules out is retaliation for insults and the pursuit of arguments with those who are not seeking the truth, and the sūrah's readers are given the boundary in a line.

**The Tradition's Reading**

The commentators and jurists treated the verse as a foundational statement of the Prophet's missionary conduct, and they noted that the command to pardon concerns wrongs done to the Prophet personally rather than the rights of others or the limits God has set. The sūrah's readers are shown how the tradition distinguished the personal and the public in applying the verse.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah**

The verse follows the long examination of the idols (7:191–198) and precedes the instruction to seek refuge (7:200), and its position gives it the character of a conclusion drawn from the argument. The sūrah's readers are shown what the doctrine looks like in conduct: the rejection of the idols is accompanied by a refusal to imitate the manner of their worshippers.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Statements of the Same Ethic**

The sūrah had described the Prophet's community as those who enjoin what is right and forbid what is evil (7:157) and had reported the warners of the town by the sea as the saved party (7:165). The sūrah's readers are given the ethic in narrative and in command, and the two forms reinforce each other.

**The Community's Practice**

The verse's instructions were received by the Muslim community as a standard for its own conduct toward those who oppose it, and the tradition's literature on daʿwah drew on it repeatedly. The sūrah's readers are told that the manner of the invitation belongs to the invitation, and the Book's readers are given the Prophet's own disposition as the model.

**The Order of the Commands**

The verse's sequence is significant: the Prophet is first to take hold of the generous course, then to call others to the recognized good, and only then to deal with the ignorant. The sūrah's readers are shown a method in which self-discipline precedes proclamation, and the passage's readers are invited to apply the order to themselves.

**The Next Verse's Refuge**

The command to turn away from the ignorant is followed by the instruction to seek refuge with God from Satan (7:200), and the pairing tells the reader where the strength for the first command comes from. The sūrah's closing verses address the community's inner life directly, and the ethic of verse 199 rests on the practice the next verse prescribes.

The verse's first command also has a juristic history: the scholars discussed whether *al-ʿafw* in this context means the surplus of generosity, the pardon of offenses, or the acceptance of what people can bear, and the readings converged on the third sense as the Prophet's manner in inviting his people. The sūrah's readers are shown a directive that the tradition applied to the conduct of the caller rather than to the content of the call.

The verse also names the community's relationship to the ignorant without permitting contempt: the instruction is to turn away, not to despise, and the Book's address to the Prophet elsewhere prescribes patience with what the opponents say. The sūrah's readers are given a policy whose motive is stated later in the passage, where the instruction to seek refuge with God locates the source of the strength required.

The verse follows the command to turn away from the ignorant (7:199), and its placement shows what the previous instruction requires: the provocation such encounters produce is answered by recourse to God rather than by retaliation. The two names closing the verse state why the recourse works: the One addressed hears the unspoken petition and knows the provocation that prompted it. The Qur'an's final sūrahs consist entirely of refuge-seeking, and their language — the Lord of the daybreak, the Lord of humankind (113:1, 114:1) — is the Book's most concentrated statement of the practice the sūrah's verse prescribes. The verse treats temptation as something external in its origin and momentary in its effect, and it prescribes an act rather than an analysis. The sūrah's readers are told that the provoked moment is not the place for argument with oneself, and the instruction's brevity is part of its point.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that the tempted person has sinned, nor that the thought is his own creation, and the Book's distinction between the provocation and the consent appears elsewhere in its treatment of responsibility. The sūrah's readers are shown that the attack is met at the threshold rather than punished after it.

The following verse describes the mind of the God-conscious under the same attack: when the devils whisper to them, they remember God and begin to see clearly (7:201). The sūrah's readers are given the practice in verse 200 and its result in verse 201, and the two verses answer each other.

**The Community's Recourse**

The Muslim community's practice of seeking refuge before recitation, before anger, and at the onset of unworthy thoughts derives from passages such as this one, and the sūrah's readers were given the habit with the doctrine. The Book's method here is to attach a small verbal act to the moment of danger, and the passage's teaching is carried in the practice.

**The Sūrah's Doctrine of Reliance**

The verse belongs with the sūrah's earlier declarations of reliance — the Prophet's Protector who revealed the Book (7:196) and the statement that the idols can neither help nor harm. The sūrah's readers are shown a religion in which protection is sought from the One who hears and knows, and the sūrah's final verses about remembrance and prostration continue in the same register.

The Muslim tradition placed the verse at the head of its teaching about the etiquette of anger, and its manuals of conduct instructed the believer to say the refuge formula at the moment of provocation. The sūrah's readers are shown a discipline that operates before deliberation, and the Book's readers are given the practice as a habit of the community rather than as advice for exceptional cases.

The verse's form is also notable: the Book does not describe the temptation in detail or invite the reader to examine it, but prescribes the turn to God at once. The sūrah's readers are shown the Book's confidence that the remedy is sufficient, and the passage's terseness is a statement about what the community is expected to need.

The two names closing the verse were read by the tradition as the ground of the supplication's efficacy: the appeal is to the One who hears the whispered word of the heart and knows the intention behind it. The sūrah's readers are told that the recourse is met with attention rather than with silence, and the verse's theology is contained in its final three words.

The sūrah's readers are told that the plunge is gradual and unnoticed, and the Book's remedy has been given in the preceding verse.

**The Passage Complete**

The sūrah's account of the two responses is now complete: refuge and remembrance on one side (7:200–201), and the deepening course on the other (7:202). The Book's arrangement leaves the reader between the two, and the sūrah's final four verses supply the practice that ends the passage.

The verse's description of the devils' persistence also implies the community's part in the process, since the associates are being drawn by beings they have chosen as brothers. The Book's readers are shown that the companionship is the result of a prior agreement, and the sūrah's earlier narrative of the devil's refusal had stated the terms: he asked for respite and was granted it until the appointed day (7:15).

The Qur'an's warnings about the devil's patience are matched by its assurances about God's protection, and the sūrah's passage places the two in sequence: the refuge and remembrance of verses 200–201 stand before the account of the plunge in verse 202. The sūrah's readers are given the remedy before the diagnosis, and the order is the Book's usual provision for its community.

The passage's vocabulary of drawing and stretching is also used by the Book for its own dealings with the obstinate, and the sūrah's readers are shown that the same image can describe the extension of a term or the deepening of a course. The verse's context leaves no doubt which of the two the associates receive.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:203

> **And when thou bringest them not a sign, they say, “Why do you not choose it?” Say, “I only follow that which is revealed unto me from my Lord. These are insights from your Lord, and a guidance and a mercy for a people who believe.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Why Do You Not Make It Yourself?"**

*Wa-idhā lam taʾtihim bi-āyatin qālū lawlā ijtabaytahā qul innamā attabiʿu mā yūḥā ilayya min rabbī hādhā baṣāʾiru min rabbikum wa-hudan wa-raḥmatun li-qawmin yuʾminūn* — if you ˹O Prophet˺ do not bring them a sign ˹which they demanded˺, they ask, "Why do you not make it yourself?" Say, "I only follow what is revealed to me from my Lord. This ˹Quran˺ is an insight from your Lord—a guide and a mercy for those who believe."

**The Demand Restated**

The sūrah's closing verses return to the demand for signs, which had occupied the sūrah's earlier polemics, and the verse gives the demand its most revealing form: the opponents ask the Prophet to produce a sign himself. The Book's readers are shown a request that has ceased to be about evidence and become a request about authority.

**The Book's Many Accounts of the Demand**

The Qur'an records the same demand in sūrah after sūrah, and each version adds a detail: the challenge to bring a sign from his Lord (6:37), the complaint that no sign had been sent (13:7), the demand that he bring one (20:133), the oath that they would believe if a sign came (6:109), and the request to produce a different Qur'an altogether (10:15). The sūrah's readers are given the demand's whole history.

**"Why Do You Not Make It Yourself?"**

The question's premise is that the Prophet's authority includes the disposal of signs, and the Book's answer denies the premise at once. The sūrah's readers are shown that the messenger is not a magician with a repertoire at his command, and the reply that follows — that he follows only what is revealed — settles the matter by stating his office.

**The Answer's Content**

*Qul innamā attabiʿu mā yūḥā ilayya min rabbī* — I only follow what is revealed to me from my Lord. The sūrah's readers are given the Prophet's position in a sentence: he is a follower of revelation before he is anything else, and the Book's accounts of the other messengers' replies to the same demand say the same thing: signs are only with Allah (6:109, 29:50).

**Why the Signs Are Withheld**

The Book states the reason in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ: the signs were not sent because the earlier peoples denied them and the demand would have been followed by the same denial (17:59), and Sūrat al-Anʿām states that even the descent of the angels and the speaking of the dead would not produce belief without God's will (6:111). The sūrah's readers are shown that the demand is not an argument but a posture.

**"An Insight From Your Lord"**

*Baṣāʾiru* — the plural of insight, of clear perception — is the Book's term for what the revelation supplies, and the Qur'an's self-descriptions in this register are numerous: a healing and mercy for the believers (17:82), a warning, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide and a mercy (10:57). The sūrah's readers are told that the sign they already have is of a different kind from the one they demanded.

**"A Guide and a Mercy for Those Who Believe"**

The verse's closing description repeats the Qur'an's standard formula for itself, and the conditional at the end is the sūrah's recurring note: the Book's guidance and mercy are realized in those who believe. The sūrah's readers have met the same qualifying phrase in the sūrah's earlier verses, and its repetition here closes the argument about signs with an account of what a sign is for.

**The Answer to the Demand for Something New**

Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt answers the same demand by pointing to what had already been given: *“Is it not enough for them that We have sent down to you the Book, ˹which is˺ recited to them. Surely in this ˹Quran˺ is a mercy and reminder for people who believe”* (29:51). The sūrah's verse belongs to that family of answers, and its readers are shown the Book's consistent reply to those who ask for something other than the recitation.

**The Demand's Real Nature**

The Book's diagnosis of the demand appears in Sūrat al-Baqarah's account of the earlier communities who said that God should speak to them or a sign should come (2:118), and in the statement that their hearts are alike. The sūrah's readers are given the demand's genealogy, and the sūrah's answer to it is the verse's declaration of the Prophet's office.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Treatment of the Messenger's Limits**

The sūrah had already stated that the Prophet knows neither the unseen nor the Hour and possesses no power over benefit or harm (7:188), and the present verse completes the portrait by adding the signs to the list. The sūrah's readers are given a consistent account of the messenger's position, and the verses are meant to be read together.

**The Community's Use of the Verse**

The Muslim community's understanding of the Prophet's relation to revelation rests on passages such as this one, and the sūrah's readers were told that the messenger's authority is the authority of what he was given. The verse's answer was also used in the tradition's discussions of the Qur'an's sufficiency as the sign, and the Book's own statement in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt supplied the argument's positive form.

**The Sūrah's Closing Movement**

The verse stands at the head of the sūrah's final four verses, and it gives them their subject: the revelation, the listening, the remembrance, and the worship. The Book's readers are shown what the alternative to the demand for signs is, and the alternative is the recitation they have been hearing.

**The Verse's Instruction**

The verse's command to the Prophet is to state his position rather than to defend himself, and the Book's readers are given a model of how the messenger handled the demand for credentials. The sūrah's readers are shown that the answer to a demand for something other than the revelation is the revelation.

**The Recitation That Follows**

The next verse commands the listeners to be silent when the Qur'an is recited (7:204), and the placement is deliberate: the sign that was demanded is being recited to them at that very moment. The sūrah's readers are given the demand, the answer, and the command to attend in three consecutive sentences, and the passage's argument is thus complete.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:204

> **And when the Quran is recited, hearken unto it, and listen, that haply you may receive mercy.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Listen to It Attentively and Be Silent"**

*Wa-idhā quriʾa al-Qurʾānu fa-stamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū laʿallakum turḥamūn* — when the Quran is recited, listen to it attentively and be silent, so you may be shown mercy.

**The Command and Its Occasion**

The verse follows the Prophet's declaration that the Book is an insight, a guide, and a mercy (7:203), and its instruction supplies the response the declaration requires. The sūrah's readers are told what to do with the sign they have just been offered, and the command's position in the sūrah's final movement gives it the weight of a summary of the community's discipline.

**The Two Verbs**

*Fa-stamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū* — the first verb is listening with the ear and the attention, and the second is the silence that accompanies it. The pair is the Book's most precise statement about the reception of recitation, and the sūrah's readers are shown that attendance on the Qur'an is an act with two parts, one of them abstention.

**The Tradition's Rulings**

The scholars drew from the verse their teaching about listening to the Qur'an in prayer and outside it, and their discussions distinguished the recitation that is obligatory to attend from the recitation that is heard incidentally. The sūrah's readers are shown that the verse was received as a legal principle as well as an exhortation, and the community's practice of silence during public recitation rests on it.

**"So You May Be Shown Mercy"**

The command's purpose is stated in the Book's vocabulary of mercy, and the sūrah's readers are told that the act of listening is not a courtesy but a means. The Qur'an attaches mercy to specific practices throughout — the sūrah's earlier commands about returning, remembering, and seeking refuge all close with a similar clause — and the verse's grammar keeps the outcome in God's hand.

**The Book's Treatment of Hearing**

The Qur'an's addresses consistently treat hearing as the first step of faith: the Book's description of the heedless is that they have ears and do not hear (7:179), and its account of the earlier communities' failure is repeatedly phrased as a refusal to listen. The sūrah's readers are shown that the command of verse 204 is the positive form of the sūrah's long indictment.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Accusations**

The sūrah had reported that the idols cannot hear those who call upon them (7:193, 7:198), and the Book's readers are given the contrast: the objects of worship are deaf, and the worshippers of God are commanded to listen. The verse's placement at the end of the sūrah's examination of the idols makes the comparison explicit.

**The Recitation in the Prophet's Practice**

The Book's accounts of the Prophet's night recitation and of the community's prayer describe the practice the verse commands, and the Qur'an's instruction to recite in a measured manner belongs to the same discipline. The sūrah's readers are shown a religion centered on a recited text, and the command to be silent is the necessary condition of its reception.

**The Verse's Audience**

The command addresses everyone present at the recitation, and the Book's readers are told that the obligation is not restricted to believers: the sūrah's readers in the Prophet's city included those who had not yet accepted the message, and the verse's requirement of silence applied to them as well.

**The Discipline of Silence**

The Book's readers are also shown the inward form of the command, since the silence of the tongue is a figure for the attention of the heart, and the sūrah's earlier verses had made the heart the seat of understanding (7:179). The community's tradition drew from the verse its teaching about attentiveness rather than mere quietness, and the two verbs of the verse were read together.

**The Qur'an's Self-Presentation**

The verse's command presupposes the Book's account of itself as a recitation that works on its hearers: the sūrah's readers have been told that the Book is an insight and a mercy, and elsewhere that the Qur'an's recitation increases the believers' humility (17:109). The sūrah's readers are shown a text that accomplishes something in the one who attends to it.

**The Mercy Extended**

The verse's closing clause gives the reason for the command in the Book's usual manner, and the sūrah's readers are told that the act of listening places them in the way of mercy. The same word appears in the sūrah's earlier descriptions of the righteous and in its closing verse's command to remember, and the passage's vocabulary is deliberately repeated.

**The Next Verse's Remembrance**

The following verse commands the remembrance of God inwardly, with humility, morning and evening (7:205), and the two commands together describe the community's daily practice: listening to the recitation and remembering the Lord. The sūrah's readers are given the outward and inward halves of the discipline, and the final verse supplies the model of those who perform it.

**The Passage's Order**

The sūrah's last three verses move from hearing to remembering to prostrating, and the sequence is the Book's account of how revelation becomes worship. The sūrah's readers are shown a progression that leads from the recited word to the daily remembrance to the posture of the angels, and the sūrah ends at the point to which its whole argument has been directed.

The verse also served the community as a rule about the conduct of the mosque: the recitation is to be heard rather than competed with, and the tradition's teaching about not raising one's voice above the imam's drew on it. The sūrah's readers are shown that the Book's final instructions concern the manner of the community's assembly as much as its private devotion.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:205

> **And remember thy Lord within thy soul, humbly and in awe, being not loud of voice, in the morning and the evening, and be not among those who are heedless.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Remember Your Lord Inwardly"**

*Wa-dhkur rabbaka fī nafsika taḍarruʿan wa-khīfatan wa-dūna al-jahri mina al-qawli bi-l-ghuduwwi wa-l-āṣāli wa-lā takun mina al-ghāfilīn* — remember your Lord inwardly with humility and reverence and in a moderate tone of voice, both morning and evening. And do not be one of the heedless.

**The Command and Its Manner**

The verse commands remembrance and specifies its manner in four particulars: inwardly, with humility, with awe, and in a voice between silence and loudness. The Book's readers are shown that the practice has a form, and the sūrah's readers are given the discipline that the recitation of the previous verse had prepared.

**"Inwardly"**

*Fī nafsika* — the sūrah's readers are directed to the interior, and the Book's teaching about the heart as the seat of understanding (7:179) gives the instruction its setting. The Qur'an's other descriptions of remembrance make the same point: the believers remember Allah standing, sitting, and lying on their sides (3:191), and the Book's readers are shown a practice that does not depend on posture or place.

**"With Humility and Reverence"**

*Taḍarruʿan wa-khīfatan* — the two words describe the disposition of the suppliant: a turning toward God in need, and a fear of His displeasure. The Qur'an's prayers are full of both notes, and the sūrah's readers have met the combination in the sūrah's narrative of Moses' entreaties and in the Book's instructions to its community.

**"In a Moderate Tone of Voice"**

*Wa-dūna al-jahri mina al-qawli* — the Book's instruction about the voice appears in almost identical form in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ: *“Do not recite your prayers too loudly or silently, but seek a way between”* (17:110). The sūrah's readers are shown that the community's worship is neither display nor concealment, and the verse's phrase supplies the standard the tradition applied to the recitation and the supplication alike.

**"Morning and Evening"**

*Bi-l-ghuduwwi wa-l-āṣāl* — the two ends of the day are named, and the Qur'an's instructions about the times of glorification recur: the command to glorify before sunrise and before sunset (20:130, 50:39), during the night (50:40, 76:26), and the statement that the shadows of all things bow in the morning and the evening (13:15). The sūrah's readers are given a rhythm rather than a single occasion.

**The Book's Times of Worship**

The Qur'an's treatment of time is structured around the daily cycle, and the sūrah's readers are shown that remembrance belongs to the ordinary course of a day rather than to exceptional moments. The tradition's collections of morning and evening supplications took their warrant from passages such as this one, and the verse's pair of times became the community's daily anchor.

**"Do Not Be One of the Heedless"**

*Wa-lā takun mina al-ghāfilīn* — the verse closes with a prohibition, and its word is the sūrah's own term for those who do not attend: the heedless are described earlier as those who have hearts and do not understand (7:179), and the sūrah's readers were told that the destruction of the towns came upon people who were unaware (7:95). The command is thus the sūrah's final warning against the condition it has been diagnosing throughout.

**The Verse's Moderation**

The verse's four specifications balance each other: inwardness without concealment, humility without display, awe without despair, and a voice that is neither raised nor extinguished. The sūrah's readers are shown the Book's characteristic insistence on the middle course, and the tradition read the verse as a definition of the conduct of worship rather than as advice about volume alone.

**The Remembrance and the Recitation**

The verse's command follows immediately on the instruction to listen to the Qur'an in silence (7:204), and the pair describes the community's practice as hearing and answering. The sūrah's readers are given the two halves of a single act: the Book is recited, and the hearer remembers the Lord who revealed it.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Commands to Remember**

The sūrah had commanded the community to remember what it had been given at the covenant of the mountain (7:171) and had reported the generations who forgot the reminder (7:165). The verse's instruction at the sūrah's close gathers those commands into a daily practice, and the sūrah's readers are shown that remembrance is the thread that runs through its narratives.

**The Tradition's Use of the Verse**

The commentators and the masters of the spiritual life drew on this verse for their teaching about the inward form of remembrance, and they noted the combination of the hidden heart and the moderate voice as the Book's balance. The sūrah's readers are given both the interior and the exterior of the practice, and the verse's wording was read as a warning against both ostentation and negligence.

**The Reader's Day**

The verse's readers are given a practice that fits the ordinary day, and the Book's teaching here is that the presence of God is acknowledged at the day's two ends rather than reserved for its crises. The sūrah's readers are shown that the community's life is a continuous conversation, and the instruction's simplicity is its accessibility.

**The Next Verse's Angels**

The sūrah's final verse describes the angels nearest to God, who are not too proud to worship, who glorify Him, and who prostrate (7:206), and the placement of the human command before the angelic example is the Book's characteristic ordering. The sūrah's readers are shown the company they join in the remembrance, and the sūrah ends with its final prostration.

The verse's closing prohibition also belongs to the sūrah's vocabulary of the heedless, a word it has used for the Egyptians who were overtaken, for those who ignore the signs, and for the objects of its warnings. The sūrah's readers are shown that forgetfulness of God is the condition the whole sūrah has been describing, and the remembrance it commands is the remedy announced at the end.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:206

> **Surely those who are with thy Lord are not too arrogant to worship Him. And they glorify Him, and prostrate unto Him.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Not Too Proud to Worship Him"**

*Inna alladhīna ʿinda rabbika lā yastakbirūna ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-yusabbiḥūnahu wa-lahu yasjudūn* — surely those ˹angels˺ nearest to your Lord are not too proud to worship Him. They glorify Him. And to Him they prostrate.

**The Sūrah's Final Verse**

The sūrah ends with the angels, and the verse is placed after the community's instructions about listening and remembrance so that the human practice is seen within a wider worship. The Book's readers are given the last word of the sūrah as a picture of service, and the sūrah's long record of refusal is answered by the creatures who do not refuse.

**"Those Nearest to Your Lord"**

The verse names the nearest angels — the Book's term of proximity used elsewhere for those in God's presence — and the sūrah's readers are shown that the rank does not exempt from worship. The Qur'an states the same about the Messiah and the angels: they would never be too proud to be servants of God, while the arrogant will be brought before Him (4:172).

**"Not Too Proud to Worship Him"**

The Book's negation is aimed at the very disposition the sūrah has been documenting in the human being, from Iblīs's refusal to prostrate (7:12–13) to the arrogance of the towns and of the idolaters. The sūrah's readers are given the contrast in its final sentence, and the angels' freedom from pride is stated as the quality the fallen creature lacked.

**"They Glorify Him"**

*Wa-yusabbiḥūnahu* — the Book's verb of praise that accompanies the declaration of God's transcendence, and the Qur'an's accounts of the angels' activity use it consistently: those near Him are not too proud to worship and do not tire (21:19), they glorify Him day and night without wavering (21:20), and the angels around the Throne glorify the praises of their Lord (39:75). The sūrah's readers are given the angels' occupation, and it is the one the idolaters' gods cannot perform because they cannot speak.

**"And to Him They Prostrate"**

The verse's last words state the posture, and the Qur'an's prostration verses echo the same formula: whatever is in the heavens and the earth bows down to Allah, as do the angels, who are not too proud (16:49), and all things bow to Him willingly or unwillingly, as do their shadows (13:15). The sūrah's readers are shown that prostration is the creation's common act, and the human refusal is placed against the background of the angels' practice.

**The Book's Prostration Verses**

The Qur'an contains a series of verses at which the tradition performs the prostration of recitation, and this verse is among them. The sūrah's readers are given a text whose end invites the posture it describes, and the community's practice of prostrating at these verses keeps the Book's instruction and the Book's example joined.

**The Contrast With the Idols**

The sūrah has just examined the associate-gods who cannot hear, cannot see, and cannot help (7:191–198), and its final verse presents the angels who listen, worship, and prostrate. The Book's readers are given the two orders of being at the sūrah's close, and the contrast makes the passage's argument about the true object of worship complete.

**The Qur'an's Cosmology of Worship**

The Book's descriptions of creation consistently present worship as the activity of everything that exists: the stars and the trees bow (55:6), the thunder and the angels glorify (13:13), and the heavens and the earth are filled with creatures that serve. The sūrah's readers are shown that the human being's worship is a participation in a universal act rather than an isolated duty.

**The Sūrah's Opening and Its Close**

The sūrah began with the Book's address to the Prophet and with the account of the creation of the human being and the refusal of Iblīs to prostrate; it ends with the angels who do prostrate. The sūrah's readers are given the bookend, and the two passages are meant to be read together: the sūrah's final verse answers its earliest narrative.

**The Implied Command**

The verse contains no imperative, and its function for the reader is exemplary: the Book's readers are shown what worship looks like among those who have no pride to overcome. The sūrah's readers have just been commanded to remember the Lord morning and evening (7:205), and the final verse supplies the company in which the practice is performed.

**The Sūrah Completed**

With this verse the sūrah closes, and the reader who has followed it has been carried from the Book's revelation to the creation of the human being, through the narratives of Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, Moses, and Israel, past the parable of the man and the signs, the names of God, the examination of the idols, and the instruction of the final passage. The Book's sūrah ends in prostration, and the reader is invited to the same act.

**The Tradition's Recitation**

The sūrah was recited in the community's prayer and in its teaching, and the final verse's prostration was among the points at which its recitation was accompanied by the posture it names. The Book's readers are given a text whose conclusion is an action, and the sūrah's last word is therefore not a statement to be considered but a movement to be performed.

**The Last Word**

The sūrah's final word is prostration, and the Book's readers are left with the act toward which its entire argument has been moving: the recognition of the One who alone is worshipped, by the creatures who are nearest to Him and by the human being who has heard the recitation. The sūrah's answer to the idolaters, the deniers, and the heedless is the posture the angels have never abandoned.
