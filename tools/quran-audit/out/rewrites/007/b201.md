**"They Remember Their Lord and Start to See Clearly"**

*Inna alladhīna ittaqaw idhā massahum ṭāʾifun mina al-shayṭāni tadhakkarū fa-idhā hum mubṣirūn* — indeed, when Satan whispers to those mindful ˹of Allah˺, they remember ˹their Lord˺ then they start to see ˹things˺ clearly.

**The Description of the Mindful**

The verse describes the effect of the refuge prescribed in the previous line, and its subject is the God-conscious rather than the Prophet alone. The Book's readers are shown the practice being performed by the community, and the sūrah's closing movement turns from the messenger's instruction to the believers' condition.

**The Word for the Whisper**

*Ṭāʾif* — a visitation, a passing touch — describes the suggestion as something that comes and goes, and the Book's vocabulary for the devil's work consistently presents it as an approach rather than a possession. The sūrah's readers are told that the intrusion is not the same as the intention, and the verse's grammar keeps the two apart.

**"They Remember Their Lord"**

*Tadhakkarū* — the Book's verb for recalling what one already knows — and the sūrah has used the same root for the community that forgot the reminder (7:165's narrative) and for the command to remember what is in the Scripture (7:171). The sūrah's readers are shown that the remedy for the whisper is a remembering, and the Book's teaching about remembrance runs through its addresses to the community.

**"Then They Start to See Clearly"**

*Fa-idhā hum mubṣirūn* — the recovered sight is stated as the immediate outcome of the remembrance, and the sūrah's readers are given the contrast with the blindness of the heedless stated earlier in the sūrah (7:179). The Book's use of sight for understanding is consistent, and the verse describes the restoration of a faculty rather than the acquisition of new information.

**The Doctrine of the Two Responses**

The sūrah's readers are shown two reactions to the same attack: the mindful remember and see, and the devils' associates are plunged deeper (7:202). The Book's presentation of the two groups in consecutive verses makes the passage a study in contrast, and the reader is invited to locate himself in the pair.

**The Book's Teaching About the God-Conscious**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the God-conscious recur throughout its sūrahs, and their common element is precisely this responsiveness: the believer is described as one who remembers when reminded. The sūrah's early verses had described the revelation as a reminder for the believers, and the verse shows what a reminder is for.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Commands to Remember**

The sūrah's community has been instructed to remember what it has been given, and the command appears at the covenant of the mountain (7:171) and in the sūrah's account of the generations who forgot. The sūrah's readers are shown that the practice recommended in verse 200 is the same faculty the earlier narratives had tested.

**The Verse's Consolation**

The verse assures the community that the attack of the devil does not define the believer, and the Book's readers are given a description of the ordinary experience of temptation followed by recollection. The sūrah's readers are told that the momentary disturbance is not the loss of the state, and the verse's sequence — the touch, the remembering, the sight — is its anatomy.

**The Tradition's Use of the Verse**

The commentators connected the verse with the practice of seeking refuge and with the doctrine of repentance after the whisper, and the tradition's literature on the heart's fluctuations drew on it. The sūrah's readers were given both the instruction and the assurance, and the passage's two verses supplied the practice and its fruit.

**The Next Verse's Contrast**

The verse that follows describes the devil's associates who are plunged deeper without resistance (7:202), and the sūrah's readers are given the pair explicitly. The Book's method in this closing passage is to place the two conditions side by side, and the reader is left with the choice that the sūrah has been presenting since its first pages.

**The Community's Condition**

The verse's description of the mindful is offered to the community as its own portrait, and the sūrah's recipients were told what they could expect of themselves. The Book's readers are shown that the standard is not the absence of the attack but the speed of the remembrance, and the verse's wording measures the response rather than the temptation.

**The Passage's Movement Forward**

The sūrah's next verses turn to the demand for signs (7:203) and to the recitation, remembrance, and prostration that end the sūrah (7:204–206). The verse's account of the community's inner life is thus the hinge of the closing passage, and the reader is carried from the devil's whisper to the angels' worship.

The verse's description of the recovery also distinguishes it from mere regret: what follows the remembrance is sight, which the Book uses for understanding the situation as it is. The sūrah's readers are shown the difference between a person who has been disturbed and one who has been deceived, and the verse's sequence is meant to convey the restoration of judgment.

The passage's assurance was also read as a statement about the ordinary life of the believer rather than about extraordinary sanctity, and the tradition's discussions of the heart's states used the verse to define the normal pattern. The sūrah's readers are given a doctrine in which the fall is momentary and the return is immediate, and the verse's verbs are in the habitual form to say so.
