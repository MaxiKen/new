**"Seek Refuge With Allah"**

*Wa-immā yanzaghannaka mina al-shayṭāni nazghun fa-staʿidh bi-llāhi innahu samīʿun ʿalīm* — if you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**The Instruction and Its Occasion**

The verse follows the command to turn away from the ignorant (7:199), and its placement shows what the previous instruction requires: the provocation such encounters produce is answered by recourse to God rather than by retaliation. The Book's readers are given the practical mechanism of the ethic stated in the line before.

**The Word for the Provocation**

*Nazgh* — a prick, a goad, the sudden incitement — describes the impulse that arrives before reflection, and the Book's use of the term is precise: what is at issue is not a settled intention but a movement that can be met at once. The sūrah's readers are shown that the cure is available at the moment of the irritation.

**"Seek Refuge With Allah"**

*Fa-staʿidh bi-llāhi* — the Book's term for seeking protection, and the Qur'an returns to it in a verse almost identical in wording: *“And if you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Indeed, He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing”* (41:36). The sūrah's readers are given the practice rather than a theory of temptation, and the repetition of the formula in two sūrahs makes it a fixed feature of the community's discipline.

**"All-Hearing, All-Knowing"**

The two names closing the verse state why the recourse works: the One addressed hears the unspoken petition and knows the provocation that prompted it. The sūrah's earlier verse about the heedless had described ears that do not hear and hearts that do not understand (7:179), and the names at the end of this verse supply the contrast.

**The Book's Other Refuge Instructions**

The Qur'an commands the same recourse in several places, and the tradition of recitation before reading the Qur'an rests on Sūrat al-Naḥl's instruction: *“When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed”* (16:98). The sūrah's readers are given a practice that the Book attaches to recitation, to provocation, and to the moments of anger.

**The Supplication in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn**

The Qur'an also provides the words: *“My Lord! I seek refuge in You from the temptations of the devils. And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, that they ˹even˺ come near me”* (23:97–98). The sūrah's readers are shown that the refuge is sought both from the incitement and from the approach that precedes it, and the two verses together define the practice.

**The Two Protecting Sūrahs**

The Qur'an's final sūrahs consist entirely of refuge-seeking, and their language — the Lord of the daybreak, the Lord of humankind (113:1, 114:1) — is the Book's most concentrated statement of the practice the sūrah's verse prescribes. The readers of the sūrah are shown the same doctrine at the beginning of the Book's closing sequence.

**The Psychology of the Verse**

The verse treats temptation as something external in its origin and momentary in its effect, and it prescribes an act rather than an analysis. The sūrah's readers are told that the provoked moment is not the place for argument with oneself, and the instruction's brevity is part of its point.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not say that the tempted person has sinned, nor that the thought is his own creation, and the Book's distinction between the provocation and the consent appears elsewhere in its treatment of responsibility. The sūrah's readers are shown that the attack is met at the threshold rather than punished after it.

**The Next Verse's Description**

The following verse describes the mind of the God-conscious under the same attack: when the devils whisper to them, they remember God and begin to see clearly (7:201). The sūrah's readers are given the practice in verse 200 and its result in verse 201, and the two verses answer each other.

**The Community's Recourse**

The Muslim community's practice of seeking refuge before recitation, before anger, and at the onset of unworthy thoughts derives from passages such as this one, and the sūrah's readers were given the habit with the doctrine. The Book's method here is to attach a small verbal act to the moment of danger, and the passage's teaching is carried in the practice.

**The Sūrah's Doctrine of Reliance**

The verse belongs with the sūrah's earlier declarations of reliance — the Prophet's Protector who revealed the Book (7:196) and the statement that the idols can neither help nor harm. The sūrah's readers are shown a religion in which protection is sought from the One who hears and knows, and the sūrah's final verses about remembrance and prostration continue in the same register.

The Muslim tradition placed the verse at the head of its teaching about the etiquette of anger, and its manuals of conduct instructed the believer to say the refuge formula at the moment of provocation. The sūrah's readers are shown a discipline that operates before deliberation, and the Book's readers are given the practice as a habit of the community rather than as advice for exceptional cases.

The verse's form is also notable: the Book does not describe the temptation in detail or invite the reader to examine it, but prescribes the turn to God at once. The sūrah's readers are shown the Book's confidence that the remedy is sufficient, and the passage's terseness is a statement about what the community is expected to need.

The two names closing the verse were read by the tradition as the ground of the supplication's efficacy: the appeal is to the One who hears the whispered word of the heart and knows the intention behind it. The sūrah's readers are told that the recourse is met with attention rather than with silence, and the verse's theology is contained in its final three words.
