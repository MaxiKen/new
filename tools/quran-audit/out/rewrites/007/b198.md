**"They Cannot Hear"**

*Wa-in tadʿūhum ilā al-hudā lā yasmaʿū wa-tarāhum yanẓurūna ilayka wa-hum lā yubṣirūn* — if you ˹idolaters˺ call them to guidance, they cannot hear. And you ˹O Prophet˺ may see them facing towards you, but they cannot see.

**The Verse and Its Companion**

The verse repeats the argument of 7:193 in a shorter form and adds a detail: the idols appear to face the worshipper. The Book's readers are shown the difference between appearance and capacity, and the sūrah's treatment of the associate-gods closes with an image rather than a syllogism.

**"They Cannot Hear"**

The Qur'an's statement about the idols' deafness appears in Sūrat al-Aḥqāf, where the gods are unaware even of the calls of those who invoke them (46:5), and in the sūrah's own earlier verse about response (7:193). The Book's readers are told that the beings addressed in the sanctuaries receive nothing, and the point is made in the plainest terms.

**"You May See Them Facing Towards You"**

The image of idols with faces turned toward the worshipper is one of the Book's most economical pieces of satire, and its target is the appearance of a relationship. The sūrah's readers are shown the semblance of attention and told that nothing accompanies it, and the contrast between the sculpted gaze and the absent perception is the verse's whole content.

**The Book's Irony**

The Qur'an's polemics against the idols frequently work by such irony: the image that seems to look, the god that must be carried, the deity that cannot defend its own temple. The sūrah's readers are invited to see the objects of the cult as their makers designed them, and the verse's last clause states what the design cannot supply.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Treatment of Sight**

The sūrah had used the vocabulary of vision and hearing in its description of the heedless who have eyes and do not see (7:179), and in the catalogue of the faculties given to the human being. The Book's readers are thus given two kinds of blindness in the same sūrah: the blindness of creatures who refuse to use their senses and the blindness of objects that have none.

**The Doctrine of Dead Objects**

The Book's readers are told that the idols are not merely powerless but inanimate, and the Qur'an's account of the calf had made the point in Israel's history: the golden image could not speak or guide (7:148). The sūrah's final verse about the idols states the same fact for the cult of Arabia, and the two scenes belong to one teaching.

**The Worshippers' Vision**

The verse leaves the worshippers to their own sight: they can see the idols, and the idols cannot see them. The Book's readers are shown the worshipper's position in its starkest form — a person standing before an object that appears to look at him and cannot — and the sūrah's argument closes with that picture.

**The Next Verse's Command**

The sūrah's next verse turns to the Prophet with an instruction about conduct: to be gracious, to enjoin what is right, and to turn away from the ignorant (7:199). The Book's order is significant: the argument against the idols is followed by the community's ethic, and the reader is carried from polemic to practice.

**The Qur'an's Understanding of Guidance Stated Again**

The verse's phrase about calling the idols to guidance repeats the sūrah's concern with the theme, and the Book's readers are reminded that guidance is God's to give. The sūrah's final verses about the recitation, the remembrance, and the prostration of the angels describe the community's participation in that guidance, and the passage moves from the denial of false sources to the practice of the true one.

**The Reader Addressed**

The verse's second person addresses both the idolaters and the sūrah's own community, and the Book's readers are invited to consider where they look for guidance. The question's relevance extends past the sanctuaries of Makkah, and the sūrah's closing instructions supply the answer in the form of a discipline.

**The Passage's End and the Sūrah's Turn**

The examination of the associate-gods is complete with this verse, and the sūrah's last eight verses address the community's conduct, refuge, recitation, and worship. The Book's arrangement places the sūrah's practice after its argument, and the reader is given the doctrine and the discipline in the order the Book chose.

**The Closing Movement Anticipated**

The sūrah's readers are about to be told to be gracious to those who wrong them, to seek refuge in God, and to listen to the Qur'an in silence — instructions that presuppose everything the sūrah has taught. The final verse's angels, who are not too proud to worship and who prostrate, complete the passage's contrast: the idols have no faces to turn and cannot see, and the nearest to God bow before Him.

The verse's clause about the idols facing the worshipper also carries a warning about the appearance of religion: the Book's readers are shown that a cult can have all the marks of devotion — a fixed direction, a gaze, a posture — and none of the reality. The sūrah's readers are asked to consider what distinguishes the worship of the living God from the service of an image, and the sūrah's final instructions supply the answer: hearing, remembrance, and prostration before the Lord.
