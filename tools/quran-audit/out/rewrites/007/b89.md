**"Then We Would Have Fabricated a Lie Against Allah"**

*Qad iftaraynā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban in ʿudnā fī millatikum baʿda idh najjānā Allāhu minhā* — we would have fabricated a lie against Allah if we returned to your religion after Allah saved us from it. The answer to the expulsion threat is the sūrah's clearest statement of the cost of a return, and it names the cost with the vocabulary the sūrah reserves for the gravest crime in its book: *iftirāʾ*, the fabrication of a lie against God.

The term has been in the sūrah since verse 37, where the interrogative formed its most solemn sentence: *who is more wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God?* For Shuʿayb to say that a return to Madyan's religion would make him one of those people is to accept the town's frame and reverse its polarity. The elite had argued in terms of public order; the messenger answers in terms of truth\. To profess a religion he knows to be false in order to keep a place in a town is not prudence but a claim about God that God has not made.

**"After Allah Has Saved Us From It"**

The clause *baʿda idh najjānā Allāhu minhā* adds a second layer: what the town offers as a homecoming, Shuʿayb describes as a rescue already accomplished. The believer's relationship to his former religion is not that of a person who has left a village but of someone who has been taken out of a ruin. The Book's vocabulary for conversion in several places uses this language of deliverance: Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own account of the followers of the messengers, Sūrat al-Māʾidah's promise that God will bring a people whom He loves, and Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's description of the one who says, *“I was among the heedless”*. The town's proposal is an offer to return to what the messenger regards as the disease from which he has been cured.

The Book's later sūrahs make the refusal the duty of the community and the point of its identity: *“Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment”* (16:106). The narrative places that absolute in the mouth of a prophet who is being offered a practical way out.

**"Unless Allah Wills"**

*Wa-mā yakūnu lanā an naʿūda fīhā illā an yashāʾa Allāhu rabbunā* — and it is not for us to return to it unless Allah our Lord wills. The exception is one of the Book's characteristic qualifications, and it is a statement about the limits of the human will rather than a loophole in the refusal. The Qur'an places the clause on the believer's tongue in matters of resolve: *“you do not will unless Allah wills”* (76:30; cf. 81:29), and it teaches the community to say *God willing* about its own plans.

The clause protects the refusal from the sin of claiming absolute autonomy. Shuʿayb does not say *we will never return even if God wills it*; he says that a return is not open to them as agents, because the human will is itself a created thing. The Book's doctrine of the will is stated most precisely in Sūrat al-Insān's closing verse: *“what you will is only what Allah wills”* (76:30, from His decree). The phrase is thus the deepest level of the verse's answer to the town: even the refusal to return is not a personal achievement.

**"Our Lord Encompasses All Things in Knowledge"**

*Wasiʿa rabbunā kulla shayʾin ʿilman* — our Lord encompasses all things in knowledge. The Book's omniscience verses typically appear at decisive moments, and this one is placed where the town's leaders are weighing a private calculation and the messenger is declaring that no calculation is private. Sūrat al-Talāq's famous verse uses the same concept: *“Allah is the One who created seven heavens... that you may know that Allah has power over all things and that Allah encompasses all things in knowledge”* (65:12). Sūrat al-Anʿām states it in a form that would make any conspiracy absurd: *“And with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them but He; and He knows whatever is on the land and in the sea; and not a leaf falls but He knows it”* (6:59).

**"In Allah We Put Our Trust"**

*ʿAlā Allāhi tawakkalnā* — in Allah we have placed our trust. The Book's *tawakkul* is the disposition of a person who has done what can be done and then released the outcome, and the sūrah of the family of ʿImrān explains the sequence: *“And when you have decided, then place your trust in Allah; surely Allah loves those who trust”* (3:159). Shuʿayb's community had delivered its case, refused the coercion, and been told to wait; the trust is the state in which the waiting is possible.

The declared trust is also the community's answer to the threat of expulsion. What Madyan controlled was a place in Madyan, and the verse's sentence says that the believers' security is held elsewhere. The Book's later account of the believers' answer to Pharaoh uses the same formula: *“Your rage towards us is only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us. Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting ˹to You˺.’”* (7:126 vicinity; 10:85).

**The Prayer for Judgment**

*Rabbanā iftaḥ baynanā wa-bayna qawminā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-anta khayru al-fātiḥīn* — our Lord, judge between us and our people with truth; You are the best of judges. The closing petition is the sūrah's own version of the prophets' final resource. ʿĀd's messenger was told to wait; Noah was given a century; Shuʿayb asks for the verdict. The verb *fataḥa* — to open, to decide, to give the opening judgment — is used for legal and military verdicts throughout the Book, and the psalm-like echo of the request in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's later narratives (26:118) shows how consistently the Qur'an's prophets place the dispute in the court of the One who cannot err.

The prayer is also the seed of the town's destruction; the Book records that Madyan's end arrived as the day of the shadow (26:189). The sūrah's narrative will report the outcome without comment. Its interest is in the sentence that precedes it, in which the messenger's case, the town's coercion, and the community's willingness to wait are all on the record — and in a prayer that reads, in retrospect, like the last correspondence of a doomed city with its Creator.
