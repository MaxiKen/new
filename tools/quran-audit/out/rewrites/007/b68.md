**The Counsel and the Commission**

*Uballeghukum rasālāti rabbī wa-anā lakum nāṣiḥun amīn* — I convey to you the messages of my Lord, and I am a sincere adviser to you. Hūd's sentence repeats Noah's double duty with one addition at the end: *amīn*, trustworthy. The word is the crown of the verse, and it belongs to the vocabulary of deposit and return. *Amīn* is the person entrusted with something — a caravan's goods, a household's stores, a secret — who returns it whole. The messenger is a trustee of the message, and the term claims for the transmission the reliability the towns were denying him in the previous verse.

The slogan is the same one that the Qur'an uses for the Prophet himself: *“I am a trustworthy messenger to you”* (26:107, 26:125, 26:143, 26:162, 26:178), and the sequence in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows it deliberately repeated by each prophet in the sūrah's own cadence. Trustworthiness is the one quality the Book insists on for its messengers: not brilliance, not eloquence, not miracles, but the faithful return of what was entrusted to them.

**What Trustworthiness Is For**

The Qur'an's arguments for revelation lean heavily on this attribute, and the way it uses it is nearly forensic. A message is only as good as its carrier; a messenger who alters the text to suit his audience destroys the evidence he is bringing. Hence the Book's repeated insistence that the Prophet recite without adjustment — *“Say: I do not say to you that the treasures of Allah are with me, or that I know the unseen, or that I am an angel; I only follow what is revealed to me”* (6:50) — and its equal insistence that the message be delivered whole, including the parts that are inconvenient to its own community: *“What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.’”* (38:70); *“Say, ‘I am not the first messenger ever sent, nor do I know what will happen to me or you. I only follow what is revealed to me. And I am only sent with a clear warning.’”* (46:9).

The makings of ʿĀd's refusal become visible under this instruction. The chiefs did not accuse their messenger of incompetence; they accused him of lying. The verse takes that charge and answers it with a claim about the messenger's reliability — which, for the reader, will be tested by whether the message survives the tongue of a man who was going to be killed for it.

**Sincerity Without Return**

*Nāṣiḥ* — the sincere adviser — reappears here, the second use of the word in the sūrah's sequence, after Noah's. It is worth noting what the messengers do not receive for it. Nowhere in the sūrah's narratives does the sincere adviser of a town profit in any way from his counsel; the Qur'an's prophets are poor, mocked, boycotted, and in most cases driven out. Hūd's audience will end the story by asking him to justify himself with a miracle and will then call for the destruction he warned them of (7:70). The Book's prophets are, by design, without leverage.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ' formula makes this explicit in the messenger's own voice: *“I ask no reward of you for it; my reward is only with the Lord of all worlds”* (26:127). The repeated phrase means that any account of religion as a profession of self-interest must fail against the Book's own description of its prophets, and ʿĀd's charges — fool and liar — collapse against a man with nothing to gain.

**The Sūrah's Doctrine of the Messenger**

This verse and its counterpart in Noah's account give the sūrah's fullest summary of what a messenger is, and everything else in the Book's account hangs on it. He does not rule, does not heal at will, does not know the unseen, and does not command obedience for its own sake — Sūrat al-Kahf instructs the Prophet to announce his servitude and mortality in one breath (18:110), and Sūrat al-Naḥl tells him *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. … You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22 in its sūrah). What he does is carry a text and give counsel.

The narrowness is the point. The Book is protecting the message from its carrier, and the towns in this sūrah are shown repeatedly trying to turn the argument into a referendum on the prophet's character — the accusation of folly, the charge of lying, the sneer at verse 75 that the believers are following a deluded man. The messenger's reply to all of it in these verses is the same: what he carries is not his, and he will not let the discussion migrate from the text to himself.

**The Two Words the Towns Never Use**

The sūrah's contrast can be stated precisely. The messengers call themselves *transmitters* and *counsellors*; the towns call them *fools*, *liars*, and *misguided*. In the five narratives of this sūrah, the elite never once accuse the messenger of altering the message or of wanting something from them — because the messengers' conduct makes those charges impossible. What remains for the towns to attack is the messenger's person, and the sūrah's readers, watching the exchange from the distance of a few thousand years and a few pages, are being trained to notice when the same substitution happens in their own arguments.
