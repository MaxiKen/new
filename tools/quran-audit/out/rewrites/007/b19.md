**The Generosity of the Command**

The narrative returns to Adam with a command that is almost all permission. *Uskun* — dwell, take up residence — and then *fa-kulā min ḥaythu shiʾtumā*: eat from wherever the two of you wish. The Qur'an's fuller account in Sūrah Ṭā-Hā fills in what the permission covers: *“Here it is guaranteed that you will never go hungry or unclothed, nor will you ever suffer from thirst or the sun's heat”* (20:118–119). Food, clothing, water, shade — the four anxieties that organize most human labor — are declared settled in advance. The Garden is presented not as a reward to be earned but as a home to be inhabited, and the single prohibition is set inside a field of open permissions.

**One Tree, Deliberately Unnamed**

*Wa-lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarah* — and do not come near this tree. Two details of the wording carry the teaching. The first is *lā taqrabā*, do not approach, rather than *lā taʾkulā*, do not eat. The Qur'an uses the same construction for adultery — *“Do not go near adultery”* (17:32) — and for the orphan's property — *“do not come near the wealth of the orphan”* (6:152). The prohibition is a fence placed around the act, not merely a line at the act itself, and the jurisprudence of *sadd al-dharāʾiʿ* — blocking the pathways to what is forbidden — is read by the jurists out of exactly this idiom.

The second detail is that the tree is never identified. The commentators hold that God withheld the name deliberately. Al-Ṭabarī records statements to the effect that it was a fig or a vine or another fruit, and then notes that no report is authoritative and none is needed; Ibn Kathīr draws the conclusion that disputing about the species is time spent on what the verse chose to leave open. The purpose of the prohibition was never information about botany. It was obedience: a single, unmistakable boundary in the middle of an unlimited gift, so that the creature's freedom would have somewhere to be exercised.

**Both of Them, and the Silence About Hawwāʾ**

The verse addresses the pair: *dwell, you and your wife* — and the wife is not named anywhere in the Qur'an. The Book has kept her identity from the text, though the commentators transmit the name Hawwāʾ from the older reports.

That silence is worth noticing, because of what the surrounding verses do with the pair. In 7:20–22 the tempter addresses *them*, both of them act, and both are exposed together. The Qur'an never assigns the first initiative to the woman. Where the older scriptural narrative presents a sequence in which the woman is deceived and then leads the man, this sūrah presents a single act of two people, and the responsibility is shared and equal: *“And their Lord called unto them, ‘Did I not forbid you that tree?’”* The point matters because a long tradition of misogynist preaching has drawn from the story a doctrine of female culpability for the first sin. The Qur'an's own text does not support it, and the careful reader of this verse has already been given the corrective: the warning was addressed to both, the command was given to both, and the failure was theirs together.

**The Anatomy of a Test**

Set within the sūrah's architecture, the verse completes the scene the reader has been prepared for. Verses 11 to 18 showed a creature who refused a command and lost everything by refusing it. Here a different creature receives a command to hold the boundary, and inside the permission there is freedom to move, to eat, to enjoy. What is being tested is not appetite — the appetite is licensed — but trust: whether the word of God about a tree will be believed over the apparent silence of everything else.

The final clause names the cost of the failure before it happens: *fa-takūnā min al-ẓālimīn* — lest you two become of the wrongdoers. It is the same word the townspeople of verse 5 used of themselves at the moment of destruction, and the same word that will reappear in Adam's own confession at verse 23. The sūrah is teaching its vocabulary as it goes: the *ẓulm* of the first verses is a misplacement, and a creature who places his own judgment above a command has displaced himself from where he was put.

**Law and Mercy in One Verse**

The theological weight of the passage sits in the coexistence of the two facts it announces at once. There is a command that must be obeyed, and there is a garden wide enough that disobeying it is never necessary. The boundary is narrow; the room around it is vast. Rabbinic and Christian traditions later built elaborate doctrines of a fallen nature on the first act; the Islamic reading, formed by these verses, insists on something more precise. The human being was created with a real capacity to obey — the permission was genuine, and no one was placed in a trap — and the same human being was created with a real capacity to fall, because a creature whose obedience was automatic would not be a moral being at all.

The rest of the sūrah will show what happens when the boundary is crossed — the exposure, the shame, the sewing of leaves, the searching call of God in the Garden — and how a creature who has fallen is to answer. But the verse's own balance should be preserved before the story moves on: the command that will be broken was given by a Lord who had first spread a table.
