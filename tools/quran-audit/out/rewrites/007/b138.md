**"Make for Us a God Like Their Gods"**

*Wa-jāwaznā bi-Banī Isrāʾīla al-baḥra fa-atā ʿalā qawmin yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmihim qālū yā Mūsā ijʿal lanā ilāhan ka-mā lahum ālihatun qāla innakum qawmun tajhalūn* — We brought the Children of Israel across the sea and they came upon a people devoted to idols. They demanded: "O Moses! Make for us a god like their gods." He replied: "Indeed, you are a people acting ignorantly!"

**The Scene Immediately After the Deliverance**

The verse's first word is the crossing, and its second is the request. The Book places the demand for an idol in the same breath as the rescue from Egypt, and the juxtaposition is the sūrah's whole judgment on the community's state of mind. The people who had watched the sea open and the army drown asked their prophet to manufacture a deity that afternoon.

**Devoted to Idols**

*Yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmihim* — the verb describes a settled attachment, a people stationed at their images. The translation's "devoted" catches the sense: the Book is describing a community's habitual posture, not an isolated ritual. The Qur'an's analysis of idolatry elsewhere treats it as an inherited practice with a social function, which is exactly what makes it attractive to a people in transition.

**The Request's Logic**

The Israelites had left a country of temples and arrived among a people with visible gods, and they wanted the visibility. The Book's narratives show this preference repeatedly: the demand to see Allah with their own eyes (2:55), the demand for a sign on paper (4:153), and the calf manufactured during Moses' absence (20:88). The request at the sea is the first entry in a pattern the sūrah will continue to document.

**Moses' Diagnosis**

*Innakum qawmun tajhalūn* — you are a people acting ignorantly. The prophet's answer is not a curse or a dismissal; it is a description. The Qur'an's vocabulary of ignorance (*jahl*) names a condition of acting without knowledge rather than a lack of intelligence, and the sūrah's sentence treats the request as a symptom to be treated rather than a crime to be punished.

**The Book's Critique of Inherited Religion**

The demand's premise is the practice it observed, and the Qur'an meets that premise head-on in sūrah after sūrah. *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing’”* (2:170). Sūrat al-Zukhruf reports the same formula in four consecutive verses: *“We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps”* (43:22), with the warner's question — *“Even if what I brought you is better guidance than what you found your forefathers practicing?”* — and the elite's answer that they reject it (43:23–24).

**Abraham's Argument Against the Ancestors**

The Qur'an's longest treatment of this logic is placed on Abraham's lips. The sūrah of al-Shuʿarāʾ records the question and the reply — *“They replied, ‘No! But we found our forefathers doing the same’”* (26:74) — followed by Abraham's demolition of the position: *“Have you ˹really˺ considered what you have been worshipping — you and your ancestors? They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds”* (26:75–77). The same scene in al-Anbiyāʾ has Abraham say to the people and their forefathers: *“Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray”* (21:54).

**The Footsteps Metaphor**

Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt uses the image of walking in another's tracks to describe the mechanism of inherited idolatry: *“Indeed, they found their forefathers astray, so they rushed in their footsteps!”* (37:69–70). The Book's complaint is not that tradition exists but that it is being used as a substitute for judgment, and its sentence about the majority is stated without qualification: *“If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's Way”* (6:116).

**The Favour They Had Just Received**

The verse's request is made by people who had been spared, fed, and freed. The Book recalls the sequence for them directly: *“O Children of Israel! We saved you from your enemy, and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and sent down to you manna and quails”* (20:80). Israel's pattern of response to blessing is one of the Qur'an's most persistent themes, and the sūrah's narrative does not soften it.

**The Refusal to Be Taught by Deliverance**

The request also shows what a deliverance cannot do. The Book's account of the exodus never suggests that the crossing produced a community of believers; it produced a freed people, whose formation would require law, testing, and time. Verse 138 is the earliest statement of that gap, and the sūrah's later narratives fill it with the golden calf, the spies, and the wilderness.

**Moses' Answer Begins a Sequence**

The prophet's response continues into the next two verses, and its structure is a lesson in how the Book deals with demands of this kind: the diagnosis of ignorance (7:138), the demonstration that the object of imitation is worthless (7:139), and the reminder of what the community already has (7:140). The sūrah gives the answer in three sentences, and each of them addresses a different level of the request.

**The Memory of the Idols' Fate**

The people the Israelites encountered were eventually overtaken by the same principle that destroyed the towns in the sūrah's earlier narratives, and the request's comparison therefore carries an unintentional prophecy. The Book's readers have just finished the story of Egypt's gods and their state; the scene of Israel asking for a version of the same thing is the sūrah's bitterest irony.

**The Warning for Later Communities**

The Book's placement of this scene immediately after the drowning makes it a permanent warning about what a community can do with its freedom. The Qur'an's readers were told the story of a people who were saved from a state and asked for its gods within sight of the sea, and the sūrah's narrative continues in the next verses to answer them.
