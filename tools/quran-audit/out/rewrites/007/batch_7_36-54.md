## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:36

> **But those who deny Our signs and treat them with disdain, it is they who are the inhabitants of the Fire. They shall abide therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rejection With Contempt**

The previous verse described the reverent who mend; this one describes the opposite, and it does not simply say that they disbelieved. The Arabic is *alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-stakbarū ʿanhā* — those who denied Our signs *and treated them with disdain*. Two acts, joined by *wa*: denial, and then the arrogance that refuses to take them seriously.

The distinction is not a redundancy. A person may deny a claim and still treat it with the respect due to a serious proposition; he may weigh it, reject it on grounds, and remain answerable for his reasoning. What this verse names is the other posture: the refusal to grant the claim a hearing, the turning away of the proud man who is offended by the thought that he might be addressed. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn's image of the arrogant is that they turn their heads aside (63:5); Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own image, six verses later, will be the camel and the needle's eye.

**The Echo of Iblīs**

The word *istakbara* is the same word the sūrah used for Iblīs in verse 12 — *abā wa-stakbara*, he refused and was arrogant — and in al-Baqarah's account of the same scene, *“he refused and acted arrogantly, becoming one of the disbelievers”* (2:34). The echo is structural. The sūrah opened its narrative with a creature destroyed by arrogance against a command; here, at the end of the passage addressed to the children of Adam, the same word describes the fate of those who meet the signs of God with disdain. The chapter's answer to its own opening story is that arrogance is not a historical curiosity in the biography of one jinn. It is the recurring disposition that turns rejection into final loss.

Narrative aside, the Book treats *istikbār* as a category of its own with a specific destination. *“Your Lord has proclaimed: Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). *“On the Day of Judgment you will see those who lied about Allah with their faces gloomy. Is Hell not a fitting home for the arrogant?”* (39:60). *“The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels who are near. And whoever is too proud to worship Him and is arrogant — He will gather them all to Himself”* (4:172). The last verse is notable for who is included: even the highest created beings are said never to be too proud, which leaves the human arrogance of 7:36 with no model to imitate and no company.

**The Definition the Prophet Gave**

The sūrah's readers would have understood the word, but the Prophet ﷺ defined it, and the definition is a small masterpiece of precision: *“Arrogance is rejecting the truth and belittling people”* (Muslim 91, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd). A single sentence, two halves, and both are present in this verse.

*Rejecting the truth* is the denial: *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā*. *Belittling people* is what the arrogant posture does to the messenger and to the community of the weak whom the message gathers — and the sūrah's own account of the Makkan leadership is a study in exactly that (see 7:75–76, where the proud ask whether they should believe as the poor do). The Prophet's definition leaves no room for the self-flattering interpretation of arrogance as dignity. In his sentence, it is not a feeling about oneself at all; it is a pair of actions, one toward God's truth and one toward His creatures.

He also stated the consequence in a form this verse's audience would have grasped immediately: *“No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise”* (Muslim 91). The sūrah will shortly translate the same verdict into the image of the gates and the needle.

**"The Inhabitants of the Fire"**

The sentence is stated flatly: *fa-ulāʾika aṣḥāb al-nār*, they are the companions of the Fire; *hum fīhā khālidūn*, they will abide there. Two words carry the theology.

*Aṣḥāb* is the vocabulary of companionship and ownership — the term Arabic uses for the owner of a thing, the companion of a person, the people of a place. The Qur'an could have said, "they will be put in the fire"; it says they are *its people*. The relationship is one of settled residence, which is what the word *khālidūn*, abiding forever, then states explicitly.

The Messenger of God's description of the same place preserves the sūrah's insistence that what ends there began as a manner of dealing with the truth. The Prophet ﷺ said that the Fire will be brought forward on the Day with seventy thousand bridles, each held by seventy thousand angels, and that it will arrive with its people. What the verse establishes is not an arbitrary sentence but the completion of a relationship: the servant who despised the signs spent his life elsewhere, and is placed with the objects of his attention.

**The Contempt That Costs Nothing Until It Costs Everything**

One final observation belongs to the psychology of the verse, and the sūrah has prepared for it. *Istikbār* was the cheapest option available to Iblīs: a feeling, requiring no act, expressed in nothing but a refusal to bend. It was, by any worldly measure, a low-cost choice. That is what makes it so dangerous, because it is the reason the proud do not recognize the moment of their loss. Nothing happened; no deed was performed; no harm was done to another; and a creature's destiny quietly closed the other way.

The children of Adam are warned at the end of a passage about clothing, food, and worship — all of it tangible, all of it actionable — and this verse names the one thing an otherwise decent life can carry into damnation. Disdain does not look like sin. It looks like standards.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:37

> **And who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God or denies His signs? For such as these, their portion of the book will reach them, till, when Our messengers come to take them away, they will say, “Where is that which you used to call upon apart from God?” They will respond, “They have forsaken us.” And they bear witness against themselves that they were disbelievers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Crimes That Head the List**

*Fa-man aẓlamu mimman iftarā ʿalā Allāh kadhiban aw kadhdhaba bi-āyātihi* — and who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie against God or denies His signs? The interrogative is rhetorical in form and absolute in force: the question has no answer other than the one the verse has just named. The sūrah has been circling this pair for ten verses. The lie against God was committed by those who said *God has commanded us thus* (7:28) and by those who forbade what God had made lawful (7:32). The denial of the signs was the disdain of verse 36.

The Book repeats this superlative with striking consistency. *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed”* (6:21). *“Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah or claims, ‘I have received revelations!’ — although nothing was revealed to him”* (6:93). *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah? They will be brought before their Lord, and the witnesses will say, ‘These are the ones who lied against their Lord’”* (11:18). *“Who then does more wrong than those who lie about Allah and reject the truth after it has reached them?”* (39:32). The cumulative effect of the repetition is to establish a hierarchy in the Qur'anic understanding of sin: the gravest wrong is not violence but the corruption of the channel through which God is known — either by inventing a message in His name or by refusing the message He sent.

**"Their Portion of the Book Will Reach Them"**

The next clause has been read in two ways by the commentators, and both readings are instructive. *Fa-ulāʾika yanāluhum naṣībuhum min al-kitāb* — those, their portion of the Book will reach them.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record the first reading: the *kitāb* is the decree, the record of what has been written for each soul, and the verse states that whatever share of provision or punishment has been apportioned will be delivered in full — nothing withheld, nothing adrift. The second reading takes *kitāb* as the scripture itself: their portion of what was revealed will reach them, whether they receive it as guidance and reject it, or hear it only as a testimony against them at the reckoning.

The two readings converge on a single idea that the sūrah has been building since its first verses. Nothing owed to a person fails to arrive. The record of verses 6 to 9 — the question, the recounting, the weighing — is a description of an accounting that cannot lose any part of its data. The phrase *will reach them* is the Book's reassurance that the encounter with the truth is not optional and not avoidable: a message that has been delivered is delivered, and the one who walks away from it has not escaped the meeting, only deferred it.

**The Messengers Who Come to Take Them Away**

*Ḥattā idhā jāʾathum rusulunā yatawaffawnahum* — until, when Our messengers come to take them away. The messengers here are the angels of death, and the Qur'an gives their work a vocabulary of precision. Sūrat al-Sajdah names the office: *“Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you; then to your Lord you will be returned”* (32:11). Sūrat al-Anʿām describes the process of the *tawaffī*, the taking of the soul: *“He reigns supreme over all of His creation, and sends recording-angels, watching over you. When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, and they never neglect their duty”* (6:61). And where the death is a punishment, the account is harsher: *“If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and their backs”* (8:50; cf. 47:27).

There is a deep congruence between the sūrah's structure and this verse. Its first scene was a courtroom — the question, the book, the scale. The same court is now shown convening at the bedside of a single person, and the questions are asked in the past tense of a life that has just closed.

**The Question and Its Answer**

*Ayna mā kuntum tadʿūna min dūni Allāh* — where is that which you used to call upon apart from God? The interrogation is the exact fulfillment of what the sūrah warned in verse 30: the patrons who were taken as protectors instead of God are now the objects of a demand. And the answer the dying person gives is the shortest sentence in the verse: *qālū ḍallū ʿannā* — they said, they have forsaken us.

The Book records this scene from other angles, and the additional detail is always the same. *“with Allah? They will cry, ‘They have failed us. In fact, we did not invoke anything real before’”* (40:74). *“Why then did those they took as gods… not help them? Rather, they failed them, and that was their lie”* (46:28). *“Those against whom the decree of torment is justified will cry, ‘Our Lord, these are the ones we misled; we misled them as we ourselves were misled. We now disown them before You’”* (28:63). *“They have taken other gods, instead of Allah, seeking strength and protection. No! They will deny their worship of them, and become opponents to them”* (19:81–82). The consistency of the record matters: at the moment of extraction, no patron answers the call. The theology of *tawḥīd* is not a philosophical preference; it is a description of what a person will discover he had access to when he has finally stopped being able to pretend.

**"They Bear Witness Against Themselves"**

The verse closes with self-condemnation: *wa-shahidū ʿalā anfusihim annahum kānū kāfirīn* — and they bear witness against themselves that they were disbelievers. The courtroom that has stamped the whole sūrah now gets its confession, and it is not extracted by the prosecutor. Sūrat al-Anʿām has the same formula: *“You have certainly come to Us individually as We created you the first time… and they will bear witness against themselves that they were disbelievers”* (6:130; cf. 41:21, where the skin itself testifies). Sūrat al-Qiyāmah puts the principle as a general law of the human constitution: *“In fact, people will testify against their own souls, despite the excuses they come up with”* (75:14–15).

The verse thus ends with the last form of the argument this sūrah has been making since verse 16. Iblīs blamed God; the townspeople of verse 5 confessed too late; the fabricators of verse 28 denied knowing better. This verse shows the door closed on every one of those positions at the hour of death, when the excuses have run out because the memory that produced them is intact and its owner is the one reading it aloud to himself. The passage began with the children of Adam and a whisper in a garden; it ends with their descendants bearing witness against themselves, and no one in the room to blame.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:38

> **He will say, “Enter the Fire among communities of jinn and men that have passed away before you!” Every time a community enters, it curses its sister, till, when they have all successively arrived there, the last of them will say of the first of them, “Our Lord, it was they who led us astray; so give them a double punishment in the Fire.” He will say, “For each of you it shall be doubled, but you know not.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence at the Gate**

The sūrah's court has reached the stage of execution. *Qāla dkhulū fī umamin qad khalat min qablikum mina al-jinni wa-l-ins* — enter the Fire among the communities of jinn and men that have passed away before you. The wording is careful in two respects. The condemned do not enter alone; they enter *among* communities, as members of a company. And the company is not a single nation but the whole accumulated population of the two kinds — jinn and human beings — that preceded them. The last generation is not judged in isolation; it is inserted into a procession.

The commentators read the verse as the answer to a specific hope, one the Makkan leadership entertained and which the Qur'an records elsewhere. Sūrah al-Aḥqāf has the leaders of the people saying, *“We found our forefathers following a particular way, and we are following in their footsteps”* (43:22), and Sūrat al-Zukhruf has the warners anticipate the objection (43:24). The verse before us closes that door at the last moment: the ancestors are not an alibi. They are the front of the line.

**"Every Time a Community Enters, It Curses Its Sister"**

*Kullamā dakhalat ummatun laʿanat ukhtahā.* The word *ukht* — sister — is doing something remarkable in this sentence. The Qur'an's ordinary vocabulary for a doomed nation is *ummah*, a group defined by its direction. Here the same groups are given a family relation: they are sisters, and the relation survives into the Fire. The curse that a community pronounces on the one before it is, in the Qur'an's image, the continuation of a quarrel between relatives.

That is the point of the clause, and it is one of the sharpest psychological observations in the Book. In this world, the relationships among the nations that reject revelation are presented as alliances: shared economy, shared ancestry, shared opposition. The verse shows what is underneath the alliance. Every newcomer to the Fire curses the one who preceded it — the generation that modelled the practice, the teachers who taught the doctrine, the fathers who made the religion of custom look like religion. The solidarities that held them together dissolve on contact with the consequence, and what is left is blame.

The tradition drew a warning from this verse that has become proverbial: the fear that one be the *mudill* — the one led away and, worse, the one who led others away. The Prophet ﷺ stated the accounting for it in the strongest terms: *“Whoever sets a bad precedent in Islam will bear its burden and the burden of whoever acts upon it after him, without their burdens being diminished in the least”* (Muslim 1017, from Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh). The verse of the Heights supplies the scene in which that hadith is enacted.

**The Plea of the Last for the First**

*Ḥattā idhā iddārakū fīhā jamīʿan qālat ukhrāhum li-ūlāhum* — until, when they have all overtaken one another therein, the last of them will say of the first: *Our Lord, these led us astray, so give them double punishment of the Fire.* The plea is precisely the one the Qur'an records in four other passages, in almost identical words. Sūrat al-Aḥzāb has the condemned in the Fire saying, *“Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray from the Right Way. Our Lord! Give them double our punishment, and condemn them tremendously”* (33:67–68). Sūrat Ṣād has the same petition (38:61). Sūrat Sabaʾ records the exchange in full (34:31–33). And Sūrat al-Baqarah states the outcome: those who misled others disown their followers, the bonds between them are cut, and the followers cry out for a second chance (2:166–167).

The pattern is so consistently repeated that it must be read as a deliberate teaching device. The Book is telling its readers, in advance, what the social architecture of religious error looks like when the scaffolding is removed: the influential and the dependent, the eloquent and the persuaded, the ones who commanded and the ones who obeyed — each standing where he placed himself, each blaming the other, and neither able to produce the other as a defense.

**"For Each Is Double"**

The divine answer is a single line: *qāla li-kullin diʿf* — He will say, for each is double, *wa-lākin lā taʿlamūn* — but you do not know. The answer does not accept the plea's allocation while granting its principle. It affirms that responsibility is real for every party — the leaders for the invitation, the followers for the acceptance — and it refuses to let either shift the burden.

The "double" is not a doubling of torture; it is the doubling that the Book attaches to whoever combines two offenses. The leader who sinned *and* caused others to sin carries both; the follower who sinned and who imitated another's authority carries what he earned. The same arithmetic is stated as a law of the Book: *“Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Surely it is an evil burden they bear”* (16:25). And it is balanced by the verse of the same Book that says every soul will receive only what it earned: *“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”* (35:18; 53:38).

**What the Verse Is For**

Read at the level of the sūrah's argument, the verse is a preemptive dismantling of the single most common alibi for inherited religion: that one was following one's betters. This is the same objection that verse 28 recorded in its polite form — *we found our fathers doing it* — now answered in its final form, when the fathers are in the same fire and the daughter-community has nothing to say except a curse.

The passage ends on a note that is easy to overrun: *but you do not know*. The clause is not mercy and not sarcasm; it is the closing of the epistemic door the sūrah has been walking through since verse 28. There are facts about the consequence of leading others astray that no human being can calculate — which is exactly why the Qur'an does not encourage the game of distributing blame and does encourage the single question the reader can answer: what am I teaching, by my words, my practice, and my silence? The cursed sister-communities of this verse never had a chance to discuss it afterwards.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:39

> **And the first of them will say to the last of them, “You are no better than us; so taste the punishment for that which you have earned.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply of the First to the Last**

*Wa-qālat ūlāhum li-ukhrāhum* — and the first of them will say to the last of them. The exchange of the previous verse is now answered, and the direction of the address is reversed. There, the later community cursed the earlier; here, the earlier turns on the later. The commentators differ on which group is which: al-Ṭabarī records readings in which *ūlāhum* means the leaders, the predecessors in time, or the predecessors in rank, and the same for *ukhrā*. What is not in dispute is the content of the reply, and it is a single sentence that dismantles every hierarchy the condemned had been relying on.

**"You Have No Favour Over Us"**

*Fa-mā kāna lakum ʿalaynā min faḍl* — you have no superiority over us. The word *faḍl* is the vocabulary of rank, merit, and advantage; it is the same word the Qur'an uses for God's bounty and for the distinctions He grants among people. The first community's reply is a claim of strict equality: whatever we did, you did; whatever we taught, you accepted; the ledger carries no favour of yours over ours.

The sentence is the reversal of the whole social logic of the world. In the marketplace of Makkah, *faḍl* was the measure of a man: lineage, wealth, the size of a following, the weight of a name. The Qur'an's earlier verses in this sūrah had shown the same logic operating in religion and being applied to the believers — the arrogant chiefs asking, *“Are you certain that Ṣāliḥ is truly a messenger from his Lord?”* while the lowly answered, *“We surely believe in what he has been sent with”* (7:75–76). Here, in the Fire, the question of *faḍl* is raised one last time, and the answer is the same for everyone: none of you is any better than the others in the only ranking that survived.

The Book pronounces the same verdict as a general law of the Hereafter. *“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”* (35:18; 53:38; 6:164). *“Whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good; and whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”* (17:15). The plain meaning of the doctrine is that the moral architecture of this life — rank, influence, dependency, the ability to make others carry one's cost — does not extend across the threshold, and every one of those arrangements which the previous verse showed collapsing is here denied the last consolation it might have claimed.

**"Taste the Punishment for What You Used to Earn"**

*Fa-dhūqū al-ʿadhāb bimā kuntum taksibūn* — so taste the punishment for what you used to earn. Two features of the clause are worth noting. The verb *dhūqū*, taste, is the same verb the sūrah used for Adam and his wife's act in the Garden — *“when they tasted of the tree”* (7:22). The Book has a vocabulary of tasting that runs from the first forbidden mouthful to the last consequence of a life; the same faculty that was designed to sample the good provision of God is now the faculty being addressed with the payment. The second feature is *kuntum taksibūn*, you used to earn: *kasb*, earning, is the Qur'an's commercial word for action. An act is a wage. The sūrah has already put a scale on the Day and called it *ḥaqq* (7:8); this verse names the currency.

**The Argument That Fails Everywhere**

The exchange between the leaders and the followers belongs to a family of scenes in which the Qur'an rehearses, in advance, every alibi a human being might carry into the reckoning. What emerges from the whole set is that the argument of *following* never works, and the Book itself gives the reason. Sūrat al-Baqarah's account of the same dispute ends with the followers saying, *“If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us”* (2:167) — that is, the regret is not that they were misled but that they obeyed. Sūrat Sabaʾ puts the rebuttal in the mouths of the leaders: *“Did we ever hinder you from guidance after it came to you? In fact, you were wicked on your own”* (34:32).

And the reason the argument fails is that the guidance was never held hostage by any human being. The Qur'an describes the obligation of the follower and the obligation of the leader as two separate accounts, and it states that the message reaches both: *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the meeting of this Day of yours?”* (6:130). Between the two groups stands a criterion the followers possessed all along — the ability to check, to ask, to refuse, to seek — and the verse's refusal of their plea is the refusal to let a person hand back the faculty with which he was created.

**The Mercy Hidden in a Verse of Blame**

It is worth pausing, finally, on what this refusal protects. A world in which the follower could transfer responsibility to the leader would be a world in which the powerful could not be blamed for anything and the weak would be blamed for everything. The Qur'an's insistence on individual accountability is the only arrangement in which each person has something to answer for that is genuinely his, and in which the imposing of authority — a teacher's, a parent's, a ruler's, a movement's — remains subject to a standard no human authority can override.

The Prophet ﷺ summarized the resulting posture in a hadith that the sūrah's own readers kept on their tongues: *“No obedience in disobedience to Allah; obedience is only in what is right”* (al-Bukhārī 7257; Muslim 1840, from ʿAlī). The verse of the Heights supplies the reason: the day is coming when the first will turn on the last and nobody's rank will count. Between now and then, there is one relation whose rank holds, and the book of this sūrah has already named it: *“Some He has guided, and some are deserving of error”* (7:30).

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:40

> **Truly those who deny Our signs and wax arrogant against them, the gates of Heaven shall not be opened for them, nor shall they enter the Garden till the camel pass through the eye of the needle. Thus do We recompense the guilty!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gates That Do Not Open**

*Inna alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-stakbarū ʿanhā lā tufattaḥu lahum abwāb al-samāʾ* — surely those who denied Our signs and treated them with disdain, the gates of heaven shall not be opened for them. The verse is a mirror of the one before it, with a single phrase exchanged: where verse 36 said *they are the inhabitants of the Fire*, this verse says what the Fire means in architectural terms. The gates do not open.

The image of the gates of heaven is a Qur'anic constant, and its use here is precise. In Sūrat al-Zumar, the gates are opened for the blessed: *“Those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in successive groups. When they arrive at its already open gates, its keepers will say, ‘Peace be upon you! You have done well, so enter it, to stay forever’”* (39:73) — while the disbelievers, driven in groups to the Fire, also find gates, and are told: *“Enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever. What an evil home for the arrogant!”* (39:72). The Book's heavens are gate-able: Sūrat al-Nabaʾ describes the sky split open, *becoming many gates* (78:19), and Sūrat al-Qamar records the opening of the gates of the sky with pouring rain (54:11). To say that the gates will not be opened for this group is therefore to say that the entire architecture of access — ascent, acceptance, entry — is closed to them, and the reason given is the one this sūrah has been developing since its first narrative: *disdain*.

**"Until the Camel Passes Through the Eye of the Needle"**

*Wa-lā yadkhulūna al-jannata ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭ* — nor shall they enter the Garden until the camel passes through the eye of the needle. The Arabic of the clause has been the subject of one of the most careful philological discussions in the tradition, and the discussion is instructive.

The word read *jamal* means camel. A second reading, well attested in the qirāʾāt and defended by lexicographers, is *jummal*: a thick ship's cable, a hawser. Al-Ṭabarī reports both and notes that under either reading the sentence's force is identical, because both images describe the impossible: a large body passing through a small aperture. The camel is the largest animal in the everyday experience of the sūrah's first audience; the cable is the largest cord in the experience of a port. The proverb's power lies in being stated as a *condition*: they will not enter *until* this happens — and the "until" is the grammatical form of an impossibility dressed as a waiting.

The Prophet ﷺ used the same register for the destination this verse denies, describing the entry of the blessed in terms no camel could satisfy: *“The first group to enter Paradise will be like the full moon”* (al-Bukhārī 3245, from Abū Hurayrah; Muslim concurs in meaning). The tradition of commentary drew the specific application from the verse itself: *those who deny Our signs and treat them with disdain*. It is not the possession of wealth that bars the gate — the sūrah's own verses on lawful enjoyment (7:31–32) forbid that reading — but the swelling self that makes a person's entry physically impossible. Al-Rāzī puts it in a sentence worth keeping: pride is a size, and the gate does not change.

**What the Verse Says About Divine Exclusion**

Read together with the garden imagery of the whole Book, the verse makes a claim that many modern readers find startling: heaven has a policy. The Qur'an is not vague about who is inside. It names the qualities that enter — reverence and reform in verse 35, faith and right action throughout — and it names the qualities that do not. The early sūrahs of the Makkan period repeat the point in dozens of images, from the balance of al-Qāriʿah (101:6–9) to the books handed right and left (69:19–25).

And yet the Book is careful not to make the gate a function of a single category of person. The verse's own clause requires two acts — denial *and* disdain — and the two together describe a settled character rather than a momentary lapse. The distinction has practical consequences, and the tradition drew them. It is the difference between the person who sins and the person who has decided; between the soul that falls and the soul that has taken up a position against the signs. The sūrah's earlier verses about repentance (7:23) and divine acceptance (2:37) remain available to everyone whose disdain has not become a permanent structure. What the camel-and-needle proverb states is what happens when it has.

**The Final Clause**

*Wa-kadhālika najzī al-mujrimīn* — and thus do We recompense the criminals. The word *mujrim* is from *j-r-m*, the root of cutting off and of earning guilt; the Qur'an uses it for the one whose conduct has severed him. The phrase "thus do We recompense" is the sūrah's returning formula of proportion: the punishment is not arbitrary, it is the fitting end of what the person built.

The verse's placement closes the unit that began in verse 32 and gives it its final shape. The sūrah asked who had forbidden God's adornment, and answered by naming what is truly forbidden (32–33); it announced a term for every community (34); it promised security to the reverent and reformer (35); it described the disdainful (36); it followed them to the moment of death (37); and it has now followed them past the gates. In between, it left one door conspicuously open, and the next two verses will show the faces of the people walking through it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:41

> **Hell shall be their resting place, with coverings above them. And thus do We recompense the wrongdoers!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bed Beneath and the Cover Above**

*Lahum min jahannama mihādun wa-min fawqihim ghawāsh* — for them there is a bed of Hell, and above them coverings. The clause arranges the punishment along the axis of the human body, below and above, and in doing so it describes not an event but an environment. Nobody in this verse is being thrown anywhere. The condemned are *installed*: a *mihād* set beneath, *ghawāsh* laid over.

The choice of words is the sūrah's usual economy. *Mihād* is the Qur'an's term for a bed prepared and spread — the cradle of an infant, the resting place made ready by someone who cares about the sleeper. Sūrat al-Nabaʾ uses it for the earth itself: *“Have We not smoothed out the earth like a bed?”* (78:6), and Sūrat al-Baqarah uses its sibling *firāsh* for the same idea: *“He Who made the earth a resting place for you”* (2:22). *Ghawāsh*, plural of *ghāshiyah*, is a covering — the counterpane, the thing drawn over a person asleep. The Book has taken the vocabulary of the most ordinary comfort a human being knows and filled it with fire.

**The Same Architecture, Elsewhere in the Book**

The image recurs because the Qur'an wants it to. Sūrat al-Zumar states it to the believers as a threat aimed at their own complacency: *“They will have layers of fire above and below them. That is what Allah warns His servants with. So fear Me, O My servants!”* (39:16). Sūrat al-Kahf encloses the wrongdoer in a *muḥīṭ*, a thing that surrounds and from which there is no direction of escape (18:29). Sūrat Ibrāhīm clothes the rebels in tar and covers their faces with flame (14:50), and Sūrat al-Ḥajj cuts garments of fire for the deniers (22:19). Sūrat al-Raʿd's contrast uses the same logic in the other direction: the good word is a tree whose shade and fruit are available to all (14:24–25).

Read next to the paradise verses of the same sūrah — rivers, garments, thrones (7:43; 18:31) — the point becomes plain. The afterlife in the Qur'an is described as a *place* with furniture, because the human beings it addresses are embodied creatures who understand the moral state of a life through the conditions of a home. The pride of verse 36 chose a posture; this verse gives the posture a room.

**"And Thus Do We Recompense the Wrongdoers"**

*Wa-kadhālika najzī al-ẓālimīn* — and this is how We reward the unjust. The phrase closes the verse on a note of exactness, and the vocabulary carries it. *Jazāʾ*, requital, is the Qur'an's term of payment in kind: the wage returned for work done. *“Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7–8). *“Whoever brings a good deed will have ten times as much; and whoever brings an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like, and they will not be wronged”* (6:160). The formula *and thus do We recompense* appears throughout the sūrahs of warning, and in every appearance it forecloses one specific objection — that the punishment of God is arbitrary, or excessive, or born of wounded pride in the way human punishment so often is.

The sūrah has already given the court where the accounting is done: *“The weighing on that Day will be just”* (7:8). This verse names the currency. What is repaid is what was spent, and the recompense is not a change of direction in God's disposition but the arrival of a bill already written.

**Who the Wrongdoers Are Here**

One word in the closing formula deserves attention: the condemned are called *ẓālimīn*, and this sūrah has been unusually careful about that root. It is the word Adam and his wife use against themselves when they turn back: *“Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves”* (7:23). It is the word the sūrah applies to the fabrication of lies about God (7:37), to those who disbelieve in His signs (7:40), and to the arrogant who despise them (7:36). There is no other vocabulary in the chapter for what went wrong, and the reason is that the chapter is not describing a category of moral opinion but a boundary crossed. *Ẓulm* in the Qur'an is the placing of a thing outside the station God gave it: the sign treated as disposable, the creature placed where the Creator belongs, the capacity of a soul spent against its own interest.

The wrongdoer of this verse is therefore not the person who lost a battle of ideas. He is the one who moved what God had set. The bed of verse 41 is what happens when a man spends a lifetime putting himself in the place of what is above him: he is finally laid down in it.

**A Furnishing That Closes Every Door**

There is a further detail that only becomes visible when the verse is read beside verse 40. There, the gates of heaven *do not open*. Here, the condemned are covered from above and bedded from below. Between the two verses, every aperture of the human body's world has been sealed: no door above, no exit below, and no direction of movement that is not fire.

That is the sūrah's terrifying consistency. It began these verses with a question — who is more wrong than the one who lies about God? — and has answered it with an itinerary whose every stage was described, centuries before, in the architecture of an ordinary room. The men being described would have gone to sleep that night on their own beds, under their own covers, and would have considered the verse a matter of rhetoric. The verse says that from now until then, God has changed one thing only: the material.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:42

> **As for those who believe and perform righteous deeds — We task no soul beyond its capacity — it is they who are the inhabitants of the Garden; they shall abide therein.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Turns the Page**

Verse 41 ended with a formula: *and thus do We recompense the wrongdoers* (7:41). Verse 42 opens with the same construction and the opposite content: *wa-lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — and those who believed and did righteous deeds. The sūrah does this repeatedly, and the repetition is the point: the same court, the same scale, the same verb of requital, and the difference is what a person brought to it.

The closing clause returns the vocabulary of verse 36 to its rightful owners: where the arrogant were *aṣḥāb al-nār*, the inhabitants of the Fire, these are *aṣḥāb al-jannah*, the inhabitants of the Garden, and the abiding is stated in the identical word — *khālidūn*, there forever. In a sūrah that has arranged its arguments in pairs since the first page — the guided and the misguided (7:30), the reverent and the disdainful (7:35–36), the bed of fire and the rivers of the garden — this symmetry is not ornament. It tells the reader that the two destinies are being described on a single map, and that the road to either is built one deed at a time.

**"Those Who Believed and Did Righteous Deeds"**

The pair *āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* is the Qur'an's most frequent description of the saved, occurring more than fifty times. Its two halves are constructed so that neither can stand alone. *Īmān* is trust — the interior act by which a person accepts what God has said — and *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* is the outward act that trust produces. The Book does not separate them anywhere: not grace without the act, and not act without the trust. The pairing is itself a commentary on the sūrah's quarrel with the Makkan leadership, who were not short of deeds and were short of nothing but trust, and on the people of the sabbath of 7:163 whom the sūrah will shortly describe — a community that possessed the form of religion and lost its substance.

The adjective *ṣāliḥ* comes from the root *ṣ-l-ḥ*, which in Arabic is the vocabulary of repair — the opposite of *fasād*, corruption. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf has already used the contrast in the call of its prophets: the messenger of this sūrah comes *commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong* (7:157), and verse 35 described the saved as those who *reform* themselves. The Qur'an's concept of good work is thus not merely legal compliance but correction: the setting right of what has been bent, beginning with oneself.

**The Parenthesis That Answers Two Questions**

Dropped into the middle of the promise is a clause that does not appear to belong there: *lā nukallifu nafsan illā wusʿahā* — We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity. It is a complete principle of obligation standing between the conditions and the reward, and its placement is deliberate.

The Qur'an states it three times in this form and several more in substance: *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286), *“We never require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (23:62), *“Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it”* (65:7), and it is embedded in the practical legislation of the Book — in the concession for the traveller who cannot fast (2:184–185), in the prohibitions against burdening the nursing mother or the father beyond means (2:233), in the declaration, *“He has laid upon you no hardship in the religion”* (22:78). The tradition drew from it the whole discipline of *taklīf*, the assignment of obligation according to ability, and the jurists built on it the principle that no duty is imposed where it cannot be performed.

But the clause answers two readers, not one. The soul tempted to despair at the standard reads that faith and right action are required, measures the height of the demand, and stops. The clause tells that soul that the demand was measured before it was made. And the soul tempted to make a system of excuses reads the same clause as permission, and is answered by the exact wording — not, "we do not require any soul", but *we do not require any soul beyond its capacity*. Grace does not abolish the order; it sets its size.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the doctrine its practical shape in a hadith the sūrah's readers have always paired with this verse: *“The deeds of any one of you will not save him” — they said, “Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?” — he said, “Not even me, unless Allah envelops me in His mercy. So be moderate, do what you can, and give glad tidings”* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah). Neither the despair nor the presumption survives that sentence.

**The People of the Garden**

The verse then states the destination with a possessive that the sūrah has already used twice: *ulāʾika aṣḥāb al-jannah*. *Aṣḥāb* is the vocabulary of belonging and company — the owner of a house, the companion of a road. The Qur'an does not say the righteous will be admitted to the Garden, though it says that too elsewhere (16:32; 39:73); here it says they *are its people*, and the clause *hum fīhā khālidūn* settles the tenure.

There is a pastoral reason the sūrah repeats the abiding so insistently in this short stretch. The community receiving these verses knew loss as a daily condition: the exile of the early Muslims from Makkah, the death of children, the confiscation of property, the long patience of people who had no reason to expect that anything in their circumstances would hold. Against that experience, the Book places a possession that cannot be taken, in a place whose gates — unlike those of verse 40 — are open.

The arrangement of the three verses is worth seeing whole. Verse 41 furnishes the Fire. Verse 42 furnishes the Garden with the simplest inventory the Qur'an possesses: belief, action, and a measure that fits the soul. Verse 43 will give the Garden its voice. Between the two descriptions, the sūrah leaves only one thing for the reader to decide, and it is not hidden in either of them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:43

> **And We shall remove whatever rancor lies within their breasts. Rivers shall run below them. And they will say, “Praise be to God, Who guided us unto this. We would not have been rightly guided, had God not guided us. The messengers of our Lord certainly brought the truth.” And a call will be made unto them, “This is the Garden. You have inherited it for that which you used to do.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Will Remove Whatever Bitterness They Had in Their Hearts"**

*Wa-nazaʿnā mā fī ṣudūrihim min ghill* — and We shall strip out whatever rancour was in their hearts. The verb *nazaʿa* is the verb of drawing a thing out that has sunk in: a nail from wood, a spear from a wound, a root from soil. *Ghill* is the particular vice it removes — not anger of the moment but the sediment of old grievance, the grudge that has been carried so long it has become part of a person's furniture.

The promise is repeated almost word for word in Sūrat al-Ḥijr, and the following clause there explains the purpose: *“We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts. In a friendly manner, they will be on thrones, facing one another”* (15:47). Facing one another. The Qur'an's Garden is a place where assembly is possible without the history that makes assembly dangerous, and the Book has prepared the reader for why this matters. The sūrah's own account of Ṣāliḥ's people (7:75–76) was a scene of division: the arrogant chiefs on one side, the lowly believers on the other, and a social order in which one group's rank was the other group's humiliation. The Muslim community receiving these verses had come out of a tribal world in which a man's grievance against a clan could last three generations.

What the verse promises is not amnesia but surgery. The memory stays; the poison is extracted. And the phrase is only one of several in which the Book describes the moral hygiene of the Garden: purified spouses (2:25), speech in which no idle talk or falsehood is heard (56:25), a place whose only sound is peace (19:62). The world to come is drawn not as a bigger version of this world's societies but as one whose members have had the thing removed that made society a contest.

**The Rivers Beneath Their Feet**

*Wa-tajrī min taḥtihim al-anhār.* The clause is almost a signature of the Qur'an — Gardens under which rivers flow — and it occurs in this form dozens of times (2:25; 3:198; 10:9; 18:31). For an audience whose wealth was measured in wells, whose travel was measured in watering places, and whose scripture was being revealed in a valley without a river, water in motion beneath one's dwelling is the most eloquent available image of settled security. The sūrah itself has already used water as its emblem of God's care in this world, in the rain that brings forth fruit (7:57), and it will describe the springs struck from rock for a whole people (7:160).

Sūrat Muḥammad gives the metaphor its full development: *“the description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey”* (47:15). Four rivers, four goods, none of them stagnant. The garden of this verse is not a possession to be guarded but an orchard that is irrigated — that is, a place that keeps producing without anyone's labour. The servants of the tradition read the clause that way and it shaped the classical descriptions of Paradise as a place of continuous provision rather than stored treasure.

**The First Words of the Saved**

The verse now records what its inhabitants say, and the first sound of the Garden is gratitude: *al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī hadānā li-hādhā* — praise belongs to Allah, who guided us to this. The opening of the Book's first sūrah is placed in the mouths of the saved in the last sūrah's communities; the doxology of the servant at the beginning of the Qur'an becomes the language of the reward at the end.

The confession continues with a sentence that carries the weight of the chapter's whole argument about guidance: *wa-mā kunnā li-nahtadiya lawlā an hadānā Allāh* — and we would never have been guided had Allah not guided us. The construction is one of total dependence: *lawlā* — were it not for — introduces a counterfactual the speaker knows to be true. These are the people who did the deeds of verse 42, and their first act in the Garden is to deny that the deeds were self-generated. The sūrah has already stated the doctrine from the other side: *“He has guided some, while others are destined to stray”* (7:30). Here the guided state the same fact from inside their own biography.

Then comes the testimony that closes the sūrah's quarrel with its own first audience: *laqad jāʾat rusul rabbinā bi-l-ḥaqq* — the messengers of our Lord did indeed come with the truth. The people who mocked those messengers in the world are here the ones who confirm them. It is worth noticing the Book's habit of putting the final confirmation of revelation in the mouth of the saved rather than the angel: the truth of the message is verified by the result of following it.

**"It Will Be Announced: This Is the Garden You Have Inherited"**

*Wa-nūdū an tilkumu al-jannatu ūrithtumūhā bimā kuntum taʿmalūn* — and it will be announced: this is the Garden, made your inheritance for what you used to do. Two grammatical details carry the theology.

The first is the passive *nūdū*: it is announced, and the announcer is unnamed. The sūrah used the same device four verses later, where a nameless caller pronounces condemnation on the wrongdoers (7:44). The message is what matters; the voice belongs to the place.

The second is the verb *ūrithtumūhā*, you have been made its heirs. The Book could have said you have been paid it, or awarded it, or admitted to it. It says inherited — the term of a transfer that no one earns in the ordinary sense. The clause continues *bimā kuntum taʿmalūn*, for what you used to do, and both halves are necessary. The sūrah's parenthetical in verse 42 insisted that no soul is burdened beyond capacity; this clause insists that the Garden is not the wage a soul's capacity could ever produce. Sūrat al-Zukhruf speaks of the same place in the same register: *“That is the Paradise which you will be awarded for what you used to do”* (43:72), and Sūrat Āl ʿImrān calls the whole arrangement *an accommodation from Allah* (3:198). The Prophet ﷺ stated the balance in one sentence: the deeds do not entitle anyone, and the deeds are the road. What the verse establishes is that the road and the destination have the same owner.

**Why the Verse Is Sung**

The sūrah has moved from court to gate to settlement, and now it records music. It is a deliberate choice of the Book of the Heights: the description of Paradise is not concluded with an inventory of its wealth but with a confession of its cause. The people of the Garden have no boast about their own performance, no lineage to recite, no list of merits; they have a Lord who guided them, a truth they can now confirm from the inside, and an inheritance they did nothing to earn and everything to reach.

The reader of the sūrah is meant to notice what is missing from the sentence: no one in the Garden says "I". The pronouns are all "we", and every "we" is the object of a divine verb. That is the last word of the sūrah's passage on the two destinies, and it is the exact opposite of the single word the sūrah's narrative placed at the beginning — the one spoken by a creature who refused, and said nothing else.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:44

> **The inhabitants of the Garden will call out to the inhabitants of the Fire, “We have found that which our Lord promised us to be true. Have you found that which your Lord promised to be true?” They will respond, “Yes.” Thereupon a herald shall proclaim in their midst, “The curse of God be upon the wrongdoers!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call Across the Divide**

*Wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-jannati aṣḥāba al-nār* — and the inhabitants of the Garden will call out to the inhabitants of the Fire. The sūrah's next six verses are built on the verb *nādā*, to call from a distance: here, and again at 7:48, and again at 7:50 where the residents of the Fire cry out to the residents of the Garden. The Book is describing a landscape in which the two populations are within sight and voice of one another, and in which nothing can be exchanged between them but words.

That is a deliberate design, and the sūrah has prepared the reader for it. In this world, the two groups are intermingled and indistinguishable to the outer eye; the sūrah's earlier narratives showed them arguing across a social distance that could still be crossed — the proud chiefs and the lowly believers of Ṣāliḥ's town (7:75–76). After death, the crossing is over, and all that remains of the relationship is the call. The voice carries. Nothing else does. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf will make that plain three verses later, when the condemned beg for a drop of water and receive a doctrinal answer instead (7:50).

**The Question That Is the Punishment**

*Innā wajadnā mā waʿadanā rabbunā ḥaqqan* — we have found what our Lord promised us to be true — *fa-hal wajadtum mā waʿada rabbukum ḥaqqan* — so have you found what your Lord promised you to be true? *Qālū naʿam* — they said, yes.

The interrogative is the most economical instrument of punishment in the entire Book. The blessed do not gloat, and the Book does not put a taunt in their mouths; they ask a question whose answer is already experienced by everyone present. The verb *wajada*, to find, is a verb of discovery in the concrete world — the verb of a man who finds water in a well or a coin in the road. It states that revelation turned out to be *there*, like a thing you can put your hand on. The sentence is thus the last form of the sūrah's central argument, made by two communities at once and answered with a single word from the condemned.

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf records the same admission in another key seven verses later: the day the warning is fulfilled, those who ignored it will say, *“The messengers of our Lord certainly came with the truth”* (7:53). Sūrat al-Anʿām closes the door on its usefulness: *“On the Day your Lord's signs arrive, belief will not benefit anyone who had not believed before or earned some good through their faith”* (6:158). The exchange of verse 44 is the moment when the whole vocabulary of the Book's warnings becomes a physical fact, and, crucially, it is the blessed who pose the question and the damned who supply the confession.

**The Herald Between Them**

*Fa-adhdhana muʾadhdhinun baynahum* — and a herald called out between them. The word is the ancestor of *adhān*, the call to prayer: a public announcement made in a raised voice for a gathered crowd. In this world the phrase *the call between them* has a mundane history — the muezzin of Madinah calling across the rows — and the sūrah borrows the image for the last announcement that will ever be made in that landscape.

The content of the herald's cry is a sentence from the vocabulary of exclusion: *laʿnatu Allāh ʿalā al-ẓālimīn* — the curse of Allah is upon the unjust. *Laʿna* in the Qur'an is not profanity but the opposite of mercy: the expulsion of a creature from the radius within which God's special favour operates (cf. 2:161; 4:52). It is the same fate the sūrah announced for Iblīs in the first narrative of the chapter, when he was driven out for his arrogance (7:13), and the reader is expected to hear the echo. The announcement is made *between* the two populations, which is to say in the hearing of both: the blessed are told what they were saved from, and the condemned what they chose.

**The Hope That Lives Inside the Verdict**

The curse of verse 44 is stated in the absolute, and the tradition of the Prophet ﷺ read it within a larger mercy that the Book states in a hundred places. The Messenger of God described what will happen after the reckoning in a report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī: when the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire have entered their homes, God will say, *“Take out whoever has faith equal to a mustard seed in his heart”* — and they will come out blackened like coal, and they will be cast into the river of al-Ḥayyāt, and they will sprout green as a seed does on the bank of a torrent (al-Bukhārī 6560). The smoke-blackened figures in that hadith are people whose heart contained something the fire could not consume.

The verse of the Heights and that hadith are not in tension; they are describing different levels of the same verdict. The curse falls on the *ẓālimīn* as those who are defined by the injustice they settled in — the fixed posture, the chosen identity, the disdain of verse 36. The mustard seed is what remains of a creature who was better than the label. The sūrah's own reader is asked to check which of the two sentences describes the direction he is walking, and the question of verse 44 is the one he will be asked by voices that were once his neighbours.

**Why the Wrongdoers Are Defined Now**

Before the hasher's voice fades, the very next verse turns to define the word *ẓālimīn* — those who have just been cursed. It is the sūrah's most characteristic habit, and one of its most humane: whenever it pronounces a sentence, it immediately tells the reader what the sentence is for, so that the reader may measure himself instead of guessing. Verse 36 named disdain; verse 40 named denial; verse 41 named the crossing of a boundary. Verse 45 will name an act that can be seen in a human life today, and the reader who finds it there has been given the warning early enough to act on it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:45

> **Those who turn from the way of God and seek to make it crooked, disbelieving in the Hereafter.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Catalogue, Not an Insult**

The herald of verse 44 has just pronounced the curse of God upon the unjust. Verse 45 tells the reader whom the word describes: *alladhīna yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāh* — those who turn others away from the path of God — *wa-yabghūnahā ʿiwajan* — and seek to make it crooked — *wa-hum bi-l-ākhirati kāfirūn* — and who deny the Hereafter.

The sentence is a three-part diagnosis, and the order of the parts is the order of a process. First a person is turned away from a road. Then the road itself is misrepresented — the seeker is told that it bends this way and does not reach where it claims to reach. Then, at the root, the one who does the misrepresenting has already decided that the destination does not exist. The Qur'an repeats the trio almost verbatim in four other places, and the repetition establishes it as a definition rather than a description of a particular historical group: *“they hinder others from Allah's path, striving to make it appear crooked, and disbelieve in the Hereafter”* (11:19); *“Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah — striving to make it appear crooked, while you are witnesses to its truth?”* (3:99); *“They favour the life of this world over the Hereafter and hinder others from the Way of Allah, striving to make it appear crooked”* (14:3; cf. 16:88, where added punishment is promised because of the corruption they spread).

**The Verb That Presupposes a Traveller**

*Ṣadda ʿan* is one of the Qur'an's most sociable verbs: it cannot be used without a second person. You cannot *block* in the abstract; the grammatical form of the sin requires somebody who was walking and was turned back. This is the crime that the sūrah's Makkan leadership is, in the reading of the commentators, most directly charged with: barring the way to the Sacred Mosque, pressuring the new believers of the town, forbidding access to the recitation of the Prophet ﷺ. The Book states that charge at length elsewhere: *“they hinder pilgrims from the Sacred Mosque”* (8:34); *“they hindered you from the Sacred Mosque”* (48:25); *“hindering others from the way of Allah and denying Him, and from the Sacred Mosque”* (2:217). The Makkan leadership's real offense, on this reading, was not merely unbelief but blockade.

And the verse's principle reaches further than Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ stated the two directions of the traffic in a hadith from Abū Hurayrah: *“Whoever calls others to guidance will have a reward equal to that of those who follow, without anything being diminished from their reward”* — and its mirror, the one who calls to misguidance, carries the load of those who follow (Muslim 2674). The verse of the Heights supplies the reason: the path is not a private opinion. It is a public road, and there is a crime in standing on it and telling travelers it goes nowhere.

**"Seeking to Make It Crooked"**

The Arabic of the next clause is a small masterpiece of precision. *Yabghūnahā ʿiwajan*: *baghā*, to seek or to covet, and *ʿiwaj*, crookedness. The lexicographers distinguish *ʿiwaj*, which Arabic uses for the crookedness of things — a road, a wall, a staff — from *ʿawaj*, which Arabic uses for the crookedness of conduct or judgment. The Qur'an chooses the first word, and the choice sharpens the charge. The accusation is not that these people behaved wickedly; it is that they *bent the road*. The path of God is described by the Book's own opening prayer as *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, the straight way (1:6). A straight line can only be opposed in two ways: by denying that it exists, or by representing it as something other than straight. The verse names the second, which is always the more sophisticated attack, and the one that requires a public relations campaign.

Sūrat Āl ʿImrān adds the detail that makes this clause especially severe: the People of the Book who do this do it *while you are witnesses to its truth* (3:99). The one who bends the road bends it in the full knowledge of where it led.

**The Engine Underneath**

The last clause supplies the motive: *wa-hum bi-l-ākhirati kāfirūn*. The denial of the Hereafter is not tacked onto the verse as an unrelated item of unbelief; it is offered as the reason the rest of the sentence is possible. This is one of the Qur'an's most consistent psychological claims: a person who is certain that no account will be demanded has no motive to keep the road clear for others and no reason to be careful about what he tells them. The Book says as much in its portraits of the heedless: *“But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world, and neglect the Hereafter”* (75:20–21), and it puts the same confession about the Fire in the mouth of the deniers themselves (74:42–46).

The verse therefore closes the circle opened at 7:8, where the scale of the Day was described as just. The people who are cursed in verse 44 are, at bottom, people who removed the scale from their world. They then removed the road, and then the travelers.

**The Reader's Place in the Sentence**

There remains one application of the verse that any honest reader has to make before he makes any other. It is not only tyrants who are addressed by *yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāh*. A parent, a teacher, a friend, an elder, a movement, a writer — anyone with a voice and a following who makes the religion look like something it is not, or who makes the straight road seem impassable, stands inside the category the verse has drawn.

The sūrah will spend its next verses on the people who stand on the heights and on the ones who cry out from below them. Before the reader arrives at that scene, the definition of verse 45 sits in his way as the sūrah's own mirror. It is not difficult to walk a crooked road; the verse says so. What it takes is somebody to make it crooked first — and the Book, characteristically, offers no third option between calling and blocking.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:46

> **And there will be a veil between them. And upon the Heights are men who know all by their marks. They will call out to the inhabitants of the Garden, “Peace be upon you!” They will not have entered it, though they hope.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Barrier**

*Wa-baynahumā ḥijāb* — and between them a barrier. The word *ḥijāb* is the Qur'an's term for a screen that divides while allowing perception through or across it: the partition that shielded Maryam from her people (19:17), the barrier God placed before the disbelievers so that they cannot grasp the revelation while it is being recited (17:45), the veil that belongs to God's own transcendence (42:51). Here it is a physical feature of the Hereafter, and the Book describes it in another sūrah with a detail this one assumes: *“On that Day a wall will be placed between them, with a gate. Inside it will be mercy, and outside of it, from the direction of the Fire, will be punishment”* (57:13).

The geography is important and easy to skim past. The Qur'an's Hereafter is not a moral abstraction; it has structure. There is a Garden, there is a Fire, and between them there is a construction. The people of this verse stand on that construction, and everything the verse says about them follows from the fact that they are *in between* — neither inside nor excluded, and able to see both directions.

**The Heights**

The Arabic word for what they stand on gives the sūrah its name: *al-aʿrāf*, the plural of *ʿurf*, the elevated part of a thing — the crest of a wall, the ridge of a dune, the high ground from which a whole valley can be seen. Just as the Book's sky has gates, its separation has high places, and the exegetes took the word literally: a wall with a top, and people on top of it.

Who they are has been a subject of discussion since the earliest generations. Al-Ṭabarī records a range of views and reports the opinion attributed to more than one of the Companions, including the reading associated with Ḥudhayfah ibn al-Yamān, that the people of the Heights are those whose scales balance — whose good deeds and bad deeds are exactly equal, so that neither the Garden nor the Fire claims them, and they wait on the ridge while the reckoning runs its course. Other reports identify them as angels watching over the crowd, or as the most honoured of the believers who have been granted a view first. The verse itself declines to name them, and this reticence is itself instructive: it fixes the reader's attention on what they are doing rather than whose identity documents they carry.

That is the sūrah's constant method. Its long narratives do not answer the question "which historical group produced this detail?" until the lesson has landed.

**"They Will Recognize Them All by Their Marks"**

*Yaʿrifūna kullan bi-sīmāhum* — they recognize each group by their marks. The word *sīmā* is the brand a man puts on his cattle, the characteristic by which a tribe is identified in the dark. The Qur'an uses it three times for the Hereafter and the world, and in each place it makes the same claim: the moral quality of a person becomes visible. *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by their forelocks and feet”* (55:41). In this world the mark is often invisible — the Book tells the Prophet that he will know the hypocrites *by the tone of their speech* (47:30), and it tells him that the impoverished believers who give in secret are known only *by their mark* to those who look carefully (2:273). On the Day, the recognition becomes the universal reading of a face.

There is a further resonance to the word choice. The place is *al-aʿrāf*; the people there *yaʿrifūn*, they know. The root is the same: *ʿ-r-f*. The ones who possessed knowledge in this world — of God's signs, of the difference between the mark of the faithful and the mark of the proud — are given the station of recognition. The Book's doctrine that knowledge is a trust now becomes a vantage point.

**"Peace Be Upon You"**

*Wa-nādaw aṣḥāba al-jannati an salāmun ʿalaykum.* The verb of the previous verse returns: they call out, and the content of the call is the greeting of Paradise itself. *Salām* is the first word the Book's own liturgy places in the mouths of the redeemed — *“their prayer therein will be: Glory be to You, O Allah, and their greeting: Peace”* (10:10) — and the greeting the angels will give the blessed at the gates: *“Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do”* (16:32; 39:73).

What makes this a moment of high composition is that the greeting is offered by people who have not yet entered. They stand on the wall, they see the gardens, they salute their inhabitants, and their own case is still pending. The Prophet ﷺ spoke of the community's faces being bright with the marks of the worship they performed in the world — *“my Ummah will come on the Day of Judgment with white marks on their faces, hands, and feet from the traces of ablution”* (al-Bukhārī 136; Muslim 246) — and the verse of the Heights shows a group identified by marks and greeting those who have arrived.

**"And They Have Not Entered It, Yet They Hope"**

The verse's last clause is the hinge of the whole scene: *lā yadkhulūnahā wa-hum yaṭmaʿūn* — they have not entered it, but they are eager in hope. The Arabic *ṭamaʿa* denotes desire tinged with confidence, and the Qur'an uses it for the hope a believer has of forgiveness: Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ places in Ibrāhīm's prayer the words, *“and Who I hope will forgive my sin on the Day of Judgment”* (26:82).

Everything in the Book's vocabulary of salvation is present in that clause. Faith is not certainty of outcome; it is hope anchored in the mercy of God and worked out in conduct. A person standing on the wall between the Garden and the Fire, having seen the faces of both populations and registered their marks, does not recite doctrine. He hopes. The sūrah leaves its readers in exactly that posture — which is to say, in the place where the Qur'an normally leaves a human being: certain of what he has done, uncertain of what he has not yet been forgiven, and asking.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:47

> **And when their eyes turn toward the inhabitants of the Fire, they will say, “Our Lord! Place us not among the wrongdoing people!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn of the Eye**

*Wa-idhā ṣurifat abṣāruhum tilqāʾa aṣḥābi al-nār* — and when their eyes are turned toward the inhabitants of the Fire. The syntax deserves the attention it takes. Their gaze is not described as wandering or as choosing; the verb is passive, *ṣurifat*, they are turned. Something or someone directs the sight of the people on the Heights when the moment comes, and the Qur'an leaves the agent unnamed, as it leaves the announcer of 7:44 unnamed and the speaker of the final command of 7:49 unnamed. The Book's Hereafter is full of arrangements made by an authority that never identifies itself, and the effect is the sense of a reality governed from behind the scenes.

The image itself is one of the Book's most affecting small scenes. These are people who have just saluted the inhabitants of Paradise, who stand close enough to see the gardens and their residents. Then their eyes are turned the other way, to the other population, and the greeting gives way to a prayer.

**The Prayer of the Almost-Saved**

*Rabbanā lā tajʿalnā maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn* — Our Lord, do not place us among the wrongdoing people. The petition is not for a rank and not for a gift. It is a request to be kept out of a *company*. *Lā tajʿalnā maʿa* — do not make us *with* — is the vocabulary of placement, and the Book uses it consistently in the mouth of those who understand that the company is the consequence.

The Qur'an's most exact rendering of the sentiment appears in the prayer of the family of ʿImrān, which the sūrah of Āl ʿImrān reports in full: *“Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive us our sins and protect us from the punishment of the Fire”* (3:193), preceded by the plea *“Our Lord! Truly, those You commit to the Fire will be disgraced”* (3:192). Sūrat al-Furqān attributes to the servants of the Merciful the habit of praying, *“Our Lord, avert from us the torment of Hell; indeed its torment is ever an inseparable affliction”* (25:65), and Sūrat al-Muʾminūn teaches the same petition in the present life: *“My Lord! I seek refuge in You from the temptations of the devils, and I seek refuge in You, my Lord, that they even come near me”* (23:97–98).

Two features of these prayers recur wherever the Book records them. The first is that they address God as *rabbanā*, "our Lord" — the possessive of devotion, the invocation of the servant who has a claim of kinship with his Maker through dependence. The second is that they ask for the avoidance of a destination rather than for the enjoyment of a reward.

**Why the Fear Is of Company, Not of Pain**

Modern readers often read the Hereafter as a payments system: quantities of pain, quantities of pleasure, each matched to a ledger. The sūrah's imagery has been pointing somewhere else, and this verse states it plainly. The thing the people of the Heights fear is not fire. It is being *with* the wrongdoers — of finding themselves in the company of the people whose lives they watched in the world.

That is the sūrah's consistent teaching about the two destinies. Verse 38 described the communities entering the fire and cursing one another; verse 39 had the first saying to the last, *you have no superiority over us*; verse 40 closed the gates; verse 44 recorded the accusation; verse 45 defined the accused. Every one of those scenes is about association — who is gathered with whom, and what follows from it. Sūrat al-Zukhruf states the principle for the whole Day: *“Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous”* (43:67). Sūrat al-Furqān records the regret of the one who took a bad companion: *“I wish I had never taken so-and-so as a friend!”* (25:28). And Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt tells the story of a man in the Garden who looks down and sees his old comrade in the Fire (37:51–57).

The Book is not squeamish about the point. What a person tolerates in his friendships, his reading, his company, and his teachers is not a detail of his morals but the shape of his end, and the Hereafter assembles people accordingly. The people on the Heights pray to be excluded from the assembly, and the prayer is the most intelligent one ever uttered in the Book's scenes.

**The Sūrah's Teaching for the Present**

There is a reason the Qur'an records these prayers in the afterlife rather than only in the world. The reader is being taught the vocabulary of the saved before he needs it. The Prophet ﷺ taught his community to ask for refuge in nearly identical terms, and the tradition carried the practice into the daily liturgies: the supplication to be spared the Fire appears in the night prayer, in the prayer for the mosque, and in the brief duas the Prophet recommended to his Companions. The line the sūrah draws is direct: whoever learns the prayer of the Heights now will not need to learn it in the hour when it must be answered immediately.

And the verse's fear has a diagnostic value for anyone who prays it honestly. It sits uncomfortably with a life built around the approval of crowds, the company of the influential, the borrowed authority of fashionable opinion. The prayer asks God to keep the speaker out of *that* company, and the man who seriously prays it will find the same request shaping his choice of whom he sits with, listens to, and answers to.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:48

> **And the inhabitants of the Heights will call out to men whom they know by their marks, “Your accumulating has not availed you, nor has your waxing arrogant.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call to Particular Men**

*Wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-aʿrāfi rijālan* — and the people of the Heights called out to certain men. The verb of the previous two verses returns for the third time: the Hereafter of this sūrah is a scene of speaking across distances. What is new is the object of the address. The blessed were addressed as a community (*aṣḥāba al-jannati*), the cursed as a community (*aṣḥāba al-nār*), but here the call goes out to *rijāl* — men, individuals, identifiable persons whose faces the callers know. The Qur'an uses the plural *rijāl* for grown men in their public capacity, and its appearance here says something the larger scene would have blurred: the leaders are not swallowed by the crowd they led. They are each still themselves, on show.

*Alladhīna yaʿrifūnahum bi-sīmāhum* — whom they recognize by their marks. The third occurrence of this phrase in three verses takes on a sharper edge now, because the marks in question belong to tyrants whose faces the world knew from coins and speeches. Sūrat al-Raḥmān describes the same reversal: *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by their forelocks and feet”* (55:41). The Book's claim is not that God needs identifying marks — it is that character, which in this world is hidden under clothing, reputation, and public relations, will end up written on the body.

**"Your Numbers Have Availed You Nothing"**

*Mā aghnā ʿankum jamʿukum* — your multitude has not enriched you in anything. *Aghnā* is the verb of sufficiency: to make someone need nothing more. *Jamʿ* is the mass — the assembled following, the party, the coalition, the mob, the congregation of clients and dependents, the crowd a man can produce when he wants to demonstrate that he matters. The sentence declares that the entire apparatus has been weighed and found to have no purchasing power.

This is one of the Book's most persistent themes and one of its least welcome. *“If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's way”* (6:116). *“Most of them do not believe in Allah without associating others with Him in worship”* (12:106). *“Competition for more gains diverts you until you end up in your graves”* (102:1–2). *“We are far superior in wealth and children, and we will never be punished”* — this is the speech of the affluent of Sabaʾ (34:35), and the answer comes in the next verses: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us”* (34:37). Sūrat al-Baqarah records an army that fled its homes in the thousands for fear of death and was told to die (2:243). The Qur'an mentions the size of the crowds that followed the truth and the size of the crowds that opposed it, and it refuses, in either case, to treat the number as an argument.

The wording *your multitude* also carries a sting that a Makkan audience would have felt precisely. The chiefs of the town had dependents, allies, clients, slaves, and the moral weight of clan solidarity behind them; their confidence in the outcome of the coming judgment was indistinguishable from their confidence in the size of their following. Verse 48 tells them the following has been left on the other side of the fire.

**"Nor What You Used to Be Arrogant About"**

*Wa-mā kuntum tastakbirūn* — and neither has the arrogance you used to practise. The phrase returns to the sūrah's single diagnosis. Verse 36 described those who *denied Our signs and treated them with disdain*; verse 40 closed the gates of heaven against the same people; verse 48 now names what the disdain was, and whom it was for. The arrogant elite of the sūrah's narratives had defined themselves by the lowliness of the people who believed: the chiefs of Ṣāliḥ's town asking *“Are you certain that Ṣāliḥ is truly a messenger from his Lord?”* while the lowly answered, *“We surely believe in what he has been sent with”* (7:75–76); the disbelieving chiefs of Noah's people telling him that only *the lowliest* follow him (11:27); the people of Shuʿayb lying in ambush on the road to turn back those who believed (7:86).

The Prophet ﷺ gave the two populations their portraits in a single hadith, and no commentary improves on it. Harithah ibn Wahb heard the Messenger of God say: *“Shall I tell you of the people of Paradise? They comprise every poor humble person, and if he swears by Allah to do something, Allah will fulfil it; while the people of the fire comprise every violent, cruel, arrogant person”* (al-Bukhārī 6657). The verse of the Heights has staged that hadith as a conversation between two populations who can see one another.

**The Failure of Social Capital**

Read economically, verse 48 is a statement about currency. Arrogance is a form of capital in this world: it purchases obedience, deference, precedence in a queue, the last word in a meeting, the silence of the people who would otherwise object. The verse declares the exchange rate on the Day: zero. There is no market in the Hereafter for any of the transactions the tyrannical elite spent a lifetime perfecting.

And the alternative assets are the ones the sūrah has already named, in the plainest language it possesses. Verse 35 defined salvation as *reform* and reverence; verse 42 as belief and right action; verse 43 as an inheritance granted for what one did and a guidance that came from God. Against the tyrants' *jamʿ* — the crowd — the sūrah files the *ṣālihāt* — the deeds — and its verdict on the comparison is the single sentence being shouted across the barrier in this verse.

**The Question That Follows**

The scene is not finished with the tyrants. Verse 49 will put a question to them in the same voices, and the question will be the last word of the passage: *Are these the ones you swore would never be shown Allah's mercy?* — pointing, in the manner of the sūrah, at the poor and lowly who followed the messengers and who stand at that moment inside the Garden while the men who mocked them watch from the Fire. The people of the Heights do not answer their own question. The next verse answers it with a command, and the command is not addressed to the tyrants.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:49

> **“Are these the ones concerning whom you swore that God would not extend any mercy?” “Enter the Garden! No fear shall come upon you, nor shall you grieve.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Names the Mockery**

*A-hāʾulāʾi alladhīna aqsamtum lā yanāluhum Allāh bi-raḥma* — are these the ones of whom you swore that Allah would never show them mercy? The verse is a question addressed to the tyrants of verse 48, and it closes the longest single argument the sūrah has constructed: the exchange between the proud and the lowly that began, in the world, with a sneer and now ends in the Hereafter with a demonstration.

The oath in the question is the point of it. *Aqsamtum* is the verb of a solemn sworn claim, and the claim is a denial of God's mercy to specific people — the poor, the dependent, the unimpressive believers who had attached themselves to the messengers. The sūrah's own account of that sneer has already appeared: the arrogant chiefs asking the lowly whether they were sure their prophet was really from God (7:75–76). The Qur'an preserves the same scene in the histories of other prophets. The chiefs of Noah's people said, *“We see you only as a human being like ourselves, and we see that no one follows you except the lowliest among us”* (11:27). The people of Shuʿayb's town asked the prophet, *“How can we believe in you, when you are followed only by the lowest of the low?”* (26:111). And the sūrah of the Cave records the instruction given to the Prophet in the middle of a Makkah that made precisely this calculation: *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxury of this worldly life”* (18:28; cf. 6:52).

**What Is Actually Being Denied**

The statement that the poor will never be shown mercy is not simply a rude opinion about the weak. It is a claim about God — an assertion that the divine decision has been settled in advance by the social order, that the gates of Paradise are staffed like the gates of a Makkan house, and that the meritoriousness of a person can be read off his standing in the world. That presumption is what makes the verse a scene of judgment rather than a scene of embarrassment.

The Book states the correction in the imperative, and it does so in the sharpest available form: *“Say, O Prophet, that Allah says, 'O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. Surely it is He Who is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful'”* (39:53). The verse does not qualify the offer by class, lineage, learning, or the approval of the notable. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself has already stated the theology that the tyrants' oath denied: *“My mercy encompasses all things”* (7:156), and its own definition of who inherits the Garden — belief and right action, no inventory of titles (7:42).

**The Answer Nobody in the Verse Gives**

The question is asked and left unanswered in the mouths of those who greeted the Garden with *Peace*, and the silence is the composition. The people of the Heights cannot admit the mockers into the Garden and cannot promise them anything; they can only ask the question whose answer is visible to everyone standing on the wall. It is answered a line later, by an authority that has been operating throughout these verses without ever introducing itself: *udkhulū al-jannata* — enter the Garden.

*Lā khawfun ʿalaykum wa-lā antum taḥzanūn* — no fear will be upon you, and you will not grieve. The formula is one of the most repeated promises in the Book: it is given to the followers of guidance in the first descent from the Garden (2:38), to all who believe in God and the Last Day and do good (2:62; 5:69), to those who submit themselves to God (2:112), to the friends of God (10:62), and to those who say, *Our Lord is Allah*, and remain steadfast (46:13). Its two halves are exhaustive by design. *Lā khawfun ʿalaykum* — nothing to fear ahead, no threat still in the pipeline, no sentence pending, no loss still possible. *Wa-lā antum taḥzanūn* — nothing to grieve behind, no ache for what was lost, no regret for the years, no memory doing damage. Sūrat Fāṭir gives the residents of the Garden the same sentiment in their own words: *“Praise be to Allah, Who has kept away from us all causes of sorrow”* (35:34), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own verse 43 will say that God has removed what was bitter in their hearts.

**The Vindication of the Lowly**

The command to enter is issued to the people on the Heights. The verse's structure is thus complete, and it is a structure the sūrah has been building since its first pages: the believers who were too insignificant to be counted in the world are the first to be told to go in, and the elites who mocked them watch from across the barrier.

The Prophetic tradition kept that scene in the centre of its social ethic. The hadith of Harithah ibn Wahb that *the people of Paradise are every poor humble person and the people of the Fire every violent, arrogant one* (al-Bukhārī 6657) was not a consolation prize for the poor; it was a standing warning to the powerful, and the Companions quoted it in their disputes about rank. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own presentation is even more pointed, because its evidence is a question asked in the presence of the men who made the wrong prediction. Nothing has to be argued. The wall, the garden, the faces, and the groups speak.

**The Sūrah's Last Word on the Two Crowds**

The passage ends, as it began in verse 36, on the question of who gets in. It began with a word — *istakbarū*, they disdained — and it ends with a door. Along the way, the sūrah showed the dishonest question (7:37), the community cursing its sister (7:38), the leaders and followers trading blame (7:39), the camel and the needle (7:40), the bed and its covers (7:41), the capacity of the soul (7:42), the song of the grateful (7:43), the call and its answer (7:44), the definition of the unjust (7:45), the people who recognize (7:46), the prayer of the almost-saved (7:47), the useless crowd (7:48), and this command. Every one of those verses made the same distinction in a different register, and the last of them makes it by pointing: these, whom you discounted, are told to enter.

The verse that follows will show the men left behind asking for a mouthful of water. The Book places them next to each other deliberately, because the sūrah's audience in Makkah was made up of people who had to choose that day — with their families, their clans, their businesses, and the opinion of everyone who mattered watching them decide.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:50

> **The inhabitants of the Fire will call out to the inhabitants of the Garden, “Pour some water down upon us, or some of that which God has provided you.” They will respond, “Truly God has forbidden them both to the disbelievers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Call**

*Wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-nāri aṣḥāba al-jannati* — and the inhabitants of the Fire called out to the inhabitants of the Garden. The verb *nādā* has now been used four times in seven verses: the Garden calls to the Fire (7:44), the Heights call to the Garden (7:46), the Heights call to the tyrants (7:48), and now the Fire calls to the Garden. The sūrah has constructed a complete traffic of voices across the barrier, and it is worth noticing what the traffic never carries: any movement of goods, any answer that changes a condition, any transfer at all. The only exchange between the two populations in the Hereafter is speech, and speech has become a spectator sport.

**What the Condemned Ask For**

*Afīḍū ʿalaynā mina al-māʾi aw mimmā razaqakum Allāh* — pour out on us some water, or any of what Allah has provided you. The request is remarkable for how little it asks. These are the inhabitants of the Fire, and they do not ask to be released, nor to have their punishment reduced, nor even to enter the Garden. They ask for water — for a share of the *provision* of the Garden, in whatever amount, in whatever form. The Book's portrait of damnation at its most concrete is a request for a mouthful.

The Qur'an's other accounts of the condemned in the Hereafter insist on the same modesty of demand. Sūrat al-Sajdah describes them saying, *“Our Lord, we have seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good”* (32:12). Sūrat Fāṭir has them crying, *“Our Lord, let us out so we may do good, unlike what we did”* (35:37). And the sūrah's own verse 53 will record the same plea. But this verse is unique in the Book for asking nothing more than a drink, which is exactly why it lands. Everything the Fire's inhabitants once possessed has been reduced to one wish, and the wish is for water.

The Gospel gives the same scene in its own idiom: the rich man in torment looks across the chasm and begs that Lazarus might dip the tip of his finger in water and cool his tongue (Luke 16:24). The Books of revelation agree on the architecture of the Hereafter to a degree that has struck every reader who has put the two passages side by side: a fixed separation, a vision of the other side, and a request that cannot be answered.

**The Reply: "It Is Forbidden"**

*Qālū inna Allāha ḥarramahumā ʿalā al-kāfirīn* — they replied: Allah has forbidden both to the disbelievers. The answer is not delivered as a taunt and not as a punishment; it is delivered as information. The people of the Garden state a fact about the order of things, which is the consistent register of the saved in this sūrah: verse 44's blessed asked a question already answered, and here they supply a prohibition.

The verb is the one the sūrah has already put on trial. At verse 32 the sūrah demanded, *“Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?”* — and the answer then was that false prohibitions belong to the fabricators, not to God. Here the same verb returns as a verdict: those who spent their lives forbidding what God made lawful now find their own share of water forbidden by God. The symmetry is not a coincidence of vocabulary; the sūrah has been building to it since verse 32, and the point is the doctrine of requital in kind that the Book states repeatedly: *“Whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:8); *“This is the reward for what your hands have done, and Allah is never unjust to His creation”* (3:182).

**Why Water?**

The choice of water as the subject of this final deprivation is not incidental to the sūrah. Water is the Book's standing emblem of mercy and of life: *“We made every living thing from water”* (21:30); *“Consider the water you drink: is it you who sent it down from the clouds, or is it We Who send it down?”* (56:68–69); *“Say: consider this — if your water were to sink into the earth, who else could bring you flowing water?”* (67:30). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf itself had opened its meditation on God's provision with the rain: *“He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy. When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land”* (7:57).

To deny a person water is, in the Book's vocabulary, to deny him the created symbol of God's care — and the men being denied spent their lives turning away from the source of that care. The verse's theology is therefore tighter than a picture of thirst. The Garden's rivers are not a beverage; they are what flows from the mercy of the One who made them flow. A man who refused the mercy will not be able to drink its sign, and the reply of verse 50 is simply the announcement that the connection is closed.

**The Silence of the Garden**

It matters that the residents of the Garden do not say *we will not give you*. Their answer names God as the one who forbade, and their sentence therefore contains no private refusal — no revenge, no satisfaction, no gloating. The saved of this sūrah have already asked and received; their concern in these scenes is not the fate of the condemned but the accuracy of the account. Verse 44: have you found your Lord's promise true? Verse 50: Allah has forbidden it.

That is a deliberate composition, and it is the sūrah's answer to the charge that religion is a system of tribal resentment. The Book refuses to let the saved enjoy the punishment of the damned. It has them state a fact about the order of the world, ask their question, offer their greeting of peace, and then — as verse 49 did with a command that mentioned nobody left behind — move on. Whatever the reader has been imagining about the satisfaction of the righteous over their enemies, the sūrah replaces it with a watering request and an answer from the Book of decrees.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:51

> **who took their religion to be diversion and play, and were deluded by the life of this world. So this Day We forget them, as they forgot the meeting with this Day of theirs, and as they used to reject Our signs.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Religion Taken as a Hobby**

*Alladhīna ittakhadhū dīnahum lahwan wa-laʿiban* — those who took their religion as amusement and play. The construction is worth pausing on. The object of the taking is *dīnuhum* — their own religion, the thing that should have been the organizing axis of their days, the affair to which they belonged. What they made of it was *lahw*, distraction, and *laʿib*, game. The two nouns are the Qur'an's standard pair for whatever occupies a person while having no consequence: the entertainment and the sport, the pastime and the joke.

The Qur'an repeats the accusation against the same category of people in three other places, and in each case the phrase sits at the end of a catalogue of serious failures. *“Leave those who have taken their religion as amusement and play, and were deluded by the life of this world”* (6:70). *“Those who took their religion as amusement and play, and were deluded by the life of this world — Today We shall forget them”* (7:51). And in Sūrat al-Ḥadīd, the divine description of the world's own texture: *“Know that this worldly life is nothing but amusement, play, adornment, boasting among yourselves, and multiplying wealth and children”* (57:20).

It is important not to misread the charge as an attack on pleasure. The sūrah itself forbade the men who banned God's adornments (7:32) and defended the lawful enjoyment of food, drink, and clothing (7:31). The Book's objection is never to delight; it is to the *demotion* of the highest concern to the level of a trifle. A religion held as a hobby cannot command a person at the moment of a real decision. That is the whole of the indictment, and the sūrah's Makkan audience knew exactly what it meant: they had gods, ceremonies, pilgrimage, poetry, and ancestral custom, and they held none of it as an obligation capable of costing them anything.

**The Mockery Behind the Description**

The verse's phrase has a second edge, and history sharpens it. The Aʿrāf's Makkah was a place where the Prophet's recitation was treated as an entertainment: *“The disbelievers say: this is nothing but a lie he has fabricated”* (25:4); *“they say: he is a poet, a madman”* (68:51); *“the wicked used to laugh at the believers, winking at each other”* (83:29–30). The Book records the mockery as a social technology, not a private opinion: it was how the strong kept the weak from listening, and how the notables of the town made the act of belief look embarrassing.

The sūrah has just finished describing what happens to the mockers. The people of the Heights have recognized them by their marks (7:48); the crowd they assembled has availed them nothing; and now, in the same breath as their plea for water, verse 51 gives the reason they are in that position. They made a game of the one thing that was not a game.

**"Deluded by the Life of This World"**

*Wa-gharrat-humu al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* — and the worldly life deceived them. The verb *gharra* is the verb of the mirage: to make a thing look like what it is not, to promise what it will not deliver. The Book uses it constantly and always with the same target: *“Let not the worldly life delude you, and let not the deceiver delude you about Allah”* (31:33; cf. 35:5); *“the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion”* (3:185); *“the life of this world is but a delusion”* (57:20).

The mechanism of the deception is worth naming, because the Book names it. The *dunyā* — from *danā*, nearness — is the near, the available, the immediately rewarding. It does not deceive by being evil at every turn; it deceives by occupying the whole of a person's attention with things that are true and good and near, so that the far and greater thing is never attended to at all. The Makkan merchants of the sūrah were not, for the most part, monsters. They were busy, successful, generous within their circles, proud of their city, and convinced that the world had always been as they knew it. The delusion is that a man can be absorbed by what he can see and still keep his bearings about what he cannot.

**"Today We Will Forget Them"**

The verse passes from description to sentence: *fa-l-yawma nansāhum kamā nasū liqāʾa yawmihim hādhā* — so today We shall forget them, as they forgot the meeting of this Day of theirs. The idiom of God "forgetting" is one the Book uses with care, and it never means a lapse of knowledge — the Qur'an's God is the One from whom nothing is hidden (40:16; 2:284). *Nisyān* here is the vocabulary of abandonment: the withdrawal of attention, the cancellation of the relationship. Sūrat al-Sajdah states the same sentence in the same words: *“So taste, because you forgot the meeting of this Day of yours; We too have forgotten you”* (32:14); Sūrat al-Jāthiyah repeats it (45:34); and Sūrat al-Tawbah applies it to the hypocrites who forgot God and were forgotten in turn (9:67).

Behind the idiom stands one of the Book's most consistent laws: the punishment is the thing itself, returned. Those who treated the meeting with their Lord as unworthy of attention are given the attention they gave. It is the exact form of the requital described in verse 41 — the bed of Hell for the man who set himself above God — and the exact form the sūrah will name at the end of the verse: *wa-mā kānū bi-āyātinā yajḥadūn* — because they used to reject Our signs.

The word *yajḥadūn* is not the general verb for disbelief. *Juḥūd* is denial of what one knows to be true — the refusal to acknowledge a thing whose evidence is already in hand. Sūrat al-Naml describes the people of the signs as rejecting them *“in their hearts though they were convinced of them”* (27:14), and the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf's own account of Iblīs's fall supplies the pattern: he did not doubt God's existence; he declined to obey. The men of verse 51 are not being condemned for an intellectual failure. They are being condemned for a habit of ignoring what they had already been shown — which is precisely why the verse says *they forgot*, and not *they never heard*.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:52

> **We have indeed brought them a Book, which We have expounded with knowledge, as a guidance and a mercy for a people who believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**After the Verdict, the Evidence**

The sūrah has just shown the condemned asking for water and listening to a prohibition. It now steps out of the Hereafter and back to the thing that decided the case: *wa-laqad jiʾnāhum bi-kitābin faṣṣalnāhu ʿalā ʿilmin* — and We brought them a Book which We explained in detail, with knowledge.

The placement is the argument. The Book is not introduced as a book that *should* have been followed; it is introduced as the exhibit filed after the sentence has been pronounced and the reason the sentence is just. The Qur'an's habit throughout is to state its own nature as a response to the accusations brought against it, and this verse summarizes the response in four words: *brought*, *explained*, *with knowledge*, and — further on — *guidance and mercy*.

**"We Explained It in Detail"**

The verb *faṣṣala* means to separate a thing into its parts so that each one is distinct: the cutting of a text into clear statements, the articulation of a body of teaching into chapters, commands, stories, warnings, and promises. The Qur'an uses it to describe its own composition at its opening pages: *“a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained. It is from the One Who is All-Wise, All-Aware”* (11:1); *“a Book whose verses are perfectly explained”* (41:3). And it uses the same verb for what the Book's opponents demand: *“Why is the Book not explained in detail?”* — the sūrah of Fuṣṣilat records the objection and answers it (41:44).

The claim *ʿalā ʿilm*, with knowledge, is the one that distinguishes this Book from the other things the Makkans classed it with. The audience of 7:52 had poets, soothsayers, genealogists, and story-tellers, and all of them produced texts out of talent and memory. The Qur'an asserts a different provenance: what is in it was put there knowingly, by the One who knows the human being from the inside. Sūrat Yā-Sīn denies the poet label outright — *“We have not taught him poetry, nor would it befit him”* (36:69) — and Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah lists the alternatives and rejects them one by one: it is not the word of a poet, not the word of a soothsayer, not the word of a soothsayer-like jinn (69:40–42).

**"Guidance and Mercy for a People Who Believe"**

*Hudan wa-raḥmatun li-qawmin yuʾminūn* — guidance and mercy for those who have faith. The pair is one of the Book's standing self-descriptions: *“O humanity! There has come to you a good admonition from your Lord, a healing for what is in the breasts, and a guidance and mercy for the believers”* (10:57). *“We send down the Qur'an as a healing and mercy for the believers, though it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”* (17:82). *“This Qur'an is a guidance and mercy for those who believe”* (27:77). *“A guidance and mercy for the doers of good”* (31:3).

The promise is generous and the qualifier is exact. The Book is not described as a source of mercy in the abstract; it is *mercy for a people who believe*, and Sūrat Fuṣṣilat explains what it does to those who will not: *“for those who do not believe, there is deafness in their ears, and it is blindness to them”* (41:44). The same revelation, in the same words, is a cure for one listener and an aggravation for another, and the difference is described by the Book as a condition of the heart rather than a quality of the text. The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf states the law bluntly a few verses later: God will turn away from His signs those who treat them with arrogance (7:146).

**The Explanation Requires Students**

If a Book is explained in detail, its hearers have an obligation that those who receive oracles do not have: the obligation to study. The Qur'an expects its audience to look things up — *“Had they referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, those who can deduce would have known it”* (4:83) — and the sūrah of Muḥammad instructs them to reflect on it directly: *“Will they not contemplate the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?”* (47:24). Sūrat al-Nisāʾ goes further and makes recitation, memorization, and teaching the identifying work of a community (4:82; 9:122).

The Prophet ﷺ summarized the resulting status in a sentence the tradition has never stopped repeating: *“The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it”* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān). Verse 52 is the doctrinal ground under that hadith. The Book that was explained in detail can only accomplish what it was sent to accomplish through people who take the explanation seriously enough to pass it on.

**What the Sūrah Does With the Book**

It is worth noting how consistently al-Aʿrāf returns to revelation as its anchor. It opened with the letters *alif lām mīm ṣād* and the announcement of a Book revealed so that its Prophet might warn (7:2). It told its readers, *“Follow what has been sent down to you from your Lord, and do not take others as guardians besides Him”* (7:3). It will say of itself that it is *“a Book which We have revealed to you so that you may lead people out of darkness and into light”* (14:1), and it will close the section on the people of the Heights with the reminder that those who hold fast to the Book and establish prayer are not forgotten (7:170).

Verse 52 therefore sits in the sūrah as the answer to every scene that has preceded it. The mockers of 7:51 treated the revelation as entertainment; the condemned of 7:50 had nothing to drink; the people of the Heights recognized everyone by their marks. And the reason none of that had to happen — the whole weight of the sūrah's grief — is contained in the phrase *We brought them a Book*. It was not hidden, not withheld, and not vague. It was explained, in detail, with knowledge, and it was offered as mercy to whoever would believe.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:53

> **Do they wait for aught save the full disclosure thereof? The Day when its full disclosure comes, those who forgot it beforehand will say, “The messengers of our Lord indeed brought the truth! Have we any intercessors who might intercede for us? Or might we be returned, that we might do other than what we used to do?” They have surely lost their souls, and that which they used to fabricate has forsaken them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of the Waiting**

*Hal yanẓurūna illā taʾwīlahu* — do they wait for anything but its fulfilment? The verb *yanẓurūn* here is not the word for looking but the word for waiting in suspense, and the noun is one of the Qur'an's most interesting technical terms. *Taʾwīl* is the coming-to-be of a statement: the point at which a proclamation stops being a prediction and becomes a fact. Sūrat Yūsuf uses it for the fulfilment of the dream (12:100); Sūrat Yūnus uses it for the fulfilment of a warning that the mockers had asked to be hurried (10:39).

The question is thus a rebuke in the form of an observation. Everyone alive is waiting for something; the sūrah asks what else these particular people could be waiting for, given that the thing they deny is the only thing on the horizon. Sūrat al-Zukhruf puts the same question to the mockers — *“Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise, when they do not even notice?”* (43:66) — and Sūrat al-Anʿām adds the warning that belief on the morning of arrival will be of no use (6:158).

**"Those Who Forgot It Beforehand"**

*Yaqūlu alladhīna nasūhu min qabul* — those who had forgotten it beforehand will say. The verb is the same one used in verse 51, where God says He will forget them as they forgot the meeting of this Day of theirs. The sūrah's argument is now complete and it circles: their forgetting was the offense; God's forgetting was the requital; and the Day itself is the memory that cannot be suppressed. The men who "forgot" were not people with a defective memory. The verse immediately preceding described them as those who had denied the signs they knew — a deliberate forgetting, maintained until the moment it could no longer be maintained.

**The Confession the Sūrah Extracts**

*Laqad jāʾat rusulu rabbinā bi-l-ḥaqq* — the messengers of our Lord certainly came with the truth. This is the fourth time the sūrah has placed a confession in the mouth of the condemned. Verse 37 had the dying say that their patrons had forsaken them; verse 39 had the leaders and followers abolish each other's rank; verse 44 had the population of the Fire answer the Garden's question with a single *yes*; verse 53 has them affirm the truth of the messengers whose coming they spent a lifetime denying.

The Book's insistence on this pattern is pastoral, and the sūrah has made no secret of it. The warnings, the signs, the scales, the conversations across barriers are all in service of the same outcome: the truth will eventually be acknowledged by everyone, and the only question is when. Sūrat al-Naml puts the recognition in the middle of history rather than the end of it — the people of the signs rejected them *“in their hearts though they were convinced of them”* (27:14) — and Sūrat al-Anʿām records the confession of the people of the Fire without any softening: *“If only we were sent back, we would not deny the signs of our Lord”* — followed immediately by the Book's verdict, *“No! They would return to what they were forbidden”* (6:27–28).

**"Are There Any Intercessors Who Can Plead for Us?"**

*Fa-hal lanā min shufaʿāʾa fa-yashfaʿū lanā* — is there anyone to intercede for us? The plea is the one the Qur'an deals with most thoroughly, because it was the central hope of the Makkan religion. Its gods were intercessors; its ancestors were intercessors; the pilgrimage was made in their names. The Book answers the hope on the highest theological level and refuses the version of it the sūrah's audience was banking on.

Intercession in the Qur'an is not a transaction that any creature can arrange. *“Who can intercede with Him except by His permission?”* (2:255). *“None can intercede except by His permission”* (10:3). *“To Him belongs all intercession”* (39:44). *“He knows what is before them and behind them; and they do not intercede except for those whom He approves”* (21:28; cf. 20:109; 53:26). The Book does not deny that intercession exists; it removes it from the possession of the influential and makes it entirely dependent on the will of God. And for those whose whole case was built on it, Sūrat al-Muddaththir records the verdict that ends the hope: *“the intercession of intercessors will be of no benefit to them”* (74:48).

**"Or We Could Be Sent Back"**

*Aw nuraddu fa-naʿmala ghayra alladhī kunnā naʿmal* — or be sent back, so that we may do other than what we did. The request for return is the Book's most repeated final plea: *“Our Lord, let us out so we may do good, unlike what we did”* (35:37); *“My Lord, send me back, so that I may do good in that which I left behind”* (23:99–100); *“If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us”* (2:167); *“Our Lord, we have seen and heard, so send us back, we will do good”* (32:12). Sūrat al-Zumar records the same request in the first person: *“If only I had a second chance, I would have been one of the good-doers”* (39:58) — and the Book states elsewhere the ransom such a speaker would offer: *“Even if the wrongdoers were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves”* (39:47; cf. 13:18; 3:91).

And the Book's refusal is equally consistent, and it is not cruelty. Sūrat al-Anʿām has already given the reason with a precision that makes the plea collapse: if they were returned they would return to exactly what was forbidden them (6:28). The request is not a change of will; it is a change of circumstances. What they want is not to become different but to have another opportunity to be the same with better odds. The sūrah of Yūnus puts the same anatomy of repentance on record: the repentance of the drowning is not counted as faith (10:90–91).

**"They Have Ruined Themselves"**

The verse's last sentence ends the scene with an accounting: *qad khasirū anfusuhum* — they have lost themselves — *wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn* — and what they used to fabricate has failed them. The word *khasira* is the trader's word for losing his capital, and the Book uses it for those whose capital was the self: *“those whose scale is light have lost themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs”* (7:9); *“the losers are those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Judgment”* (39:15). The parable is exact. Investment is what a person does with a life; the profit and loss statement is the balance of the Day; and the people in this verse have lost the principal.

The second half of the sentence is the quieter horror. *Mā kānū yaftarūn* — what they fabricated — covers the idols they carved, the doctrines they invented, the ancestral customs they defended, and the status they built on those foundations. The Book's statement is that none of it will be there. *“Whatever they had fabricated will fail them”* (16:87); *“their partners will fail them, and that was their lie”* (46:28); *“the gods they fabricated will abandon them”* (7:53 here; 41:48). Sūrat al-Anʿām puts the whole thing in six words: *“Whatever they were inventing was lost to them”* (10:30).

The passage that began with a question about what the mockers were waiting for ends with the answer: they were waiting to discover that nothing they had built existed, and that they had spent an entire life on the only available opportunity to build something that did.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:54

> **Truly your Lord is God, Who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then mounted the Throne. He causes the night to cover the day, which pursues it swiftly; and the sun, the moon, and the stars are made subservient by His Command. Do not creation and command belong to Him? Blessed is God, Lord of the worlds!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Hereafter to the Maker**

The sūrah has spent a long stretch among the last things: the gates, the bed and its coverings, the wall, the calls across it, the request for water, the confession of the lost. At verse 54 it turns to the first things — *inna rabbakumu Allāh alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa fī sittati ayyām* — and the effect is deliberate. The Hereafter has just been shown to be an established order with gates and barriers and accounts; verse 54 names the One who established it, and does so with the vocabulary of creation rather than of judgment.

This is one of the Qur'an's several "Throne verses," and they share a common form: *“Surely your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne, conducting every affair”* (10:3); *“He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days — and His Throne was upon the waters — in order to test which of you is best in deeds”* (11:7); *“He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne”* (57:4); *“It is Allah Who has created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne”* (32:4; cf. 25:59). The repetition is not a set of variations on a theme; it is the Book's way of anchoring the whole of its moral teaching in a doctrine of the Creator whose authority is total.

**"In Six Days"**

The phrase *sittati ayyām* has drawn the attention of every commentator, and two lines of reading are worth preserving. The first concerns the measure: a "day" in the Qur'an's own usage is not necessarily a solar day. Sūrat al-Ḥajj states that *“a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of your counting”* (22:47), and Sūrat al-Maʿārij gives the measure of a day as fifty thousand years (70:4). What the verse tells the reader, therefore, is not a schedule but a manner: the creation was brought about in ordered stages, not in a single stroke.

The second line concerns the purpose of the number. Sūrat Hūd's version of the verse continues, *“in order to test which of you is best in deeds”* (11:7), connecting the six days to a moral project rather than to a mechanics. And the Book is emphatic about one implication the number has been misused to draw: *“We created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, and We were not even touched with fatigue”* (50:38). The verse is placed directly against the claim, current among the sūrah's audience's neighbours, that God rested on the seventh day. In the Qur'an, the completion of creation in six days demonstrates deliberateness; it does not imply exhaustion, and it does not establish a sabbath of divine recuperation.

**"Then He Rose over the Throne"**

*Thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh.* The Throne — *al-ʿarsh* — is the Book's image of the seat of universal authority: the angels carry it (69:17), circle it (39:75), and glorify God around it (40:7); God's All-Encompassing Seat, the *Kursī*, extends over the heavens and the earth (2:255). The verse's claim is that the One who made the world governs it from that authority, and the sūrah later adds its practical consequence: *“Call upon your Lord with humility and in secret”* (7:55) — the God being addressed is the owner, not a distant first cause.

The verb *istawā* has been discussed by the exegetes and theologians with great care and some heat, and the classical position of the Muslims' early generations is worth stating in its own terms: the verse is affirmed as it stands, with its meaning understood in the language it uses, and without the imposition of a "how" — the quality of the act being beyond human analogy (*laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾ*, 42:11). Al-Ṭabarī, reporting the views of the Companions and their students, records the reading that God *rose above* the Throne in the sense of ascending and settling, while strongly rejecting any notion of a body or of a creature's attributes. Other leading scholars of the later generations read the verb as "He turned to the Throne" or "He took command of the affair," in keeping with the verse's second clause, *conducting every affair* (10:3).

What is not disputed is the point being made. The Prophet ﷺ tested the belief of a slave girl whom her master intended to free, asking her, *“Where is Allah?”* She answered, *“In the heaven,”* and he ruled that she was a believing woman and ordered her freed (Muslim 537, from Muʿāwiyah ibn al-Ḥakam). The verse of the Heights belongs to the same plain doctrinal grammar: God is above His creation, the Throne is His, and the affairs of the world are run from there.

**"The Night Covers the Day; It Pursues It Relentlessly"**

*Yughshī al-layl al-nahār yaṭlubuhu ḥathīthan* — He covers the night with the day, and it pursues it unceasingly. The verb *yughshī* is a word for throwing a covering over something — the same root that supplied *ghawāsh*, the coverings of fire in verse 41. It is worth noticing the sūrah's reuse of it: the same image of a cover is used for the punishment of the arrogant and for the mercy of the alternation of night and day, because the Book is consistent about the fact that God is the author of both conditions.

The Quranic picture of the day and night as garments is developed elsewhere. *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and sleep for resting, and the day for rising”* (25:47); *“We made the night as a cover, and made the day for livelihood”* (78:10–11); *“a sign for them is the night, from which We strip the day, so they are left in darkness — and the sun runs to its resting place”* (36:37–38). The phrase *yaṭlubuhu ḥathīthan*, it pursues it swiftly, is the Book's way of expressing the relentlessness of time: the day does not wait for the night and the night does not negotiate with the day. A reader who has just been shown the reckoning at the Day of Judgment is being reminded, in the middle of a creation verse, that the clock which brings that Day is itself a creature under command.

**The Sun, the Moon, the Stars Under Command**

*Wa-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara wa-l-nujūma musakhkharātin bi-amrih* — and the sun, the moon, and the stars, subjected by His command. The word *musakhkhar* is the term for a thing compelled into service, and the Book uses it of everything in the natural order that serves human life: the day and the night, the sun and the moon, the ships at sea, the rivers, the winds, the clouds (16:12; 14:33; 45:13).

The clause has a polemical edge that its plainness can hide. The Meccan religion was not only the worship of stone; it was the veneration of the heavenly bodies, whose names and personae appear in the Qur'an's refutations: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them”* (41:37). Sūrat Yūnus states the principle and supplies the reason: *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and the reckoning”* (10:5). Sūrat Nūḥ calls them what they are — a light and a lamp (71:16) — and Sūrat al-Naḥl tells the reader that *“the stars have been subjected by His command”* and then asks, *“Will you not then be mindful?”* (16:12).

**"His Is the Creation and the Command"**

*Alā lahu al-khalqu wa-l-amr* — surely His are the creation and the command. The pairing is one the later scholars made into a technical distinction: the bringing of a thing into being and the directing of it once it exists both belong to God, and no created power holds either. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's next verses will draw the practical consequences — prayer, supplication, and hope (7:55–56) — and the sūrah's argument has been building to precisely this.

*Tabāraka Allāh rabbu al-ʿālamīn* — blessed is Allah, Lord of all worlds. *Tabāraka* is the Book's formula of exalted generosity, used at the openings of Sūrat al-Furqān and Sūrat al-Mulk over precisely the same doctrine — the One who sent the Criterion, the One in whose hand is dominion (25:1; 67:1). *Rabb al-ʿālamīn*, the Lord of all worlds, is the appellation with which the Book's own opening sūrah names Him (1:2), and the phrase *al-ʿālamīn* includes every order of creation — the angels, the jinn, humanity, and every species and system the reader does not know.

The verse's function in the sūrah is to convert the whole preceding passage into a claim on the present. The gates, the scale, the wall, the requests, the confessions, and the two crowds all belong to the One who made the heaven and the earth in six days and covers the night with the day. The next verses will explain what a person is supposed to do about that: call on Him, in humility, in secret, with hope and fear, and not corrupt the earth after it has been set right (7:55–56).
