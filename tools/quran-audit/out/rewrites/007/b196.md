**"Indeed, My Protector Is Allah"**

*Inna waliyyi Allāhu alladhī nazzala al-kitāba wa-huwa yatawallā al-ṣāliḥīn* — indeed, my Protector is Allah Who has revealed this Book. For He ˹alone˺ protects the righteous.

**The Declaration of Protection**

The verse answers the challenge of the previous sentence with a statement about the Prophet's Protector, and the Book's readers are given the ground of the messenger's confidence. The Qur'an's declarations of reliance are a fixed feature of its addresses to the Prophet, and this one names the protector as the One who revealed the Book.

**The Word for Protection**

*Walī* — the Book's word for the guardian, the ally, the one who takes charge of a person's affairs — describes a relationship of both authority and care, and the Qur'an uses it for God throughout. The same word appears in the Book's account of Jacob's trust: *“But ˹only˺ Allah is the best Protector, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful”* (12:64).

**"Who Has Revealed This Book"**

The verse identifies the Protector by His act rather than by an attribute: the One who sent down the Book is the One who protects. The sūrah's readers are shown the connection between the messenger's mission and his security — the same authority that commissioned him sustains him — and the Book's language of revelation and protection is joined in a single sentence.

**"For He Protects the Righteous"**

*Wa-huwa yatawallā al-ṣāliḥīn* — the protection is extended to the righteous as a class, and the sūrah's readers are told that the messenger's security is an instance of a general law. The Book's promises to the righteous run through the Qur'an, and the sūrah's readers have met the description of the righteous repeatedly in its narratives.

**The Prophet's Reliance in the Sūrah**

The sūrah had already stated the Prophet's disavowal of power and knowledge in the previous passage (7:188), and the present declaration completes it: he possesses nothing, and he is protected by the One who possesses everything. The Book's portrait of the messenger is consistent in both directions, and the sūrah's readers are given the two statements side by side.

**The Ground of the Messenger's Challenge**

The invitation to the idolaters to conspire against him (7:195) makes sense only in the light of this declaration, and the sūrah's readers are shown the reasoning of a man who does not rely on his own strength. The Book's readers are given a model of confidence that is not self-confidence, and the passage states the difference explicitly.

**The Book's Other Statements of Protection**

The Qur'an's statements about God's protection of the believers and the messengers are numerous, and their vocabulary is the same as the verse's. The Book's readers are told that the protection is not a privilege of the messenger's office alone but the standing provision for those who act rightly, and the sūrah's readers are invited to place themselves among them.

**The Verse's Structure**

The sentence has two parts: a personal declaration and a general statement, and the transition from *my Protector* to *the righteous* widens the address to include the reader. The Book's rhetoric in such verses consistently moves from the particular to the general, and the sūrah's readers are shown the doctrine's application to themselves.

**The Tradition's Reading**

The commentators took the verse as a statement of the Prophet's reliance and as a promise to the believers, and the tradition's literature of trust in God drew on it and on its companions. The sūrah's readers were meant to understand that the protection described is available to those who fulfill the conditions the sūrah has been describing.

**The Contrast With the Idols**

The verse's positive statement stands directly against the previous verses: the idols can help neither their worshippers nor themselves (7:192, repeated in the next verse), while the Protector of the Prophet is the One who revealed the Book and who takes charge of the righteous. The sūrah's arrangement makes the contrast unavoidable.

**The Reader's Reliance**

The sūrah's readers are asked to identify what they rely on, and the verse supplies the criterion: what is relied on must be able to act, and the only being who acts without limit is the One named here. The Book's teaching about reliance is thus stated in positive form at the end of the passage's critique of the idols.

**The Next Verse's Repetition**

The next verse repeats the idols' incapacity once more (7:197) before the sūrah turns to the question of hearing (7:198), and the repetition is the Book's way of concluding the argument. The passage's readers are given a final negative statement and then the sūrah's closing instructions, and the transition is from refuge in God to the practice of the community.

The verse's identification of the Protector with the giver of the Book also tells the sūrah's readers something about how the messenger's authority and his safety are connected: the same act that made him a messenger made him a dependent, and the Book's readers are shown that the office carries the protection of the One who conferred it. The passage's readers are given the ground of the Prophet's confidence in a single sentence.
