## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:12

> **He said, “What prevented thee from prostrating when I commanded thee?” He said, “I am better than he. Thou hast created me from fire, while Thou hast created him from clay.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Accused**

The scene turns to the interrogation, and its form repeats the pattern established in verses 6 and 7: God asks, and the answer exposes. The question is *mā manaʿaka* — literally, *what prevented you?* The verb *manaʿa* is the vocabulary of withholding and of barriers; a *māniʿ* is a thing that stands in the way. The question is therefore not a request for information. It puts before the accused the one thing he must now name: the obstacle. And since the command had come from God and nothing external had blocked the way, the only possible answer was the barrier he had built inside himself. The Qur'an's question strips away every third-party explanation before Iblīs speaks a word.

His answer does not name that barrier. It supplies, instead, an argument.

**The First Analogy in the Tradition's Telling**

*“I am better than him: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* The classical scholars read these words as the first act of reasoning by analogy (*qiyās*) in the history of the creation, and they examined its form with the care of logicians. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī set out the objections in sequence.

First, the premise is asserted, not established. Iblīs claims a nobility of fire above clay as though the proposition were self-evident. It is not; it is a claim about material substances whose ranking Iblīs had no revelation to support. The Qur'an, for its part, treats the composition of both as dust-bound and God-given: the jinn are *“created from a smokeless flame of fire”* (55:15), and the human being from *“sounding clay moulded from black mud”* (15:26). Arranging these ingredients in a hierarchy of superiority is a human projection, not a scriptural datum.

Second, even a true premise could not have produced that conclusion. The value of a thing before God is not set by what it is made of, but by its obedience and its reverence: *“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you”* (49:13). Iblīs reasoned from origin to rank, and the Qur'an's entire moral architecture refuses that move. A creature of fire and a creature of clay stand equally as creatures; only the command of God differentiates their duties.

Third, and decisively, the argument was raised against a direct command. Theologians of every school drew the line here: no demonstration — even a sound one — abrogates an explicit divine obligation. A person's reasoning is authorized to conclude that God is one, that the Messenger speaks for Him, that the prayer is due; it is not authorized to override what is thus concluded. To reason so is not to reason, but to use reasoning as an alibi.

**When Intelligence Goes to Work for Desire**

The most instructive feature of the answer is that it is *articulate*. Iblīs does not say “I did not want to.” He constructs a case. The Qur'an thus presents, at the first moral crisis of the created order, the psychology that will recur in every subsequent one: the mind's capacity to convert a preferred outcome into a reasoned position. Al-Rāzī notes that this is why the lesson of the verse is not confined to Iblīs. Any argument can be assembled backward from the conclusion one is unwilling to surrender; and the sharper the intelligence, the more respectable the assembly looks.

The criterion that separates sound reasoning from this corruption is stated by the angels in the parallel scene of al-Baqarah. When God announced the creation of a deputy on earth and the angels asked their question, the answer given for them — and given by them — was: *“Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise”* (2:32). The angels also reasoned — but they closed their reasoning with submission, whereas Iblīs closed his with a verdict. That is the whole difference, and the sūrah intends it to be remembered.

**The Lie in the Phrase “I Am Better”**

The comparative carries its own refutation. Iblīs accepted God's act of creation and even cited it as an argument; what he refused was God's authority to command. He was, in effect, a theist in metaphysics and an atheist in ethics, dividing lordship into departments so that the department that inconvenienced him could be reserved for himself. The Qur'an will answer the posture directly in another sūrah: *“Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60).

And the final irony is the criterion he chose. *“I am better than he”* — better in substance, by a material comparison. The Book's answer is that the creature he despised would receive what he lost: *“We have indeed honoured the children of Adam”* (17:70). Rank in the divine court is not a property of matter; it is a matter of response. It is the one measurement Iblīs never applied, because applying it would have cost him the argument — and it is the same measurement this sūrah put before its readers four verses earlier, when it asked who among the settled, provided, and shaped would give thanks.
