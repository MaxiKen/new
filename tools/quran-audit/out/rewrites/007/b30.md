**Two Parties on One Road**

*Farīqan hadā wa-farīqan ḥaqqat ʿalayhimu al-ḍalālah.* The verse divides humanity into two groups, and the two verbs are not symmetrical. For the first, guidance is something done *to* them — *hadā*, He guided, with God as the subject. For the second, straying is something that has *settled upon* them — *ḥaqqat*, from a root meaning to be due, to fall upon a person as a right or a consequence.

The asymmetry is the Qur'an's careful position on one of its most discussed questions. Guidance is an act of God's grace; misguidance is a settled consequence. The Book never says that God "made a party stray" in the way it says He guided a party; at most it says He *left* them, *turned away* from them, or *did not guide* them — and it never allows these statements to be detached from the condition of those concerned. Sūrat al-Ṣaff states the sequence explicitly: *“So when they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5). The deviation of the heart is a recompense, not a cause. The same law appears in 2:26, where straying happens to *“the rebellious”*; in 6:125, where the chest of the one left astray is constricted because of what he chose; and in 42:44, where the one left astray has no protector after Him.

The sūrah's readers are not being told that God arbitrarily assigns fates. They are being told that after a certain point, a direction hardens into a destiny. The verse states it in the past tense of a settled condition, and the whole of the passage that surrounds it — the ancestral argument, the lie against God, the warning about the enemy — is a description of how the settlement is reached.

**"They Took Satans as Protectors Instead of God"**

The cause is then named: *ittakhadhū al-shayāṭīn awliyāʾa min dūni Allāh*. The construction *min dūni Allāh* — apart from, to the exclusion of God — is the Qur'an's standard formula for the displaced loyalty that constitutes *shirk*. The verse is not speaking of a formal cult of the jinn, though such cults existed and are addressed elsewhere (72:6; 6:100). It is speaking of the appointment of a guardian: the habit of turning to something other than God for protection, validation, and direction — an appetite, a patron, a custom, an ideology, an inner voice.

The commentators note how this connects to the verse two lines earlier. There the divine act was named — *We made the satans the friends of those who disbelieve* (7:27) — and here the human act is named: *they took* them as protectors. The two are one event seen from the two sides of the same covenant, and the Book keeps both because neither alone tells the truth about responsibility. God's decree and the human being's choice meet in the same act, and the person's accountability survives the encounter.

The second sūrah of the Qur'an states the outcome of the two guardianships in one verse: *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers — He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire, where they will stay forever”* (2:257). The guidance of verse 30 is simply the first half of that verse in practice.

**"And They Think They Are Guided"**

Then the clause that makes the verse frightening: *wa-hum yaḥsabūna annahum muhtadūn*. Not merely lost — lost while convinced of the opposite. The Qur'an treats this as a distinct condition with its own vocabulary. Sūrat al-Kahf describes people *“whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good”* (18:104). Sūrat Fāṭir asks the question the sūrah of the Heights has been building toward: *“Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good like those who are rightly guided?”* (35:8). And Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn shows it operating at the social level: a group swearing oaths of loyalty while the Qur'an names them liars (63:1; 58:18).

The psychology of this sentence is the reason the verse pairs it with the ancestral argument of 7:28 and the enemy of 7:27. There are three roads into the same ditch: blaming one's failure on God, as Iblīs did; defending a practice because one's fathers did it; and taking the enemy as a protector while believing one has taken God. The common feature of all three is that none of them feels like error from inside. It is the settled condition, not the momentary sin, that the verse describes — and it is why the sūrah keeps returning to the criterion of revelation rather than the criterion of feeling.

**The Reflection That Is Owed**

The verse's placement at the end of the sūrah's opening argument gives it a pastoral function. Having just described the two parties, the Qur'an does not tell the reader which one he is in; it hands him the instruments to find out. The rest of the sūrah — the answer of verse 29, the prohibition of verse 33, the stories of the nations that follow — is the criterion applied.

The third sūrah of the Qur'an states the point about misguidance and its victims in one line that preserves hope: *“Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us”* (3:8). The Prophet ﷺ taught the same supplication in his own practice: *“O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion.”* The verse before us explains why such a prayer is necessary. Certainty is not the same as guidance, and the person who has never asked God to keep his heart has no guarantee that his own confidence is anything more than the settled state of a misdirected road.
