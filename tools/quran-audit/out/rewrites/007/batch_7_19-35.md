## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:19

> **“O Adam! Dwell thou and thy wife in the Garden, and eat from wheresoever you two will, but approach not this tree, lest you two be among the wrongdoers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Generosity of the Command**

The narrative returns to Adam with a command that is almost all permission. *Uskun* — dwell, take up residence — and then *fa-kulā min ḥaythu shiʾtumā*: eat from wherever the two of you wish. The Qur'an's fuller account in Sūrah Ṭā-Hā fills in what the permission covers: *“Here it is guaranteed that you will never go hungry or unclothed, nor will you ever suffer from thirst or the sun's heat”* (20:118–119). Food, clothing, water, shade — the four anxieties that organize most human labor — are declared settled in advance. The Garden is presented not as a reward to be earned but as a home to be inhabited, and the single prohibition is set inside a field of open permissions.

**One Tree, Deliberately Unnamed**

*Wa-lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarah* — and do not come near this tree. Two details of the wording carry the teaching. The first is *lā taqrabā*, do not approach, rather than *lā taʾkulā*, do not eat. The Qur'an uses the same construction for adultery — *“Do not go near adultery”* (17:32) — and for the orphan's property — *“do not come near the wealth of the orphan”* (6:152). The prohibition is a fence placed around the act, not merely a line at the act itself, and the jurisprudence of *sadd al-dharāʾiʿ* — blocking the pathways to what is forbidden — is read by the jurists out of exactly this idiom.

The second detail is that the tree is never identified. The commentators hold that God withheld the name deliberately. Al-Ṭabarī records statements to the effect that it was a fig or a vine or another fruit, and then notes that no report is authoritative and none is needed; Ibn Kathīr draws the conclusion that disputing about the species is time spent on what the verse chose to leave open. The purpose of the prohibition was never information about botany. It was obedience: a single, unmistakable boundary in the middle of an unlimited gift, so that the creature's freedom would have somewhere to be exercised.

**Both of Them, and the Silence About Hawwāʾ**

The verse addresses the pair: *dwell, you and your wife* — and the wife is not named anywhere in the Qur'an. The Book has kept her identity from the text, though the commentators transmit the name Hawwāʾ from the older reports.

That silence is worth noticing, because of what the surrounding verses do with the pair. In 7:20–22 the tempter addresses *them*, both of them act, and both are exposed together. The Qur'an never assigns the first initiative to the woman. Where the older scriptural narrative presents a sequence in which the woman is deceived and then leads the man, this sūrah presents a single act of two people, and the responsibility is shared and equal: *“And their Lord called unto them, ‘Did I not forbid you that tree?’”* The point matters because a long tradition of misogynist preaching has drawn from the story a doctrine of female culpability for the first sin. The Qur'an's own text does not support it, and the careful reader of this verse has already been given the corrective: the warning was addressed to both, the command was given to both, and the failure was theirs together.

**The Anatomy of a Test**

Set within the sūrah's architecture, the verse completes the scene the reader has been prepared for. Verses 11 to 18 showed a creature who refused a command and lost everything by refusing it. Here a different creature receives a command to hold the boundary, and inside the permission there is freedom to move, to eat, to enjoy. What is being tested is not appetite — the appetite is licensed — but trust: whether the word of God about a tree will be believed over the apparent silence of everything else.

The final clause names the cost of the failure before it happens: *fa-takūnā min al-ẓālimīn* — lest you two become of the wrongdoers. It is the same word the townspeople of verse 5 used of themselves at the moment of destruction, and the same word that will reappear in Adam's own confession at verse 23. The sūrah is teaching its vocabulary as it goes: the *ẓulm* of the first verses is a misplacement, and a creature who places his own judgment above a command has displaced himself from where he was put.

**Law and Mercy in One Verse**

The theological weight of the passage sits in the coexistence of the two facts it announces at once. There is a command that must be obeyed, and there is a garden wide enough that disobeying it is never necessary. The boundary is narrow; the room around it is vast. Rabbinic and Christian traditions later built elaborate doctrines of a fallen nature on the first act; the Islamic reading, formed by these verses, insists on something more precise. The human being was created with a real capacity to obey — the permission was genuine, and no one was placed in a trap — and the same human being was created with a real capacity to fall, because a creature whose obedience was automatic would not be a moral being at all.

The rest of the sūrah will show what happens when the boundary is crossed — the exposure, the shame, the sewing of leaves, the searching call of God in the Garden — and how a creature who has fallen is to answer. But the verse's own balance should be preserved before the story moves on: the command that will be broken was given by a Lord who had first spread a table.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:20

> **Then Satan whispered to them, that he might expose to them that which was hidden from them of their nakedness. And he said, “Your Lord has only forbidden you this tree, lest you should become angels, or among those who abide [forever].”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whisper: A Method, Not an Announcement**

The previous episode showed the tempter declaring war openly — the vow to sit in ambush on the straight path (7:16), the four-sided approach (7:17). Here he changes his register entirely. *Fa-waswasa lahumā al-shayṭān*: he whispered to them. The Arabic *waswasah* names a sound at the edge of hearing — the rustle of a garment, a step half-made, the murmur that a person cannot quite attribute to anyone. The word is used in the Qur'an for the work of *al-waswās al-khannās*, the retreating whisperer who works in the breast (114:4–5).

The choice of verb is diagnostic. The enemy who has been defeated in argument does not return with a better argument; he returns with a method that does not require an audience. He does not say, "I am the one who refused to prostrate." He speaks in the mode of an inner suggestion, so that the thought can be mistaken for the person's own.

**The Aim: Exposure**

The verse names the tempter's objective with uncomfortable precision: *li-yubdiya lahumā mā wūriya ʿanhumā min sawʾātihimā* — that he might expose to them what was hidden from them of their nakedness. The purpose was not the fruit. The fruit was the instrument; the exposure was the goal.

The psychology here has been noted by the commentators, and it is the reason this verse functions as a warning rather than a chronicle. Shame in the Qur'an's account is not a moral invention imposed from outside; it is the response of a creature to a covering that has been withdrawn. The covering in the Garden — before any transgression — was *wūriya*, hidden and concealed, a condition of dignity given rather than created. What the whisperer wants is the collapse of that condition, and he is willing to use anything at hand to bring it about. Whatever the particular tree was, the mechanism was the same as it is in every generation: a lawful thing in itself, offered on a condition that only God has the authority to require.

**Two Promises: Transcendence and Permanence**

His argument is preserved in the fuller account: *“Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?”* (20:120). Here it is given in its compressed form: your Lord has forbidden you this tree *“only that you two should become angels, or become of the immortals”* (7:20).

The two lures are chosen with a craftsman's knowledge of the human interior. The first appeals to the desire to rise — to be more than one is, to occupy a larger station. That is the hunger that destroyed Iblīs himself in verse 12, and it is now offered to Adam as the reason for the prohibition. The second appeals to the desire not to lose — immortality, *khuld*, the refusal of the horizon that makes a creature a creature. Between them, they cover the two directions in which human longing runs: upward, and backward from death.

The claim about God is its own offense. *Mā naha-kumā rabbukumā ʿan hādhihi al-shajarah illā an takūnā malakayn* — the prohibition is made to appear a restriction on human advancement, an act of divine jealousy rather than of care. This is the tempter's oldest strategy and his most durable one: not "God has forbidden nothing," but "God has forbidden this for a reason that is not to your advantage." The sūrah will deal with that accusation directly in the next verses, and the answer is not a rebuttal but an outcome.

**Suggestion and Consent**

The Islamic account of temptation that this verse anchors was given its classic shape in a single image from the Prophet ﷺ, recorded in the two Ṣaḥīḥs: *“Satan flows through the human being as blood flows through the veins”* (al-Bukhārī 3281; Muslim 2175). The image is precise in what it does and does not claim. It claims nearness and passage — the whisper has no distance to cross. It does not claim compulsion.

The Qur'an's distribution of responsibility follows the image exactly. Adam is blamed for his act and named as the one who forgot (20:115) and disobeyed (20:121). Satan is blamed for the deception. No verse of the Book permits a human being to say that the whisperer is the reason he fell. The whisper is not obedience; it borrows a voice that sounds like one's own, and the sūrah teaches its reader to recognize the accent now, before the story reaches the exposure, because after the exposure it is always too late to say that the suggestion was never recognized as foreign.

**The First Crack in the Cover**

The verse's final clause is where Christian and Islamic readings part, and the parting deserves to be stated plainly. What the Qur'an records is an act, its motive, and its loss — not a transmitted condition that propagates through the species. The Book will say in Sūrah al-Zumar that *no soul bears the burden of another* (6:164; 39:7), and in al-Najm that *the human being has only what he strives for* (53:39). Adam's descendants inherit the circumstances of his life — the earth, the term, the enemy — but not his guilt.

What the verse does hand on, though, is the outline of a permanent risk. The whisper comes to a creature who is already provided for, already clothed, already placed; the suggestion is not hunger, but ambition; the weapon is not the fruit but a claim about the One who prohibited it. Every later verse of this sūrah about eating and drinking, about clothing and about the temptation of excess (vv. 26–32) is a reworking of the same scene, because the sūrah's original audience knew precisely the danger: a litany of permissions, one unidentified boundary, and a voice suggesting that the boundary was never meant for their good.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:21

> **And he swore unto them, “Truly I am a sincere adviser unto you.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Perjured Oath**

*Wa-qāsamahumā* — and he swore to them. The verb is from *qasam*, the oath, and it is chosen for its weight in a society where an oath was the ultimate instrument of trust. The tempter had no standing to be believed on his word, so he manufactured standing by appealing to the one thing the Arabs of the sūrah's audience treated as beyond manipulation: the sworn word.

The commentators note the formal completeness of the deception. He did not assert; he promised. He did not persuade with evidence; he offered a guarantee. And in doing so he produced the first false oath in the moral history of the creation — a detail the sūrah does not dwell on but the reader should notice, because the Book will later make oath-taking by the divine name a matter of severe account: *“Do not use Allah's name in your oaths as a barrier to doing good”* (2:224), and *“those who sell the covenant of Allah and their oaths for a paltry price — they will have no share in the Hereafter”* (3:77). The ancestor of all swearing-away of the truth is the oath of Iblīs.

**The Theft of a Religious Word**

The claim itself is a single phrase: *innī lakumā la-min al-nāṣiḥīn* — surely I am to you two among the sincere advisers. *Nāṣiḥ* comes from *n-ṣ-ḥ*, the root of purity and of counsel: the sincere advice that seeks the good of the one advised and nothing else. The emphasis is piled on, with both the *inna* and the *lām* of asseveration.

The word is not accidental; the sūrah has been using it of the prophets. The messengers of 7:21's surrounding chapters say to their peoples, *“but you love not sincere advisers”* (7:79), and Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Shuʿayb each announce: *“I am to you a trustworthy messenger”* and *“I am to you a sincere adviser”* (7:68; 7:62; 7:79; 7:93). The tempter takes the signature phrase of the prophetic office and wears it. This is a deliberate arrangement by the sūrah: it teaches the reader that the counterfeit of a category is not the absence of that category but its imitation, and that the imitation will sound exactly like the original.

**Why the Voice Cannot Be the Criterion**

The verse's practical force lies here. A person cannot verify sincerity by tone; the perjurer is often warmer than the truthful man, and the Qur'an says so of the very people who oppose religion: *“When you see them, their appearance might impress you, and when they speak, you listen to their words”* (63:4). The Book, therefore, refuses to make sincerity self-authenticating. It gives criteria outside the speaker's delivery.

The first criterion is verification. *“O you who believe! If a troublemaker comes to you with a report, verify it, lest you harm a people in ignorance and then regret what you have done”* (49:6). The instruction is general and applies to counsel as much as to news: the weight of a claim is not established by the confidence of the claimant. The second criterion is the content's relation to God's command. The tempter's speech was sweet and its content was permission to cross a boundary. Where counsel trends toward the loosening of what God has legislated, the *nāṣiḥ* label becomes evidence against the speaker, whatever the timbre of his concern. The third criterion is outcome, which is precisely what the next verses provide — and the sūrah intends that the lesson be learned from the outcome rather than repeated.

**The Guardian's Warning Against Words Alone**

The Prophet ﷺ trained his community to distinguish the surface from the substance in this very area. He warned of those whose speech would be more pleasing than it deserved and whose piety would be of the tongue; he described a people who would recite the Qur'an but whose recitation would not pass their throats (al-Bukhārī 3344; Muslim 1064, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, of the Khawārij). The warning is about sincerity of a specific kind: words that borrow the authority of religion while serving something other than God.

There is a further layer for the reader of this sūrah. The oath of 7:21 was sworn to two people who had no reason to distrust the speaker, because they had never met a lie. The human race has no such excuse. We have the record of the first deception, and the record of every deception it fathered. The verse therefore functions as a vaccination: it introduces the pattern of the counterfeit adviser early, at the first opportunity, so that a person encountering the same voice in a marketplace, a classroom, a screen, or his own private reasoning will recognize what he is hearing before he acts on it.

**The Sincerity That Keeps Its Address**

One further note belongs here, because it is the difference between the tempter's *nāṣiḥ* and the prophets' *nāṣiḥ*. The messengers of this sūrah never demand to be trusted for their own sake; each of them says, in the same breath, *“I ask of you no reward for it; my reward is only from the Lord of the worlds”* (26:109; 26:127; 26:145; 26:164; 26:180). Sincere counsel in the Qur'anic sense is counsel that can name where it is going — to the commanded good, and to God. The oath of the tempter names nothing beyond itself. The safest question a person can put to any adviser, including the advice that rises inside his own chest, is the one this verse leaves unspoken but answers: toward whose good does this counsel actually point?

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:22

> **Thus he lured them on through deception. And when they tasted of the tree, their nakedness was exposed to them, and they began to sew together the leaves of the Garden to cover themselves. And their Lord called out to them, “Did I not forbid you from that tree, and tell you that Satan is a manifest enemy unto you?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Descent into Delusion**

*Fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūr* — so he brought them down by delusion. The verb *dallā* is the transitive of *tadallā*, to hang down, to let a cord out gradually; the picture is of lowering something by degrees. Beside it stands *ghurūr*, the noun of deception, formed from the same root the Qur'an uses for the deceitful world (*al-ghurūr*) and for the delusion of the one who trusts it — *“Do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the chief deceiver deceive you concerning Allah”* (31:33; 35:5).

The two words together describe a mechanism rather than a moment. Adam and his wife were not struck down; they were let down, step by step, with the road appearing to run upward the whole way. That is the anatomy of the first temptation, and it is the one the reader of the sūrah will meet again in the surah's own warnings about wealth, adornment, and appetite (7:26–32): a slow gradualism in which each step is justified by the promise of the next.

**"When They Tasted"**

Then the act itself: *fa-lammā dhāqā al-shajarah*. The verb is *dhāqa*, to taste — not *akala*, to eat a meal; not *shabiʿa*, to be filled. Even in the telling of the disobedience, the Qur'an does not describe the couple as banqueting. A taste was enough.

The choice of verb has been read in every generation as the sharpest possible comment on the act. The offense was minimal in quantity and total in kind. Nothing was gained — a taste — and everything given was jeopardized: residence, covering, dignity, ease. The reason the sūrah does not need to harangue its reader about the gravity of small sins is that it has already staged the demonstration: the measurement of a transgression is the One against whose command it was committed, not the size of the object taken. The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle in a hadith that has been memorized in the same spirit — *“Beware of the small sins”* — in the imagery of a people gathering twigs until they had built a fire (Aḥmad, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd).

**The Exposure and the First Kind of Work**

*Badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā* — their nakedness became plain to them. Then the sūrah's most human detail: *wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraq al-jannah* — they began to patch together upon themselves leaves of the Garden. The verb *yakhṣifāni* is the vocabulary of stitching and of mending, and it is, in the whole Book, the first recorded human labor after the fall.

Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī both note the poignancy of the moment: the covering they were given by God is gone, and what they can assemble with their own hands is leaves. This is the precise condition of a fallen creature that the sūrah's later verse will describe directly — *“O children of Adam! We have sent down upon you raiment to cover your nakedness and as adornment; but the raiment of righteousness, that is best”* (7:26). The leaves are the first attempt at a covering that will not hold; the *taqwā* named two verses later is the covering that will.

**The Call in the Garden**

Then comes the divine address, and its shape is a lesson in how God deals with a fallen creature: *“Did I not forbid you that tree, and say to you that Satan is unto you an open enemy?”* The construction is interrogative but the content is a reminder. Two things are called back to mind: the command that was given, and the warning that had accompanied it.

The sūrah has, in fact, already delivered that warning to the reader — *“Do not let Satan deceive you as he tempted your parents out of Paradise”* (7:27a; and cf. 20:117, where Adam himself is told, *“This is surely an enemy to you and to your wife”*). The call therefore closes no gap of information. It exposes a gap of attention: the enemy was named in advance, the boundary was pointed out in advance, and both were held in the same hand. The wording *ʿaduww mubīn* — an open, manifest enemy — is one of the Qur'an's recurring formulas about Satan, and it recurs so often (2:168; 2:208; 6:142; 7:22; 12:5; 17:53; 28:15; 36:60; 43:62) because the Book is teaching a single habit of mind: whereas a person's dealings with enemies in the visible world are conducted with caution, the enemy who works through the inner suggestion is regularly treated as a friend.

**The Excuse That Was Not Offered**

One negative observation is worth making, because it is what the sūrah's next verse will make positive. When God calls them out, Adam and his wife do not say: the tempter swore to us. They do not say: the whisper sounded like our own thought. They do not say: the tree was unmarked, or the prohibition was ambiguous. The silence of the narrative is complete on this point, and it is deliberate — because the very next verse gives the answer they did make, and it contains no third party at all.

That is the difference between the fall of 7:22 and the fall of 7:12. Both creatures broke a command after being warned. One produced an argument against the command and never returned from it; the others are about to produce a sentence against themselves. The sūrah's placement of the two falls side by side, in a single continuous narrative, is the whole moral of the passage: what happens after the act is what determines where the act will end.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:23

> **They said, “Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If Thou dost not forgive us and have Mercy upon us, we shall surely be among the losers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words That Were Given**

This verse is the turning point of the entire narrative, and the Qur'an tells us where the words came from. *“Then Adam was inspired with words of prayer by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance”* (2:37). The sentence in al-Baqarah uses *talaqqā*, to receive — the words were not composed by the fallen man in the Garden; they were given to him. The tradition's commentators read a mercy in that grammar. A creature who had just broken a command does not know what to say; the Book's answer is that the formula of return is itself supplied from above, which is why the Prophet ﷺ taught his community to ask God to teach them how to ask Him.

**"Our Lord" — The Address That Survives the Fall**

The sentence opens with *rabbanā*. It would have been possible for a shame-stricken creature to address God by the Name of power, of judgment, of wrath; the pairing chosen is the Name of nurture and cultivation. The first word after the failure is therefore a claim on the relationship, not a renunciation of it. The Qur'an's model of repentance begins here, and this is why the same opening recurs on the lips of the returning: *“Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves”* (7:23), *“Our Lord, we have believed, so forgive us”* (3:16), *“Our Lord, forgive us our sins”* (3:193).

**"We Have Wronged Ourselves"**

*Ẓalamnā anfusanā.* Three words, and the whole architecture of Islamic accountability is inside them. The object of the wrong is the self — *anfusanā* — not the majesty of God in the abstract, and not another party. The verse thus contains a diagnosis before it contains a request: sin is first of all an injury to the one who commits it, and the Qur'an uses this self-directed language consistently. *“Whoever does wrong, does wrong against himself”* (4:111); *“whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger harms no one but himself”* (33:36); *“Indeed, you have wronged yourselves”* (2:54).

The verb is in the perfect tense, with the *nā* of the first person plural. It reports a finished act and accepts it as one's own. Now compare it with the sentence spoken by Iblīs eleven verses earlier — *“because You have caused me to err”* (7:16) — and the contrast the sūrah has built becomes unavoidable. One creature files the blame upward; the other files the blame inward. The Book does not present both as mistakes with different flavors. It presents one as a position from which no return was ever sought, and the other as the exact posture from which return always begins.

**The Two Requests and Their Precision**

*In lam taghfir lanā wa tarḥamnā* — if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us. The two verbs cover the two halves of what a wrongdoer needs. *Ghufrān* is covering and cancellation: the record erased, the debt discharged. *Raḥmah* is positive: the gift, the restoration, the good supplied where nothing was owed. The pair runs through the Book's prayers — *“My Lord, forgive and have mercy, for You are the Best of the merciful”* (23:118) — and its order is significant. Forgiveness comes first because mercy has nothing to land on until the account is cleared.

The conditional formulation is worth a moment. *If You do not forgive us…* is not doubt; the Arabic conditional here expresses surrender rather than uncertainty, a placing of the matter entirely in God's hand. The servant does not argue his entitlement to pardon. He does not present the balance of his good deeds, or the extenuation of a tempter's oath. He states the consequence of not being forgiven — *we shall surely be of the losers* — and leaves the decision where it belongs.

**The Same Words from a Different People**

The sūrah does something remarkable with this formula: it puts the identical sentence in the mouths of a later generation. When the Children of Israel repent of the calf, the Qur'an reports: *“And when they regretted it and saw that they had gone astray, they said, ‘If our Lord does not have mercy upon us and forgive us, we shall surely be of the losers’”* (7:149). The parallel is deliberately exact, and it makes a doctrinal point that the sūrah wants carried forward through its long history of Israelite failure: the *mechanism* of return never changes. It is not a Christian or a Jewish rite, not a particular shrine, not an intercession purchased from a priest. It is the acknowledgment of self-inflicted harm, addressed by a creature to his Lord, without a mediator and without an excuse.

**The Master Formula**

The Prophet ﷺ gave this pattern its most complete written form in the *sayyid al-istighfār*, the master supplication: *“O God, You are my Lord; there is no god but You. You created me and I am Your servant, and I abide by Your covenant and promise as much as I am able. I seek refuge with You from the evil I have done. I acknowledge before You Your favour upon me and I acknowledge my sin, so forgive me, for none forgives sins but You.”* (al-Bukhārī 6306, from Shaddād ibn Aws). The components are exactly those of 7:23 — lordship addressed, fault owned, mercy requested, credit given to God alone — and the hadith adds the detail that makes the earlier scene intelligible: the one who says this in the morning and dies that day enters the Garden.

The verse therefore closes the first fall with hope rather than condemnation, and the sūrah's readers should note carefully what is *not* in the sentence. No protest of manipulation, no appeal to the tempter's oath, no minimizing of the act. Adam and his wife do the one thing the previous episode established as decisive: they speak to God, and they tell the truth about themselves. The Book's verdict on that turning is given in the verse that follows in Sūrah al-Baqarah — *“Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”* — and the sūrah of the Heights will spend the rest of its length teaching the children of Adam to use the same door.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:24

> **He said, “Get down, each of you an enemy to the other! There will be for you on earth a dwelling place, and enjoyment for a while.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Descent: A Sentence That Is Also a Grant**

*Qāla ihbiṭū* — He said, get down. The command is plural, and the Qur'an's parallel accounts specify who is included. In al-Baqarah the instruction is to the pair, *“descend from the heavens as enemies to one another”* (2:36); in Ṭā-Hā it is expanded to include the tempter, *“Descend, both of you, from here together with Satan as enemies to each other”* (20:123). The sūrah of the Heights does not need the fuller statement; its readers have just watched the whole exchange.

The wording deserves attention because of what it does *not* say. There is no mention of a punishment being imposed. What is announced is a condition — the earth, a dwelling, a provision, a term — and everything inside that announcement is a good. The same verse that marks the end of the Garden records the beginning of the human world as the Qur'an will treat it in verse 10 above: a place made habitable, supplied, and governed by a schedule known to God. The fall is real; so is the provision that immediately accompanies it.

**"Each of You an Enemy to the Other"**

*Baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin ʿaduww* — some of you are enemies to others. The plural is broad and the commentators read it widely: Iblīs and the human race; the human being and his own kind; the human being and the demons; and, in the reading al-Qurṭubī reports, the two members of the couple with respect to a domestic strife the temptation had opened. The Book returns to this condition repeatedly, because it is the structure of life in the world the descent created: *“And We said, ‘Descend, as enemies to one another’”* (2:36); *“Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy”* (35:6); *“Do not follow Satan's footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy”* (2:168).

The verse therefore supplies the realistic premise for everything the sūrah will legislate afterwards. The Qur'an does not describe the earth as neutral ground. It describes it as a contested one, in which the human being lives with a declared opponent and his own appetites, and in which the guidance that arrives from God is the map. The sūrah's later chapters on food, clothing, worship and wealth make sense only against this premise.

**mustaqarr and matāʿ: Dwelling and Provision**

Two nouns are given for the earthly condition. *Mustaqarr* — a place of settlement, from the root *qarra*, to settle, to remain still — is the word the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the stability of created things: *“The sun travels for its fixed term”* (36:38); *“He has made the night and day, the sun and moon; each travelling in an orbit”* (21:33). The earth is given to the human being as something firm enough to build a life on. Beside it stands *matāʿ*, provision or enjoyment — with the nuance of a thing borrowed and used for a while, since the same word describes the goods of this life generally: *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting…”* (57:20).

The pairing is the sūrah's doctrine of the world in two words. It is a home and it is not a permanent one. Read without the second term, the verse produces worldliness; read without the first, it produces the kind of contempt for the earth that makes the human vocation unintelligible. The Book holds both: a place to settle, and a limit on the settlement.

**"Until a Time"**

*Ilā ḥīn* — until a time. The phrase is deliberately indefinite, and the indefiniteness is one of the Book's recurring devices: the term is fixed (*“When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it”*, 7:34) and unknown, and it is the unknown part that produces the moral seriousness of a life. The same phrase closes Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's description of the provision given to creatures: *“…as provision for a while”* (37:148; cf. 2:36).

The verse thus hands the reader the three coordinates within which everything in this sūrah will happen: *where* — a settled earth; *with what* — provision that can be received with gratitude or with greed; *for how long* — a term no one knows. It is the same framework verse 10 laid out at the beginning of the sūrah — *“We have established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood”* — now restated after the fall, which is to say: the fall did not withdraw the earlier gift.

**The Silence About the Garden**

One theological observation belongs at the end of this scene, and the commentators draw it out. The Qur'an never says that Adam and his wife were expelled from the Garden and forbidden to return. What it says is that they descended into a different mode of existence — that of testing — and that the guidance they were promised in the next verse (7:35) would determine where they ended. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same sequence with an explicit promise attached: *“Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (2:38).

The world, in other words, is not the wreckage of the Garden; it is the arena of the return. The sūrah's opening beats — the towns destroyed at night (vv. 4–5), the question and the weighing (vv. 6–9), the provision of verse 10 — have already laid out the terms, and the Adam narrative has now embedded them in the story of the first human beings. Every reader who continues into the sūrah's long confrontation with the Children of Israel is reading, in a sense, an extended commentary on this verse: what a settled creature does with a provision, in the time remaining, before the term arrives.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:25

> **He said, “Therein you shall live, and therein you shall die, and from there shall you be brought forth.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Clauses, One Place**

The verse is built on a single repeated pronoun. *Fīhā* — "therein", "in it" — governs all three clauses, and the antecedent is the earth named in the command to descend that precedes it. Life is in it, death is in it, and the coming forth is from it. Nothing in the sequence happens anywhere else.

That is the whole force of the construction, and it is easy to read past. The verse does not say that man will live, die and be raised; it says that all three will happen *in relation to one place*, and that the place is the same. The earth is not a corridor the human being passes through on the way to somewhere more important. It is the location of the beginning, the location of the end, and the location of the return.

> *"He said: Therein you shall live, and therein you shall die, and from there shall you be brought forth."*

The third clause changes its preposition deliberately. The first two use *fīhā*, in it; the third uses *minhā*, from it. Life and death are contained by the earth; the rising is an emergence out of it. The same ground that closes over a body is the ground that gives it back.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The command to descend in verse 24 is plural — *ihbiṭū*, get down, all of you — and al-Ṭabarī reads it as addressed to Adam and Eve, to their future progeny, and to Iblīs together. The enmity named in the same breath, *each of you an enemy to the other*, is therefore not only a statement about the human being and the tempter but about human beings among themselves.

The scope matters for what follows. Verse 13 had banished Iblīs from the Garden specifically: *Get down from it.* This command is wider — a descent from the celestial realm altogether — and it carries the whole human race with it. Verse 25 is what the descent leads to, and it is addressed to everyone who descended.

The verse is also a statement of the terms. Nothing is negotiated; nothing is offered back. The three clauses are declarative, and the human being is told where his life will run and where it will end before he has taken a step.

**A Fall Read as Mercy**

Some Sufi commentators read the whole sequence providentially, and even mercifully. Al-Jīlī and those who follow him note that the act of disobedience led to Adam's assumption of the vicegerency on earth, and that it opened a way of drawing near to God which is only available after distance has been experienced. Proximity that has never been lost is not the same thing as proximity recovered.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh, the eighth-/fourteenth-century Shādhilī master, put the principle in a single line of his *Ḥikam*:

> *"An act of disobedience that bequeaths humility and need is better than an act of obedience that bequeaths might and pride."* (Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh, al-Ḥikam 96)

The comparison is not between disobedience and obedience but between two outcomes. What is being weighed is what the act leaves in the soul. An obedience that produces self-sufficiency has lost the thing worship exists for; a fall that produces need has found it. On this reading the descent is not only a punishment but the condition of a perfection that was not reachable otherwise — because it requires repentance, and repentance requires something to return from.

The same reading gives the fall a second consequence, on God's side rather than man's. It occasions the manifestation of an attribute that innocence would never have disclosed. Forgiveness is only manifest where there is something to forgive.

**The Second Account**

This verse closes the second telling of the Adam narrative in the order of the muṣḥaf. The first is at 2:30–39; later accounts run at 17:61–65 and 20:115–24, and at 15:28–43 and 38:71–85 the same story is told of the first human being without naming him.

The repetition is not redundancy, and the differences are where the meaning lies. Each account closes differently, and this one closes on the earth: on where the story's descendants will actually live, die and rise. The telling in Sūrah Ṭā Hā closes on the warning not to forget; this one closes on a geography.

That is why the three clauses are worth taking as a unit rather than as a summary. The verse is the Qur'an's most compressed statement of the human condition — one place, three events, no exit from the sequence and no alternative venue for any of them.

**What the Sequence Forecloses**

The order is fixed and no stage can be skipped. The man who hopes to arrive at the being-brought-forth without passing through the dying has misunderstood the verse; the one who hopes to live somewhere other than the earth has misunderstood it too.

The practical weight of that is not morbid. A life lived on the assumption that it will end in the same ground it began in is a life in which the ground has a claim. The verse makes the earth a witness to the whole of a human career — present at the living, present at the dying, and the thing that hands the person back. It is not scenery.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:26

> **O Children of Adam! We have indeed sent down upon you raiment to cover your nakedness, and rich adornment. But the raiment of reverence, that is better. This is among the signs of God, that haply they may remember.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Garments in One Verse**

The verse that follows the fall turns immediately to clothing, and it does so with a wordplay only Arabic can carry. *Libās* is any covering worn on the body; *rīsh* is the plumage of a bird — the feathers that make a creature not merely covered but beautiful; and *libās al-taqwā* is a third garment, made of neither cloth nor skin. Human beings, in this verse, are given three layers: a cover for the body, an adornment for the eye, and a covering for the soul.

The sequence deserves to be read slowly, because the sūrah has just left a scene of leaves stitched together in panic (7:22). The first garment given to the first couple was God's; the leaves were their own attempt at replacement; and this verse announces that the original arrangement stands — covering itself is from God, *anzalnā*, We sent down.

**"Sent Down": Clothing as a Divine Gift**

The verb *anzalnā* is the same verb the Qur'an uses for rain, for the Book, and for iron (*“We sent down iron, in which is great might and benefits for people”*, 57:25). Clothing is placed in that company deliberately. The wool of sheep, the strength of flax, the growth of cotton, the shade of a tree — none of these were manufactured by the human being who wears them; what the human being contributes is knowledge of how to use what was supplied. The same point is made in Sūrah al-Naḥl, where garments and their purposes are traced to the Creator: *“He has provided you with clothing to protect you from the heat, and garments to protect you from your violence”* (16:81). For the mercantile Makkans, whose cloth trade was a livelihood, the verse quietly relocates the origin of their merchandise: the wealth of their caravans was borrowed.

**rīsh: The Case for Beauty**

The second garment is not a concession; it is named with affection. *Rīsh* is plumage, and the commentators record two overlapping readings. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as the adornment of this world — the fine clothing and the beauty that makes a person pleasing to look at. Others, following a lexically attested sense of the root *r-y-sh* (prosperity, the means by which one lives at ease), read it as provision and sufficiency. The two readings converge: God is not embarrassed by beauty, and He supplies the conditions for it.

This is the verse that Muslim tradition cites against a spirituality of self-neglect. The Prophet ﷺ, when a man came to him looking disheveled, remarked that God is beautiful and loves beauty, and that the display of God's favour upon a servant is part of what is due to Him. The Qur'an's own term for the good things of life is *ṭayyibāt*, and it treats their enjoyment as worship when gratitude accompanies it (2:172; 7:32). *rīsh* is the licence for that enjoyment, written into the earliest instruction given to the human race after the fall.

**libās al-taqwā: The Garment That Cannot Be Bought**

Then the verse introduces its comparative: *wa-libāsu al-taqwā dhālika khayr* — and the raiment of reverence, that is best. The *khayr* is not a dismissal of the earlier garments; it is a ranking. Cloth covers a body that will decay; *taqwā* covers a soul that will be presented to God. The Qur'an elsewhere names *taqwā* as the thing that survives when all provisions are counted: *“And take provision, but the best provision is taqwā”* (2:197). The metaphor works because it shares the function of clothing exactly. Taqwā hides what ought not to be seen — the faults, the appetite that has not yet been disciplined — and it protects its wearer from exposure. In the sūrah's own story, the first thing sin produced was exposure; the remedy offered to the children of Adam is a covering that cannot be lost by taste or theft.

**A Sign to Be Remembered**

The verse closes by naming itself a sign: *that haply they may remember*. And the reason clothing functions as a sign is anthropological. Human beings are the only creatures in the world whose dignity depends on covering; a horse needs no garment, a bird is already dressed. The nakedness that shamed Adam and his wife was not a bodily defect but a moral fact — the loss of the covering that God had given. Every garment a person puts on is therefore a reminder, whether he notices or not, that he is a being with a shame to guard and a Lord who provided for it.

That is why the verse ends with memory rather than command. The argument is not that clothing is obligatory; it is that clothing is evidence. A person who sees the evidence will not spend the rest of his life treating his outer covering as though it were the whole story, or (as the next passage will warn) treating the loss of God's command as a small thing because the world is warm and there are leaves enough.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:27

> **O Children of Adam! Let not Satan tempt you, as he caused your parents to go forth from the Garden, stripping them of their raiment to show them their nakedness. Surely he sees you — he and his tribe — whence you see them not. We have indeed made the satans the friends of those who do not believe.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Repeated to the Children**

The verse opens with the same address as the one before it — *O children of Adam* — and it is not a repetition of the earlier instruction but a repetition of the earlier *danger*. God had warned Adam directly: *“This is surely an enemy to you and to your wife”* (20:117). Now the warning passes to the sons: *do not let Satan tempt you, as he caused your parents to go forth from the Garden.*

The structure is the Book's model of education. Information is not the shortage; the shortage is attention. The Qur'an therefore tells the story of the first fall not once but many times, from different angles, in different sūrahs, and returns to it here immediately after clothing and reverence have been discussed. The sūrah's reader has just been told what the best garment is; he is now told who is most interested in removing it.

**"Stripping Them of Their Raiment"**

*Yanziʿu ʿanhumā libāsahumā* — he strips them of their garment, to show them their nakedness. The verb *nazaʿa* is the vocabulary of forcible removal: to pull off, to take away, to dispossess. The Qur'an's account is unsparing about the aim of the enemy. He does not merely want a particular act performed; he wants the removal of protection, so that the shame can be displayed.

That aim has not changed in the slightest. The temptations of every generation are refinements of the same project: to get a person to loosen the covering of reverence — one verse, one appetite, one compromise at a time — and then to leave him standing exposed in the middle of his own garden, sewing whatever there is at hand. The Arabic word for the result, *sawʾāt*, is the same in this verse and in 7:20 and 7:22, tying the earlier scene to this warning as a single, repeated phenomenon.

**The Asymmetry of Vision**

Then comes the sentence that makes the whole passage practical: *innahu yarākum huwa wa-qabīluhu min ḥaythu lā tarawanahum* — surely he sees you, he and his tribe, from where you do not see them. The word *qabīl* is kinship language: a clan, a tribe, an organized body. The jinn are described as a community with forces, and the Qur'an elsewhere names their orders and ranks — infantry and cavalry, incursion and collaboration (17:64) — and the four-sided approach of 7:17.

The verse thus removes from the reader the comfort of an even fight. The opponent can observe the human being's movements and habits; the human being cannot observe him. And this is precisely why the sūrah's remedy for the ambush is not intelligence-gathering but refuge — *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah”* (7:200) — and why the Book supplies the missing visibility by naming the enemy's techniques in advance. What the human being cannot see, God has described.

The hadith tradition handles the same reality with a metaphor that has stayed in the Muslim imagination: *“Satan circulates in the human being as blood circulates in him”* (al-Bukhārī 3281, from Ṣafiyyah). The Prophet ﷺ said it while warning one of his wives that the enemy might throw a suggestion into her heart — a remark that tells the reader this is not a subject for those who consider themselves immune. The nearness is the point: no wall, no distance, no rank provides separation. What protects is not insulation but vigilance, and beside it, the divine word that names the danger.

**"We Have Made the Satans the Friends of Those Who Disbelieve"**

The final clause is the verse's theology. *Innā jaʿalnā al-shayāṭīn awliyāʾa li-lladhīna lā yuʾminūn* — We have appointed the devils as allies for those who do not believe. *Walī* is the nearest of relations: protector, patron, the one to whom loyalty is given and from whom protection is expected. The Qur'an uses the term for the divine guardianship over the believers (*“Allah is the Protector of those who believe”*, 2:257) and here for its inversion.

The construction *jaʿalnā*, We made, has drawn the attention of theologians: is this a decree or a consequence? The Book's usage answers by holding the two together. The alliance is a settlement that the person's own movement has produced — the same law stated in Sūrah al-Zukhruf, *“And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each one a devil as a companion”* (43:36) — and once settled, it is administered by God's decree. Responsibility is not dissolved by the fact that the outcome was decreed; the human being, not God, chose the road that led to the companion.

The verse therefore closes with the plainest of statements. There are two guardianships available to a human being, and the choice of the first is the choice of the enemy of the second. Everything else in the sūrah's long confrontation with its two audiences happens inside that framework.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:28

> **When they commit an indecency, they say, “We found our fathers practicing it, and God has commanded us thus.” Say, “Truly God commands not indecency. Do you say of God that which you know not?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument from the Fathers**

*Wa-idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan qālū wajadnā ʿalayhā ābāʾanā* — when they commit an indecency, they say, we found our fathers doing it. The verse is a portrait of a religious mentality, drawn from life. The people in question are not denying the practice; they are defending it, and their defense rests on inheritance. The practice is old, the ancestors did it, therefore it stands.

The commentators record the occasion the first audience would have recognized. Reports transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and repeated by Ibn Kathīr describe the pre-Islamic Arabs circumambulating the House naked — men by day and women by night — and describing their nakedness as a religious act, on the reasoning that one should not perform the circuit in clothes in which one had sinned. Other reports attach the verse to particular customs of the tribes. Whichever specific practice is intended, the sūrah is addressing a general habit of mind, and it names it with precision: an act that is objectively shameful, defended by the fact that it is customary.

**Taqlīd: The Qur'an's Sustained Objection**

This is not an isolated complaint. The Qur'an returns to the ancestral argument again and again and rejects it in nearly identical words: *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We only follow what we found our forefathers practicing.’ Even if their forefathers had no understanding and no guidance?”* (2:170; cf. 5:104; 31:21; 43:22–24). The rejoinder in al-Baqarah is the sūrah's own rejoinder here, sharpened into a question about the competence of the ancestors to be followed.

The point is not hostility to tradition. Islam is, in its own self-description, the religion of Abraham and of the prophets before Muhammad ﷺ, and the sūrah will spend a great part of its length appealing to the inheritance of the Children of Israel. What the Book rejects is not inherited practice but inherited practice used *as a proof*. A custom can be a legitimate vehicle of a genuine command, but its antiquity is not the reason the command binds; the command binds because God commanded it, and the command must be traceable to Him. The moment a practice is defended by the fact that it is ancestral, it has been removed from the jurisdiction of revelation altogether.

**The Worse Claim: "God Has Commanded Us Thus"**

The verse's second clause escalates the error. *Wa-llāhu amaranā bihā* — and God has commanded us thus. To follow one's fathers is a mistake; to attribute the following to a divine command is a fabrication about God. The distinction is one the sūrah will make explicit three verses later, when it lists what God has actually forbidden (7:33), and it explains why this verse's answer is framed as a flat denial: *qul inna Allāha lā yaʾmuru bi-l-faḥshāʾ* — say, God does not command indecency.

The principle underneath is fundamental to Islamic theology. God's commands are not arbitrary; they are consistent with His names and His revealed law. A command to commit what revelation names *fāḥishah* — an obscenity, a thing the sound conscience recoils from — cannot come from God, because God has already declared such things forbidden in the Book and in the innate disposition He created (7:157; 16:90; 30:30). Any claim of a divine command that contradicts the divine law has just refuted itself. This is the criterion the tradition later used to sift an enormous body of purported reports and rulings, and it begins here.

**The Epistemic Sins**

The verse closes with the question that gives the passage its teeth: *a-taqūlūna ʿalā Allāhi mā lā taʿlamūn* — do you say of God what you do not know?

Speaking about God without knowledge is treated in this sūrah as a distinct and grave category of offense. Verse 33 will list it among the four things God has forbidden — indecencies, sin, wrongful aggression, and *“that you associate with Allah that for which He has sent down no authority, and that you say of Allah what you do not know.”* Sūrah al-Baqarah warns that the same enemy who incites indecency also incites *“that you claim against Allah what you do not know”* (2:169), and Sūrah al-Naḥl condemns the fabrication of permissions and prohibitions by the tongue alone: *“Do not falsely declare with your tongues, ‘This is lawful, and that is unlawful,’ fabricating lies against Allah”* (16:116).

The reason for the severity is that this particular error cannot be corrected by information. A person who follows an inherited custom can be shown the text; a person who reports God's own opinion on the matter has placed himself beyond the reach of correction, because he has made his claim unimpeachable by construction. The verse therefore asks the one question that can reopen the case: *how do you know?* It is a question the Muslim tradition turned into a discipline — the science of *isnād*, of chains of transmission, of the scrutiny of claims attributed to God and His Prophet — and the chapter that legislates it is this one.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:29

> **Say, “My Lord has commanded justice. So set your faces [toward Him] at every place of prayer, and call upon Him, devoting religion entirely to Him. Just as He originated you, so shall you return.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer in Four Movements**

After refuting the argument from the fathers, the sūrah gives the answer: *qul amara rabbī bi-l-qisṭ* — say, my Lord has commanded justice. The command to answer is itself significant. Verse 28 ended with a lie about God; verse 29 begins with a command to speak, and what is put in the Prophet's mouth is not a counter-argument but a summary of what God actually requires. The refutation of a fabricated law is the real law, stated plainly.

The verse then unfolds in four movements, and each deserves its own attention: justice, orientation in worship, sincerity, and the return.

**Justice First**

The first item is *qisṭ*, and the choice of the word is deliberate. Arabic has several words for justice — *ʿadl* for balance and equity, *ḥukm* for judgment, *qisṭ* for the fair share that is due. *Qisṭ* is the word of the scale, and its use here, in a sūrah that has just placed a balance on the Day of Judgment (7:8–9), ties the ethics of this life to the accounting of the next. Justice is not one virtue among many in the Qur'an; it is the first thing named when a prophet is asked to describe what his Lord commands. The Book reinforces this repeatedly: *“Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, and courtesy to close relatives”* (16:90); *“when you judge between people, judge with fairness”* (4:58); *“Be just — that is closer to righteousness”* (5:8), the verse that commands justice even toward those one hates.

The placement matters for the sūrah's argument. Its audience was a trading city whose religious life had been handed down by custom, and the Qur'an's first answer to inherited religion is not a ritual but a moral standard. God commands what is fair. Any tradition, however venerable, that has licensed unfairness has failed the first test of revelation.

**Faces Set Toward Him**

*Wa-aqīmū wujūhakum ʿinda kulli masjid* — and set your faces at every place of worship. The verb *aqāma* is from *q-w-m*, the root of standing upright, and the Qur'an uses it for establishing prayer (*aqāma al-ṣalāh*) throughout. The commentators give several readings of the phrase. Al-Ṭabarī takes it as a command to direct oneself wholly to God in every act of worship, whatever place one is in. Others read it as a statement about consistency: at every mosque, in every prayer, in every gathering, let the face be set — not turned now to one loyalty and now to another. The word *wajh*, face, in the Qur'an's idiom carries the sense of the whole directed self, which is why *aslamtu wajhī* means not "I turned my face" but "I submitted myself" (2:112; 3:20).

The instruction answers the polytheist practice of the previous verse with an argument by contrast. They came to the House in a manner that was neither dignified nor directed; the command is to arrive with the whole person oriented to God. The place of prayer is not a stage for performance but a point of alignment.

**Sincerity as the Definition of Religion**

*Wa-dʿūhu mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — and call upon Him, sincere to Him in religion. The phrase *mukhliṣīn lahu al-dīn* recurs through the Book as the definition of the religion Abraham founded — in the call of 10:22 and 29:65, in the supplication of 31:32, and in the two addresses of 40:14 and 40:65 — and it is given its final summary in Sūrat al-Bayyinah: *“And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion, inclining to truth, and to establish prayer and give alms; and that is the upright religion”* (98:5).

The doctrine of *ikhlāṣ* rests on this phrase. The Arabic root *kh-l-ṣ* carries the sense of being free of mixture, and in the terminology of the tradition it means that an act has been purified of every motive except the pleasure of God. The Qur'an's own definition of the failure is instructive: a person who prays to God in difficulty and forgets Him in ease has not purified anything (10:22). The command therefore polices the interior of worship — the very thing a religion of inherited custom never examined, because custom can be performed without any interior at all.

**"As He Originated You, So Shall You Return"**

The verse closes with the resurrection, and the argument it uses is one of the Qur'an's most persistent: the appeal to the beginning as proof of the return. *Kamā badaʾakum taʿūdūn.* Sūrat al-Rūm states it with the reasoning spelled out — *“He is the One Who originates the creation then resurrects it — which is even easier for Him”* (30:27) — and the same proof is pressed in 21:104, 29:19, 27:64, and 10:4. Al-Anbiyāʾ puts it as a promise: *“Just as We produced the first creation, so shall We reproduce it”* (21:104). And when the Makkans posed the challenge in its hardest form, asking who would restore bones to life, the answer given was the same: *“He will bring them to life Who created them the first time”* (17:51).

There is a second layer in the phrase, and the sūrah exploits it immediately. *As He originated you* — the human being began as nothing, without property, without name, without a tribe. The verse is a reminder that this is also how the account will be resumed: as the Qur'an says elsewhere, *“you have come to Us alone as We created you the first time”* (6:94), and *“you will come to Us individually, as We created you the first time”* (18:48). Justice in this life, worship in this life, sincerity in this life — and then a return to the condition in which one began, where all that will distinguish a person is what he earned by those three things and not what his fathers did before him.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:30

> **Some He has rightly guided, and some are deserving of error. Truly they took satans as their protectors apart from God and deem them rightly guided.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Parties on One Road**

*Farīqan hadā wa-farīqan ḥaqqat ʿalayhimu al-ḍalālah.* The verse divides humanity into two groups, and the two verbs are not symmetrical. For the first, guidance is something done *to* them — *hadā*, He guided, with God as the subject. For the second, straying is something that has *settled upon* them — *ḥaqqat*, from a root meaning to be due, to fall upon a person as a right or a consequence.

The asymmetry is the Qur'an's careful position on one of its most discussed questions. Guidance is an act of God's grace; misguidance is a settled consequence. The Book never says that God "made a party stray" in the way it says He guided a party; at most it says He *left* them, *turned away* from them, or *did not guide* them — and it never allows these statements to be detached from the condition of those concerned. Sūrat al-Ṣaff states the sequence explicitly: *“So when they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate”* (61:5). The deviation of the heart is a recompense, not a cause. The same law appears in 2:26, where straying happens to *“the rebellious”*; in 6:125, where the chest of the one left astray is constricted because of what he chose; and in 42:44, where the one left astray has no protector after Him.

The sūrah's readers are not being told that God arbitrarily assigns fates. They are being told that after a certain point, a direction hardens into a destiny. The verse states it in the past tense of a settled condition, and the whole of the passage that surrounds it — the ancestral argument, the lie against God, the warning about the enemy — is a description of how the settlement is reached.

**"They Took Satans as Protectors Instead of God"**

The cause is then named: *ittakhadhū al-shayāṭīn awliyāʾa min dūni Allāh*. The construction *min dūni Allāh* — apart from, to the exclusion of God — is the Qur'an's standard formula for the displaced loyalty that constitutes *shirk*. The verse is not speaking of a formal cult of the jinn, though such cults existed and are addressed elsewhere (72:6; 6:100). It is speaking of the appointment of a guardian: the habit of turning to something other than God for protection, validation, and direction — an appetite, a patron, a custom, an ideology, an inner voice.

The commentators note how this connects to the verse two lines earlier. There the divine act was named — *We made the satans the friends of those who disbelieve* (7:27) — and here the human act is named: *they took* them as protectors. The two are one event seen from the two sides of the same covenant, and the Book keeps both because neither alone tells the truth about responsibility. God's decree and the human being's choice meet in the same act, and the person's accountability survives the encounter.

The second sūrah of the Qur'an states the outcome of the two guardianships in one verse: *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers — He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire, where they will stay forever”* (2:257). The guidance of verse 30 is simply the first half of that verse in practice.

**"And They Think They Are Guided"**

Then the clause that makes the verse frightening: *wa-hum yaḥsabūna annahum muhtadūn*. Not merely lost — lost while convinced of the opposite. The Qur'an treats this as a distinct condition with its own vocabulary. Sūrat al-Kahf describes people *“whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good”* (18:104). Sūrat Fāṭir asks the question the sūrah of the Heights has been building toward: *“Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good like those who are rightly guided?”* (35:8). And Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn shows it operating at the social level: a group swearing oaths of loyalty while the Qur'an names them liars (63:1; 58:18).

The psychology of this sentence is the reason the verse pairs it with the ancestral argument of 7:28 and the enemy of 7:27. There are three roads into the same ditch: blaming one's failure on God, as Iblīs did; defending a practice because one's fathers did it; and taking the enemy as a protector while believing one has taken God. The common feature of all three is that none of them feels like error from inside. It is the settled condition, not the momentary sin, that the verse describes — and it is why the sūrah keeps returning to the criterion of revelation rather than the criterion of feeling.

**The Reflection That Is Owed**

The verse's placement at the end of the sūrah's opening argument gives it a pastoral function. Having just described the two parties, the Qur'an does not tell the reader which one he is in; it hands him the instruments to find out. The rest of the sūrah — the answer of verse 29, the prohibition of verse 33, the stories of the nations that follow — is the criterion applied.

The third sūrah of the Qur'an states the point about misguidance and its victims in one line that preserves hope: *“Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us”* (3:8). The Prophet ﷺ taught the same supplication in his own practice: *“O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion.”* The verse before us explains why such a prayer is necessary. Certainty is not the same as guidance, and the person who has never asked God to keep his heart has no guarantee that his own confidence is anything more than the settled state of a misdirected road.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:31

> **O Children of Adam! Put on your adornment at every place of worship, and eat and drink, but be not prodigal. Truly He loves not the prodigal.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Be Beautiful in Worship**

*Khudhū zīnatakum ʿinda kulli masjid* — take your adornment at every place of worship. The verse answers the previous two by legislation. Verse 28 had condemned an act defended as ancestral; verse 29 had stated what God actually commands; now this verse supplies the practice that fills the gap. The sūrah turns what the polytheists had inverted into a command: they came to the House stripped, and the Qur'an instructs the believers to come dressed, deliberately and well.

The commentators record the occasions this addresses. Al-Ṭabarī transmits reports that the pre-Islamic Arabs would circumambulate the Kaʿbah naked — men by day, women by night — with a rationale drawn from the world of the jinn and from the idea that one should not circle the House in garments in which one had sinned. Other reports attach the verse to the Jews and Christians, who had forbidden themselves certain good food and certain clothing in ascetic observance, and to those among the Arabs who had dedicated certain of their livestock and crops and would not eat or wear them. The command answers all of them at once: what God has made lawful is lawful, and the place of worship is precisely where the gifts of God should be brought into view.

**Worship and Dignity**

The word *zīnah* is the same word used in 7:32, where God defends the adornments He has brought forth for His servants — the two verses are a pair, one granting and one commanding. Taken together they form the Qur'an's refusal of two opposite errors. The first is the ascetic error: the notion that God is honored by the rejection of the good things He gave, that piety is measured in shabbiness and self-denial. The second is the error of the naked circumambulation, where religion was used to strip away the very covering God provided.

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the middle path. He wore clean clothes, disliked disorder in appearance, and told a man who came looking unkempt that God loves to see the traces of His favour upon His servants. His guidance on arriving for prayer was cleanliness of body and garment, the use of scent for the assembly, and a deliberate attention to appearance that distinguished the Muslim gathering from a performance of poverty. That is the practice this verse legislates: the place of worship is where a human being presents himself before his Lord, and the presentation is part of the worship.

**"Eat and Drink, But Do Not Be Prodigal"**

The second half of the verse turns from dress to appetite, and it does so with the same reasoning. *Kulū wa-shrabū wa-lā tusrifū* — eat and drink, and do not exceed. The permission comes first, in the imperative, and it is not qualified by any condition of poverty of spirit. The prohibition that follows is not against enjoyment but against excess, and the Qur'an's term for that excess is *isrāf*, from the root *s-r-f*: to go beyond the limit, to pour water past the brim.

The Book repeats the prohibition as a settled principle: *“He is the One Who produces gardens… eat of their fruit when they bear, and give its due on the day of harvest, and do not waste — indeed, He does not like the wasteful”* (6:141); *“Do not spend wastefully; surely the wasteful are like brothers to the devils, and the Devil is ever ungrateful to his Lord”* (17:26–27); *“They are those who spend neither wastefully nor stingily, but moderately in between”* (25:67). The last verse describes the servants of the All-Merciful, and the standard it gives — neither the open hand of the wastrel nor the clenched fist of the miser — is the balance this sūrah has been teaching since it placed a scale on the Day of Judgment.

**The Hadith of the Stomach**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the ethic of 7:31 its most concrete expression. From Miqdām ibn Maʿdī Karib, he said: *“The human being does not fill any vessel worse than his stomach. It is enough for the son of Adam to eat a few morsels to keep his back straight. But if he must eat more, then a third for his food, a third for his drink, and a third for his breath.”* (al-Tirmidhī 2380; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). The tradition read this hadith as a rule of bodily discipline with spiritual consequences: the full stomach produces a sluggish heart, and the appetite that is never denied becomes the door through which every other appetite enters.

The verse's final clause makes the prohibition theological rather than hygienic: *innahu lā yuḥibbu al-musrifīn* — He does not love the extravagant. The Quranic formula of God's love and its absence is the Book's way of ranking conduct on the scale of relationship rather than of legality. Extravagance is not merely unwise; it is the kind of conduct a person engages in when he has stopped seeing the One who provided. That is why the verse appears where it does, after a chapter on provisioning (7:10), a chapter on covering (7:26), and a chapter on the enemy who strips cover away (7:27). Ingratitude, exposure, excess — three names for the same misplacement — and the remedy offered is one: to take, deliberately and gratefully, what God has sent down, and not one measure more.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:32

> **Say, “Who has forbidden the adornment of God, which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things among [His] provision?” Say, “These are for those who believe, in the life of this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they are for them alone.” Thus do We expound the signs for a people who know.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Indicts the Questioner**

*Man ḥarrama zīnata Allāh* — who has forbidden the adornment of God? The construction is a question, but its force is that of a challenge, because the answer it invites is silence. No one in the sūrah's audience could claim a divine warrant for the prohibitions they had invented, and the verse makes the absence of a warrant visible by asking for it in public.

The Qur'an is careful with what follows. The adornment is not described as a human possession; it is *Allāh's* adornment, *allatī akhraja li-ʿibādihi* — which He brought out for His servants. The possessive construction repeats in two directions: the beauty belongs to God, and its purpose is the welfare of the people who serve Him. To forbid it is not austerity but expropriation — the removal of a gift from the beneficiaries God Himself designated.

**The Long History of Pious Prohibition**

The verse is addressing a pattern, not a single practice. The commentators attach it to several: the Arabs who set aside certain livestock and crops as *baḥīrah*, *sāʾibah*, and *waṣīlah*, declaring them forbidden by religious vow (5:103; 6:138–139); the People of the Book who forbade themselves foods that God had never forbidden (3:93; 4:160; 6:146); and the ascetics, referred to in the Qur'an's own words about monasticism: *“They invented monasticism which We had not prescribed for them, seeking the pleasure of Allah, but they did not observe it as it should have been observed”* (57:27).

The Book's attitude to all of them is consistent. Self-denial is not a virtue in itself, and legislation that God did not issue is not worship but a claim upon His authority. The Prophet ﷺ made the principle explicit when he heard that some of his companions had resolved to fast continuously, to stand all night in prayer, and to abstain from marriage: he refused to have anything to do with such a discipline and said, *“But I fast and break the fast, I pray and sleep, and I marry — so whoever turns away from my practice is not of me”* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401). The response is not a rebuke of devotion; it is the rejection of devotion conducted by a law that God did not write.

**"For the Believers in the Life of This World, and for Them Alone in the Hereafter"**

The verse's second half is a statement about ownership, and it answers a question the first audience disputed. Some had argued that the good things of the world were destined for the believers alone, or that non-Muslims enjoyed them illegitimately; others held the opposite, that the Hereafter offered something thinner than the pleasures of the present life. The verse settles both: *khāliṣatan lahum yawma al-qiyāmah* — on the Day of Resurrection they are exclusively for them.

Al-Ṭabarī records the readings. The dominant one is that in this world the good things are shared — the rain falls on everyone, the fields of the ungrateful yield as well as those of the grateful — and in the Hereafter the good things will be pure, reserved, and without the mixing that characterizes a world in which the righteous and the wicked live side by side. This is what the Book means elsewhere when it says of the Garden that it contains *“what no eye has seen, no ear has heard”* and that its people *“will have whatever they desire”* (41:31; 50:35). The promise is not that the believer will be repaid in kind; it is that the enjoyment of the good will finally be untroubled.

**The Sentence "a People Who Know"**

The verse closes: *Thus do We expound the signs for a people who know.* The phrase is not decorative. The dispute about permitted and forbidden is, in the Qur'an's analysis, an epistemic dispute: it arises where knowledge has been replaced by custom, fear, or self-importance. Al-Rāzī makes the point sharply: the question of what God has permitted is answered by revelation, and where revelation has spoken, no further authority may add to it.

This is also why the Islamic legal tradition built its discipline of the lawful and unlawful on evidence rather than on taste — and why the verse remains a warning for religious communities in every century, including Muslim ones. A practice can become forbidden in the popular mind with no verse and no hadith behind it, until abstaining becomes the test of piety and the God-given table is emptied by human decree. The Qur'an's question stands at the door of every such development: *who* has forbidden it, and on whose authority?

**Two Pieties the Verse Rejects by Name**

The question *who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought out for His servants?* answers two opposite errors, and the Book answers each of them elsewhere in its own terms. The first is the piety that treats the good things of the world as inherently defiling: Sūrat al-Māʾidah commands, *“Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors”* (5:87), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf itself had laid the foundation two verses earlier: *“Eat and drink, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful”* (7:31). The second error is the licence that reads the verse as permission for excess, and the same clause rules it out: the lawful is *ṭayyib*, good, and the Book's command is to eat it with gratitude (2:172; 16:114), not to gorge on it.

The Prophet ﷺ settled the first error with a scene that has become the standard commentary on this verse. Anas ibn Mālik reports that three men came to the houses of the Prophet's wives asking about his worship; when it was described to them, they treated it as insufficient and each vowed an extreme: prayer all night, fasting without a break, and celibacy. The Prophet came out and said, *“Are you the ones who said such and such? By Allah, I am the most God-fearing and pious among you, yet I fast and I break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me”* (al-Bukhārī 5063; Muslim 1401).

The sūrah's case is doctrinal rather than merely disciplinary, and its final clause states it: the good things of God are *for those who believe during the life of this world, exclusively for them on the Day of Resurrection*. The believer's enjoyment of what God permits is not a concession to weakness, and it is not shared with the Hereafter, where it becomes his alone — a reallocation that quietly reverses the whole economy of Makkah, whose notables had assumed that the pleasant things of God were theirs in this world and would remain theirs in the next.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:33

> **Say, “My Lord has only forbidden indecencies — both outward and inward — and sin, and tyranny without right, and that you should ascribe partners unto God, for which He has sent down no authority, and that you should say of God that which you know not.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counter-List**

Verse 33 answers the question of verse 32 by supplying the actual prohibitions: *qul innamā ḥarrama rabbī* — say, my Lord has only forbidden. The *innamā* is a tool of restriction: this list, and nothing but this list, is what the divine prohibition contains. The verse is therefore simultaneously a prohibition and a defence of liberty — an authoritative statement that whatever lies outside these categories has not been forbidden, and that no one may place it under interdict on God's behalf.

**The Five Forbidden Things**

The list that follows has a considered order.

*Al-fawāḥish* — indecencies, *mā ẓahara minhā wa-mā baṭan*: what is open of them and what is hidden. The word *fāḥishah* covers sexual immorality in the broadest Qur'anic sense — the act that is shameful by nature and that every sound tradition has recognized as such. The phrase *outward and inward* is the verse's own insistence that the prohibition is not merely about conduct. It reaches the intention, the imagination, the private arrangement, and the intention that never reaches conduct. The Book repeats the same pairing elsewhere — *“Abandon the outward and the inward sin”* (6:120) — and the Prophet ﷺ summarized the whole doctrine in a single sentence: *“God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but at your hearts and your deeds”* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah).

*Wa-l-ithm* — and sin. Commentators differ on the specific extension: some read it as the class of sins generally, others as intoxicants by a well-attested usage of *ithm* in the Arabic of the Qur'an (cf. 2:219; 5:90), others as the inward malice that leads to wrong. The placement between indecency and aggression is instructive, because it is the middle term: an act whose harm is not bodily, whose shame may be unacknowledged, and which lies between the violation of the self and the violation of others.

*Wa-l-baghy bi-ghayr al-ḥaqq* — and wrongful aggression, transgression without right. *Baghy* is the overstepping of a boundary against another person: the seizure of what is not one's own, the abuse of power, the arrogant exercise of superiority. The qualifier *without right* is a reminder that not every use of force is aggression; the verse acknowledges a legitimate claim without defining it here, and the sūrah's later legal passages will. The Qur'an ranks *baghy* among the most serious of social sins, and the Islamic tradition identified it as the characteristic sin of the powerful.

*Wa-an tushrikū bi-Allāh mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānā* — and that you associate with Allah that for which He has sent down no authority. The word is *sulṭān*: evidence, warrant, authorization. The Qur'an does not merely say that idolatry is incomplete; it says that the classes of things worshiped besides God are, without exception, unsupported by any evidence. This is a challenge to produce a warrant, and it is issued repeatedly in the Book with the same confidence (10:68; 16:116; 34:22; 46:4).

*Wa-an taqūlū ʿalā Allāh mā lā taʿlamūn* — and that you say of God what you do not know. The list closes with the offense the sūrah has already met: the invented religious claim of verse 28. Its placement last, after the great sins, is a deliberate ranking. Speech about God without knowledge is the root from which fabricated laws, corrupted religion, and misdirected worship grow; it is the sin that makes all the others possible to legislate.

**The Parallel Lists**

This verse sits inside one of the Qur'an's most systematic treatments of the divine moral law. Sūrat al-Anʿām gives the longer version: *“Come, let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden to you: do not associate others with Him; be kind to parents; do not kill your children for fear of poverty; do not come near the indecencies, whether open or hidden; do not kill the soul God has made sacred except by right…”* (6:151), continuing through the orphan's property, honest measure, and truthful speech (6:152). Sūrat al-Isrāʾ gives a third version in a passage of some thirty verses (17:22–39). The three passages are usually read together by scholars, who observe that the Qur'an restates the same moral core in different contexts — which itself tells the reader that the essentials of the divine law are stable, known, and not adaptable by the preferences of a generation.

**What the Verse Does with Guilt**

One practical effect of the list deserves to be named. By restricting the divine prohibition to named categories, the verse liberates the conscience from invented burden. People who live under a religion of unnumbered prohibitions — the anxious, the scrupulous, those who fear that pleasure itself is a trespass — are given here an authoritative short list and told that the graces God brought out for His servants are theirs to enjoy.

But the list also closes a door in the other direction. A person may not conclude that the moral life is exhausted by avoiding the five categories; the same sūrah is full of commands, and the Book contains hundreds of others. What the verse establishes is a jurisdictional limit: the right to declare a thing forbidden belongs to God, exercised through revelation; and the one who exercises it in God's name without knowledge has placed himself, by this verse's own account, inside the list.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:34

> **And for every community there is a term appointed. When their term comes, they shall not delay it by a single hour, nor shall they advance it.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Law Written into History**

*Wa-li-kulli ummatin ajal* — and for every community there is a term appointed. Having closed the list of what God has forbidden, the sūrah returns to the theme it opened with: the destroyed towns of verses 4 and 5, the account of verses 6 and 9. The verse supplies the principle behind those scenes. The nations of the past were not struck at random; each had a span, and each span ended.

The pairing of the two nouns deserves attention. *Ummah* is not merely a political unit; the word comes from *amm*, to head toward, and it names a body of people oriented by a shared direction — a community following a way. *Ajal* is the term set for a debt, the moment at which a thing's time has run out. The two together describe something the Qur'an treats as a fixed feature of the created order: communities have a lifetime as surely as individuals do, and their orientation determines what fills it.

**The Same Sentence, Repeated in the Book**

The verse's second half is one of the most repeated formulas in the Qur'an: *lā yastaʾkhirūna sāʿatan wa-lā yastaqdimūn*. It appears in the sūrah of Yūnus, in Sūrat al-Naḥl, in al-Muʾminūn, and elsewhere with the same insistence (10:49; 16:61; 23:43; cf. 7:34; 34:30). The repetition is not a stylistic habit; the Book repeats what its audience is most inclined to forget.

*Sāʿah* means an hour, and the verse's point is its smallness: not that a community may not receive an extension of years, but that not even an hour can be negotiated. The two verbs are the crucial ones — *yastaʾkhirūn*, to put off, and *yastaqdimūn*, to bring forward. The first closes the hope of the procrastinator, who assumes that the deadline can be renegotiated. The second closes the door on the anxious, who fears that his hour may come too soon and who tries to make his arrangements as though he could hurry or delay the decree. Neither is possible, and the verse's grammar states the symmetry exactly: the term is neither postponed nor advanced by a single hour.

Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn restates the law at the level of the individual soul: *“Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes”* (63:11). And Sūrat Āl ʿImrān says the same thing from the opposite angle, as a comfort: *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time”* (3:145) — no one dies early, no one is cheated of a moment that was his.

**What the Verse Is Doing in This Place**

The placement is deliberate. The preceding verses listed the forbidden things; the verses that follow describe the messengers who come to warn and the fate of those who deny them (7:35–36). Between the law and the warning, the sūrah inserts the fact of the term. The logic is the logic of the whole chapter: the warning is urgent because the time in which it can be answered is not unlimited.

Al-Ṭabarī notes a second function of the verse here. The sūrah's original audience was watching the Prophet's community grow and calculating its odds in the terms of Quraysh's own experience — a new movement in a city of established powers. The verse tells them what the history of the nations of the Book had already demonstrated: no community is destroyed before its term, and no community survives past it. The Qurayshi leaders who dismissed Muhammad ﷺ as a passing fever were themselves a community with a term, and so, for that matter, was the community of the believers.

**The Use of the Doctrine**

The Islamic tradition drew two practical conclusions from this verse and its parallels. The first is that urgency belongs to the individual and not to the world. A person cannot know whether his own term will end today; the Book's counsel is that this uncertainty is the structure of moral seriousness, which is why it says that *no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow* (31:34) and *no soul knows in what land it will die* (31:34). The Prophet ﷺ reinforced it with an instruction reported from Ibn ʿUmar: *“Be in this world as a stranger or a traveler”* (al-Bukhārī 6416) — a saying that turns the doctrine of the term into a way of carrying one's possessions.

The second conclusion is the patience it produces in communal life. A fixed term means that the rise and fall of nations is administered by a decree rather than by the maneuverings that seem to decide events. The sūrah's readers were told, in effect, that they did not need to worry about the calendar of history — only about what they did inside the hours assigned to them. The whole of the Book's attitude toward political defeat, economic decline, and the appearance of the enemies' triumph rests on this verse's arithmetic. There is no hour to be regained from the past and none to be borrowed from the future. What exists is the present term, and the account that will be taken when it closes.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:35

> **O Children of Adam! Should there come unto you messengers from among yourselves, recounting My signs unto you, then whosoever is reverent and makes amends, no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Messengers From Among You**

The verse returns to the address that has organized this whole passage: *O children of Adam.* And this time the subject is the arrival of revelation in human form: *immā yaʾtiyannakum rusulun minkum* — should there come to you messengers from among yourselves. The phrase *min anfusikum*, from your own selves, is the sūrah's standing answer to the Makkans' most persistent objection.

That objection is recorded in the Qur'an's own words in at least four places. The Makkans said, *“What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places?”* (25:7). They said that a messenger should be accompanied by an angel (17:92–93). They asked why the Qur'an had not been given to some great man of influence (43:31). The Book's answer is consistent and this verse is part of it: the messenger is *from among you*, because a revelation addressed to human beings must reach them through a channel that shares their condition. Sūrah Yūsuf states the underlying principle more than once: *“We have not sent before you except men to whom We gave revelation”* (12:109; 16:43; 21:7).

The wisdom is worth spelling out. A messenger who was not one of us could not demonstrate that the life he demands is livable; his purity would be a fact about a different kind of being. A messenger who is one of us can be scrutinized, followed, touched, and copied — and the Qur'an's insistence on the humanness of its messengers is precisely the Islamic answer to every later doctrine that made a mediator of another nature. Islam's Prophet is a man who ate in the marketplace, whose wives and neighbors knew him, and whose conduct has been transmitted with a biographical precision unmatched in the history of religion.

**"Recounting My Signs Unto You"**

The message is described with the verb *yaquṣṣūna*, from the same root as *qiṣṣah* — the step-by-step recounting. Revelation in this verse is not a formula to be accepted but a narrative to be followed: the messengers *recount* the signs, one after another, so that the hearer can see the pattern. It is the same verb the sūrah used in verse 7 for the divine recounting on the Day of Judgment, and the echo is intended: what is recounted here in warning will be recited there in judgment.

**The Two Conditions**

The promise that follows has two conditions: *fa-mani ittaqā wa-aṣlaḥa* — then whoever is reverent and makes amends. The pair deserves to be examined, because it is the sūrah's summary of what is demanded of the hearer of a prophet, and it names the two dimensions of a life that is being renewed.

*Ittaqā* is the verbal form of *taqwā* — the reverent caution that guards against the divine displeasure, the inward disposition the sūrah named two verses ago as the best of all garments (7:26). It is the vertical axis, and it comes first, because the horizontals of religion are parasitic on it.

*Wa-aṣlaḥa* is the form of *iṣlāḥ*, to repair. Its object is deliberately unspecified, and the commentators have drawn the silence out: the person who is reverent repairs whatever in his condition needs repair — the wrong he has done, the harm he has caused, the property he has taken, the relationship he has broken. The Qur'an uses the word for the making of peace between people (49:9–10), for the improvement of one's own conduct (4:128), and in the mouth of Shuʿayb as the banner of his mission: *“I desire naught but reform, so far as I am able”* (11:88).

The order matters. Reverence without repair produces a piety that leaves its damages in place; repair without reverence produces an ethics with no anchor. The verse asks for both, and it puts the fear of God first for the reason the whole sūrah has been demonstrating: a person who does not measure himself against the command will never quite see what needs repairing.

**The Formula of Security**

The verse closes with a phrase that recurs in the Qur'an in almost identical form: *fa-lā khawfun ʿalayhim wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn* — no fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve. It appears in al-Baqarah for the followers of guidance (2:38), for the believers in the broadest sense (2:62; 5:69), for those who reform (6:48), for the friends of God (10:62), and for those who remain steadfast (46:13). The tradition's commentators found a distinction in its two halves: no fear for what lies ahead, and no grief for what is behind — the two directions in which human anxiety runs, closed by one clause.

The word *khawf* means fear of a coming evil; *ḥuzn* means sorrow for something lost. Between them they cover the whole of what unsettles a human life. And the verse locates their removal not in external security but in the two conditions it has just named. The reverent and repairing person has nothing to fear from the future, because his account is in order, and nothing to grieve over in the past, because what was amiss he has mended. That is the promise held out to the children of Adam at the end of the passage that began with a whisper in a garden.
