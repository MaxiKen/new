**The Answer in Four Movements**

After refuting the argument from the fathers, the sūrah gives the answer: *qul amara rabbī bi-l-qisṭ* — say, my Lord has commanded justice. The command to answer is itself significant. Verse 28 ended with a lie about God; verse 29 begins with a command to speak, and what is put in the Prophet's mouth is not a counter-argument but a summary of what God actually requires. The refutation of a fabricated law is the real law, stated plainly.

The verse then unfolds in four movements, and each deserves its own attention: justice, orientation in worship, sincerity, and the return.

**Justice First**

The first item is *qisṭ*, and the choice of the word is deliberate. Arabic has several words for justice — *ʿadl* for balance and equity, *ḥukm* for judgment, *qisṭ* for the fair share that is due. *Qisṭ* is the word of the scale, and its use here, in a sūrah that has just placed a balance on the Day of Judgment (7:8–9), ties the ethics of this life to the accounting of the next. Justice is not one virtue among many in the Qur'an; it is the first thing named when a prophet is asked to describe what his Lord commands. The Book reinforces this repeatedly: *“Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, and courtesy to close relatives”* (16:90); *“when you judge between people, judge with fairness”* (4:58); *“Be just — that is closer to righteousness”* (5:8), the verse that commands justice even toward those one hates.

The placement matters for the sūrah's argument. Its audience was a trading city whose religious life had been handed down by custom, and the Qur'an's first answer to inherited religion is not a ritual but a moral standard. God commands what is fair. Any tradition, however venerable, that has licensed unfairness has failed the first test of revelation.

**Faces Set Toward Him**

*Wa-aqīmū wujūhakum ʿinda kulli masjid* — and set your faces at every place of worship. The verb *aqāma* is from *q-w-m*, the root of standing upright, and the Qur'an uses it for establishing prayer (*aqāma al-ṣalāh*) throughout. The commentators give several readings of the phrase. Al-Ṭabarī takes it as a command to direct oneself wholly to God in every act of worship, whatever place one is in. Others read it as a statement about consistency: at every mosque, in every prayer, in every gathering, let the face be set — not turned now to one loyalty and now to another. The word *wajh*, face, in the Qur'an's idiom carries the sense of the whole directed self, which is why *aslamtu wajhī* means not "I turned my face" but "I submitted myself" (2:112; 3:20).

The instruction answers the polytheist practice of the previous verse with an argument by contrast. They came to the House in a manner that was neither dignified nor directed; the command is to arrive with the whole person oriented to God. The place of prayer is not a stage for performance but a point of alignment.

**Sincerity as the Definition of Religion**

*Wa-dʿūhu mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn* — and call upon Him, sincere to Him in religion. The phrase *mukhliṣīn lahu al-dīn* recurs through the Book as the definition of the religion Abraham founded — in the call of 10:22 and 29:65, in the supplication of 31:32, and in the two addresses of 40:14 and 40:65 — and it is given its final summary in Sūrat al-Bayyinah: *“And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion, inclining to truth, and to establish prayer and give alms; and that is the upright religion”* (98:5).

The doctrine of *ikhlāṣ* rests on this phrase. The Arabic root *kh-l-ṣ* carries the sense of being free of mixture, and in the terminology of the tradition it means that an act has been purified of every motive except the pleasure of God. The Qur'an's own definition of the failure is instructive: a person who prays to God in difficulty and forgets Him in ease has not purified anything (10:22). The command therefore polices the interior of worship — the very thing a religion of inherited custom never examined, because custom can be performed without any interior at all.

**"As He Originated You, So Shall You Return"**

The verse closes with the resurrection, and the argument it uses is one of the Qur'an's most persistent: the appeal to the beginning as proof of the return. *Kamā badaʾakum taʿūdūn.* Sūrat al-Rūm states it with the reasoning spelled out — *“He is the One Who originates the creation then resurrects it — which is even easier for Him”* (30:27) — and the same proof is pressed in 21:104, 29:19, 27:64, and 10:4. Al-Anbiyāʾ puts it as a promise: *“Just as We produced the first creation, so shall We reproduce it”* (21:104). And when the Makkans posed the challenge in its hardest form, asking who would restore bones to life, the answer given was the same: *“He will bring them to life Who created them the first time”* (17:51).

There is a second layer in the phrase, and the sūrah exploits it immediately. *As He originated you* — the human being began as nothing, without property, without name, without a tribe. The verse is a reminder that this is also how the account will be resumed: as the Qur'an says elsewhere, *“you have come to Us alone as We created you the first time”* (6:94), and *“you will come to Us individually, as We created you the first time”* (18:48). Justice in this life, worship in this life, sincerity in this life — and then a return to the condition in which one began, where all that will distinguish a person is what he earned by those three things and not what his fathers did before him.
