## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:15

> **He said, “Truly thou art among those granted respite.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Bare Answer to a Large Request**

The answer is a single sentence, without a rebuke attached and without a condition: *innaka min al-munẓarīn* — you are among those granted respite. In the other accounts of this exchange the granting is given with the same economy, and in al-Ḥijr and Ṣād it is completed by the clause *“until the appointed Day”* (15:37–38; 38:80–81).

The brevity deserves attention because of what it does not contain. No lecture follows, no warning, no invitation to reconsider. The request is granted as stated. This is the Qur'an's way of teaching the difference between guidance and coercion: God's will is that creatures act freely, and freedom has a cost that the divine government does not suppress. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the respite was granted because the trial of humanity required a tempter who is present — the human being is being tested with his eyes open, not trapped.

**"Among Those Granted Respite"**

The phrasing is partitive: *min al-munẓarīn*, *among* the deferred, a member of a class. Iblīs is not handed a unique exemption; he is enrolled in a standing law. Everything mortal lives on borrowed time: *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time”* (3:145); *“For each community there is an appointed term”* (7:34); *“Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes”* (63:11). The indefinite plural is the point. He had asked to be made an exception to the general rule of ends; the answer tells him that he has been given a longer rope, not a different rope.

It is worth pausing on the subtlety the grammarians saw in the construction. Had the verse said *you are deferred* without the partitive, the sentence would still have been true; the addition of *min* places him inside a category shared by all creatures whose terms are set. The proudest being in the story is told, in the very sentence that grants his wish, that he is one of a crowd.

**The Delay That Builds Two Different Men**

The Qur'an treats delay as a moral substance, and it divides its users. For the wrongdoer it is accumulation: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178). For the rebellious community it is a trap that feels like kindness: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise”* (6:44). And against that, the same delay is the space in which a person returns: *“Say: O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy”* (39:53).

The three verses describe one clock with three faces. Iblīs' respite produced a strategy against humanity (vv. 16–17). A human being's respite can produce either the same ruthless accumulation or the quiet correction of a life. Nothing in the calendar tells the difference; only the use does.

**istidrāj: The Respite That Looks Like Generosity**

Arabic gives this delay a technical name, and the Qur'an builds a doctrine on it. *Istidrāj* is the drawing-on of a person stage by stage, so that he is led further while believing he is being blessed. *“Leave to Me those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend”* (68:44), and the verse that follows seals it: *“I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (68:45). The sūrah of the Heights says the same thing in its own voice: *“I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (7:183), immediately after describing those who reject the signs and are given increase.

The psychological mechanism is what makes the doctrine so uncomfortable, and the Qur'an states it without softening: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired”* (6:44). The gifts keep arriving; the warnings stop feeling urgent; the recipient concludes that the silence of heaven is approval. This is why the Qur'an repeatedly forbids the interpretation of prosperity as a verdict — the wealthy denier's comfort is not a certificate, and the sufferer's hardship is not a sentence. The delay granted to Iblīs, extended to the end of time, is the purest case of *istidrāj* in the Book: an unlimited opportunity, mistaken for an endorsement.

**The Appointed Day and the Hidden Hour**

The specification in the parallel accounts — *“until the appointed Day”* — has occupied the commentators. Some take it as the Day of Resurrection, which is what Iblīs had asked for in 7:14; others read it as the end of the world as it is known, the first blast at which all things die, the moment after which only God remains. Al-Ṭabarī records both positions and their chains. What the disagreement reveals is a deliberate silence in the text: the term is *known* — fixed, recorded, unpostponable — and *unknown* to the creature who holds it. That combination is the structure of human responsibility. If the hour were known, the life before it would become a countdown, and virtue would be arithmetic; if no hour existed, nothing would carry consequence.

The verse therefore does two things at once. It answers Iblīs with a grant, and it answers every reader with a warning, using the same words. Whatever one is building, the permission to keep building has a ceiling. The creature who asked for the extension believed he had obtained a room to work in; the sūrah's later verses will show what he built in it — an ambush on the road to God — and will also show, in the same sūrah, what the refuge from that ambush is: *“If you are tempted by Satan, then seek refuge with Allah. Surely He is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”* (7:200).
