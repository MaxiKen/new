## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:7

> **Then We shall recount unto them with knowledge, for We were never absent.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Recounting That Matches the Fact**

From the question the verse moves to the narration: *fa-la-naquṣṣanna ʿalayhim bi-ʿilm*. The verb is *qaṣṣa*, to follow a thing step by step — the root that also gives *qiṣṣah*, a story, because a story is a tracing of events in their order. To recount in the Arabic of the Qur'an is not to summarize; it is to reproduce the sequence so faithfully that the hearer recognizes his own life inside it. The instrument is named: *with knowledge* — not evidence reconstructed after the fact, not inference from testimony, but the knowledge that was present when the act itself occurred.

The final clause states the ground of that completeness: *wa-mā kunnā ghāʾibīn* — We were never absent. *Ghāʾib* is the one who is elsewhere; the negation removes not merely a lapse but the whole category of distance. The Qur'an expresses the same fact elsewhere in images the imagination can hold: *“We are closer to them than their jugular vein”* (50:16); *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything fresh or dry which is not in a clear record”* (6:59). The Book does not say that God was informed afterwards. It says that the record was never left unwritten.

**The Reading of the Record**

The sequence of this sūrah — question, recounting, weighing — matches the courtroom imagery the Qur'an uses for the final accounting. The record is brought out and opened: *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it”* (18:49). Each person is handed his own book: *“We have bound every human's destiny to their neck, and on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each person a record which they will find laid open”* (17:13) — and is told: *“Read your record. You alone are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14). Before the prophets and witnesses are brought forward, the earth shines with the light of its Lord and judgment is passed (39:69).

The purpose of that architecture is that a person is not merely judged; he is made to read. The recounting of 7:7 is that reading, and *with knowledge* is why it cannot be contested. No one can plead that the file was mistaken or that a context was missing or that the intention was better than the deed — because the One who recounts was present at the intention as well.

**The Hours No One Saw**

The Prophet ﷺ gave this certainty a name and a set of questions. The feet of the servant will not move on the Day of Judgment, he said, until he is asked about his life and how he spent it, his youth and how he wore it out, his wealth and how he earned it and where he spent it, and his knowledge and what he did with it (al-Tirmidhī 2417, from Abū Barzah; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; a fourfold version is reported from Muʿādh in al-Dārimī 554). Not one of those four can be answered by reputation. All of them belong to the interior — to hours that no one but God observed.

That is the practical force of *We were never absent*. Human accountability is limited to what can be seen; the divine recounting is not — and this is a mercy before it is a dread. A person who did good that nobody ever learned of has lost nothing at all, and a person who kept an evil behind a locked door has hidden it from everyone except the One who is now doing the recounting. The verse makes secret-keeping as impossible as it already was, and makes integrity a matter of fact rather than of performance: the only kind that survives the reading of the record.
