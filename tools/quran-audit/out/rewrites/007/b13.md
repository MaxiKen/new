**The Verdict and Its Exact Fit**

Iblīs had offered an argument; the verse answers with a sentence. *Fa-hbiṭ* — get down. The command is abrupt, and its brevity is part of its meaning: the case is closed, and no further exchange is permitted. What precedes it in the sūrah is now visible as a whole: a command was given, a creature measured himself against another, a rationale was produced, and the answer is a two-word eviction.

The proportionality is worth pausing over. The punishment is not an arbitrary penalty attached to a rule; it is the *reversal* of the thing he did. He placed himself above his station, so his station is lowered. He judged himself great, so he is told he is small — *min al-ṣāghirīn*, of the humbled, the reduced. The Qur'an states this correspondence elsewhere as a settled law: *“Is Hell not a fitting home for the arrogant?”* (39:60), and *“Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). The grammar of the hereafter is the grammar of a mirror.

**A Dwelling That Cannot Hold Arrogance**

The verse states the reason for the expulsion with precision: *lā yakūnu laka an tatakabbara fīhā* — it is not for you to be arrogant *in it*. The pronoun has been discussed by the commentators: the Garden, or the exalted assembly, or the presence in which the command was given. Whichever is intended, the clause carries a principle that runs through the whole Book. There are places and states that exclude self-exaltation not by legislation but by nature. Paradise is one of them: it is the house of those who submitted. The Prophet ﷺ described its inhabitants as *“the humble”* and said that its gates are entered by the one who acknowledges; in another report, the poor and the meek enter it before the wealthy and the proud.

This is why the verse says *it is not for you* rather than merely *you are not allowed*. The wording is ontological before it is legal: arrogance is not an offense that may be committed in that house and then repented of there; it is a condition incompatible with residence. The person who insists on being above others is not being denied a room; he is being told that he is asking for the wrong building.

**Two Expulsions: ihbiṭ and ukhruj**

The sūrah contains two removal commands, and the commentators distinguished them. The first is here — *ihbiṭ*, get down — and the second comes at verse 18: *ukhruj*, go forth, with the added description *madhʾūman madḥūran*, disgraced and banished. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the two as stages of a single sentence: the first removes him from the place in which the transgression occurred; the second confirms the state of ruin, the permanent expulsion from mercy. Others observe that the first descent precedes his request for respite (vv. 14–15) and his vow to mislead (vv. 16–17), and that the second expulsion follows those words — the sentence deepening with each further act of defiance. On either reading, the structure teaches something the reader of this sūrah needs: the fall was not a single slip followed by a settled punishment, but a path he continued to walk, one hardened statement at a time.

**ṣāghirīn: The Weight of Smallness**

The final word of the verse, *al-ṣāghirīn*, comes from *ṣ-gh-r*, the root of smallness and of willing submission. It is the direct antonym of *kibr* — the very thing that caused the fall. Arabic makes the reversal audible: the one who claimed *kabīr* (great) is made *ṣāghir* (small). The Qur'an does not use the language of size casually. In its idiom, greatness belongs to God alone — *al-Kabīr*, *al-Mutakabbir* — and any creature who appropriates the attribute contracts an absurdity that the moral order must eventually correct. The correction is rarely instantaneous in this life, which is precisely what makes this verse's placement instructive: the correction in the story is immediate, before the eyes of the angels, while in the reader's experience it is usually deferred, and the deferral is the space in which repentance is still possible.

That last point is the mercy hidden inside a verse of punishment. Iblīs' sentence was pronounced and stood; there was no door of return opened for him, because his refusal was not a lapse but a position. For human beings the Qur'an keeps the door open to the very end, and it does so explicitly: the same Adam who will slip in the verses that follow receives words from his Lord and is forgiven (2:37). The sūrah's first fall is therefore a warning and not a template. It teaches what arrogance costs and how it is answered, and it leaves the reader standing where Iblīs no longer stood — in the time when the correction can still be made by choice rather than by sentence.

**The Vocabulary of the Expulsion**

The two words that close the sentence — *madhʾūman madḥūran*, disgraced and rejected — are a fixed pair in the Qur'an, and the reader who notices where else the Book uses them will hear the sūrah's alarm more clearly. *Dh-ʾ-m* is the root of blame and disgrace; *d-ḥ-r* is the root of driving a thing away from oneself, of putting it out of reach. The pair describes a condition in which both the judgment and the separation are final.

The expulsion itself is narrated three times in the Book with the same elements, and each version adds something. Sūrat al-Ḥijr and Sūrat Ṣād record the command to leave and the curse that follows the creature *until the Day of Judgment* (15:34–35; 38:77–78). This sūrah's own account returns to the scene later and gives the sentence its final form: *“Get out of Paradise! You are disgraced and rejected! I will certainly fill up Hell with you and your followers all together”* (7:18). And Sūrat al-Isrāʾ applies the same two words to a human being for the first time in the Book's usage, in a verse that could be describing the sūrah's own audience: *“Whoever desires this fleeting world alone, We hasten in it whatever We please to whoever We will; then We destine them for Hell, where they will burn, condemned and rejected”* (17:18).

That last verse is the one that gives 7:13 its pastoral weight. What happened to Iblīs is not presented as a unique event in the biography of a jinn; it is the template of a verdict that human beings can reach by the same route. The sūrah has already shown its readers the mechanism — the refusal to bow, performed as a matter of dignity — and it will spend the next forty verses showing the human versions of the same posture, ending with the gates that do not open. The word *disgraced* in verse 13 is spoken to a creature who thought he was maintaining his rank.
