**The Sentence That Turns the Page**

Verse 41 ended with a formula: *and thus do We recompense the wrongdoers* (7:41). Verse 42 opens with the same construction and the opposite content: *wa-lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — and those who believed and did righteous deeds. The sūrah does this repeatedly, and the repetition is the point: the same court, the same scale, the same verb of requital, and the difference is what a person brought to it.

The closing clause returns the vocabulary of verse 36 to its rightful owners: where the arrogant were *aṣḥāb al-nār*, the inhabitants of the Fire, these are *aṣḥāb al-jannah*, the inhabitants of the Garden, and the abiding is stated in the identical word — *khālidūn*, there forever. In a sūrah that has arranged its arguments in pairs since the first page — the guided and the misguided (7:30), the reverent and the disdainful (7:35–36), the bed of fire and the rivers of the garden — this symmetry is not ornament. It tells the reader that the two destinies are being described on a single map, and that the road to either is built one deed at a time.

**"Those Who Believed and Did Righteous Deeds"**

The pair *āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* is the Qur'an's most frequent description of the saved, occurring more than fifty times. Its two halves are constructed so that neither can stand alone. *Īmān* is trust — the interior act by which a person accepts what God has said — and *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* is the outward act that trust produces. The Book does not separate them anywhere: not grace without the act, and not act without the trust. The pairing is itself a commentary on the sūrah's quarrel with the Makkan leadership, who were not short of deeds and were short of nothing but trust, and on the people of the sabbath of 7:163 whom the sūrah will shortly describe — a community that possessed the form of religion and lost its substance.

The adjective *ṣāliḥ* comes from the root *ṣ-l-ḥ*, which in Arabic is the vocabulary of repair — the opposite of *fasād*, corruption. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf has already used the contrast in the call of its prophets: the messenger of this sūrah comes *commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong* (7:157), and verse 35 described the saved as those who *reform* themselves. The Qur'an's concept of good work is thus not merely legal compliance but correction: the setting right of what has been bent, beginning with oneself.

**The Parenthesis That Answers Two Questions**

Dropped into the middle of the promise is a clause that does not appear to belong there: *lā nukallifu nafsan illā wusʿahā* — We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity. It is a complete principle of obligation standing between the conditions and the reward, and its placement is deliberate.

The Qur'an states it three times in this form and several more in substance: *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286), *“We never require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (23:62), *“Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it”* (65:7), and it is embedded in the practical legislation of the Book — in the concession for the traveller who cannot fast (2:184–185), in the prohibitions against burdening the nursing mother or the father beyond means (2:233), in the declaration, *“He has laid upon you no hardship in the religion”* (22:78). The tradition drew from it the whole discipline of *taklīf*, the assignment of obligation according to ability, and the jurists built on it the principle that no duty is imposed where it cannot be performed.

But the clause answers two readers, not one. The soul tempted to despair at the standard reads that faith and right action are required, measures the height of the demand, and stops. The clause tells that soul that the demand was measured before it was made. And the soul tempted to make a system of excuses reads the same clause as permission, and is answered by the exact wording — not, "we do not require any soul", but *we do not require any soul beyond its capacity*. Grace does not abolish the order; it sets its size.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the doctrine its practical shape in a hadith the sūrah's readers have always paired with this verse: *“The deeds of any one of you will not save him” — they said, “Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?” — he said, “Not even me, unless Allah envelops me in His mercy. So be moderate, do what you can, and give glad tidings”* (al-Bukhārī 5673; Muslim 2816, from Abū Hurayrah). Neither the despair nor the presumption survives that sentence.

**The People of the Garden**

The verse then states the destination with a possessive that the sūrah has already used twice: *ulāʾika aṣḥāb al-jannah*. *Aṣḥāb* is the vocabulary of belonging and company — the owner of a house, the companion of a road. The Qur'an does not say the righteous will be admitted to the Garden, though it says that too elsewhere (16:32; 39:73); here it says they *are its people*, and the clause *hum fīhā khālidūn* settles the tenure.

There is a pastoral reason the sūrah repeats the abiding so insistently in this short stretch. The community receiving these verses knew loss as a daily condition: the exile of the early Muslims from Makkah, the death of children, the confiscation of property, the long patience of people who had no reason to expect that anything in their circumstances would hold. Against that experience, the Book places a possession that cannot be taken, in a place whose gates — unlike those of verse 40 — are open.

The arrangement of the three verses is worth seeing whole. Verse 41 furnishes the Fire. Verse 42 furnishes the Garden with the simplest inventory the Qur'an possesses: belief, action, and a measure that fits the soul. Verse 43 will give the Garden its voice. Between the two descriptions, the sūrah leaves only one thing for the reader to decide, and it is not hidden in either of them.
