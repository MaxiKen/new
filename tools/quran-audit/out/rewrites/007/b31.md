**The Command to Be Beautiful in Worship**

*Khudhū zīnatakum ʿinda kulli masjid* — take your adornment at every place of worship. The verse answers the previous two by legislation. Verse 28 had condemned an act defended as ancestral; verse 29 had stated what God actually commands; now this verse supplies the practice that fills the gap. The sūrah turns what the polytheists had inverted into a command: they came to the House stripped, and the Qur'an instructs the believers to come dressed, deliberately and well.

The commentators record the occasions this addresses. Al-Ṭabarī transmits reports that the pre-Islamic Arabs would circumambulate the Kaʿbah naked — men by day, women by night — with a rationale drawn from the world of the jinn and from the idea that one should not circle the House in garments in which one had sinned. Other reports attach the verse to the Jews and Christians, who had forbidden themselves certain good food and certain clothing in ascetic observance, and to those among the Arabs who had dedicated certain of their livestock and crops and would not eat or wear them. The command answers all of them at once: what God has made lawful is lawful, and the place of worship is precisely where the gifts of God should be brought into view.

**Worship and Dignity**

The word *zīnah* is the same word used in 7:32, where God defends the adornments He has brought forth for His servants — the two verses are a pair, one granting and one commanding. Taken together they form the Qur'an's refusal of two opposite errors. The first is the ascetic error: the notion that God is honored by the rejection of the good things He gave, that piety is measured in shabbiness and self-denial. The second is the error of the naked circumambulation, where religion was used to strip away the very covering God provided.

The Prophet ﷺ embodied the middle path. He wore clean clothes, disliked disorder in appearance, and told a man who came looking unkempt that God loves to see the traces of His favour upon His servants. His guidance on arriving for prayer was cleanliness of body and garment, the use of scent for the assembly, and a deliberate attention to appearance that distinguished the Muslim gathering from a performance of poverty. That is the practice this verse legislates: the place of worship is where a human being presents himself before his Lord, and the presentation is part of the worship.

**"Eat and Drink, But Do Not Be Prodigal"**

The second half of the verse turns from dress to appetite, and it does so with the same reasoning. *Kulū wa-shrabū wa-lā tusrifū* — eat and drink, and do not exceed. The permission comes first, in the imperative, and it is not qualified by any condition of poverty of spirit. The prohibition that follows is not against enjoyment but against excess, and the Qur'an's term for that excess is *isrāf*, from the root *s-r-f*: to go beyond the limit, to pour water past the brim.

The Book repeats the prohibition as a settled principle: *“He is the One Who produces gardens… eat of their fruit when they bear, and give its due on the day of harvest, and do not waste — indeed, He does not like the wasteful”* (6:141); *“Do not spend wastefully; surely the wasteful are like brothers to the devils, and the Devil is ever ungrateful to his Lord”* (17:26–27); *“They are those who spend neither wastefully nor stingily, but moderately in between”* (25:67). The last verse describes the servants of the All-Merciful, and the standard it gives — neither the open hand of the wastrel nor the clenched fist of the miser — is the balance this sūrah has been teaching since it placed a scale on the Day of Judgment.

**The Hadith of the Stomach**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the ethic of 7:31 its most concrete expression. From Miqdām ibn Maʿdī Karib, he said: *“The human being does not fill any vessel worse than his stomach. It is enough for the son of Adam to eat a few morsels to keep his back straight. But if he must eat more, then a third for his food, a third for his drink, and a third for his breath.”* (al-Tirmidhī 2380; graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ). The tradition read this hadith as a rule of bodily discipline with spiritual consequences: the full stomach produces a sluggish heart, and the appetite that is never denied becomes the door through which every other appetite enters.

The verse's final clause makes the prohibition theological rather than hygienic: *innahu lā yuḥibbu al-musrifīn* — He does not love the extravagant. The Quranic formula of God's love and its absence is the Book's way of ranking conduct on the scale of relationship rather than of legality. Extravagance is not merely unwise; it is the kind of conduct a person engages in when he has stopped seeing the One who provided. That is why the verse appears where it does, after a chapter on provisioning (7:10), a chapter on covering (7:26), and a chapter on the enemy who strips cover away (7:27). Ingratitude, exposure, excess — three names for the same misplacement — and the remedy offered is one: to take, deliberately and gratefully, what God has sent down, and not one measure more.
