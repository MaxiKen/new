**The Call to Particular Men**

*Wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-aʿrāfi rijālan* — and the people of the Heights called out to certain men. The verb of the previous two verses returns for the third time: the Hereafter of this sūrah is a scene of speaking across distances. What is new is the object of the address. The blessed were addressed as a community (*aṣḥāba al-jannati*), the cursed as a community (*aṣḥāba al-nār*), but here the call goes out to *rijāl* — men, individuals, identifiable persons whose faces the callers know. The Qur'an uses the plural *rijāl* for grown men in their public capacity, and its appearance here says something the larger scene would have blurred: the leaders are not swallowed by the crowd they led. They are each still themselves, on show.

*Alladhīna yaʿrifūnahum bi-sīmāhum* — whom they recognize by their marks. The third occurrence of this phrase in three verses takes on a sharper edge now, because the marks in question belong to tyrants whose faces the world knew from coins and speeches. Sūrat al-Raḥmān describes the same reversal: *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by their forelocks and feet”* (55:41). The Book's claim is not that God needs identifying marks — it is that character, which in this world is hidden under clothing, reputation, and public relations, will end up written on the body.

**"Your Numbers Have Availed You Nothing"**

*Mā aghnā ʿankum jamʿukum* — your multitude has not enriched you in anything. *Aghnā* is the verb of sufficiency: to make someone need nothing more. *Jamʿ* is the mass — the assembled following, the party, the coalition, the mob, the congregation of clients and dependents, the crowd a man can produce when he wants to demonstrate that he matters. The sentence declares that the entire apparatus has been weighed and found to have no purchasing power.

This is one of the Book's most persistent themes and one of its least welcome. *“If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's way”* (6:116). *“Most of them do not believe in Allah without associating others with Him in worship”* (12:106). *“Competition for more gains diverts you until you end up in your graves”* (102:1–2). *“We are far superior in wealth and children, and we will never be punished”* — this is the speech of the affluent of Sabaʾ (34:35), and the answer comes in the next verses: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us”* (34:37). Sūrat al-Baqarah records an army that fled its homes in the thousands for fear of death and was told to die (2:243). The Qur'an mentions the size of the crowds that followed the truth and the size of the crowds that opposed it, and it refuses, in either case, to treat the number as an argument.

The wording *your multitude* also carries a sting that a Makkan audience would have felt precisely. The chiefs of the town had dependents, allies, clients, slaves, and the moral weight of clan solidarity behind them; their confidence in the outcome of the coming judgment was indistinguishable from their confidence in the size of their following. Verse 48 tells them the following has been left on the other side of the fire.

**"Nor What You Used to Be Arrogant About"**

*Wa-mā kuntum tastakbirūn* — and neither has the arrogance you used to practise. The phrase returns to the sūrah's single diagnosis. Verse 36 described those who *denied Our signs and treated them with disdain*; verse 40 closed the gates of heaven against the same people; verse 48 now names what the disdain was, and whom it was for. The arrogant elite of the sūrah's narratives had defined themselves by the lowliness of the people who believed: the chiefs of Ṣāliḥ's town asking *“Are you certain that Ṣāliḥ is truly a messenger from his Lord?”* while the lowly answered, *“We surely believe in what he has been sent with”* (7:75–76); the disbelieving chiefs of Noah's people telling him that only *the lowliest* follow him (11:27); the people of Shuʿayb lying in ambush on the road to turn back those who believed (7:86).

The Prophet ﷺ gave the two populations their portraits in a single hadith, and no commentary improves on it. Harithah ibn Wahb heard the Messenger of God say: *“Shall I tell you of the people of Paradise? They comprise every poor humble person, and if he swears by Allah to do something, Allah will fulfil it; while the people of the fire comprise every violent, cruel, arrogant person”* (al-Bukhārī 6657). The verse of the Heights has staged that hadith as a conversation between two populations who can see one another.

**The Failure of Social Capital**

Read economically, verse 48 is a statement about currency. Arrogance is a form of capital in this world: it purchases obedience, deference, precedence in a queue, the last word in a meeting, the silence of the people who would otherwise object. The verse declares the exchange rate on the Day: zero. There is no market in the Hereafter for any of the transactions the tyrannical elite spent a lifetime perfecting.

And the alternative assets are the ones the sūrah has already named, in the plainest language it possesses. Verse 35 defined salvation as *reform* and reverence; verse 42 as belief and right action; verse 43 as an inheritance granted for what one did and a guidance that came from God. Against the tyrants' *jamʿ* — the crowd — the sūrah files the *ṣālihāt* — the deeds — and its verdict on the comparison is the single sentence being shouted across the barrier in this verse.

**The Question That Follows**

The scene is not finished with the tyrants. Verse 49 will put a question to them in the same voices, and the question will be the last word of the passage: *Are these the ones you swore would never be shown Allah's mercy?* — pointing, in the manner of the sūrah, at the poor and lowly who followed the messengers and who stand at that moment inside the Garden while the men who mocked them watch from the Fire. The people of the Heights do not answer their own question. The next verse answers it with a command, and the command is not addressed to the tyrants.
