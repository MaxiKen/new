## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:10

> **And We have indeed established you upon the earth and placed means of livelihood for you therein. Little do you give thanks!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Courtroom Back to the Earth**

The three verses before this one stood inside the final court: the question, the recounting, the weighing. Verse 10 returns the reader to the ground he is standing on. The transition is deliberate. Before the sūrah names the first refusal in the universe (vv. 11–18), it reminds the human being of the position he occupies: he has been *settled*, and he has been *provided for*. The two facts together form the premise of every command that follows, and the pivot of the verse is the last clause, which turns provision into a question about the recipient: *Little do you give thanks!*

**makkannākum: The Verb of Being Settled**

The verb is *makkanā*, from *m-k-n*, the root of place and of power. *Tamkīn* is not merely to drop someone somewhere; it is to give him a firm place, the ability to act from it, and the security to remain. The Qur'an uses the same root for the establishment of the believers in the land (22:41; 24:55) and for the power given to Joseph in Egypt (12:56). Here it is granted to humanity as such, without distinction of nation or creed.

The Qur'an describes the earth with the vocabulary of a house prepared for a guest: *“Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy”* (2:22); *“It is Allah Who made the earth a place of settlement for you”* (40:64); *“He is the One Who smoothed out the earth for you, so move about in its regions and eat from His provisions”* (67:15). The last of these is the closest twin to this verse: the earth is *smooth*, *tamed*, *made traversable* — a planet whose gravity holds instead of crushing, whose atmosphere filters instead of burning, whose soil yields food when a seed is placed in it. None of that was arranged by the person who benefits from it.

**maʿāyish: Livelihood in the Plural**

Provision is named with a plural that is significant: *maʿāyish*, from *ʿ-y-sh*, to live. Not one livelihood but many, distributed across soils and climates and temperaments. The commentators drew the practical consequences. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the plural shows the variety of ways God has opened for the creature to eat — agriculture, trade, craft, pasture — so that no one is forced into a single channel and no one may claim that the earth offered him nothing. Al-Qurṭubī adds the juridical consequence: since the livelihoods are *placed* by God, they are not owed to any person by right of cleverness; they are received, and receiving creates the duty of thanks.

The verse also quietly settles a question that occupies the sūrah elsewhere. If provision is placed by God, then striving is a means, not a guarantee; effort is required, but the outcome is not owned by the effort. That single adjustment disarms both envy and arrogance at once: envy, because another's plenty is not a theft from my share; arrogance, because my plenty is not a proof of my superiority.

**The Ingratitude Clause**

The verse closes with *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — “little is it that you give thanks.” The construction (*qalīlan mā* + imperfect) is one of the Arabic forms of emphatic rebuke, and the translation's “Little do you give thanks!” carries it. The wording is careful: the verse does not say that human beings are never grateful. It says that gratitude is *little* — occasional, partial, easily displaced by complaint. The Qur'an returns to this diagnosis repeatedly: *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them”* (14:34; 16:18), with 14:34 ending in the same verdict — *“Indeed, humankind is truly ungrateful.”*

The Qur'an also states the law that governs the relationship: *“If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe”* (14:7). The logic deserves to be stated plainly, because it is not a bargain in the manner of a merchant. Gratitude is not a payment that purchases increase; it is a *seeing* that makes increase bearable and fruitful. The person who acknowledges the Giver receives the same gift and is enlarged by it; the person who credits himself receives it and is narrowed by it, because everything he has becomes a monument to himself — and monuments have to be defended forever.

**Gratitude as a Discipline of the Eye**

Islam treats *shukr* as a practice, not a mood. The Prophet ﷺ trained his companions to compare downward rather than upward in the things of this world: *“Look at those below you and not those above you, for that is more likely to keep you from belittling the favour of God upon you.”* (Muslim 2963). It is a remedy against precisely the disease this verse names. Ingratitude rarely announces itself as denial of God; it appears as a habitual focus on the one thing missing, in a life that has received a thousand things.

The circle of gratitude is also horizontal before it is vertical. *“Whoever is not grateful to the people, he is not grateful to Allah.”* (al-Tirmidhī 1954, from Abū Hurayrah). A person who cannot bring himself to thank a teacher, a parent, a driver, a colleague has already lost the reflex by which the Creator is thanked, because both are acts of the same faculty: the willingness to admit that what I have came through someone other than me.

Read in the sūrah's sequence, then, verse 10 is not a pleasant interlude before the story of Iblīs. It is the foundation of that story. The earth is prepared, the livelihoods are placed, the human being is settled in dignity — and the next thing the sūrah shows is a creature who had been given a station and lost it by believing the station was his own achievement.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:11

> **Indeed, We created you, then We formed you, then We said unto the angels, “Prostrate yourselves before Adam.” And they all prostrated, save Iblīs; he was not among those who prostrated.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Story of Adam Comes Here**

This verse begins the sūrah's longest narrative, the account of Adam, Iblīs, and the first disobedience — a story that will run to verse 25 and will be told again, from different angles, in al-Baqarah, al-Ḥijr, al-Isrāʾ, Ṣād, and Ṭā-Hā. Its placement here answers the verse before it. Verse 10 described a creature settled on a prepared earth and provided for; verse 11 shows what that creature *is* — the being for whom the angels were commanded to bow. The dignity is stated before the failure, so that the failure may be read correctly: not as evidence that humanity is contemptible, but as the collapse of something noble.

**Two Verbs: Created, Then Formed**

*Khalaqnākum thumma ṣawwarnākum* — We created you, then We formed you. The two verbs are not synonyms. *Khalaqa* names the bringing into being of what was not; *ṣawwara* names the giving of shape — proportion, features, the arrangement that makes a thing recognizable as itself. The Qur'an uses the second verb for the human form with evident pleasure: *“He shaped you in the womb, perfecting your form”* (40:64); *“He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. He shaped you, perfecting your form”* (64:3); *“Who has perfected everything He created”* (32:7); and the summary statement of Sūrat al-Tīn, *“Indeed, We created humans in the best form”* (95:4).

The plural address — *you* — has been explained in two ways. Al-Ṭabarī prefers that it addresses humanity as a whole in Adam: you were created in him, and decreed in him, and then brought into existence from him. Others read the sequence as stages of the species: created as matter, formed as a body, then animated. Either reading carries the same theological point, which the next clauses make explicit: the human being did not design himself, and his form is not an accident of chemistry but the product of an intention.

**The Prostration and What It Confers**

Then God addressed the angels: *“Prostrate before Adam.”* The angels obeyed, and the Qur'an states their obedience without qualification. The command requires theological care, because prostration appears to be an act of worship and worship belongs to God alone. The classical commentators resolve this by distinguishing the *act* from its *object of worship*: the prostration was commanded by God, performed for God, and directed at Adam as a *qiblah* of honor — a salute, in the manner of a subject greeting a king. Al-Qurṭubī cites the analogy of Jacob and his sons prostrating before Joseph (12:100), which the Qur'an records without censure: the prostration of greeting was a known courtly form, and its permissibility or prohibition is set by the divine command. What the angels performed was obedience to God expressed in the direction He chose.

That the direction was a human being is the astonishing part. The angels are described elsewhere as creatures of uninterrupted worship — *“they do not disobey God in what He commands them, and they do exactly what they are commanded”* (66:6) — and here they are told to bow to dust that has been shaped and animated by God. The honor of humanity, in this scene, is not intrinsic but *conferred*, and it is conferred by the very act that Iblīs will refuse. Dignity, in the Qur'an's account, is always a grant; the moment it is treated as a personal possession, it becomes the occasion of a fall.

**Iblīs and the Grammar of the Exception**

*Illā Iblīs* — except Iblīs. The verse's final clause is unusual: it does not say that he refused, but that *he was not among those who prostrated*. The Qur'an distributes the facets of his act across sūrahs: he *refused and was arrogant* (2:34); he *was one of the jinn, but he rebelled* (18:50); he *protested* (17:61); he *said, “It is not for me to prostrate to a human You created from sounding clay”* (15:33). Each wording adds a detail. From 18:50 we learn that he was not an angel at all but a jinn, whose free will was the very test the command was designed to exercise. From 2:34 we learn that the refusal was accompanied by *istikbār* — the attribution of greatness to oneself. And from this verse we learn that his non-prostration was a settled state: he did not merely miss the moment; he placed himself outside the ranks of the obedient and remained there.

**The Warning Stored in the Honor**

The sūrah's first audience was a proud merchant leadership, and this narrative was addressed to them. The first rebellion in creation was not caused by disbelief in God's existence; Iblīs believed, and even invoked God's act of creation as his proof. It was caused by a ranking instinct — the conviction that a position undeserved by another belonged properly to oneself. The Prophet ﷺ stated the danger in a single hadith: *“No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise.”* When a man asked how one could like his clothes and his shoes to be good, the Prophet ﷺ clarified the definition: *“Arrogance is rejecting the truth and belittling people.”* (Muslim 91, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd). Both halves of that definition are present in the verses that follow — and both were present in the court of the angels, where a creature with free will was shown what his freedom was for.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:12

> **He said, “What prevented thee from prostrating when I commanded thee?” He said, “I am better than he. Thou hast created me from fire, while Thou hast created him from clay.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Put to the Accused**

The scene turns to the interrogation, and its form repeats the pattern established in verses 6 and 7: God asks, and the answer exposes. The question is *mā manaʿaka* — literally, *what prevented you?* The verb *manaʿa* is the vocabulary of withholding and of barriers; a *māniʿ* is a thing that stands in the way. The question is therefore not a request for information. It puts before the accused the one thing he must now name: the obstacle. And since the command had come from God and nothing external had blocked the way, the only possible answer was the barrier he had built inside himself. The Qur'an's question strips away every third-party explanation before Iblīs speaks a word.

His answer does not name that barrier. It supplies, instead, an argument.

**The First Analogy in the Tradition's Telling**

*“I am better than him: You created me from fire and him from clay.”* The classical scholars read these words as the first act of reasoning by analogy (*qiyās*) in the history of the creation, and they examined its form with the care of logicians. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī set out the objections in sequence.

First, the premise is asserted, not established. Iblīs claims a nobility of fire above clay as though the proposition were self-evident. It is not; it is a claim about material substances whose ranking Iblīs had no revelation to support. The Qur'an, for its part, treats the composition of both as dust-bound and God-given: the jinn are *“created from a smokeless flame of fire”* (55:15), and the human being from *“sounding clay moulded from black mud”* (15:26). Arranging these ingredients in a hierarchy of superiority is a human projection, not a scriptural datum.

Second, even a true premise could not have produced that conclusion. The value of a thing before God is not set by what it is made of, but by its obedience and its reverence: *“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you”* (49:13). Iblīs reasoned from origin to rank, and the Qur'an's entire moral architecture refuses that move. A creature of fire and a creature of clay stand equally as creatures; only the command of God differentiates their duties.

Third, and decisively, the argument was raised against a direct command. Theologians of every school drew the line here: no demonstration — even a sound one — abrogates an explicit divine obligation. A person's reasoning is authorized to conclude that God is one, that the Messenger speaks for Him, that the prayer is due; it is not authorized to override what is thus concluded. To reason so is not to reason, but to use reasoning as an alibi.

**When Intelligence Goes to Work for Desire**

The most instructive feature of the answer is that it is *articulate*. Iblīs does not say “I did not want to.” He constructs a case. The Qur'an thus presents, at the first moral crisis of the created order, the psychology that will recur in every subsequent one: the mind's capacity to convert a preferred outcome into a reasoned position. Al-Rāzī notes that this is why the lesson of the verse is not confined to Iblīs. Any argument can be assembled backward from the conclusion one is unwilling to surrender; and the sharper the intelligence, the more respectable the assembly looks.

The criterion that separates sound reasoning from this corruption is stated by the angels in the parallel scene of al-Baqarah. When God announced the creation of a deputy on earth and the angels asked their question, the answer given for them — and given by them — was: *“Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise”* (2:32). The angels also reasoned — but they closed their reasoning with submission, whereas Iblīs closed his with a verdict. That is the whole difference, and the sūrah intends it to be remembered.

**The Lie in the Phrase “I Am Better”**

The comparative carries its own refutation. Iblīs accepted God's act of creation and even cited it as an argument; what he refused was God's authority to command. He was, in effect, a theist in metaphysics and an atheist in ethics, dividing lordship into departments so that the department that inconvenienced him could be reserved for himself. The Qur'an will answer the posture directly in another sūrah: *“Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60).

And the final irony is the criterion he chose. *“I am better than he”* — better in substance, by a material comparison. The Book's answer is that the creature he despised would receive what he lost: *“We have indeed honoured the children of Adam”* (17:70). Rank in the divine court is not a property of matter; it is a matter of response. It is the one measurement Iblīs never applied, because applying it would have cost him the argument — and it is the same measurement this sūrah put before its readers four verses earlier, when it asked who among the settled, provided, and shaped would give thanks.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:13

> **He said, “Get down from it! It is not for thee to wax arrogant here. So go forth! Thou art surely among those who are humbled.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verdict and Its Exact Fit**

Iblīs had offered an argument; the verse answers with a sentence. *Fa-hbiṭ* — get down. The command is abrupt, and its brevity is part of its meaning: the case is closed, and no further exchange is permitted. What precedes it in the sūrah is now visible as a whole: a command was given, a creature measured himself against another, a rationale was produced, and the answer is a two-word eviction.

The proportionality is worth pausing over. The punishment is not an arbitrary penalty attached to a rule; it is the *reversal* of the thing he did. He placed himself above his station, so his station is lowered. He judged himself great, so he is told he is small — *min al-ṣāghirīn*, of the humbled, the reduced. The Qur'an states this correspondence elsewhere as a settled law: *“Is Hell not a fitting home for the arrogant?”* (39:60), and *“Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). The grammar of the hereafter is the grammar of a mirror.

**A Dwelling That Cannot Hold Arrogance**

The verse states the reason for the expulsion with precision: *lā yakūnu laka an tatakabbara fīhā* — it is not for you to be arrogant *in it*. The pronoun has been discussed by the commentators: the Garden, or the exalted assembly, or the presence in which the command was given. Whichever is intended, the clause carries a principle that runs through the whole Book. There are places and states that exclude self-exaltation not by legislation but by nature. Paradise is one of them: it is the house of those who submitted. The Prophet ﷺ described its inhabitants as *“the humble”* and said that its gates are entered by the one who acknowledges; in another report, the poor and the meek enter it before the wealthy and the proud.

This is why the verse says *it is not for you* rather than merely *you are not allowed*. The wording is ontological before it is legal: arrogance is not an offense that may be committed in that house and then repented of there; it is a condition incompatible with residence. The person who insists on being above others is not being denied a room; he is being told that he is asking for the wrong building.

**Two Expulsions: ihbiṭ and ukhruj**

The sūrah contains two removal commands, and the commentators distinguished them. The first is here — *ihbiṭ*, get down — and the second comes at verse 18: *ukhruj*, go forth, with the added description *madhʾūman madḥūran*, disgraced and banished. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the two as stages of a single sentence: the first removes him from the place in which the transgression occurred; the second confirms the state of ruin, the permanent expulsion from mercy. Others observe that the first descent precedes his request for respite (vv. 14–15) and his vow to mislead (vv. 16–17), and that the second expulsion follows those words — the sentence deepening with each further act of defiance. On either reading, the structure teaches something the reader of this sūrah needs: the fall was not a single slip followed by a settled punishment, but a path he continued to walk, one hardened statement at a time.

**ṣāghirīn: The Weight of Smallness**

The final word of the verse, *al-ṣāghirīn*, comes from *ṣ-gh-r*, the root of smallness and of willing submission. It is the direct antonym of *kibr* — the very thing that caused the fall. Arabic makes the reversal audible: the one who claimed *kabīr* (great) is made *ṣāghir* (small). The Qur'an does not use the language of size casually. In its idiom, greatness belongs to God alone — *al-Kabīr*, *al-Mutakabbir* — and any creature who appropriates the attribute contracts an absurdity that the moral order must eventually correct. The correction is rarely instantaneous in this life, which is precisely what makes this verse's placement instructive: the correction in the story is immediate, before the eyes of the angels, while in the reader's experience it is usually deferred, and the deferral is the space in which repentance is still possible.

That last point is the mercy hidden inside a verse of punishment. Iblīs' sentence was pronounced and stood; there was no door of return opened for him, because his refusal was not a lapse but a position. For human beings the Qur'an keeps the door open to the very end, and it does so explicitly: the same Adam who will slip in the verses that follow receives words from his Lord and is forgiven (2:37). The sūrah's first fall is therefore a warning and not a template. It teaches what arrogance costs and how it is answered, and it leaves the reader standing where Iblīs no longer stood — in the time when the correction can still be made by choice rather than by sentence.
