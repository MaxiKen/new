**The Call Across the Divide**

*Wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-jannati aṣḥāba al-nār* — and the inhabitants of the Garden will call out to the inhabitants of the Fire. The sūrah's next six verses are built on the verb *nādā*, to call from a distance: here, and again at 7:48, and again at 7:50 where the residents of the Fire cry out to the residents of the Garden. The Book is describing a landscape in which the two populations are within sight and voice of one another, and in which nothing can be exchanged between them but words.

That is a deliberate design, and the sūrah has prepared the reader for it. In this world, the two groups are intermingled and indistinguishable to the outer eye; the sūrah's earlier narratives showed them arguing across a social distance that could still be crossed — the proud chiefs and the lowly believers of Ṣāliḥ's town (7:75–76). After death, the crossing is over, and all that remains of the relationship is the call. The voice carries. Nothing else does. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf will make that plain three verses later, when the condemned beg for a drop of water and receive a doctrinal answer instead (7:50).

**The Question That Is the Punishment**

*Innā wajadnā mā waʿadanā rabbunā ḥaqqan* — we have found what our Lord promised us to be true — *fa-hal wajadtum mā waʿada rabbukum ḥaqqan* — so have you found what your Lord promised you to be true? *Qālū naʿam* — they said, yes.

The interrogative is the most economical instrument of punishment in the entire Book. The blessed do not gloat, and the Book does not put a taunt in their mouths; they ask a question whose answer is already experienced by everyone present. The verb *wajada*, to find, is a verb of discovery in the concrete world — the verb of a man who finds water in a well or a coin in the road. It states that revelation turned out to be *there*, like a thing you can put your hand on. The sentence is thus the last form of the sūrah's central argument, made by two communities at once and answered with a single word from the condemned.

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf records the same admission in another key seven verses later: the day the warning is fulfilled, those who ignored it will say, *“The messengers of our Lord certainly came with the truth”* (7:53). Sūrat al-Anʿām closes the door on its usefulness: *“On the Day your Lord's signs arrive, belief will not benefit anyone who had not believed before or earned some good through their faith”* (6:158). The exchange of verse 44 is the moment when the whole vocabulary of the Book's warnings becomes a physical fact, and, crucially, it is the blessed who pose the question and the damned who supply the confession.

**The Herald Between Them**

*Fa-adhdhana muʾadhdhinun baynahum* — and a herald called out between them. The word is the ancestor of *adhān*, the call to prayer: a public announcement made in a raised voice for a gathered crowd. In this world the phrase *the call between them* has a mundane history — the muezzin of Madinah calling across the rows — and the sūrah borrows the image for the last announcement that will ever be made in that landscape.

The content of the herald's cry is a sentence from the vocabulary of exclusion: *laʿnatu Allāh ʿalā al-ẓālimīn* — the curse of Allah is upon the unjust. *Laʿna* in the Qur'an is not profanity but the opposite of mercy: the expulsion of a creature from the radius within which God's special favour operates (cf. 2:161; 4:52). It is the same fate the sūrah announced for Iblīs in the first narrative of the chapter, when he was driven out for his arrogance (7:13), and the reader is expected to hear the echo. The announcement is made *between* the two populations, which is to say in the hearing of both: the blessed are told what they were saved from, and the condemned what they chose.

**The Hope That Lives Inside the Verdict**

The curse of verse 44 is stated in the absolute, and the tradition of the Prophet ﷺ read it within a larger mercy that the Book states in a hundred places. The Messenger of God described what will happen after the reckoning in a report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī: when the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire have entered their homes, God will say, *“Take out whoever has faith equal to a mustard seed in his heart”* — and they will come out blackened like coal, and they will be cast into the river of al-Ḥayyāt, and they will sprout green as a seed does on the bank of a torrent (al-Bukhārī 6560). The smoke-blackened figures in that hadith are people whose heart contained something the fire could not consume.

The verse of the Heights and that hadith are not in tension; they are describing different levels of the same verdict. The curse falls on the *ẓālimīn* as those who are defined by the injustice they settled in — the fixed posture, the chosen identity, the disdain of verse 36. The mustard seed is what remains of a creature who was better than the label. The sūrah's own reader is asked to check which of the two sentences describes the direction he is walking, and the question of verse 44 is the one he will be asked by voices that were once his neighbours.

**Why the Wrongdoers Are Defined Now**

Before the hasher's voice fades, the very next verse turns to define the word *ẓālimīn* — those who have just been cursed. It is the sūrah's most characteristic habit, and one of its most humane: whenever it pronounces a sentence, it immediately tells the reader what the sentence is for, so that the reader may measure himself instead of guessing. Verse 36 named disdain; verse 40 named denial; verse 41 named the crossing of a boundary. Verse 45 will name an act that can be seen in a human life today, and the reader who finds it there has been given the warning early enough to act on it.
