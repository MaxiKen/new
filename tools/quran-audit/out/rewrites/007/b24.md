**The Descent: A Sentence That Is Also a Grant**

*Qāla ihbiṭū* — He said, get down. The command is plural, and the Qur'an's parallel accounts specify who is included. In al-Baqarah the instruction is to the pair, *“descend from the heavens as enemies to one another”* (2:36); in Ṭā-Hā it is expanded to include the tempter, *“Descend, both of you, from here together with Satan as enemies to each other”* (20:123). The sūrah of the Heights does not need the fuller statement; its readers have just watched the whole exchange.

The wording deserves attention because of what it does *not* say. There is no mention of a punishment being imposed. What is announced is a condition — the earth, a dwelling, a provision, a term — and everything inside that announcement is a good. The same verse that marks the end of the Garden records the beginning of the human world as the Qur'an will treat it in verse 10 above: a place made habitable, supplied, and governed by a schedule known to God. The fall is real; so is the provision that immediately accompanies it.

**"Each of You an Enemy to the Other"**

*Baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin ʿaduww* — some of you are enemies to others. The plural is broad and the commentators read it widely: Iblīs and the human race; the human being and his own kind; the human being and the demons; and, in the reading al-Qurṭubī reports, the two members of the couple with respect to a domestic strife the temptation had opened. The Book returns to this condition repeatedly, because it is the structure of life in the world the descent created: *“But Satan deceived them—leading to their fall from the ˹blissful˺ state they were in, and We said, ‘Descend from the heavens ˹to the earth˺ as enemies to each other. You will find in the earth a residence and provision for your appointed stay.’”* (2:36); *“Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy”* (35:6); *“Do not follow Satan's footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy”* (2:168).

The verse therefore supplies the realistic premise for everything the sūrah will legislate afterwards. The Qur'an does not describe the earth as neutral ground. It describes it as a contested one, in which the human being lives with a declared opponent and his own appetites, and in which the guidance that arrives from God is the map. The sūrah's later chapters on food, clothing, worship and wealth make sense only against this premise.

**mustaqarr and matāʿ: Dwelling and Provision**

Two nouns are given for the earthly condition. *Mustaqarr* — a place of settlement, from the root *qarra*, to settle, to remain still — is the word the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the stability of created things: *“The sun travels for its fixed term”* (36:38); *“He has made the night and day, the sun and moon; each travelling in an orbit”* (21:33). The earth is given to the human being as something firm enough to build a life on. Beside it stands *matāʿ*, provision or enjoyment — with the nuance of a thing borrowed and used for a while, since the same word describes the goods of this life generally: *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting…”* (57:20).

The pairing is the sūrah's doctrine of the world in two words. It is a home and it is not a permanent one. Read without the second term, the verse produces worldliness; read without the first, it produces the kind of contempt for the earth that makes the human vocation unintelligible. The Book holds both: a place to settle, and a limit on the settlement.

**"Until a Time"**

*Ilā ḥīn* — until a time. The phrase is deliberately indefinite, and the indefiniteness is one of the Book's recurring devices: the term is fixed (*“When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it”*, 7:34) and unknown, and it is the unknown part that produces the moral seriousness of a life. The same phrase appears at the end of Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt's account of the people of Jonah: *“who then believed ˹in him˺, so We allowed them enjoyment for a while.”* (37:148; cf. 2:36).

The verse thus hands the reader the three coordinates within which everything in this sūrah will happen: *where* — a settled earth; *with what* — provision that can be received with gratitude or with greed; *for how long* — a term no one knows. It is the same framework verse 10 laid out at the beginning of the sūrah — *“We have established you on earth and provided you with a means of livelihood”* — now restated after the fall, which is to say: the fall did not withdraw the earlier gift.

**The Silence About the Garden**

One theological observation belongs at the end of this scene, and the commentators draw it out. The Qur'an never says that Adam and his wife were expelled from the Garden and forbidden to return. What it says is that they descended into a different mode of existence — that of testing — and that the guidance they were promised in the next verse (7:35) would determine where they ended. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same sequence with an explicit promise attached: *“Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (2:38).

The world, in other words, is not the wreckage of the Garden; it is the arena of the return. The sūrah's opening beats — the towns destroyed at night (vv. 4–5), the question and the weighing (vv. 6–9), the provision of verse 10 — have already laid out the terms, and the Adam narrative has now embedded them in the story of the first human beings. Every reader who continues into the sūrah's long confrontation with the Children of Israel is reading, in a sense, an extended commentary on this verse: what a settled creature does with a provision, in the time remaining, before the term arrives.
