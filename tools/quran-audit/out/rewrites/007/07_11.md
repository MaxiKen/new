## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:11

> **Indeed, We created you, then We formed you, then We said unto the angels, “Prostrate yourselves before Adam.” And they all prostrated, save Iblīs; he was not among those who prostrated.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Story of Adam Comes Here**

This verse begins the sūrah's longest narrative, the account of Adam, Iblīs, and the first disobedience — a story that will run to verse 25 and will be told again, from different angles, in al-Baqarah, al-Ḥijr, al-Isrāʾ, Ṣād, and Ṭā-Hā. Its placement here answers the verse before it. Verse 10 described a creature settled on a prepared earth and provided for; verse 11 shows what that creature *is* — the being for whom the angels were commanded to bow. The dignity is stated before the failure, so that the failure may be read correctly: not as evidence that humanity is contemptible, but as the collapse of something noble.

**Two Verbs: Created, Then Formed**

*Khalaqnākum thumma ṣawwarnākum* — We created you, then We formed you. The two verbs are not synonyms. *Khalaqa* names the bringing into being of what was not; *ṣawwara* names the giving of shape — proportion, features, the arrangement that makes a thing recognizable as itself. The Qur'an uses the second verb for the human form with evident pleasure: *“He shaped you in the womb, perfecting your form”* (40:64); *“He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. He shaped you, perfecting your form”* (64:3); *“Who has perfected everything He created”* (32:7); and the summary statement of Sūrat al-Tīn, *“Indeed, We created humans in the best form”* (95:4).

The plural address — *you* — has been explained in two ways. Al-Ṭabarī prefers that it addresses humanity as a whole in Adam: you were created in him, and decreed in him, and then brought into existence from him. Others read the sequence as stages of the species: created as matter, formed as a body, then animated. Either reading carries the same theological point, which the next clauses make explicit: the human being did not design himself, and his form is not an accident of chemistry but the product of an intention.

**The Prostration and What It Confers**

Then God addressed the angels: *“Prostrate before Adam.”* The angels obeyed, and the Qur'an states their obedience without qualification. The command requires theological care, because prostration appears to be an act of worship and worship belongs to God alone. The classical commentators resolve this by distinguishing the *act* from its *object of worship*: the prostration was commanded by God, performed for God, and directed at Adam as a *qiblah* of honor — a salute, in the manner of a subject greeting a king. Al-Qurṭubī cites the analogy of Jacob and his sons prostrating before Joseph (12:100), which the Qur'an records without censure: the prostration of greeting was a known courtly form, and its permissibility or prohibition is set by the divine command. What the angels performed was obedience to God expressed in the direction He chose.

That the direction was a human being is the astonishing part. The angels are described elsewhere as creatures of uninterrupted worship — *“they do not disobey God in what He commands them, and they do exactly what they are commanded”* (66:6) — and here they are told to bow to dust that has been shaped and animated by God. The honor of humanity, in this scene, is not intrinsic but *conferred*, and it is conferred by the very act that Iblīs will refuse. Dignity, in the Qur'an's account, is always a grant; the moment it is treated as a personal possession, it becomes the occasion of a fall.

**Iblīs and the Grammar of the Exception**

*Illā Iblīs* — except Iblīs. The verse's final clause is unusual: it does not say that he refused, but that *he was not among those who prostrated*. The Qur'an distributes the facets of his act across sūrahs: he *refused and was arrogant* (2:34); he *was one of the jinn, but he rebelled* (18:50); he *protested* (17:61); he *said, “It is not for me to prostrate to a human You created from sounding clay”* (15:33). Each wording adds a detail. From 18:50 we learn that he was not an angel at all but a jinn, whose free will was the very test the command was designed to exercise. From 2:34 we learn that the refusal was accompanied by *istikbār* — the attribution of greatness to oneself. And from this verse we learn that his non-prostration was a settled state: he did not merely miss the moment; he placed himself outside the ranks of the obedient and remained there.

**The Warning Stored in the Honor**

The sūrah's first audience was a proud merchant leadership, and this narrative was addressed to them. The first rebellion in creation was not caused by disbelief in God's existence; Iblīs believed, and even invoked God's act of creation as his proof. It was caused by a ranking instinct — the conviction that a position undeserved by another belonged properly to oneself. The Prophet ﷺ stated the danger in a single hadith: *“No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise.”* When a man asked how one could like his clothes and his shoes to be good, the Prophet ﷺ clarified the definition: *“Arrogance is rejecting the truth and belittling people.”* (Muslim 91, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd). Both halves of that definition are present in the verses that follow — and both were present in the court of the angels, where a creature with free will was shown what his freedom was for.
