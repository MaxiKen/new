**The Counter-List**

Verse 33 answers the question of verse 32 by supplying the actual prohibitions: *qul innamā ḥarrama rabbī* — say, my Lord has only forbidden. The *innamā* is a tool of restriction: this list, and nothing but this list, is what the divine prohibition contains. The verse is therefore simultaneously a prohibition and a defence of liberty — an authoritative statement that whatever lies outside these categories has not been forbidden, and that no one may place it under interdict on God's behalf.

**The Five Forbidden Things**

The list that follows has a considered order.

*Al-fawāḥish* — indecencies, *mā ẓahara minhā wa-mā baṭan*: what is open of them and what is hidden. The word *fāḥishah* covers sexual immorality in the broadest Qur'anic sense — the act that is shameful by nature and that every sound tradition has recognized as such. The phrase *outward and inward* is the verse's own insistence that the prohibition is not merely about conduct. It reaches the intention, the imagination, the private arrangement, and the intention that never reaches conduct. The Book repeats the same pairing elsewhere — *“Abandon the outward and the inward sin”* (6:120) — and the Prophet ﷺ summarized the whole doctrine in a single sentence: *“God does not look at your bodies or your forms, but at your hearts and your deeds”* (Muslim 2564, from Abū Hurayrah).

*Wa-l-ithm* — and sin. Commentators differ on the specific extension: some read it as the class of sins generally, others as intoxicants by a well-attested usage of *ithm* in the Arabic of the Qur'an (cf. 2:219; 5:90), others as the inward malice that leads to wrong. The placement between indecency and aggression is instructive, because it is the middle term: an act whose harm is not bodily, whose shame may be unacknowledged, and which lies between the violation of the self and the violation of others.

*Wa-l-baghy bi-ghayr al-ḥaqq* — and wrongful aggression, transgression without right. *Baghy* is the overstepping of a boundary against another person: the seizure of what is not one's own, the abuse of power, the arrogant exercise of superiority. The qualifier *without right* is a reminder that not every use of force is aggression; the verse acknowledges a legitimate claim without defining it here, and the sūrah's later legal passages will. The Qur'an ranks *baghy* among the most serious of social sins, and the Islamic tradition identified it as the characteristic sin of the powerful.

*Wa-an tushrikū bi-Allāh mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānā* — and that you associate with Allah that for which He has sent down no authority. The word is *sulṭān*: evidence, warrant, authorization. The Qur'an does not merely say that idolatry is incomplete; it says that the classes of things worshiped besides God are, without exception, unsupported by any evidence. This is a challenge to produce a warrant, and it is issued repeatedly in the Book with the same confidence (10:68; 16:116; 34:22; 46:4).

*Wa-an taqūlū ʿalā Allāh mā lā taʿlamūn* — and that you say of God what you do not know. The list closes with the offense the sūrah has already met: the invented religious claim of verse 28. Its placement last, after the great sins, is a deliberate ranking. Speech about God without knowledge is the root from which fabricated laws, corrupted religion, and misdirected worship grow; it is the sin that makes all the others possible to legislate.

**The Parallel Lists**

This verse sits inside one of the Qur'an's most systematic treatments of the divine moral law. Sūrat al-Anʿām gives the longer version: *“Come, let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden to you: do not associate others with Him; be kind to parents; do not kill your children for fear of poverty; do not come near the indecencies, whether open or hidden; do not kill the soul God has made sacred except by right…”* (6:151), continuing through the orphan's property, honest measure, and truthful speech (6:152). Sūrat al-Isrāʾ gives a third version in a passage of some thirty verses (17:22–39). The three passages are usually read together by scholars, who observe that the Qur'an restates the same moral core in different contexts — which itself tells the reader that the essentials of the divine law are stable, known, and not adaptable by the preferences of a generation.

**What the Verse Does with Guilt**

One practical effect of the list deserves to be named. By restricting the divine prohibition to named categories, the verse liberates the conscience from invented burden. People who live under a religion of unnumbered prohibitions — the anxious, the scrupulous, those who fear that pleasure itself is a trespass — are given here an authoritative short list and told that the graces God brought out for His servants are theirs to enjoy.

But the list also closes a door in the other direction. A person may not conclude that the moral life is exhausted by avoiding the five categories; the same sūrah is full of commands, and the Book contains hundreds of others. What the verse establishes is a jurisdictional limit: the right to declare a thing forbidden belongs to God, exercised through revelation; and the one who exercises it in God's name without knowledge has placed himself, by this verse's own account, inside the list.
