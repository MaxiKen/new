## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:105

> **obligated to speak naught about God save the truth. I have brought you a clear proof from your Lord; so send forth with me the Children of Israel.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Let the Children of Israel Go With Me"**

The verse completes Moses' opening speech, and its three parts are the Qur'an's whole theory of prophethood in miniature: a statement of the messenger's constraint, a statement of his evidence, and a concrete demand. *Ḥaqīqun ʿalā an lā aqūla ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqq* — "obliged to say nothing about Allah except the truth." The word *ḥaqīq* is stronger than a moral preference; it is the vocabulary of an incumbent obligation, of something owed. Moses opens his address to the Egyptian court by declaring that he is not free to say anything else.

**A Claim That Arrives as a Demand**

The third clause makes it a political sentence: *fa-arṣil maʿiya banī Isrāʾīl* — "so let the children of Israel go with me." The Book never treats the demand for a people's freedom as a separate humanitarian concern appended to a message about God; it is the message's own consequence, because a people enslaved by a king who claims to be a god cannot be worshippers of anything but that king. Sūrat Ṭāhā preserves the same instruction given to Moses and Aaron before they went:

> *“So go to him and say, ‘Indeed we are both messengers from your Lord, so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them. We have come to you with a sign from your Lord …’”* (20:47)

and the sentence that follows shows what was at stake in the audience's own terms: *“It has indeed been revealed to us that the punishment will be upon whoever denies ˹the truth˺ and turns away”* (20:48).

**Why the Demand Belongs in the Message**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the confrontation its sharpest political edge. When Pharaoh reminds Moses of the favour of having been raised in the palace, Moses refuses the ledger: *“How can that be a ‘favour,’ of which you remind me, when ˹it was only because˺ you ˹have˺ enslaved the Children of Israel?”* (26:22). The Book's Moses therefore begins his mission not by asking permission to preach but by demanding the release of the people among whom the message would be preached.

The sūrah's own sequence of towns has prepared the reader for this. Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb all delivered messages to communities that could hear them in their own tongue and in their own assembly; Moses is sent to a foreign court whose business was the enslavement of his people, and the message arrives with a demand attached.

**Truth, Proof, and Demand**

The order of the three clauses is the Book's standard sequence for prophecy. Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt's account of the same mission preserves it: *“Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺. And say, ‘Would you ˹be willing to˺ purify yourself, and let me guide you to your Lord so that you will be in awe ˹of Him˺?’”* (79:17–19). First the diagnosis, then the invitation, then the guidance — and behind the invitation, the truth about who God is.

Moses' evidence is the *bayyinah*, the clear proof that the next two verses will produce. The Qur'an's later summary counts the signs he carried: *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs. ˹You, O Prophet, can˺ ask the Children of Israel. When Moses came to them, Pharaoh said to him, ‘I really think that you, O Moses, are bewitched’”* (17:101). Verse 105 announces the proof; verses 107 and 108 exhibit the first two of the nine.

**The Constraint on the Messenger**

The clause about saying nothing but the truth has a second edge, aimed at the audience that has just heard a man claim to be sent by the Lord of all worlds. In the Book's logic, a messenger's authority is not his own; it is delegated, bounded, and audited. The Prophet of the sūrah receives the same instruction in the frame of the whole Book: *“Your fellow man is neither misguided nor astray. Nor does he speak of his own whims”* (53:2–3). Moses' first claim in Pharaoh's court is not power but restriction.

The Book records what the court did with it. They did not ask about the truth of the claim; they asked for a sign, and the sign they got became the material of the sūrah's longest narrative of resistance. What the verse establishes is that the initiative belonged to God from the beginning: the demand for Israel's release, the proof that would follow, and the outcome that would drown the Egyptian army in the sea the court treated as its own property, are all one motion.

**What the Reader Is Meant to Carry Away**

Placed where it is, immediately after Moses' claim to be a messenger from the Lord of all worlds (7:104), the verse gives the sūrah's readers the model of a message in action. The truth about God is stated, not negotiated. The proof is offered publicly. The demand is specific — the release of an enslaved people — and it is made to the man who personally benefited from that enslavement.

The Book's later laws are consistent with it: the Qur'an makes the freeing of the enslaved a form of worship (90:13's steep path begins with *the freeing of a slave*, and 2:177 lists it among the marks of true faith), and it forbids the subjugation of one people as a claim against God. Verse 105's demand is the first political consequence of the sūrah's theology, and the narrative that follows tests whether the court's power can outlast it. The answer is delivered in a single line at 7:136.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:106

> **He said, “If you have brought a sign, then bring it forth, if you are among the truthful.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Bring It, If What You Say Is True"**

*Qāla Firʿawnu in kunta jiʾta bi-āyatin fa-ʾti bihā in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīn* — Pharaoh said: "If you have come with a sign, then bring it if what you say is true." The reply is the court's first move in the narrative, and it is a move the Qur'an reports elsewhere with the same words: *“Pharaoh demanded, ‘Bring it then, if what you say is true’”* (26:31).

**The Test the Court Sets**

Read carefully, the sentence is not an inquiry. It is a condition attached before any evidence has been examined, and its operative clause is the last one — *if what you say is true*. The challenge makes the sign's significance depend on the outcome the court will later have to deny; by demanding a proof and then reserving the right to name it, Pharaoh has already prepared the strategy that the sūrah will expose when the elite call Moses a skilled magician (7:109).

The Book's narratives of this scene record what the challenge produced. Sūrat Ṭāhā's summary is terse: *“And We certainly showed Pharaoh all of Our signs, but he denied them and refused ˹to believe˺”* (20:56). Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt gives the same sequence in four verbs: *“Then Moses showed him the great sign, but he denied and disobeyed ˹Allah˺, then turned his back, striving ˹against the truth˺”* (79:20–22) — and what followed the refusal was not silence but a political counter-offensive: *“Then he summoned ˹his people˺ and called out, saying, ‘I am your lord, the most high!’”* (79:23–24).

**The Demand for Signs as a Mode of Refusal**

The Qur'an treats the demand for a sign as one of the ways unbelief keeps the initiative. Sūrat al-Baqarah reports the formula in the mouth of those who do not know: *“Those who have no knowledge say, ‘If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!’ The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike”* (2:118). Sūrat al-Anʿām states the doctrine that makes the demand irrelevant: *“Even if We had sent them the angels, made the dead speak to them, and assembled before their own eyes every sign ˹they demanded˺, they still would not have believed — unless Allah so willed”* (6:111).

The point is not that signs are worthless; the sūrah's next two verses are signs, and the Book records their effect on the ones who watched them. The point is that the sign is not a lever the observer can pull. It arrives as a summons, and the response it produces reveals what was already there: Sūrat al-Naml says of the Egyptian audience that *“their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly”* (27:14).

**The Cost of the Challenge**

Al-Shuʿarāʾ records what the court's demand cost it. When the challenge is issued, Pharaoh's own advisers frame the crisis as a threat to the land — *“who seeks to drive you from your land by his magic. So what do you propose?”* (26:35) — and the answer they give is the machinery of a state defending its monopoly on the spectacular: *“Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities to bring you every skilled magician”* (26:36–37).

The sūrah's own account introduces the same professionals at 7:113, negotiating their fee. By the end of that contest, the hierarchy would be inverted: the experts in illusion would fall in prostration, and the court's challenge would be the device that publicized the message it was meant to discredit (7:120–122).

**The Question Behind the Demand**

What Pharaoh does not ask is the question the challenge presupposes: what the sign is evidence *for*. Verse 105 had stated the message — a messenger, a proof, and the release of a people — and verse 106 reduces it to an audition. The Book's verdict on such reductions appears in its account of the magicians' own conversion; they had come for a reward and discovered the difference between an effect and a sign, which is the distinction the court's demand was designed to blur.

Sūrat al-Zukhruf preserves the sequence of the signs that followed the challenge, and it ends with the court's own vocabulary turning against it: *“But as soon as he came to them with Our signs, they laughed at them, although every sign We showed them was greater than the one before. Ultimately, We seized them with torments so that they might return ˹to the Right Path˺”* (43:47–48). Then the plea: *“˹Then˺ they pleaded, ‘O ˹mighty˺ magician! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. We will certainly accept guidance’”* (43:49). The men who demanded a proof and then renamed it magic were reduced to asking the magician to intercede for them — which shows what their challenge had become: not a method of verification but a bargain struck under pressure.

The sūrah gives the answer to the challenge without commentary. The messenger throws down what he is holding, and the narrative, having been asked for proof, produces it in the most ordinary material available: a staff and a hand.

The narrative's economy serves the same point: a challenge is answered in two clauses, and the reader is left to weigh what the court did with the answer.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:107

> **So he cast his staff and, behold, it was a serpent manifest.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"It Became a Real Snake"**

*Fa-alqā ʿaṣāhu fa-idhā hiya thuʿbānun mubīn* — so he threw down his staff, and behold, it was a clear serpent. The translation's "behold!" is the Book's *idhā*, the particle of sudden appearance, and the sūrah uses it here for the first of the two signs that answer Pharaoh's challenge in the previous verse.

**The Staff That Had a History**

The Book does not treat the staff as a neutral prop. Sūrat Ṭāhā reports the dialogue in which Moses is asked about the object in his hand, and gives the answer in the first person:

> *“And what is that in your right hand, O Moses?” He replied, “It is my staff! I lean on it, and with it I beat down ˹branches˺ for my sheep, and have other uses for it.”* (20:17–18)

The exchange is one of the Book's quietest studies in prophecy. Before the staff becomes the instrument of the signs, it is described in the vocabulary of ordinary work — a shepherd's support and a tool for knocking down leaves for the flock. Then comes the command and its consequence, in the same chapter: *“Allah said, ‘Throw it down, O Moses!’ So he did, then — behold! — it became a serpent, slithering”* (20:19–20), followed by the reassurance that the power is not loose: *“Take it, and have no fear. We will return it to its former state”* (20:21).

**One Object, Several Uses**

Within the sūrah, the same staff will be thrown again on the day of the contest, and its work will be described as swallowing rather than merely frightening: *“Then We inspired Moses, ‘Throw down your staff,’ and — behold! — it devoured the objects of their illusion”* (7:117). Its later service at the sea is part of the same chain of events. The Book's choice of a shepherd's stick as the instrument of Egypt's defeat is a deliberate answer to the symbolism of the confrontation: the court's power was displayed in sceptres, crowns, and the uraeus of the cobra, and the sign that undid it came out of a herdsman's hand.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ describes the same moment with identical wording — *“So he threw down his staff and — behold! — it became a real snake”* (26:32) — and the Qur'an's other accounts use a different noun for the creature: in Sūrat al-Naml and al-Qaṣaṣ, the staff becomes a *jānn*, a serpent moving like a flash: *“But when he saw it slithering like a snake, he ran away without looking back. ˹Allah reassured him,˺ ‘O Moses! Do not be afraid!’”* (27:10). The variety is a feature of the Book's storytelling: the same event narrated to different audiences with the detail each account needs.

**The Difference Between a Sign and a Trick**

The sūrah's narrative will insist on the distinction that the court's next sentence tries to erase. After the magicians perform, the Book reports the outcome in three short clauses: *“So the truth prevailed and their illusions failed. So Pharaoh and his people were defeated right there and put to shame”* (7:118–119). The vocabulary is forensic: truth against illusion, with the illusion named as such. Sūrat Ṭāhā states the principle in God's instruction to Moses before the contest: *“Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go”* (20:69).

The difference is not in the impressiveness of the effect but in what stands behind it. The sign belongs to the One who made snakes; the trick belongs to men who borrowed the appearance of movement. The Book's audience in Makkah knew both — the fairs and shrines of Arabia had their performers, and the Qur'an's own challenge to the doubters rested on a book rather than a spectacle.

**What the Sign Was For**

The serpent sign is addressed to a civilization that read the world through its gods, and the Book's choice of material is precise. Egypt's royal iconography carried a cobra at the brow; its wisdom literature and its magic were prized across the region; and its court employed specialists precisely for encounters like this one. When Moses' staff becomes a snake, the sign does not introduce a new creature to the audience — it takes a creature the audience already considered sacred and makes it serve a messenger it had just told to produce proof.

The same staff serves the whole arc of Moses' mission, and the Book records each service. At the sea the command comes again: *“So We inspired Moses: ‘Strike the sea with your staff,’ and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain”* (26:63). In the wilderness, when the people ask for water, it is used once more: *“And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, ‘Strike the rock with your staff.’ Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place”* (2:60). In the contest it devours the illusions (7:117). One shepherd's tool, carried through the deliverance of a people — the Book's Moses narratives are built around the fact that the object most closely identified with him was a piece of wood he had owned before the mission began.

The reader of the sūrah is meant to notice the ordinary object and the extraordinary result, and then to watch the court do the only thing it knows how to do with an unexplainable event: give it a name and send for reinforcements.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:108

> **And he drew forth his hand and, behold, it was white to the onlookers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"A White Hand, for All to See"**

*Wa-nazaʿa yadahu fa-idhā hiya bayḍāʾu li-l-nāẓirīn* — and he drew out his hand, and behold, it was white to the beholders. The second sign follows the first without a command being reported and without any speech; the sequence of verse 106's challenge is answered twice, in silence.

**The Qur'an's Guard on the Detail**

The Book's other accounts of the sign add a phrase the sūrah leaves out, and the phrase is the one that matters: *“And put your hand under your armpit, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished, as another sign”* (20:22). Sūrat al-Naml gives the same words, and adds the count: *“Now put your hand through ˹the opening of˺ your collar, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished. ˹These are two˺ of nine signs for Pharaoh and his people”* (27:12).

*Min ghayri sūʾ* — "without blemish," without disease — is a precise guarantee. In the ancient world, a white hand was read in one of two ways: as leprosy or as the pallor of illness, and both readings would have made the sign a sign against its bearer. The Qur'an forecloses both: the whiteness is light, not infection, and the hand is shown unspoiled.

**Two Signs, Nine, and a Pattern of Signs**

The Book's later summary of Moses' mission counts the signs he carried into Egypt: *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs. ˹You, O Prophet, can˺ ask the Children of Israel. When Moses came to them, Pharaoh said to him, ‘I really think that you, O Moses, are bewitched’”* (17:101). Sūrat al-Naml's verse identifies the staff and the hand as the first two of the nine; the remaining seven are named as a group in the sūrah's own account of the plagues at 7:130–135 — the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood, and the years of drought and failing crops.

The pairing is significant. The staff sign speaks the language of power: it turns an object of the shepherd's trade into a living creature and then devours the magicians' illusions. The hand sign speaks the language of light: the body itself becomes a source of radiance, and its radiance is shown to *the beholders*, a public disclosure rather than a private wonder. In the Book's narratives, signs of deliverance are typically public and unmistakeable — the sea splits before an army, the fire does not burn the prophets thrown into it, the well comes to a people in the desert — because their function is to be remembered by a community, not to convince a specialist.

**Nature, Not Necromancy**

The distinction matters for the whole contest that follows. The Egyptian understanding of the supernatural drew no line between the natural world and the realm of magic; the gods, the Nile, and the magicians' arts were continuous with one another. The Book's signs are, by contrast, events in the created order that only its Maker can perform — and the Qur'an consistently describes them as such: the splitting of the sea, the feeding of the people in the wilderness, the water from the rock, and, here, a hand that radiates light.

That is why the sign can be shown to *all* who look and why the sūrah's reader is not asked to admire an effect but to recognize an owner. The Book states the principle in the same narrative in another sūrah: when the truth comes from God, its arrival is a mercy to those who accept it and a proof against those who do not (13:1's sense). The hand does not compel; it makes the choice visible.

**The Court's Verdict Already Written**

The verses that immediately follow this sign are its interpretation. Sūrat al-Naml records what the audience did with a display their own conscience accepted: *“But when Our enlightening signs came to them, they said, ‘This is pure magic.’ And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly. See then what was the end of the corruptors!”* (27:13–14).

The remaining signs appear in the sūrah's own account of the plagues, and it gives them as a list: *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood — all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people”* (7:133). Sūrat al-Zukhruf's comment on the sequence is that the signs *were greater than the one before* (43:48), and the Book's narration of the plagues records the same pattern with each of them: the sign arrives, the court asks for relief, relief comes, and the promise made under pressure is broken. The two signs of verses 107 and 108 are therefore the opening of a sustained argument rather than a single demonstration.

That sentence is the sūrah's own verdict on the court, previewed. Its final clause — "see then what was the end of the corruptors" — repeats, almost word for word, the line the sūrah itself uses at 7:103 before Moses' story begins and at 7:84 before Lot's town is destroyed. The narrative of al-Aʿrāf thus reads the two signs of verses 107 and 108 with its conclusion already in hand: the court's response is not in doubt, and the reader's attention is directed instead to what the sign says about the One who sent it.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:109

> **The notables among Pharaoh’s people said, “Truly this is a knowledgeable sorcerer.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"A Skilled Magician"**

*Qāla al-malaʾu min qawmi Firʿawna innahū la-sāḥirun ʿalīm* — the chiefs of Pharaoh's people said: he is indeed a skilled magician. The verse hands the floor back to the court, and it does so with the sūrah's own vocabulary of power. *Al-malaʾ*, the elite, are the cast of every narrative in this sūrah: the notables who mocked Noah (7:60), the chiefs who argued with Hūd (7:65–66), the arrogant leaders of Thamūd (7:75), Madyan's establishment (7:88–90). The same word now introduces the political class of Egypt.

**Naming the Event Away**

The court's judgment is a piece of category-work, and the Qur'an reports it as a recurring procedure. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves the fuller version: *“Pharaoh said to the chiefs around him, ‘He is indeed a skilled magician, who seeks to drive you from your land by his magic. So what do you propose?’”* (26:34–35). Sūrat Yūnus records the same reflex when the truth arrives: *“When the truth came to them from Us, they said, ‘This is certainly pure magic!’”* (10:76). Sūrat al-Naml's verdict, quoted above, adds that they knew better: the denial was *wrongful and arrogant*, not mistaken.

The label does two things at once. It keeps the event inside a category the court controls — magic is a discipline with professional practitioners, and a discipline can be answered by better practitioners — and it converts the messenger's claim into a threat to the land, which then justifies the mobilization that follows.

**The Threat to the Land**

The framing is familiar from the sūrah's earlier towns, where the elite's defence of the existing order took the form of an expulsion demand: Sodom's people told each other to expel the family of Lot from their town, and Madyan's chiefs warned that following Shuʿayb meant ruin. In Egypt the same move is executed at the scale of a state: the messenger's sign is reframed as an attempt at dispossession, and the response is a national recruitment drive. *“Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities to bring you every skilled magician”* (26:36–37).

**The Specialists and Their Fee**

This sūrah's own account of the duel begins with the professionals negotiating. *“The magicians came to Pharaoh, saying, ‘Shall we receive a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?’ He replied, ‘Yes, and you will certainly be among those closest to me’”* (7:113–114). The exchange tells the reader what kind of institution the court is: it hires the men who will attempt to refute a prophet, and it pays them in rank.

The Book's narration of the contest that follows is compressed to a handful of clauses, but its outcome is total. The truth prevails, the illusions fail, and the court is defeated in public (7:117–119). Then the specialists reach a conclusion of their own: *“And the magicians fell down, prostrating. They declared, ‘We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds — the Lord of Moses and Aaron’”* (7:120–122).

**The Experts Who Recognized the Line**

The sūrah's arrangement gives the magicians' conversion its full weight: the men hired to demonstrate that Moses' sign was an effect of the same kind as theirs are the first to know that it is not. Their craft had taught them the boundary of illusion, and the boundary had just been crossed in front of them. The Book's principle is stated by Moses in Sūrat al-Ṭāhā before the contest opens: *“what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go”* (20:69).

The word *al-malaʾ* recurs throughout this sūrah, and always as the group that speaks for a community's power. It is the elite who dismiss Noah — *“But the chiefs of his people said, ‘We surely see that you are clearly misguided’”* (7:60) — the chiefs of Thamūd who taunt the believers, the establishment of Madyan whose threat the sūrah reports verbatim: *“The disbelieving chiefs of his people threatened, ‘If you follow Shuʿaib, you will surely be losers!’”* (7:90). Egypt's notables belong to the same literary class, and their counsel is the engine of the persecution that follows.

The Book's verdict on the magicians' craft is stated by Moses at the contest: *“What you have produced is mere magic, Allah will surely make it useless, for Allah certainly does not set right the work of the corruptors. And Allah establishes the truth by His Words — even to the dismay of the wicked”* (10:81–82). The sentence does not despise the profession; it locates it. What the court called expertise, the Book calls a trade in appearances, and the truth needs no such trade: it is established by words — the same words that made everything the magicians could only seem to move.

The court's word for Moses — *ʿalīm*, skilled, expert — is thus the exact reverse of the truth the reader is meant to hold. A court competent in the techniques of illusion recognized expertise in the man who had just done something outside the discipline. The next sentence in the sūrah will show them responding in the only way left to them.

The reader of the sūrah knows what the court does not: the sign it has just named is the first of nine (17:101; 27:12), and the contest it provokes ends with the court's own experts on the floor in prostration.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:110

> **He desires to expel you from your land; so what do you command?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Who Seeks to Drive You From Your Land"**

The verse completes the chiefs' sentence and hands the floor to the king: *“who seeks to drive you from your land.” ˹So Pharaoh asked,˺ “What do you propose?”* The charge is dispossession, and the question is a ruler asking his advisors what to do about it — the sūrah's portrait of power at the moment it decides that an argument has become a threat.

**The Charge and Its Irony**

The Book records the accusation in several accounts, and it always comes from the party whose own position is the actual usurpation. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ puts the words in Pharaoh's own mouth: *“who seeks to drive you from your land by his magic. So what do you propose?”* (26:35). Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ has the court dismiss the signs as inherited trickery and appeal to ancestral custom — *“This is nothing but conjured magic ˹tricks˺. We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers”* (28:36) — a defence that reveals what the throne understood itself to be defending: not truth but continuity.

Against that, the Book has already shown the reader who actually owns the land. The same sūrah's Moses will later say of God that He is the Lord of the worlds, and the throne's own claim is recorded elsewhere with devastating brevity: *“I am your lord, the most high!”* (79:24) and *“O chiefs! I know of no other god for you but myself. So bake bricks out of clay for me, O Hamân, and build a high tower so I may look at the God of Moses”* (28:38). A man who builds a tower to inspect the God of a shepherd is charging the shepherd with eviction.

**The Expulsion Demand in This Sūrah**

The sūrah has used the same weapon before. Sodom's townspeople answered Lot's preaching by demanding the removal of the preachers — *“But his people's only response was to say, ‘Expel them from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!’”* (7:82) — and Madyan's chiefs threatened Shuʿayb directly: *“O Shuʿaib! We will certainly expel you and your fellow believers from our land, unless you return to our faith”* (7:88). Egypt's version is the same move with the state's vocabulary behind it: an idea is described as a foreign body, and the remedy is removal.

**A King Asking for Advice**

The sentence's second half is the sūrah's quiet comment on royal power. Pharaoh does not declare what he will do; he asks *what do you propose*, and the Book's other accounts show the court filling the silence. Sūrat Ṭāhā describes the same king orchestrating a contest — *“Pharaoh then withdrew, orchestrated his scheme, then returned”* (20:60) — and the sequel to the sūrah's own verse is the machinery of recruitment that occupies the next two verses.

There is no courage in the portrait and no conviction. The throne's response to a proof is a procedure, and the procedure is designed to make the proof reappear as a spectacle the state can win.

**What Moses Answers**

Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ preserves the reply to the charge, and it is the same reply the sūrah's other messengers have given their towns: the verdict is not in the court's hands. *“Moses responded, ‘My Lord knows best who has come with ˹true˺ guidance from Him and will fare best in the end. Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed’”* (28:37). The Book's answer to an accusation of dispossession is not a defence of the messenger's loyalty but a statement of where the land's real title rests.

Sūrat al-Ḥajj states the general principle for such confrontations: *“Allah will judge between you ˹all˺ on Judgment Day regarding your differences”* (22:69) — a sentence that applies to every court in the sūrah's narratives, from the assembly of the towns to the throne room of Egypt.

**The Floor Given to the Court**

Verse 110 ends with a question, which means the sūrah's next word belongs to the advisors of Pharaoh. The Book's arrangement of the scene is exact: the messenger has stated his claim (7:104), delivered the demand (7:105), produced two signs (7:107–108), and now waits while the state decides what to call them.

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf shows where the charge leads. A few verses later the chiefs return to it with the vocabulary of corruption: *“Are you going to leave Moses and his people free to spread corruption in the land and abandon you and your gods?”* (7:127). The word they choose — corruption — is the one the sūrah applied to the towns God destroyed and to the earth-spoilers the Book names as the losers. The court's defence of Egypt turns out to be a description of a persecution it was already conducting in the name of order.

The court's confidence also rests on an assumption the Book addresses in another sūrah: that rank is evidence of divine favour. *“Is it they who distribute your Lord's mercy? We ˹alone˺ have distributed their ˹very˺ livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some of them in rank above others”* (43:32). Pharaoh's throne is, on this account, a distribution of livelihood and rank like any other — and the Book's narratives repeatedly show its holders mistaking the distribution for a verdict on truth.

That is the pattern of the sūrah's previous narratives as well, and the reader knows how such deliberations end. What he does not know yet is the size of the apparatus that will be assembled against two men and a staff — and the sūrah spends the next verses describing it in the vocabulary of procurement.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:111

> **They said, “Put him and his brother off for a while, and send marshalers to the cities**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"'Let Him and His Brother Wait'"**

*Qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-arsil fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn* — they said: "Let him and his brother wait, and send mobilizers to all cities." The translation's "let him wait" renders *arjiʾhu*, defer him; and the sentence that opens the court's strategy is a decision about time.

**Two Brothers, One Mission**

The court's phrasing treats Moses and Aaron as a pair, and the Book has prepared the reader for why. Moses had asked for his brother before leaving for Egypt: *“and grant me a helper from my family”* (20:29), a request the Qur'an places among the supplications Moses makes at his commissioning. Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ records the argument behind it: *“And my brother Aaron is more eloquent than I, so send him with me as a helper to support what I say, for I truly fear they may reject me”* (28:34), and the answer he receives: *“We will assist you with your brother and grant you both authority, so they cannot harm you. With Our signs, you and those who follow you will ˹certainly˺ prevail”* (28:35).

The narrative's two brothers therefore arrive with a promise attached, and the court's counsel — *let him and his brother wait* — is an attempt to handle a messenger and his support at once.

**The Strategy of Delay**

Deferral is the sūrah's oldest technique of resistance. Madyan's elite had answered Shuʿayb's preaching by treating prosperity as a verdict (7:95) and by treating the apparent safety of the ordinary day as proof that no verdict was coming (7:97–99); Noah's people had been given a century, and their answer was to wait and see. The Book's verdict on such postponement is that it is never neutral: the interval is either repentance or accumulation.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the identical counsel with the same word for the assembling of the professionals: *“Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities to bring you every skilled magician”* (26:36–37). The Book reports the same policy from the king's side later in the narrative, when Israel finally leaves: *“Then Pharaoh sent mobilizers to all cities”* (26:53) — the machinery of the state, used first against a proof and then against a people.

**The Appointment the Court Sets for Itself**

Sūrat Ṭāhā records how the delay was converted into a public trial with a date. The court proposes a contest on terms it believes favorable: *“We can surely meet you with similar magic. So set for us an appointment that neither of us will fail to keep, in a central place”* (20:58). Moses' answer fixes the occasion in the people's calendar: *“Your appointment is on the Day of the Festival, and let the people be gathered mid-morning”* (20:59).

The Book's detail is exact and deliberate: the confrontation is scheduled for the day when Egypt's population is assembled for its festival. The state's own institution of public spectacle is thereby made the setting in which the state's experts will be defeated.

**The Assumption Behind the Counsel**

The counsel assumes that truth is a category of performance and that the better-funded performer wins. Sūrat Yūnus's version of the preparation — *“Pharaoh demanded, ‘Bring me every skilled magician’”* (10:79) — states the assumption in a sentence, and the Qur'an's placement of it is instructive: the demand for a large enough team is made before anyone has examined what the sign is.

The Book's answer to the assumption is not a denial of power; it is a statement about where power is held. Sūrat al-Ḥajj's line about the disputes of the nations being settled on the Day of Judgment (22:69) applies to this courtroom as much as to the towns, and the sūrah's own narrative will demonstrate the point within a day.

**What the Brothers Had**

The story's interest in the delay is that it changes nothing. Moses and Aaron carry the authority they were promised, and the postponement gives the signs a larger audience. The Book's final word on the episode comes at the sea, where the same king's mobilizers are drowned pursuing a people they had already released under duress.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows what the court hoped the interval would buy. The magicians were assembled at a fixed time — *“So the magicians were assembled at the set time on the appointed day”* (26:38) — and the public was invited to take sides in advance: *“And the people were asked, ‘Will you join the gathering, so that we may follow the magicians if they prevail?’”* (26:39–40). The state's calculation was that a visible victory would settle the question of Moses for the crowd.

The calculation was half right, and the sūrah's own narration concedes the half: *“Moses said, ‘You first.’ So when they did, they deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic”* (7:116). The illusion worked on the eyes; it did not work on the creature. That distinction is the Book's entire theory of sorcery, and the very next verse supplies its demonstration: *“Then We inspired Moses, ‘Throw down your staff,’ and — behold! — it devoured the objects of their illusion!”* (7:117).

Between the court's sentence here and its end at the water, the sūrah will spend four verses on recruitment and a few lines on the contest's outcome — a proportion that is itself the Book's verdict on what the court thought it was doing.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:112

> **to bring you every knowledgeable sorcerer.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Every Clever Magician"**

*Li-yaʾtūka bi-kulli sāḥirin ʿalīm* — "to bring you every clever magician." The second half of the counsel is a procurement order, and it fills the state's cities with recruiters. The sūrah will spend the next verses on the men who answered the call.

**The Same Order in the Other Accounts**

The Book reports the mobilization in identical terms across its Moses narratives. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version is word for word the same counsel: *“Let him and his brother wait and dispatch mobilizers to all cities to bring you every skilled magician”* (26:36–37). Sūrat Yūnus compresses it into a royal command: *“Pharaoh demanded, ‘Bring me every skilled magician’”* (10:79). And Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ reuses the machinery at the exodus, when the same mobilizers are sent after a departing people: *“Then Pharaoh sent mobilizers to all cities”* (26:53).

The repetition matters because it shows what the Book takes the Egyptian state to be: an apparatus that answers challenges by assembling specialists. The same government that had put a people to forced labour now commissions a performance.

**A Market in Illusion**

The Qur'an treats sorcery as a trade, and trades can be hired. Its verdict on the craft appears in Moses' words at the contest's opening: *“What you have produced is mere magic, Allah will surely make it useless, for Allah certainly does not set right the work of the corruptors”* (10:81). The sentence does not deny that the magicians can do something; it classifies what they do.

Egypt's professional class is presented in the Book with none of the exotic colour that later storytelling would add. Its members negotiate a fee (7:113), assemble at an appointed time (26:38), and perform before an audience gathered for the purpose (20:59). They are, in the Qur'an's telling, contractors — and the first thing the reader learns about them is the price of their loyalty.

**The Irony of Scale**

The contrast the sūrah builds is between the resources of an empire and the contents of one shepherd's hand. The court can summon every practitioner in the land; the messenger has a staff and, in another account, a brother. The Book's theology makes the disparity the point rather than an embarrassment, and its own description of the divine response to such mobilizations is compact: Sūrat al-Naml's verdict on the Egyptian audience is that their hearts were convinced and their denial was wilful (27:14), and Sūrat al-Zukhruf notes that the signs kept surpassing one another while the court kept laughing (43:47–48).

What quantity cannot do is make a lie true, and what a state cannot buy is the one thing the contest needs: the ability to perform an act no one can reverse.

**The Recruits Who Defect**

The Book's narrative gives the recruited experts a conclusion of their own. When the truth prevails and the illusions fail, the professionals do not retreat into their contract: *“And the magicians fell down, prostrating. They declared, ‘We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds — the Lord of Moses and Aaron’”* (7:120–122). Sūrat Ṭāhā's account has them name the same Lord (20:70), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records their hope — *“We really hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe”* (26:51).

The state's recruiters had scoured the cities for men who could defend the throne, and the result was the conversion of the entire delegation.

**The Proportion of the Telling**

The sūrah devotes two verses to the recruitment and a single line to the contest's reversal — *“Then We inspired Moses, ‘Throw down your staff,’ and — behold! — it devoured the objects of their illusion”* (7:117). The proportion is the Book's judgment on the two sides of the confrontation: the empire's preparations are extensive and predictable, and the answer requires one sentence of revelation.

Sūrat Ṭāhā's narration adds the detail that keeps the scene from being a formality. When the professionals performed, the Book reports what the bearer of the sign felt: *“Moses responded, ‘No, you go first.’ And suddenly their ropes and staffs appeared to him — by their magic — to be slithering. So Moses concealed fear within himself”* (20:66–67). The messenger's confidence is not the absence of fear; the next verse records the reassurance that follows it. That honesty about the moment is part of what distinguishes the Qur'an's account from a legend of invincibility.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ records what the recruited experts believed they were doing when they finally performed: *“So they cast down their ropes and staffs, saying, ‘By Pharaoh's might, it is we who will surely prevail’”* (26:44). Their oath names the source of their confidence, and the Book's answer is a single verse — the staff that devours (26:45) — followed by the prostration of the men who had just sworn by a throne (26:46).

Sūrat Ṭāhā puts the charge of dispossession in the king's own mouth, which shows how quickly the accusation of verse 110 hardened into policy: *“He said, ‘Have you come to drive us out of our land with your magic, O Moses?’”* (20:57). By the time the professionals are hired, the court has already decided what kind of case it is trying — and the sūrah's reader is meant to notice that the accusation is being amplified in proportion to the state's inability to answer the signs.

The narrative now turns to the men themselves, who arrive at court with a question that reveals what they think they are doing.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:113

> **And the sorcerers came unto Pharaoh. They said, “We shall surely have a reward if it is we who are victorious.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Shall We Have a Reward If We Prevail?"**

*Wa-jāʾa al-saḥaratu Firʿawna qālū inna lanā la-ajran in kunnā naḥnu al-ghālibīn* — the magicians came to Pharaoh and said, "Shall we receive a suitable reward if we prevail?" Before any sign has been performed, the professionals negotiate terms; and the sūrah records the negotiation because it defines the scene that follows.

**The Same Question in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ**

The Book's other account of the contest preserves the exchange as well: *“When the magicians came, they asked Pharaoh, ‘Shall we have a ˹suitable˺ reward if we prevail?’ He replied, ‘Yes, and you will then certainly be among those closest to me’”* (26:41–42). The details agree across the two sūrahs, which is the Book's way of underlining the point: the confrontation is being handled as a piece of state business with a fee attached.

**The Messenger's Wage**

The Qur'an sets its prophets' practice against every such contract, and it does so on nearly every page. Each of the messengers in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ says the same thing in the same words: *“I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds”* (26:109, and its parallels for Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, and Shuʿayb). The Prophet of the sūrah is addressed in the same terms: *“Or are you asking them for a reward ˹for the message˺ so that they are overburdened by debt?”* (68:46).

The contrast is one of the Book's deliberate structures. The court's men work for rank; the messenger works because he was sent. That is why the contest cannot be settled by payment: one side has no price the other can meet.

**The Currency of Patronage**

Pharaoh's answer, in the next verse, promises proximity — being among those closest to him. The Book's narratives show what proximity to the throne is worth over the course of a reign: the same king who offers a court rank here will order crucifixion a few verses later when those closest to him do something he dislikes. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves the exchange with the patron's fury in full: *“Pharaoh threatened, ‘How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic, but soon you will see’”* (26:49).

A reward that can be revoked by the grantor and that he revokes the moment it is earned is, in the Book's analysis, not a reward at all but a lease.

**What the Question Assumes**

The magicians' first sentence contains an entire philosophy of work: skill is exchanged for payment, and the exchange is the whole transaction. It is the same calculus the sūrah's readers met in Madyan, where the elite measured Shuʿayb's preaching by its effect on their trade (7:85–90), and it recurs in the Book wherever a society has learned to price everything.

The Book's answer is not that the magicians were greedy villains. Its narrative gives them dignity, converts them, and records their prayer. What it exposes is the assumption that all loyalty is for sale — because on that assumption the truth has to compete as a performance, and the court has already decided the rules of the competition.

**The Last Time They Negotiate**

Verse 113 is also a hinge in the narrative. The next time the magicians speak in this sūrah, they will not be asking for anything: they will have seen something that removes the question of payment from the table entirely, and their answer to the king's threat will be a statement about return (7:125–126). The contract they came to sign is dissolved before it can be paid.

The Book also records the courtesy with which the messenger met the men who had been hired against him: *“When the magicians came, Moses said to them, ‘Cast whatever you wish to cast!’”* (10:80). The side that possesses the truth can afford to let the other side perform first, and the sūrah's own account of the day includes the same offer in the form of a question (7:115). What the professionals do with the courtesy is the next thing the narrative reports.

The reward they were promised has a counterpart in the Book's description of the ranks God distributes: *“We ˹alone˺ have distributed their ˹very˺ livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some of them in rank above others”* (43:32). A court's hierarchy is a human arrangement of livelihoods and grades; the Book treats it as such, and it never confuses the arrangement with the standing of the people arranged. The magicians' question in verse 113 asks to be moved up one such arrangement; the answer to their deeper need arrives later in the same courtyard, when they prostrate.

The sūrah's reader is left to notice the sequence: the state recruits its experts, the experts name their price, and the price is the reason the state believes it will win.

The scene also fixes the terms of every later confrontation between the Book's messengers and established power. The court never argues the theology of verse 104 or the truth of the demand of verse 105; it converts the encounter into a test of performance and pays for the performance. What verse 113 shows is that the arrangement is acceptable to everyone except the one party whose claim cannot be settled that way.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:114

> **He said, “Yes, and indeed you shall be among those brought nigh.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"And You Will Certainly Be Among Those Closest to Me"**

*Qāla naʿam wa-innakum idhan la-mina al-muqarrabīn* — he replied, "Yes, and you will certainly be among those closest to me." The promise is the state's highest currency: nearness to the throne. The sūrah places the sentence directly before the contest so that the reader can measure what such nearness is worth.

**The Word the Court Borrows**

*Al-muqarrabūn*, those brought near, is one of the Qur'an's terms for the highest company in the hereafter. Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah describes that company with a single clause — *“They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (56:11) — and Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn says of the heavenly register that it is *“witnessed by those nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (83:21). The Book uses the word sparingly, and always of the people whom God has brought close.

The court's moment of generosity appropriates that vocabulary for a bureaucratic rank. A few verses later the same king will threaten the same men with mutilation and crucifixion (7:124), and the Book's readers are being taught to weigh the two uses of the term: one that a ruler grants and withdraws, and one that is not in any creature's gift.

**What Nearness to the Throne Meant**

The Book's narrative supplies the answer in the sequel. When the contest turns, the magicians' new loyalty is treated as a conspiracy: the court accuses them of plotting with the messenger to drive the people out, and the penalty is a public execution (7:123–124). Proximity, in this kingdom, is a lease that ends the moment it is no longer useful.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records the same scene from the king's side with the same emphasis on permission — *“How dare you believe in him before I give you permission?”* (26:49) — as if faith were an appointment the throne had failed to authorize. It is one of the Book's clearest portraits of a state that regards itself as the owner of its subjects' consciences.

**The Conversion the Offer Failed to Prevent**

What actually happens to the men who were promised rank is the reversal the sūrah has been building toward: *“And the magicians fell down, prostrating. They declared, ‘We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds — the Lord of Moses and Aaron’”* (7:120–122). Sūrat Ṭāhā gives the same declaration (20:70), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ adds their hope: *“We really hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe”* (26:51).

The first Egyptians in the Book to believe are the specialists the state had hired to refute the message, and they believe in the middle of the contest, before its outcome is public.

**The Answer to the King's Threat**

The sūrah records their response to the death sentence in two verses that read as a doctrine of patronage: *“They responded, ‘Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return. Your rage towards us is only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us. Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting ˹to You˺’”* (7:125–126). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version is even more compact: *“˹That would be˺ no harm! Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return”* (26:50).

The men who once asked what their reward would be have stopped pricing the transaction. Their last request is for steadfastness, which is the one thing the court cannot buy or withhold.

**The Nearness They Got**

Verse 114's promise is therefore fulfilled in a sense the king did not intend. The court offered rank *among those closest to me*; the Book gives the same men the closeness that matters, and the sūrah's subsequent verses will show them dying under a ruler's sentence with their prayer intact. The Book's irony is exact: the state's reward turned out to be an apprenticeship in the reward it parodied.

Sūrat Ṭāhā preserves the sentence in which the threatened men put the court's offer in its place: *“They responded, ‘By the One Who created us! We will never prefer you over the clear proofs that have come to us. So do whatever you want! Your authority only covers the ˹limited˺ life of this world’”* (20:72). The nearness the king had promised is measured against the proofs they had seen, and found to be a jurisdiction with an expiry date.

The Book's verdict on the patron arrives later in its account of the same reign: *“So when they enraged Us, We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all. And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them”* (43:55–56). The man who distributed ranks among those closest to him ended as the Book's standing illustration of a claim to divinity answered by water; the ranks he granted dissolved with his court, while the men he sentenced were given the closeness that the sūrah's verse had promised as a reward and God granted as a reality.

The narrative now turns back to the contest itself, which occupies the following verses and ends with the failure of the state's entire apparatus of illusion.

The sūrah's arrangement of the episode is thus a lesson in the vocabulary of honour: a king can make a man near to himself, and the same king can have him killed the same afternoon. What cannot be granted or revoked by a court is the nearness the Book names in verse 114 for its own purposes — and which, in the narrative's following verses, the accused men receive from the One they refused to prefer the king to.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:115

> **They said, “O Moses! either you cast, or we will be the ones who cast.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Will You Cast, or Shall We Be First?"**

*Qālū yā Mūsā immā an tulqiya wa-immā an nakūna naḥnu al-mulqīn* — they asked: "O Moses! Will you cast, or shall we be the first to cast?" The question is the last moment in the sūrah's Egyptian narrative at which the court controls the terms of the encounter, and the Book reports it before it takes that control away.

**The Same Offer in the Other Accounts**

Sūrat Ṭāhā preserves the exchange almost word for word: *“They said, ‘O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast’”* (20:65). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ records the answer rather than the question, and the answer is a concession: *“Moses said to them, ‘Cast whatever you wish to cast’”* (26:43). The messenger who has been challenged to produce a proof after the court demanded one (7:106) hands the initiative to the men hired to refute him.

**The Warning They Had Already Received**

Before the contest began, Moses had spoken to the professionals directly, and the Book records it: *“Moses warned the magicians, ‘Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against Allah, or He will wipe you out with a torment. Whoever fabricates ˹lies˺ is bound to fail’”* (20:61). The warning is not a threat about Egypt's politics; it is about the seriousness of the performance they were being paid for. They had been told, in advance, which side of the ledger a fabricated sign would fall on.

**What the Magicians Said Among Themselves**

Sūrat Ṭāhā then reports what the warning produced, and it is one of the Book's sharpest interior scenes: *“So the magicians disputed the matter among themselves, conversing privately. They concluded, ‘These two are only magicians who want to drive you out of your land with their magic, and do away with your most cherished traditions’”* (20:62–63). The experts disagree in private and then settle on the court's line in public.

The vocabulary they reach for is the same vocabulary the court used in verse 110: a threat of dispossession and the loss of inheritance. The narrative does not explain the decision; it merely records that the men who had been warned chose their employer's story.

**The Strategy They Set**

Having decided, they state their plan with a confidence the sūrah lets the reader hear in full: *“So orchestrate your plan, then come forward in ˹perfect˺ ranks. And whoever prevails today will certainly be successful”* (20:64). The sentence is a theory of the day: the outcome will belong to whoever performs better, and performance is a matter of organization.

The Book's counter-theory is stated by Moses in Sūrat Yūnus, after the performance is over: *“What you have produced is mere magic, Allah will surely make it useless, for Allah certainly does not set right the work of the corruptors”* (10:81). Between those two sentences — orchestrate and it will be useless — lies the whole contest.

**The Stage and the Crowd**

The confrontation had been scheduled by the state itself for the day of its own festival, as Sūrat Ṭāhā records (20:59), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows the crowd being recruited as spectators in advance: *“And the people were asked, ‘Will you join the gathering, so that we may follow the magicians if they prevail?’”* (26:39–40). Egypt's public was invited to stake its allegiance on the outcome.

The magicians' question to Moses therefore carries the weight of an audience behind it. What they offer him is the choice of order: first or second, with the crowd watching either way.

**The Messenger's Answer**

Moses answers in the sūrah's own words — *“You first”* (7:116) — and the Book lets the concession stand without commentary. The choice is consistent with everything the sūrah has said about him: he has refused to claim a wage, he has refused to overawe the court with the signs' results, and here he refuses to take the advantage that a first performance would give.

The Book's messengers are not described as tacticians of spectacle. Their confidence rests on something other than the order of casting, and the sūrah's structure proves the point: the sequence of performances turns out to be irrelevant to the outcome, which is decided not by timing but by whose act belongs to the created order.

**The Last Question the Court Asks**

Read in the narrative's sequence, verse 115 is the final sentence in this sūrah in which the initiative is the court's. What follows is the performance, the reversal, the defeat, and the prostration of the state's own experts — a chain of events that no one in the courtyard, including Moses, is controlling.

The choice offered to Moses also belongs to a larger pattern in the Book: the response to a messenger is never coerced, and the Book states the matter in the plainest terms. *“And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve’”* (18:29). Sūrat al-Insān ends its address with the same logic — *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord”* (76:29) — and the contest's etiquette in verse 115 is a small institutional instance of it: the court is allowed to go first.

That is the pattern the Book's histories of power keep repeating. The tyrant's last free act is to set the conditions of his own collapse, and the Book reports the conditions with the same unemphatic attention it gives the collapse itself. The question of verse 115 is answered in verse 116 with two words, and after that the day belongs to the sign.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:116

> **He said, “Cast!” And when they cast, they bewitched the eyes of the people and struck them with awe, and they brought forth a mighty sorcery.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Deceived the Eyes of the People"**

*Fa-lammā alqaw saḥarū aʿyuna al-nās wa-stahwadhūhum wa-jāʾū bi-siḥrin ʿaẓīm* — Moses said, "You first." So when they did, they deceived the eyes of the people, stunned them, and made a great display of magic. The verse describes the performance in the precise vocabulary the Book reserves for sorcery, and every word in it is chosen.

**Magic as an Operation on Perception**

*Saḥarū aʿyuna al-nās* — they bewitched the eyes of the people — states the sphere in which the art works: perception, not creation. The Qur'an's accounts of sorcery consistently locate its effects in the eye and the imagination, and its verdict on the practice follows from that: a thing that can make ropes appear to move has done something to the observer, not to the world. The next verse's serpent does not deceive anyone's eyes; it eats.

**The Audience's Role in the Display**

The verse gives the crowd a verb of its own: *wa-stahwadhūhum*, they carried them away, stunned them. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ had already described the public being organized for exactly this (26:38–40), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf now reports the effect the state had purchased: a population watching a spectacle and being told what it proved.

The Book's narration of the state's power consistently includes this dimension. Pharaoh's authority in these sūrahs rests on display — the magicians' fees, the festival crowd, the ranks and titles — and the contest is designed as a demonstration for the population rather than a search for the truth.

**The Fear the Messenger Felt**

Sūrat Ṭāhā supplies a detail that keeps the scene from being a formality. When the professionals performed, the Book says of the man holding the sign: *“So Moses concealed fear within himself”* (20:67) — and then it records the reassurance he received: *“We reassured ˹him˺, ‘Do not fear! It is certainly you who will prevail’”* (20:68). The messenger's confidence is not the absence of fear; it is what he is given in the face of it.

The Book's honesty in this detail is a matter of doctrine. Prophethood in the Qur'an does not make a human being immune to the pressure of a crowd or the memory of what a state can do to a man; it gives him a sentence to hold on to.

**The Alternative Account the Magicians Had Heard**

The magicians had been warned personally, before the contest, against fabricating a sign (20:61). The Book places that warning early in the scene for a reason: the men now deceiving the eyes of the crowd are not ignorant of what they are doing. Their own private discussion had already concluded that the two brothers were rivals to be removed (20:62–63), and their oath before the performance names the source of their confidence — *“By Pharaoh's might, it is we who will surely prevail”* (26:44).

**What the Display Could Not Do**

The verse's limit is stated by its final words. *Siḥr ʿaẓīm* — a great magic — is still magic, and the Book's verdict on the category appears in Moses' sentence after the display: *“What you have produced is mere magic, Allah will surely make it useless”* (10:81). The greatest possible success in that art remains inside the art's boundaries.

Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ states the principle in a single image: *“In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes”* (21:18). Sūrat Sabaʾ gives it as a promise: *“Say, ‘The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return’”* (34:49).

**The Restraint of the Narration**

The Book does not describe the tricks. It reports the effect on the eyes and the impression on the crowd, and it leaves the technique to the professionals. That restraint is characteristic of the Qur'an's treatment of magic across its narratives: the occult is never the object of curiosity, and the reader is never invited to admire the craft.

What the reader is shown instead is what the craft cannot reach — the creature, the household, the conscience. The next verse begins with the word *waḥy*, revelation, and the contest is over in a clause.

**The Shape of the Contest**

The state arranged the day, paid the men, seated the crowd, and set the order of casting. Everything in the scene belongs to the machinery of Egyptian power, and the machinery had its maximum effect in this verse: the eyes of the people were deceived, and the display was judged great.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ reports the immediate sequel to the display, and it is not the one the state had paid for: *“So the magicians fell down, prostrating. They declared, ‘We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds’”* (26:46–47). The men who had just stunned the crowd with their craft were the first to recognize that the performance they had witnessed was outside their discipline — and their prostration happened before the state's own verdict on the contest was announced.

The Book's commentary on such displays is consistent and bleak. *“They swear by Allah their most solemn oaths that if a sign were to come to them, they would certainly believe in it. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Signs are only with Allah’”* (6:109), and Sūrat Yūnus states the corollary: *“Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth! Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe”* (10:101). The Egyptian crowd had the sign in front of it and the prearranged meaning in its ears.

The sūrah's answer occupies one sentence, and its subject is not the messenger. *Then We inspired Moses* — the reversal is God's act, announced in the passive of revelation, and the Book gives it the same brevity it gives the making of the world.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:117

> **And We revealed unto Moses, “Cast thy staff!” And, behold, it devoured all their deceptions.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"It Devoured the Objects of Their Illusion"**

*Thumma awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an alqi ʿaṣāka fa-idhā hiya talqafu mā yaʾfikūn* — then We inspired Moses, "Throw down your staff," and behold, it devoured the objects of their illusion. The verse answers the display of the previous one, and it does so with a verb that names the difference between the two performances.

**The Command Comes as Revelation**

The verse opens with *awḥaynā*, We inspired: the act that decides the contest is not attributed to Moses' skill, courage, or timing. The command is given, the staff is thrown, and the result follows in the same sentence. Throughout the sūrah's Moses narrative, the signs are identified as God's work performed through a servant — the hand, the staff, the plagues, and the sea are all described this way.

**The Object That Was Already in His Hand**

The staff had been established as an ordinary possession before it became an instrument. Sūrat Ṭāhā's dialogue gives it its first biography — *“It is my staff! I lean on it, and with it I beat down ˹branches˺ for my sheep”* (20:18) — and the command to throw it down had already produced a serpent once before (20:19–20). Verse 117 is therefore the second appearance of the same sign, this time in public and against Egypt's professionals.

**The Verb: Talqaf**

*Talqafu* is the swallowing of something solid, and the Book uses it for what the staff does: it devours *mā yaʾfikūn*, what they were fabricating. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the same verb with the same object — *“it devoured the objects of their illusion”* (26:45) — and Sūrat Ṭāhā states the mechanism in God's instruction to Moses: *“it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick”* (20:69).

The precision is worth pausing over. What disappears down the serpent's throat is not ropes and sticks but an illusion's output. The Book is saying that the thing being destroyed had no substance to begin with; it is removed as appearance is removed, by the arrival of what is real.

**The Instant of Reversal**

*Fa-idhā hiya* — and behold it — the construction the sūrah has used for the staff's first transformation and for the world's signs elsewhere. The Book's narratives of divine intervention characteristically compress the decisive moment into a particle and a pronoun, and the compression is a claim about scale: what changes the situation is not a process but an event.

Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ gives the same act a cosmological form: *“In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes”* (21:18). The staff against the ropes is that verse's image made concrete in a courtyard.

**The Crowd That Had Been Brought to See**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ had reported the public being organized to follow the magicians if they prevailed (26:39–40). The same crowd is present when the illusion vanishes, which means the state's own instrument of legitimacy — the assembled people — became the witness of the reversal. The Book's narratives repeatedly stage the humiliation of power in front of the audience power had gathered.

**What Truth Did Not Need**

The verse contains no counter-illusion. Moses does not produce a better trick, a larger serpent, or a greater spectacle; he throws what he is holding, and the false is gone. Sūrat Sabaʾ states the principle as a sentence God gives the Prophet to speak: *“The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return”* (34:49).

Sūrat al-Raʿd's parable of the valleys adds the image of what remains: the flood carries foam on its surface, and the foam passes while the water that does the work continues (13:17). The Qur'an's thinking about illusion is agricultural and hydraulic — surface versus substance — and its verdict is always the same.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Hinge**

Everything before verse 117 in the Egyptian narrative had belonged to the court's initiative: the accusation, the mobilization, the contract, the terms, the order of casting, and the display. From verse 117 the initiative passes, and the remaining verses of the contest report the consequences in the passive voice of events that no one can stop.

The Book treats the question of signs as settled by its own record of them. *“Nothing keeps Us from sending the ˹demanded˺ signs except that they had ˹already˺ been denied by earlier peoples. And We gave Thamûd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they wronged her”* (17:59), and Sūrat al-Baqarah asks the Children of Israel to count what they had been given: *“Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. And whoever trades Allah's favour — after receiving it — ˹for disbelief˺ should know that Allah is severe in punishment”* (2:211). Egypt's contest is one more entry in that ledger.

What the verse establishes has a name in the Book's vocabulary: the truth is established by God's words. Sūrat al-Shūrā states it against the accusation that the Prophet had fabricated the revelation — *“And Allah wipes out falsehood and establishes the truth by His words”* (42:24) — and the staff that swallowed the illusions is the same principle enacted in front of a crowd. The instrument is a piece of wood; the agent is the word that made the wood.

The next verse names what happened in the courtyard in four words, and the one after it names the state of the men who had arranged the day.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:118

> **Thus the truth came to pass, and whatsoever they did was shown to be false.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The Truth Prevailed"**

*Fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqqu wa-baṭala mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — so the truth prevailed and their illusions failed. Two clauses, and the contest is over. The verse's vocabulary is forensic rather than military: the terms are truth and void, what happened and what was worked.

**The Sūrah's Word for Truth**

*Al-ḥaqq* has been the sūrah's own key word for several verses. Moses claimed in his opening speech that he was *obliged to say nothing about Allah except the truth* (7:105); Shuʿayb prayed that God would judge between his people and him *with truth* (7:89); and the Book's refrain in the towns' narratives is the interrogative about the ones who fabricate against God. The same word now stands as the subject of the decisive sentence.

**What Fail Ḍaʿa Means**

*Baṭala* — to be void, to come to nothing — is the Book's standard verb for the fate of falsehood. Sūrat Sabaʾ's promise is that falsehood *will vanish, never to return* (34:49), and Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ's image is of something crushed and dispersed (21:18). The verse does not say the magicians were arrested or the state was bankrupted; it says what they had made stopped existing as an accomplishment.

**The Illusion as Work**

*Mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — what they had been doing — is the Book's word for human labor, and its choice here is exact. The magicians were professionals: they had a craft, an investment of years, a contract, and a plan. The verse's second clause treats all of it as the thing that failed, and the failure is not moralized; it is simply reported.

**The Public Nature of the Verdict**

The Book stages the reversal in the courtyard the state had prepared and before the crowd it had assembled. There is no private correction, no quiet explanation given to the king afterwards, no negotiated settlement. The Book's habit is to locate its verdicts where the claims were made.

That pattern runs through the sūrah's towns as well: the punishment of Thamūd arrives after the she-camel is killed in the open, and Lot's town is destroyed while its men are in the streets. The Book's God answers in public because the offense was public.

**What the Sign Established**

The verse does not describe the state as convinced; the crowd is not reported as converted; the court's own experts are the next actors. What verse 118 establishes is narrower and more important for the Book's argument: it establishes the possibility of a demonstration that no one in the audience can describe as a performance.

Sūrat Yūnus's version of the same moment gives Moses' sentence: *“Allah establishes the truth by His Words — even to the dismay of the wicked”* (10:82). The words that established it are the words that established the world.

**The Brevity of the Report**

The Qur'an's narration of this contest spends a verse on the state's preparations and one clause on their failure. That proportion is deliberate. The Book is not interested in the drama of a duel; it is interested in what the duel exposes, which is the difference between an act and an imitation of an act.

The same restraint governs the Book's treatment of the plagues that follow in this sūrah (7:133): the signs are listed, the court's broken promises are noted, and the reader is left with the pattern.

**The Men Who Had Come to Work**

The next verse turns from the state's defeat to the state's employees, and the transition is part of the Book's verdict. Pharaoh's hired professionals are the ones who recognize what has happened before the court does; the sūrah's attention moves immediately to them, because the question that matters after a demonstration is not what a government thinks but what the people in the room do.

The Book's account of that decision occupies the next two verses, and it is the only conversion the sūrah records from Egypt's ruling order.

**The Sentence's Standing**

The Book locates the same failure in every nation that treated its position as a license. *“Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺, as did ˹other˺ enemy forces afterwards. Every community plotted against its prophet to seize him, and argued in falsehood to refute the truth by it — so I seized them, and how ˹severe˺ was My punishment!”* (40:5). Egypt's courtyard is one scene in that series, and the sūrah's own summary of the series is the law of adversity it stated at 7:94.

The verse's two nouns also give the reader the standard by which the sūrah has been judging its towns. *Al-ḥaqq* is what God sent; *al-bāṭil* is what human beings fabricate in its place — the measures of Madyan, the idols of ʿĀd, the mountains of Thamūd's confidence, and now an afternoon's display. The sūrah's sequence of destructions follows exactly the line the two words draw, and its narratives end wherever that line is crossed in the open.

Read on its own, verse 118 is one of the Book's plainest statements of its own metaphysics: truth occurs, voidness appears, and the working of human hands is measured against the two. Sūrat al-Raʿd's parable of the flood carrying foam (13:17) gives the same thought as an image of the natural world, and the sūrah gives it here as the report of an afternoon in a courtyard.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:119

> **Then and there they were vanquished and turned back, humbled.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Defeated There and Put to Shame"**

*Fa-ghulibū hunālika wa-nqalabū ṣāghirīn* — so Pharaoh and his people were defeated right there and put to shame. The verse concludes the contest with a verb of defeat, a particle of place, and a word for humiliation that the Book reserves for the enemies of its messengers.

**The Particle of Place**

*Hunālika* — right there, in that place — is the verse's quietest word and one of its most pointed. The Book could have said that the court was defeated; instead it locates the defeat at the site the court had chosen, in the city whose specialists it had hired, before the crowd it had summoned. The humiliation is geographical.

**Being Turned Back, Small**

The second verb, *inqalabū*, means they turned back or were overturned, and the adjective *ṣāghirīn* describes someone made small. The Book uses the same term elsewhere for those brought low before a power they had mocked, and its choice here completes the arc the sūrah opened with Pharaoh's question about what should be done with Moses (7:110).

**The State's Own Instruments**

The humiliation was achieved with the state's own apparatus. Egypt's recruiters had scoured the cities (7:111–112), the professionals had been paid and promised rank (7:113–114), the crowd had been gathered (26:39–40), and the terms had been set by the court itself (7:115). All of it worked as machinery and none of it worked as defense.

**The Book's Pattern for Tyrants**

The sūrah's Egypt narrative is one instance of a pattern the Qur'an applies across its histories. Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt closes the story of the king's god-claim with the same verdict in miniature — *“So Allah overtook him, making him an example in this life and the next”* (79:25) — and Sūrat al-Zukhruf reports the end of the same career with the same economy: *“So when they enraged Us, We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all. And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them”* (43:55–56).

**The Defeat the Crowd Saw**

The audience had been invited to follow the magicians *if they prevail* (26:40). The conditional was the state's confidence stated in public, and the Book records that the condition failed in the same public place. The Qur'an's narratives repeatedly make this point about spectacle: the crowd is never a reliable instrument, because the crowd is watching the event and not the prearranged meaning.

**What Was Not Said**

The verse does not report that Egypt converted, that the court admitted anything, or that the crowd changed sides. The Book never pretends that a demonstration produces faith; Sūrat al-Naml had already stated, of this very audience, that their hearts were convinced and their denial was wilful (27:14). The defeat of verse 119 is a defeat in the open, and the inner verdicts remain what they were.

**The Moment After**

The next verse reports what happened in the courtyard immediately afterwards, and it is the one result the state did not plan for: the experts it had hired fell down in prostration (7:120). The Book's narrative moves from the humiliation of a government to the conversion of its employees in consecutive sentences, which is the Book's own way of ranking the two events.

**The Sūrah's Own Refrain**

Twice already the sūrah has told its readers what happens to those who oppose a messenger's sign: the towns became as though they had never lived, and the corruptors' end was held up for inspection (7:84, 7:103). Verse 119 gives the Egyptian version of that refrain in a single sentence, and it leaves the king alive to continue the story — which the next verses do, with threats of mutilation and a campaign of reprisal against the Israelites (7:123–127).

**The Word the Reader Should Notice**

The humiliation of the court was completed later in the same story, and the Book tells the end of the king with the same brevity: *“So We seized him and his soldiers, casting them into the sea. See then what was the end of the wrongdoers!”* (28:40). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's account of the same event is a single clause — *“Then We drowned the others”* (26:66) — and Sūrat al-Baqarah's telling places the event in the memory of the people who were saved: *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh's people before your very eyes”* (2:50).

Sūrat al-Ṭāhā's narration adds the image that makes the drowning the precise end of the sūrah's story of power: *“Then Pharaoh pursued them with his soldiers — but how overwhelming were the waters that submerged them!”* (20:78). Sūrat al-Dukhān records the command that closed the trap: *“And leave the sea parted, for they are certainly an army bound to drown”* (44:24). The same state that mobilized every magician to defend a throne was drowned in pursuit of a people it had enslaved.

*Ṣāghirīn*, made small, is the exact opposite of what the court had been offered in verse 114: rank among those closest to the throne. The men it was offered to are the ones who will now be raised in the Book's sense, and the king who offered it is the one the sūrah describes as small.

The sūrah's reader is left with the courtyard's own geometry: the men who had ranked themselves for a contest were prostrating, and the king who had promised them rank was still standing.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:120

> **And the sorcerers were cast down prostrate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**They Fell Down in Prostration**

*Wa-ulqiya al-saḥaratu sājidīn* — and the magicians fell down, prostrating. The verse reports the first thing that happened in the courtyard after the illusions failed, and it happens to be a physical act of worship performed by the men the state had hired.

**The Suddenness of the Verb**

The Arabic *ulqiya* is passive and violent: they were thrown down. The Book does not describe a decision, a conference, or a negotiation among the professionals; it describes a body's arrival on the floor. The same verb recurs in the other accounts — *“Then the magicians fell down, prostrating”* (26:46) and *“So the magicians fell down in prostration, declaring, ‘We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses’”* (20:70) — and its consistency across the sūrahs is part of the narrative's force.

**The Sūrah's Own Prostration Scene**

This sūrah had opened with prostration commanded and refused. *“Surely We created you, then shaped you, then said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam,’ so they all did — but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others”* (7:11). The refusal's argument was the argument of rank: *“I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay”* (7:12). In the Egyptian courtyard, the professionals make the opposite choice, and the Book lets the reader hear the echo: the same command, the same clay, a different answer.

**The Order of Events**

The prostration arrives before the state's verdict, before any interrogation, and before Moses has said another word. The men who had gone to the palace to earn a reward stop working for it the moment the staff swallows their craft. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ places the prostration between the devouring and the people's amazement: *“Then Moses threw down his staff, and — behold! — it devoured the objects of their illusion! So the magicians fell down, prostrating”* (26:45–46).

**What Prostration Means in the Book**

The Qur'an treats prostration as the body's acknowledgment of what the mind has already conceded, and it describes the believers by exactly this reflex: *“The only ˹true˺ believers in Our revelation are those who — when it is recited to them — fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud”* (32:15). The sentence names the quality that the professionals demonstrated and the court did not: they were not too proud.

**The Argument That Was Never Made**

The court's case had rested on hierarchy from the beginning: the king's authority over the land, his permission over belief (7:123), his ranks and rewards (7:114). Iblīs's argument at the sūrah's opening was the same argument in another register — fire is better than clay — and the Book's narrator recorded it as arrogance. The magicians' prostration is the Book's quiet refutation of the whole register: what settles the matter is not comparison but submission.

**The Experts Who Recognized the Limit of Their Craft**

The verse's most striking feature is who is on the floor. Not the crowd, not the court, not the philosophers: the trained professionals, the ones who had spent the day demonstrating the outer edge of illusion. Their own craft taught them the difference between a display and an act, and when they saw the act, they stopped classifying and knelt.

Sūrat Ṭāhā's account has them name what they believed: *“We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses”* (20:70). The Book gives them the honor of being Egypt's first believers, and it gives them that honor in the middle of a performance, before the outcome has been announced.

**The State's Loss of Its Own Men**

For the court, the prostration was a defection. The machinery of the day had been assembled so that the crown's specialists would refute a shepherd; instead the specialists joined him. The Book's narratives of power repeatedly end this way — the instruments of coercion turning on their users — and here it happens without a single word of persuasion from the messenger.

**The Book's Word for the Act**

*Sājidīn* is used in the Qur'an for angels (2:34), for believers (32:15), and for the created order generally. Its appearance here is therefore a category claim: the professionals have moved from the audience of a spectacle to the company of those who worship.

**What the Prostration Replaced**

The court had offered them nearness to the throne (7:114). The floor of the courtyard offered nearness to something else, and the Book makes no comment on the exchange. It simply reports the king's reaction in the next verses, and the king's reaction is the best evidence of what the prostration meant.

**A Silence Before the Sentence**

Between the prostration and the confession there is no negotiation, and between the confession and the king's threat there is no pause. The sūrah compresses the moment because everything important in it has already happened in the sign, and the rest of the Egyptian narrative is the state's response to a decision it cannot reverse.

**The Continuity of the Sign**

The prostration is also the narrative's answer to the question the court had asked in verse 110. The chiefs wanted to know what should be done about Moses; their own professionals answered the question by going to the floor. No deliberation in the sūrah has produced a clearer verdict, and it was delivered in the one posture that leaves no room for argument.

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's account makes the sequence public in a single line: the prostration follows the devouring immediately, and the court's own threat follows the prostration (26:46–49). The Book arranges the three events like a syllogism — the sign, the submission, the sentence — and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion about which of the three actually decided the day.

The prostration also completes the logic of verse 117. The illusions were swallowed, and the men who made them stopped defending the swallowed thing. That is the difference the sūrah has been drawing between a message and a spectacle: a spectacle needs its performers to keep performing, and the performers had seen enough.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:121

> **They said, “We believe in the Lord of the worlds,**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Believe in the Lord of All Worlds"**

*Qālū āmannā bi-rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — they declared, "We believe in the Lord of all worlds." The confession takes the title Moses had used in his opening speech to the court — *I am truly a messenger from the Lord of all worlds* (7:104) — and returns it from the mouth of the men the court had hired to destroy his message.

**The Title the Court Had Mocked**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows what the court did with the phrase when it first heard it. Pharaoh asks his question — *“And what is ‘the Lord of all worlds’?”* (26:23) — and Moses answers in three escalating statements: the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between (26:24), the Lord of Pharaoh and his forefathers (26:26), and the Lord of the east and the west and everything between them (26:28). The sūrah's magicians now confess the title their king had refused to understand.

**The Same Confession in the Other Accounts**

Sūrat Ṭāhā's version names the two messengers: *“We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses”* (20:70). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves the declaration at the point of prostration: *“They declared, ‘We ˹now˺ believe in the Lord of all worlds — the Lord of Moses and Aaron’”* (26:47–48). The accounts agree on the statement, and their agreement is the Book's way of establishing the event's public character.

**What the Confession Asserts**

*Rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, the Lord of all worlds, is a claim of universal jurisdiction. In the Egyptian political vocabulary, such claims belonged to the throne and to the kingdom's gods, each with a domain and a temple. The magicians' sentence therefore does two things at once: it names the God who sent Moses, and it removes the king and Egypt's pantheon from the position they had occupied.

**The God of the Worlds and the God of the Courtyard**

The Book's opening sūrah begins with the same title — *“All praise is for Allah — Lord of all worlds”* (1:2) — and its placement there establishes the Qur'an's frame before any story is told. The whole argument of the sūrahs is contained in the contrast the Egyptian courtyard dramatizes: a word that describes everything that exists against a claim that covers one river and one throne.

**Faith Through the Messengers**

The confession's second half, when it comes in the next verse, names Moses and Aaron, and the naming is significant. The Book refuses every attempt to divide loyalty to God from loyalty to the messengers He sent: *“Surely those who deny Allah and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers, saying, ‘We believe in some and disbelieve in others’”* (4:150) are described in the same passage as the ones who have gone far astray. The magicians confess a universal Lord and name the two men through whom He spoke.

**The First Believers of Egypt**

The Book's narratives often mark the first person to respond as a lesson in where faith comes from. Sūrat Yā Sīn's account of the man who came rushing from the farthest end of the city to advise his people to follow the messengers (36:20) gives the type; the Egyptian magicians give another instance, and the Book records their hope at Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ 51: *“We really hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe.”*

**The Political Weight of the Sentence**

In a court where belief required royal permission — a point the king will make explicit in the next verse — the magicians' declaration is an act of insubordination. They had come as contractors; they leave as confessors, and the Book gives them neither apology nor explanation, because the sign they had seen made the choice obvious to the men best equipped to know.

**The Sentence Left Unfinished**

The translation marks verse 121 with a dash, and the pause is deliberate: the confession continues into the next verse, where the two messengers are named. The Book's syntax keeps the reader inside the sentence while the sūrah's next scene shows the state deciding what to do with the men who spoke it.

**What the Court Had Demanded**

The confession also gives the lie to the court's earlier description of the messengers. Verse 110 had called Moses a man who seeks to drive the people from their land; the magicians' sentence names him as a servant of the Lord of everything. Between the accusation and the confession stands nothing but a sign the professionals could evaluate better than anyone present.

The Qur'an's habit of recording the words of converts is itself instructive. The Book preserves the sentences of the magicians, the man from the far end of the city (36:20), the sorcerers of Pharaoh's court, and the believers of the towns, because the faith of the first to speak carries the community's memory. What the Book does not do is report a single word from the crowd that had been assembled to follow the winners.

The Book's placement of the confession is worth noting as well: it comes after the display, after the prostration, and before the state's response. The sūrah gives the magicians a voice in the middle of the crisis and never gives one to the court's other servants, which is the Book's way of ranking the two testimonies.

In verse 106 the court had asked for proof; in verse 109 it had called the proof magic; in verse 113 it had priced its own response. The confession of verse 121 is the answer that the court's demands could not produce and could not prevent, and it arrives from the least likely quarter in the story.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:122

> **the Lord of Moses and Aaron.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The Lord of Moses and Aaron"**

*Rabbi Mūsā wa-Hārūn* — the Lord of Moses and Aaron. The confession ends with two names, and the sūrah's sentence is complete: faith in the Lord of all worlds is faith in the God who sent these two men with a message to this court.

**Why Name the Brothers**

The Qur'an's faith is never left as an abstraction. The Book identifies its messenger in the same breath as the One who sent him, and the magicians' phrase follows that pattern: they do not say "we believe in a God" but "in the Lord of Moses and Aaron," naming the men the court had been arguing with all day. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the formula in the same form (26:48), and Sūrat Ṭāhā reverses the order of the names: *“the Lord of Aaron and Moses”* (20:70).

**Two Men, One Mission**

The narrative had established the pair before the confrontation. Moses had asked for support: *“and grant me a helper from my family”* (20:29), and Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ supplies the reason — *“my brother Aaron is more eloquent than I, so send him with me as a helper to support what I say, for I truly fear they may reject me”* (28:34) — along with the promise: *“We will assist you with your brother and grant you both authority, so they cannot harm you”* (28:35). The confession names both recipients of that grant.

**The Book's Denial of a Divide**

Sūrat al-Nisāʾ states the doctrine against splitting the two loyalties: those who distinguish between Allah and His messengers, believing in some and rejecting others, are described as having gone far astray (4:150). The magicians' sentence keeps the two together: the Lord of all worlds is known through the messengers He sent, and to confess the first is to accept the second.

**Egypt's Own Categories Turned Against It**

Egyptian religion was organized by name and domain. Its gods had temples, priests, festivals, and a monarch who served as their patron; the court knew exactly how to relate a divine name to a human servant. The magicians' confession uses that structure and empties it: the Lord whom they name is the Lord of two fugitives, one of whom Egypt had raised in the palace and the other of whom the court had never heard of.

**The Men the State Had Ordered Arrested**

The Book's account of the confrontation began with Moses' demand for the release of Israel (7:105) and the court's plan to handle him and his brother together (7:111). The magicians' confession takes the pair the state had designated as its enemies and calls them the messengers of the world's Lord. In the political language of the scene, that is the most dangerous sentence of the day.

**The Arc of the Sūrah's Vocabulary**

The sūrah's Moses narrative opened with a claim about *the Lord of all worlds* (7:104) and the narrative now closes the circle with the same phrase on other lips. The Bible's and the Qur'an's shared memory of Egypt is a memory about a name: whom the king claims to be, and whom the enslaved people serve. The Book's method is to have the truth confessed by the group whose function was to protect the claim.

**What Comes Next in the Story**

The king's response is immediate and material: he treats the confession as a breach of protocol and a conspiracy, and answers it with a threat of mutilation and crucifixion (7:123–124). The Book's narrator reports the exchange in the order it happened, which means the reader sees the theology of the verse before he sees the state's sentence on it.

**A Confession Without an Interpreter**

The sūrah gives the magicians no teacher, no instruction, and no further argument from Moses. The sign was sufficient, and the Book's own comment on the crowd's condition — hearts convinced, denial still chosen (27:14) — makes the professionals' alternative the more striking: they accepted what they had seen.

**The Names as Evidence**

The Sūrah's own arrangement of the confession puts the universal title first and the two names second, which is the order of the Muslim testimony itself: the Lord and His messenger. The magicians arrived at the structure without instruction, in the middle of a courtyard, from the ruins of a performance — which is the Book's way of showing that the doctrine is not a formula but a discovery.

The two names also settle the question of mediation that the court had been pressing. Pharaoh's objection throughout had been the human messenger — a man, a shepherd, a fugitive raised in the palace; the confession accepts the mediation rather than dodging it, and in doing so states the Book's consistent position: the Lord of all worlds is known through the men He sends, and the two cannot be separated.

The Book's later communities preserved the memory of the Egyptian believers with precision. The Qur'an identifies them as *the magicians* in four separate sūrahs, always in connection with prostration and always with their confession intact. Their place in the Book's history of Egypt is the one the state had tried hardest to prevent: the first fruits of a message the court had declared a threat to the land.

On the day of the contest, the court's case had been that Moses was a magician like its own men. The sentence *the Lord of Moses and Aaron* is the state's own specialists testifying to the opposite, and the sūrah's reader is left to notice that the testimony was not extracted, purchased, or negotiated. It was volunteered by men who had just watched a staff eat an afternoon's work.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:123

> **Pharaoh said, “You believe in him before I grant you leave! This is surely a plot you have devised in the city, that you might expel its people therefrom. Soon you shall know.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"How Dare You Believe Before I Give You Permission"**

*Qāla āmantum lahu qabla an ādhana lakum* — he said: "How dare you believe in him before I give you permission?" The king's first words to his experts are not about the sign, the trial, or the truth of the message. They are about jurisdiction.

**The Sentence's First Assumption**

The question treats belief as an act requiring authorization, like a transaction or a border crossing. The Book reports the assumption without comment because the rest of the narrative is the comment: the state's men had just done something the state could not regulate, and the reaction is the reaction of a system that has discovered the limits of its reach.

**The Same Scene in the Other Accounts**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves the opening of the sentence in identical words: *“How dare you believe in him before I give you permission?”* (26:49). Sūrat Ṭāhā's version follows the same line: *“How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic”* (20:71). Three sūrahs, one sentence, and the Book's repetition of it makes the point that the doctrine being defended here is not Egyptian but general: the ruler who confuses himself with the Lord.

**The Conspiracy Charge**

The second half of the verse converts a confession into a plot: *“This must be a conspiracy you devised in the city to drive out its people.”* The move is a familiar one in the Book's narratives of power. When the truth cannot be explained as error, it is explained as coordination — the same logic that had produced the mobilization of every magician in the land is now used to explain why the magicians agreed with each other.

**The Accusation as Confession**

The charge is that the messenger's followers wish to drive the people out. The sūrah's reader already knows who is doing the driving: the court had been killing the sons and enslaving the people Israel for generations (7:127's sequel), and the messenger's demand in verse 105 was precisely that the enslaved be released. The court accuses its victims of its own policy.

**What the Towns Had Said**

The sūrah's earlier narratives used the same rhetoric against their messengers. Sodom's people answered Lot by demanding the removal of his followers — *“Expel them from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”* (7:82) — and Madyan's chiefs threatened Shuʿayb with expulsion for the crime of believing (7:88). The Egyptian version adds the apparatus of a state to a sentence that the sūrah has already taught its readers to recognize.

**The Believers of the Book and the States They Faced**

The Book's memory of believers who confronted ruling power is consistently written in this key. Sūrat Yā Sīn's man from the far end of the city advised his people to follow the messengers and was killed for it (36:20–26); the Qur'an records his reward and his regret that his people could not know it. The sūrah's magicians are another entry in that register, and their sentence begins with the same officials-lawyer's question about permission.

**"Soon You Will See"**

The threat is deferred — *fa-sawfa taʿlamūn*, soon you will know — and the deferral is itself a confession of weakness. The court cannot answer the sign, so it shifts the subject to consequences. The next verse supplies the content of the threat, and the Book's reader is meant to notice the order in which the state produces its arguments.

**The Political Theology of Permission**

The Qur'an's answer to the claim of authority over belief is stated to the Prophet in the same terms elsewhere in the Book: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not a controller over them”* (88:21–22). The ruler of Egypt held the opposite doctrine, and the sūrah shows it in operation: a decree about who may believe, issued by a man who had just claimed to know of no god but himself.

**The Men Who Had Believed Without Permission**

The magicians had believed on the strength of a proof, and their faith belongs to the category the Qur'an describes as the recognition of truth by those who see it clearly. The Book's verdict on the court's earlier audience — hearts convinced, denial chosen (27:14) — turns the permission question into its opposite: the real question is who was refusing the evidence, not who authorized its acceptance.

**What the Verse Reveals About the Contest**

Read with verse 109, the sentence shows that the contest had never been about magical skill. The court's men had been selected to preserve a claim about the throne's relation to the divine, and their conversion did not damage a performance — it damaged the claim. That is why the response is a security matter and why the vocabulary in the next verse is the vocabulary of a prison.

**A State Alone With Its Claim**

The Book's portrait in these verses has no supporters left in it. The professionals have prostrated, the crowd has nothing to say, the messenger has not been touched, and the king is left issuing permissions to men who no longer recognize his authority. The narrative's next sentence is the last instrument such a state possesses.

**The Answer Held Back**

The sūrah does not let the magicians reply yet. The king's threat is completed in the following verse, and only then do the believers speak — a sequence that gives the state's violence the floor and the believers the last word.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:124

> **I shall surely cut off your hands and your feet on alternate sides; then I shall surely crucify you all!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"I Will Crucify You All"**

*La-uqaṭṭiʿanna aydiyakum wa-arjulakum min khilāfin thumma la-uṣallibannakum ajmaʿīn* — "I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, then crucify you all." The verse is the state's answer to a confession, and the Book reports it without ornament.

**The Same Sentence in Three Sūrahs**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ preserves it almost identically: *“I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, then crucify you all”* (26:49). Sūrat Ṭāhā's version reports the palace's fury and the same penalty (20:71). The Qur'an's repetition of the phrasing is deliberate: the Book is documenting the standard response of a state that has lost an argument and still controls an army.

**The Penalty in the Book's Legal Vocabulary**

The combination of cross-amputation and crucifixion appears in the Qur'an in one other place, and there it is prescribed by God for those who wage war against Him and His Messenger and spread corruption in the land (5:33). The Book's use of the same words in both passages is one of its sharpest ironies: Egypt's throne applies the penalty of the corruptor to the men who had just submitted to the truth, and it does so in the name of a law that belonged to no one but itself.

**From Performance to Torture**

The court's instruments change with this verse. Up to now the state had worked through spectacle: festivals, crowds, experts, contracts, ranks. When the spectacle fails, the apparatus switches in a sentence to mutilation and death — the same authority that had paid the magicians now proposes to dismember them. The Book's narratives of power treat this switch as the proof of what the power was always for.

**The Book's Restraint in Reporting Violence**

The Qur'an reports the sentence in one line and gives the state no further scenes of cruelty. The Book's narratives elsewhere are similarly sparing: the destruction of the towns is reported in a clause, and the killings of the prophets are noted without description. What the Book attends to is the response of the condemned, which it now supplies.

**The Believers' Reply Begins Here**

Sūrat Ṭāhā records the answer to the threat, and its first clause relocates the entire dispute: *“They responded, ‘By the One Who created us! We will never prefer you over the clear proofs that have come to us. So do whatever you want! Your authority only covers the ˹limited˺ life of this world’”* (20:72). The king's jurisdiction is granted — and then dated.

**The Hope That Replaces Fear**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version of the answer adds the believers' hope and its reason: *“˹That would be˺ no harm! Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return. Indeed, we hope that our Lord will forgive our sins, as we are the first to believe”* (26:50–51). The men who had come to the palace for a fee now measure the day's events against a return they intend to make.

**What the State Could Not Reach**

The Book's point about the punishment is that it operates on the body and nothing else. The magicians had already given what a state can confiscate — their labor, their loyalty, their public standing — and what remained was the life they were being told to surrender. Their answer to the threat is a prayer, and the prayer is for the one thing the court cannot take.

**The Prayer the Sūrah Records**

Al-Aʿrāf's own account closes the episode with their words: *“Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting ˹to You˺”* (7:126). The petition does not ask for escape, reprieve, or the king's change of heart. It asks for endurance and for a death that keeps the confession intact — the exact opposite of what the throne's sentence was designed to produce.

**The Tyrant's Signature**

The Book's histories give crucifixion and mutilation a specific association: they are what established power does when it decides that a claim must be enforced by the body. The Qur'an records the practice in Egypt (here and at 20:71), in the story of the men who opposed Joseph's faith (12:41's sequel), and in the tradition of the communities that tortured believers. The Book's readers are being taught to recognize a state's signature.

**What Rome and Egypt Have in Common in the Book**

The Qur'an does not treat Egyptian cruelty as an exotic detail of a distant kingdom. It reports the language of permissions and punishments in a way that its first audience — living under a Makkan oligarchy that also knew how to pressure, boycott, and threaten — could read directly. Sūrat al-Burūj's account of the persecutors of the believers and their fire is the same lesson in another register.

**The Last Word the State Has**

The Book keeps the sequel in view while reporting the sentence. The same king who threatens crucifixion here will be drowned in the sea a few years later, and the people he refused to release will be made the inheritors of his land's wealth — a reversal the sūrah states in a single line at 7:137. The sentence of verse 124 is therefore not the last word, and the narrative's arrangement is designed to make sure the reader knows it.

Verse 124 is the high point of the court's power in this narrative, and the sūrah's reader knows what follows: the release of Israel, the parting of the sea, and the drowning of the army that pursued them. The Book arranges its narratives so that the tyrant's most absolute sentence is followed immediately by the sentences of the believers, which is the only reply the Book considers necessary.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:125

> **They said, “Truly we turn unto our Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Surely to Our Lord We Will All Return"**

*Innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn* — surely to our Lord we will all return. The sentence is the whole of the believers' answer to a death threat, and its grammar is the reason it works: the king can dismember a body, and he cannot alter a destination.

**The Same Words in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ**

The Book preserves the answer in the other account of the scene as well: *“˹That would be˺ no harm! Surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return”* (26:50). The two sūrahs give the same words in the same situation, which is the Book's way of fixing the sentence's place in the community's memory: this is what the believers of Egypt said when they were told they would be crucified.

**The Vocabulary of Return**

*Munqalibūn* is the participle of turning back or returning, and its root runs through the Qur'an's most familiar sentence: *“Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return”* (2:156), the words the Book assigns to those who meet disaster. Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ states the general law — *“Every soul will taste death. And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned”* (21:35) — and Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt repeats it in a single verse (29:57).

**Why the Threat Lost Its Object**

A state's ultimate instrument is the body, and the body's ultimate fate is death. The Book's doctrine removes death from the category of loss and renames it a return, which leaves the court's threat with nothing to threaten. The magicians are not being brave about dying; they are being matter-of-fact about where they are going.

**The Book's Other Martyrs**

The Qur'an tells the same story in other settings. Sūrat Yā Sīn's man from the far end of the city told his people to follow the messengers and was killed, and the Book's next line is his welcome: *“˹But they killed him, then˺ he was told ˹by the angels˺, ‘Enter Paradise!’”* (36:26). The Egyptian believers answer the sentence before it is carried out, because in the Book's universe the sentence and the outcome are not the same event.

**What the Believers Have Learned**

The men who speak here had begun the day negotiating a fee (7:113). Their first sentence in the sūrah's narrative was a question about wages and their last is a statement about return, and the sūrah gives the reader the distance between the two without a word of explanation. The sign did the teaching.

**The State's Assumption**

Pharaoh's threat assumes that the highest good his subjects can be denied is life. The Book's narratives of power repeatedly expose that assumption: the ruler who can destroy a body believes he has the last word, and the believer who knows where he is going treats the ruler's power as a jurisdiction with a boundary. Sūrat Ṭāhā's version of the believers' answer states the boundary explicitly: *“Your authority only covers the ˹limited˺ life of this world”* (20:72).

**The Sentence's Brevity**

The reply is four words in Arabic. The Book does not give the magicians an argument, a sermon, or a farewell; it gives them a statement of direction. That brevity is characteristic of the Qur'an's martyrs and is part of the Book's theology of speech: when the truth is clear, long answers are a form of doubt.

**The Doctrine Behind the Sentence**

The Qur'an's teaching about death is stated most compactly in the verse that says every soul will taste it (21:35, 29:57), and its practical consequence is a reordering of fear. The Book tells the Prophet not to grieve over the heedless, and it tells believers that the loss of the world is not the loss of anything that belongs to them. The magicians' sentence is that doctrine applied in the middle of a courtroom.

**The Politics of Not Fearing Death**

A state that rules through the threat of death loses its principal instrument when a group of its subjects stops being moved by it. The Egyptian narrative demonstrates the consequence: the king's sentence turns from a punishment into a testimony, and the men he intends to make examples of become the Book's examples instead.

**The Crowd Watching**

The audience gathered for the contest is still present for this exchange, and the Book's narratives never lose track of the public dimension. What the crowd saw that afternoon was a group of the state's own employees choosing the state's death sentence over the state's religion, and saying so in a sentence that anyone could remember.

**The Prayer That Follows**

The reply of verse 125 is completed in the next verse by a petition, and the two belong together: the confession of return is the ground on which the request for steadfastness makes sense. The Book's martyrs do not ask to survive the hour; they ask to be equal to it.

**The Last Word of the Confession**

The men who had asked what their reward would be now state their destination without naming any reward at all. The Book's placement of the two sentences is the sūrah's comment on what the contest had really been about: the court offered ranks, the sign offered nothing but itself, and the men chose the sign and took the consequence.

The Book's readers in Makkah knew the same arithmetic from their own situation, and the sūrah's placement of the sentence — immediately after the king's threat and before his son-killing decree — makes the believers' answer the pivot on which the Egyptian narrative turns from a contest into a persecution.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:126

> **You take vengeance upon us only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came unto us. Our Lord, shower us with patience, and let us die as submitters.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Our Lord! Shower Us With Perseverance"**

*Wa-mā tanqimu minnā illā an āmannā bi-āyāti rabbinā lammā jāʾatnā rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran wa-tawaffanā muslimīn* — "Your rage towards us is only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us. Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting ˹to You˺." The verse diagnoses the persecution and then prays, in that order.

**The Diagnosis**

*Mā tanqimu minnā illā an āmannā* — you have nothing against us except that we believed. The Book states the charge against the persecutor with the same formula in its account of the makers of the ditch: those who tortured the believers *“resented them for no reason other than belief in Allah — the Almighty, the Praiseworthy”* (85:8). The Qur'an's analysis of religious persecution is consistently this: the offense is the faith itself, and everything else is pretext.

**The Signs They Believed In**

The clause *when they came to us* matters. The magicians are not speaking of an inherited faith or a family tradition; they are speaking of something they saw that day in the courtyard and could not unsee. The confession's grounds are the signs of the Lord, and the Book's narratives of belief frequently turn on that moment of recognition — the point at which a person's own competence confirms what the message claims.

**The Petition for Patience**

*Rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran* — our Lord, pour out patience upon us. The image is of a vessel being emptied over its recipients, and the Qur'an uses the same verb in the prayer of the Believers' community facing Goliath's army: *“Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, make our steps firm, and give us victory over the disbelieving people”* (2:250). In both scenes, the request is not for the removal of the trial but for the capacity to endure it.

**The Book's Doctrine of Patience**

Sūrat al-Baqarah states the pairing that the Qur'an recommends to the anxious: *“O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient”* (2:153). Sūrat al-Zumar's version adds the promise: those who are mindful of their Lord and do good in this world have a good reward, and Allah's earth is spacious for those who need to move (39:10). The Egyptian believers ask for the first of the two, and the narrative grants them both.

**"Let Us Die While Submitting"**

*Wa-tawaffanā muslimīn* — and take us while we are submitting. The phrase is the Book's name for a death that arrives before the faith does. The Qur'an records the same request on Joseph's lips: the prayer of a man who had lived through imprisonment and power and wanted his ending to match his beginning. The magicians' petition is a single sentence about the one thing they can still control — the state of their souls at the moment the king's sentence is carried out.

**What the Prayer Does Not Ask**

There is no request for escape, for the king's conversion, for the court's embarrassment, or for the sea to open. The Book's whole account of the Egyptian believers' martyrdom is their confession and this prayer; they do not negotiate for relief. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's version names the only hope they express — forgiveness and the honor of being the first to believe (26:51).

**The Target the State Had Chosen**

The throne's purpose in executing them was to produce recantation or terror. The prayer dismantles both aims: the men ask for the strength to keep the confession and for a death inside the faith they are being killed for. The ruler's sentence thus becomes, in the Book's telling, an instrument the believers use to complete a life rather than an instrument that destroys one.

**The Book's Martyrs and Their Words**

The Qur'an's martyrs speak sparingly, and their sentences have the same shape across the sūrahs. The man of Yā Sīn wishes his people knew what his Lord had given him (36:26); the magicians ask for patience and a good death; the Believers at the battle of Badr ask for firm footing. The Book's pattern suggests that martyrdom in the Qur'an is a religious act with a vocabulary, and that the vocabulary is about the person's own end rather than the enemy's defeat.

**The Court's Silence**

The narrative gives the accused the floor twice and the prosecutor one sentence. The exchange's proportions are the sūrah's judgment: the state speaks to threaten, and the believers speak to confess, and only one of the two speeches survives the moment. The crowd that had been assembled to watch a magic show hears a prayer instead.

**The Doctrine of the Deathbed**

The Qur'an's instruction about endings is not reserved for martyrs. The Book teaches Muslims to ask for a good end in ordinary circumstances, and the magicians' petition is the same request made under a sentence of crucifixion. The verse's teaching is therefore portable: what a person wants at the end is a summary of what he has wanted all along.

**The Answer Recorded**

The sūrah does not report the execution. It reports the prayer, and then turns to the state's politics in the next verse. The Book's refusal to describe the killing is itself a verdict: the story it wants told is what the condemned said, and the crown's machinery is left to be inferred from the sentence it passed.

**The Sentence That Outlived the Court**

The words *our Lord, shower us with perseverance, and let us die while submitting* are recited by Muslims facing their own trials in every generation, which is the form the Book's promise takes: the believers' prayer in a courtyard in Egypt became the community's inheritance.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:127

> **The notables among Pharaoh’s people said, “Will you leave Moses and his people to work corruption in the land and to leave you and your gods?” He said, “We shall slay their sons and spare their women. Truly we are above them, dominant.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Kill Their Sons and Keep Their Women"**

*Qāla al-malaʾu min qawmi Firʿawna atadhru Mūsā wa-qawmahu li-yufsidū fī al-arḍi wa-yadharaka wa-ālihatak* — the chiefs of Pharaoh's people protested: "Are you going to leave Moses and his people free to spread corruption in the land and abandon you and your gods?" He responded, "We will kill their sons and keep their women. We will completely dominate them."

**The Charge of Corruption**

The elite's protest uses the sūrah's own word for the crime God destroys societies for. The towns of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Sodom, and Madyan were condemned for corrupting the earth, and the sūrah told its readers to look at their end (7:84, 7:103). Egypt's notables apply the label to a people whose complaint, in the same sūrah, is that they have been slaughtered and enslaved.

**The Real Fear**

The second clause names what the court actually feared: the loss of the throne and the gods in whose name it ruled. The sūrah's earlier towns' elites had the same anxiety — Madyan's chiefs feared the loss of their commercial standing, ʿĀd's leaders feared the loss of their preeminence — and the pattern shows the Book's consistent view of established power: its arguments are always about what it stands to lose.

**The Apparatus of a Genocide**

The king's answer is a policy statement, and the Book has recorded its history earlier in the same narrative: *“Indeed, Pharaoh ˹arrogantly˺ elevated himself in the land and divided its people into ˹subservient˺ groups, one of which he persecuted, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women”* (28:4). Sūrat al-Baqarah reminds Israel of the same fact when it recounts the deliverance: *“˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women”* (2:49).

**The Strategy and Its Failure**

The policy's aim was demographic: destroy the rising generation that could challenge the state and keep the labor force that served it. The Book reports the aim and its outcome in the same breath. Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ introduces the persecution and the divine answer together: *“But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land, making them models ˹of faith˺ as well as successors; and to establish them in the land; and through them show Pharaoh, Hamân, and their soldiers ˹the fulfilment of˺ what they feared”* (28:5–6).

**The Child Who Escaped the Policy**

The Book makes the strategy's failure concrete by placing the deliverer inside the families being targeted. Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ records the instruction given to Moses' mother: *“We inspired the mother of Moses: ‘Nurse him, but when you fear for him, put him then into the river, and do not fear or grieve. We will certainly return him to you’”* (28:7). The state's policy was applied to the household that produced the man who would end the state's claim on the people.

**What Repression Produces**

The Book's histories of persecution share a pattern: the violence intended to eliminate a threat manufactures the conditions for deliverance. Israel's numbers grow under the policy; the deliverer is raised in the palace that ordered the killing; and the same sūrah that reports the oppression reports the inheritance of the oppressor's land by his victims (7:137). The Qur'an's narratives treat this as a law rather than a coincidence.

**The Elites' Function**

The verse's opening word is *al-malaʾ*, and the sūrah has used it for the elite of every community it has described. The class appears here in its Egyptian form: not advisors of conscience but managers of the crown's fears, pressing the king toward maximum violence in the name of the throne and the gods. Their intervention is what converts a stalled contest into a campaign.

**The Gods in the Sentence**

The notables' mention of the gods is the verse's most revealing detail: their case for killing a people is that monotheism threatens the kingdom's pantheon. The Book's narratives of idolatry consistently connect the religious claim to the political economy that sustains it — the temples, the festivals, the tribute — and the Egyptian court states the connection without embarrassment.

**The Terms of the Policy**

"We will kill their sons and keep their women" is a sentence about labor, lineage, and power, stated with the flatness of an administrative order. The Book records it without commentary, which is the technique it uses for the worst things its histories contain. The reader is left to supply the judgment, and the narrative's next two verses supply the human response instead.

**The State's Confidence**

The king's final clause — "we will completely dominate them" — is the throne's assessment of its own position, and the Book lets it stand as such. The narrative will test it against the sea, and the reader who has followed the sūrah's other towns knows that the confidence is the last thing such a court is ever allowed to keep.

**The Verses That Answer the Policy**

Moses' response is placed immediately after the sentence: a call to seek God's help and to be patient, followed by a promise about the earth's inheritance (7:128–129). The Book's arrangement sets the regime's policy and the prophet's counsel side by side, and the reader is invited to see which of the two was speaking about the future.

**The Outcome Recorded Elsewhere**

The sūrah will later report the end of the policy in a single verse: *“And ˹so˺ We made the oppressed people successors of the eastern and western lands, which We had showered with blessings”* (7:137). Egypt's plan for its subject population is answered by the Book with a clause about inheritance, and the answer is given before the narrative has finished describing the persecution.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:128

> **Moses said unto his people, “Seek help from God and be patient. Truly the land belongs to God; He bequeaths it to whomsoever He will among His servants. And the end belongs to the reverent.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"The Earth Belongs to Allah"**

*Istaʿīnū bi-llāhi wa-ṣbirū inna al-arḍa li-llāhi yūrithuhā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi wa-l-ʿāqibatu li-l-muttaqīn* — "Seek Allah's help and be patient. Indeed, the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺. He grants it to whoever He chooses of His servants. The ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous." The verse is Moses' counsel to a people whose persecution has just been renewed, and it has three parts: a discipline, a doctrine, and a promise.

**The Discipline of Seeking Help**

*Istaʿīnū bi-llāhi* — seek help from Allah — is the Book's instruction to the overwhelmed, and the Qur'an pairs it with patience in its most familiar form: *“O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient”* (2:153). The counsel given to Israel under a death decree is therefore not a program of resistance but a discipline of dependence: the community is told where to take its fear.

**Patience as a Political Position**

*Ṣabr* in the Qur'an is not passivity; it is the holding of a position without despair. The sūrah's previous chapter of Israel's story is the state's decision to kill its sons (7:127), and the prophet's answer is a discipline that can be practiced under such a policy: patience, supplication, and the refusal to accept the regime's account of reality as final.

**Who Owns the Land**

The verse's central claim is jurisdictional, and it answers the court's politics directly. Pharaoh's authority had been presented as a property right in the land — *“Have you come to drive us out of our land”* (20:57), *“These ˹outcasts˺ are just a handful of people, who have really enraged us”* (26:54–55). Moses' sentence removes the premise: the earth belongs to Allah, and Egypt's throne is a tenant.

**The Doctrine of Succession**

*Yūrithuhā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi* — He gives it in inheritance to whoever He wills of His servants. The Qur'an develops the theme throughout: *“Your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, Full of Mercy. If He wills, He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills”* (6:133), and Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own verse about the earth's inheritance is echoed in Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ's citation of the Psalms: *“My righteous servants shall inherit the land”* (21:105).

**The Promise to the Oppressed**

The Book states the doctrine as a promise to precisely the people the throne had decided to eliminate: *“But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land, making them models ˹of faith˺ as well as successors; and to establish them in the land”* (28:5–6). Sūrat al-Nūr generalizes it for the Prophet's community: *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good that He will certainly make them successors in the land, as He did with those before them”* (24:55).

**The Outcome Belongs to the Righteous**

*Wa-l-ʿāqibatu li-l-muttaqīn* — the end result belongs to the mindful. The sūrah has used the term *ʿāqiba* as its refrain for the destruction of the towns: Sodom's end was held up for inspection (7:84), the corruptors' end was named before Moses' story began (7:103). The word returns here as a promise to those on the receiving end of imperial policy.

**The Test Inside the Promise**

The Book consistently attaches a condition to inheritance, and the condition is moral rather than ethnic. Sūrat al-Anʿām states the arrangement in the vocabulary of examination: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you”* (6:165). Sūrat Yūnus's version of the same theme is addressed to communities as such: *“Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act”* (10:14).

**Moses the Leader**

The verse shows the Book's model of prophetic leadership: not a call to arms but a steadying of a frightened community. The Book's Moses is a man who will later remind his people of what they have been given — *“O my people! Remember Allah's favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign”* (5:20) — and whose counsel here prepares them for a deliverance they cannot organize themselves.

**The Book's Realism About the Oppressed**

The very next verse reports the people's complaint, which means the sūrah does not idealize the community that receives this counsel. The Qur'an's narratives of Israel are full of such moments — the demand for a different god (7:138), the refusal to enter the land (5:22), the calf in the wilderness (20:85) — and the Book's honesty about them is what makes its portrait of deliverance credible.

**No Call to Revolt**

It is worth noting what verse 128 does not contain. There is no instruction to fight the state, organize a rebellion, or take the land by force. The Book's Egypt narrative assigns the decisive act to God — the sea, the plagues, the collapse of the army — and assigns the community the discipline of patience. Later Islamic law would develop its own rules for resistance; this passage's emphasis is elsewhere.

**A Maktan Community's Text**

For the sūrah's first audience, living under an oligarchy that controlled their trade and their safety, the verse's counsel had an obvious application, and the Qur'an's Makkan material repeats it in various forms. What is offered is not a strategy for winning the city but a way of remaining a community under pressure.

**The Condition Repeated**

The promise of the outcome to the righteous is not a guarantee to the passive. The Book's later history of Israel shows what the condition means in practice: the generation that left Egypt is tested in the wilderness, and the sūrah will report their failures in the verses that follow this narrative (7:138–141).

**What Moses Does Not Say**

Moses gives no timetable, no account of how the enemy will fall, and no description of the people's future state. He gives the doctrine of ownership and the discipline of patience, and the Book's narrative supplies the rest in its own time. That reticence is the measure of the Book's trust in its own story.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:129

> **They said, “We were persecuted before you came to us, and after you came to us.” He said, “It may be that your Lord will destroy your enemies and make you vicegerents upon the earth, that He may observe how you behave.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Have Always Been Oppressed"**

*Qālū ūdhīnā min qabli an taʾtiyanā wa-min baʿdi mā jiʾtanā* — they complained: "We have always been oppressed — before and after you came to us ˹with the message˺." The complaint is direct, and the Book records it without softening because the community's honesty about its condition is part of the story.

**The Memory of Suffering**

*Ūdhīnā* — we have been harmed — states the community's experience: a persecution that predates the messenger's arrival and continues after it. The Qur'an validates the memory elsewhere in its address to Israel, and the deliverance it will shortly describe is presented as an answer to exactly this history (2:49; 28:4).

**The Book's Honesty About Israel**

The Qur'an's narratives of the Children of Israel are remarkable for their refusal of hagiography. The Book records the complaints about food in the desert, the refusal to enter the Holy Land — *“we will never ˹be able to˺ enter it”* (5:22) — and the worship of the calf while Moses was on the mountain (20:85). The sūrah's readers are being given a community they can recognize.

**The Prophet's Answer**

Moses does not rebuke the complaint. He answers it with a promise laced with a rare word: *ʿasā rabbukum* — perhaps your Lord will destroy your enemy and make you successors in the land. The particle *ʿasā*, "perhaps," is the Book's grammar of reverent hope: the prophet speaks of God's future action without presuming to schedule it.

**The Vocabulary of Succession**

*Yastakhlifakum fī al-arḍ* — He may make you successors in the land. The root *khalafa* is the Qur'an's term for the transfer of stewardship, and the Book uses it for individuals, communities, and generations: *“Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act”* (10:14); *“He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills”* (6:133); and the promise to the believers of succession with security (24:55).

**The Clause That Tests Everything**

*Fa-yanẓura kayfa taʿmalūn* — to see how you will do. The Book's promises of inheritance are consistently attached to an examination, and the clause here is the sūrah's warning that the deliverance it is describing will not be the end of the story. What a community does with a state it has inherited is, in the Book's scheme, the real question.

**What the Book's History Shows**

The Qur'an supplies the answer to that question in its later narratives of the same people: a generation that saw the sea split asks for an idol like the ones they had seen (7:138), refuses to enter the land that had been promised (5:21–22), and needs prophets and kings to be reminded of the covenant. The sūrah's Moses is speaking to the parents of that generation.

**Deliverance as a Beginning**

The Book never presents political liberation as the goal of its narratives. Egypt's end at the sea is followed immediately by the wilderness, the law, and the tests; the inheritance of the land is followed by the demand to live justly in it. Verse 129 states that structure in advance, and the Qur'an's readers are meant to hear in it a warning about every later victory.

**Why the Complaint Is Not a Defect**

Read in the Book's terms, the people's complaint is not faithlessness; it is an accurate description. They had been enslaved and their sons killed. The Qur'an's narrator gives them the sentence because the Book's moral universe includes the possibility of speaking honestly about suffering, and because the answer Moses gives requires the situation to be stated plainly first.

**The Prophet Who Does Not Blame**

Moses' response contains no accusation of weak faith. The Book's prophets correct their communities for idolatry and injustice, and they also give counsel in the register of this verse: a doctrine, a discipline, and a hope. The sūrah's presentation of prophetic speech has room for both, and the reader learns the difference from the context.

**A Community Between Two Statements**

Israel in these verses stands between the court's policy of killing sons and the promise of succession, and the narrative gives them only a counsel of patience to hold. The Book's use of the interval is a consistent feature of its histories: the period between a promise and its fulfilment is where the Qur'an locates the lives of most believers.

**The Promise's Condition Stated Later**

The sūrah will report what the community did with its deliverance in the very next chapter of the narrative, including the request for a visible god and Moses' rebuke (7:138–141). Verse 129's "to see how you will do" is therefore answered within the same sūrah, and the answer is not flattering.

**The Sūrah's Own Instruction to Its Readers**

For the Prophet's community in Makkah, the verse's sequence was directly instructive: the promise of relief was real, and the time between the oppression and the relief was to be spent in the discipline of verse 128. The Book's later community would read the same passage under its own governments and draw the same conclusion.

**The Sentence That Follows**

The narrative now moves from the community's condition to the state's, and the sūrah's next verses report the pressure applied to Egypt itself: the famine, the shortage, and the signs that follow one another until the sea.

The Qur'an's later history of the same people shows what the clause was guarding against. The generation that crossed the sea would ask for a visible god, refuse the land, and worship the calf; the Book records all of it in the same sūrah, and it records it without surprise, because the question of verse 129 had already been asked and answered in advance.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:130

> **And We indeed afflicted the House of Pharaoh with drought and a shortage of crops, that haply they would be reminded.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Famine and Shortage of Crops"**

*Wa-laqad akhadhnā āla Firʿawna bi-l-sinīna wa-nuqṣin mina al-thamarāti laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn* — indeed, We afflicted Pharaoh's people with famine and shortage of crops so they might come back ˹to their senses˺. The verse opens the sūrah's account of the plagues, and it states the purpose of the affliction before it lists any of them.

**What Was Sent**

*Al-sinīn* are the lean years, and *nuqṣ mina al-thamarāt* is the shortfall in produce — the two forms a premodern famine took in a river economy. The Qur'an describes an Egypt whose prosperity depended on the Nile and the harvest, and it reports that both were struck while a court was occupied with a shepherd and his signs.

**The Purpose Clause**

*Laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn* — so that they might remember. The phrase is the sūrah's own grammar of hardship, and it repeats the law stated earlier in the same sūrah: *“Whenever We sent a prophet to a society, We afflicted its ˹disbelieving˺ people with suffering and adversity, so perhaps they would be humbled”* (7:94). What was a general rule in verse 94 is now applied to Egypt by name.

**Hardship as Summons**

The Book's doctrine is that afflictions are addressed to a community's attention rather than merely to its treasury. Sūrat al-Anʿām states the mechanism and its typical failure: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired”* — and the sūrah's earlier passage in al-Aʿrāf records how often the summons was refused. Egypt's famine is one more instance of the same summons.

**The Book's Earlier Egyptian Famine**

The Qur'an knows another famine in Egypt, and it was survived by foresight. Sūrat Yūsuf reports the king's dream of seven fat cows and seven lean ones (12:43) and Joseph's interpretation, which included seven years of hardship to be provisioned against (12:48). The state in that story listened to a prisoner; the state in this one is busy commissioning magicians.

**The Plagues That Follow**

The sūrah expands the affliction into a series: *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood — all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people”* (7:133). The Book's list runs from the agricultural to the domestic, and its last item, blood, touches the Nile itself — the substance of Egypt's life and of its religion.

**Signs Addressed to a Civilization**

Egypt's gods presided over the river, the sun, the harvest, and the household, and the plagues are chosen to reach each domain. Sūrat al-Zukhruf notes that the signs surpassed one another as the sequence continued (43:48), and the sūrah's own account shows the court's response varying between panic and amnesia.

**The Court's Reading of the Famine**

The next verse reports how the palace interpreted what was happening, and its interpretation is a case study in the Book's analysis of unbelief. In prosperity the court said, *“This is what we deserve”* — credit claimed — and in adversity it blamed Moses and his people: *“they blamed it on Moses and those with him”* (7:131). The famine was thus read as evidence for whichever position the court already held.

**The Political Problem of Hunger**

A throne that claims divine sanction and cannot feed its people has a legitimacy problem, and the sūrah's narrative records how the problem was handled: by finding a foreigner to blame. The Book's histories of power repeatedly show this sequence — the failure of the regime's claims, followed by the manufacture of an internal enemy.

**The Difference Between Warning and Punishment**

The sūrah's distinction between the two is stated by its purpose clauses. The famine aims at remembrance; the drowning at the end of the narrative is judgment. The Book's narratives preserve the order — warning, then judgment — and the sūrah's readers are shown a court that exhausted the first and met the second.

**The Request That Follows**

When the plagues become intolerable, the court asks Moses to pray: *“O Moses! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. If you help remove this torment from us, we will certainly believe in you and let the Children of Israel go with you”* (7:134). The men who had called him a magician and threatened his followers plead with him as an intercessor.

**The Broken Promise**

The sūrah's next verse records the outcome of that plea, and it is the pattern the Book applies to the whole sequence: *“But as soon as We removed the torment from them — until they met their inevitable fate — they broke their promise”* (7:135). Relief did not produce remembrance in a court that had decided the question already.

**The Summons to Remember**

*Yadhdhakkarūn* — the word the verse chooses is the vocabulary of reminder rather than repentance, and the Book's use of it here is exact: the plagues were meant to make the court reconsider, and the court's failure was a failure of memory, repeated after every respite.

**The Narrative's Next Move**

With the purpose clause stated and the afflictions named, the sūrah turns to the two responses the plagues produced — the court's self-serving interpretation (7:131) and its outright refusal of signs (7:132) — before it reaches the sea.

The Book's summary of the sequence is given later in the sūrah, in the list of the signs themselves: floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood (7:133). The Book's God does not send affliction as a bare display of power — the purpose clause of verse 130 governs the whole series, and the court's refusal to remember is what turns a summons into the reason for judgment.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:131

> **But whenever good came to them, they would say, “This is ours.” And if an evil befell them, they would consider it an ill omen on account of Moses and those who were with him. Nay, their ill omen lies with God, though most of them know not.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"This Is What We Deserve"**

*Fa-idhā jāʾat-humu al-ḥasanatu qālū lanā hādhihi wa-in tuṣibhum sayyiʾatun yaṭṭayyarū bi-Mūsā wa-man maʿahu alā inna-mā ṭāʾiruhum ʿinda Allāhi wa-lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn* — in times of prosperity they said, "This is what we deserve," but in adversity they blamed it on Moses and those with him. Surely all is destined by Allah. Yet most of them did not know.

**The Book's Analysis of Credit and Blame**

The verse describes a disposition the Qur'an returns to repeatedly: a person reads good fortune as a verdict on his own worth and misfortune as evidence about someone else's. Sūrat al-Fajr states the pair almost in so many words: *“Now, whenever a human being is tested by their Lord through ˹His˺ generosity and blessings, they boast, ‘My Lord has ˹deservedly˺ honoured me!’ But when He tests them by limiting their provision, they protest, ‘My Lord has ˹undeservedly˺ humiliated me!’”* (89:15–16).

**The Claim of Ownership**

*Lanā hādhihi* — "This belongs to us" — is a claim about entitlement rather than gratitude. Sūrat Fuṣṣilat reports the same sentence in response to relief: *“And if We let them taste a mercy from Us after being touched with adversity, they will certainly say, ‘This is what I deserve’”* (41:50). Sūrat al-Zumar gives it in the first person singular of prosperity — *“I have been granted all this only because of ˹my˺ knowledge”* (39:49) — and Sūrat Hūd names the tone: *“totally prideful and boastful”* (11:10).

**The Verse's Word for Superstition**

*Yaṭṭayyarū bi-Mūsā* — they took an evil omen from Moses — is the Book's term for the transfer of blame onto a person or a group in hard times. The Qur'an reports the same reflex in other towns: the people of the messengers said *“We definitely see you as a bad omen for us”* (36:18), and the reply to them was *“Your bad omen lies within yourselves”* (36:19). The Egyptian court blames its own failed harvest on foreigners.

**The Corrective Clause**

*Alā inna-mā ṭāʾiruhum ʿinda Allāh* — surely their omen lies with Allah. The sūrah's clause restores the question of causes to its proper place and simultaneously refuses the two errors the court had committed: attributing good to themselves and evil to the messenger. The Book's teaching is stated in similar terms about the believers' own battles: *“Say, ‘Nothing will ever befall us except what Allah has destined for us’”* (9:51).

**The Sovereignty Behind the Argument**

The verse's final clause before the reproach, *all is destined by Allah*, is the sūrah's declaration of where the whole sequence is being decided. The Qur'an repeats the formulation in many forms — *“To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth; and to Allah all matters will be returned”* (3:109) — and its function in narratives of famine and plague is to close the conversation about proximate causes.

**The Qur'an's Diagnosis**

Sūrat al-Rūm's verse supplies the Book's deepest account of what the plagues were for: *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their misdeeds, so that perhaps they may return ˹to the right path˺”* (30:41). Egypt's disasters belonged to that economy, and the court's interpretation of them was therefore part of what was being tested.

**"Most of Them Did Not Know"**

The verse's closing clause is the Book's standard observation about the failure of diagnosis. The court had the fact of the famine and the question it was meant to raise; it did not have the reading. The sūrah's earlier narratives had already taught its readers how the pattern works — *“So We seized them suddenly while they were unaware”* (7:95) — and the phrase reappears here as a summary of a whole policy.

**The Scapegoat's Function**

The court's decision to blame the prophet also served a political purpose: the palace's legitimacy could not afford an admitted failure of the kingdom's gods and the king's claim. The Book notes the rhetorical function of the accusation without arguing with it, because the narrative's subsequent verses show what the accusation bought: nothing.

**The Pattern the Sūrah Established**

The rules had already been stated. *“Whenever We sent a prophet to a society, We afflicted its ˹disbelieving˺ people with suffering and adversity, so perhaps they would be humbled”* (7:94); the next verses described the substitution of good days for bad and the people's conclusion that their fathers had been touched by the same things (7:95). Egypt is the sūrah's demonstration case, and the court's reaction of verse 131 is the reaction the earlier verses predicted.

**The Book's Warning About Relief**

The Qur'an's constant warning is that ease tests a person as much as hardship. Sūrat al-Anʿām states the mechanism that follows repeated failure — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44) — and the court's confident reading of its own prosperity is exactly the condition described.

**The Court's Final Posture**

By the time the plagues end, the court's position is fully stated: prosperity is its due, adversity is the work of a foreigner, and the whole question of who is behind events is settled in its own favour. The Book's verdict on that posture is contained in the two clauses of this verse: the omen is with Allah, and the court did not know.

**The Verse's Quiet Irony**

The men who called Moses a magician and his followers a plot (7:109, 7:123) now find in him the cause of failed crops. The sūrah's reporting of the accusation gives the reader the measure of a state that had run out of explanations and had not yet run out of confidence.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:132

> **And they said, “Whatever sign you may bring to bewitch us thereby, we will not believe in you.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"No Matter What Sign You Bring, We Will Never Believe"**

*Wa-qālū mahmā taʾtinā bihi min āyatin li-tasḥaranā bihā fa-mā naḥnu laka bi-muʾminīn* — they said: "No matter what sign you may bring to deceive us, we will never believe in you." The sentence is the court's final position on evidence, and the Book preserves it for what it reveals about the logic of refusal.

**The Charge of Sorcery**

*Li-tasḥaranā* — in order to bewitch us — is the label the Book's unbelievers attach to revelation in sūrah after sūrah. The people of Noah called the messenger's message sorcery (54:9), Pharaoh's court described the sign as *clear magic* (10:76), and Qur'anic towns reached for the same word when they could find no other. The Egyptian court's version is stated in the register of formal refusal: the signs are tricks by definition.

**The Shape of Unconditional Rejection**

The sentence does not say "we will not believe in this sign." It says that no sign, however presented, will produce belief. The Book treats that posture as the point at which prophecy confronts something other than ignorance — Sūrat al-Aʿrāf had already stated the divine correspondence: *“I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant in the land without right, and even if they see every sign they will not believe in it”* (7:146).

**The Same Refusal in the Makkan Sūrahs**

The Qur'an records countless versions of the sentence for the Prophet's own audience. *“They challenge ˹the Prophet˺, ‘We will never believe in you until you cause a spring to gush forth from the earth for us’”* (17:90), and the passage continues through gardens, a house of gold, and a book brought down from heaven (17:91–93). *“They say, ‘Our hearts are veiled against what you are calling us to, there is deafness in our ears, and there is a barrier between us and you’”* (41:5).

**The Demand That Would Not Have Been Satisfied**

Sūrat al-Anʿām anticipates every variation of the demand and closes the door: *“If We had sent down to you a written scripture on a sheet of paper and they touched it with their hands, the disbelievers would still have said, ‘This is nothing but clear magic’”* (6:7). The Book's point is that the obstacle is not evidential. Sorcery is the word a settled refusal uses when a proof arrives.

**The Earlier Refusal in This Sūrah**

This is not the court's first refusal in the story. Verse 109 had already classified the signs as magic and submitted the question to the throne; verse 123 had answered a confession with threats; verse 131 had read the plagues as evidence about Moses. Verse 132 is the conclusion of the sequence: the state stops arguing and states a policy.

**The Sūrah's Own Precedent**

The pattern had been established in the sūrah's earlier narratives. The people of the towns had also seen the signs: ʿĀd's leaders had asked for a punishment to be brought (7:70), the people of Lot had demanded the same (7:82's sequel), and the sūrah's own summary of them all — *“So We seized them suddenly while they were unaware”* (7:95) — applies to a refusal of exactly this kind. What Egypt adds is the machinery of the state and the record of a policy.

**The Crowd and the Court**

The verse is the compound voice of palace and people: the translation's "they said" covers the same crowd that had been organized for the contest (26:39–40). The sūrah's narratives never distinguish the ruler's refusal from the population's; the Book treats a state's official position and a people's settled disposition as one phenomenon when both have access to the same signs.

**The Doctrine of the Sealed Heart**

The sūrah will state the correspondence again in its general form after the towns' chronicle: *“Thus Allah seals the hearts of the disbelievers”* (7:101). Verse 132 is the human side of the operation, and the Book presents the two sides without embarrassment: a refusal stated in public, and a heart closed to what the refusal has already seen. Sūrat al-Anʿām's list of those who would not believe even in the presence of the sheet of paper belongs to the same doctrine (6:7).

**The Refusal as Its Own Judgment**

The Qur'an's narratives do not require the state to be struck down in the verse itself; the refusal is the verdict. Egypt will be given more signs — the plagues, the sea, the rescue of Israel — and the court's position will survive all of them. What the Book reports, in a sentence its audience could memorize, is the moment a ruling establishment decides that no further evidence will be admitted.

**The Grammar of the Sentence**

The refusal is stated as an oath-like absolute: *fa-mā naḥnu laka bi-muʾminīn*, we are not, with regard to you, believers. Its form is a predicate of identity rather than a decision about a particular claim. The court had moved from evaluating a message to defining itself against it, and the sūrah marks the transition in a single verse.

**What the Verse Contributes to the Story**

The court's policy on evidence is now on record before the plagues reach their climax and before the sea. The reader who continues into the deliverance narrative — the departure at night, the pursuers, the drowning — is reading the consequences of a refusal that had already been made absolute.

**The Book's Address to Its Readers**

The Qur'an records these positions, as sūrahs elsewhere say, so that later communities can recognize the posture in themselves. The Book does not present the Egyptian court as a unique collection of monsters; it presents it as a case of a pattern whose mechanism — credit for good, blame for evil, and a settled verdict on signs — the sūrah states in a clause about what most people do not know.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:133

> **So We sent against them the flood and the locusts, and the lice and the frogs and the blood—signs expounded. But they waxed arrogant, and they were a guilty people.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Floods, Locusts, Lice, Frogs, and Blood"**

*Fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim al-ṭūfāna wa-l-jarāda wa-l-qummal wa-l-ḍafādiʿa wa-l-dama āyātin mufaṣṣalātin fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman mujrimīn* — so We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood — all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people.

**The Book's Most Detailed List of Signs**

The Qur'an typically summarizes the Egyptian signs: *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs”* (17:101); *“˹These are two˺ of nine signs for Pharaoh and his people”* (27:12). This verse is the sūrah's enumeration, and it is the most concrete of the accounts. The Egyptian prostration of the sūrah's first narrative and the court's stubbornness here are the two poles the signs produced.

**The Sequence in the Other Sūrahs**

Sūrat al-Zukhruf supplies the pattern within the sequence: *“although every sign We showed them was greater than the one before. Ultimately, We seized them with torments so that they might return ˹to the Right Path˺”* (43:48). Sūrat Ṭāhā states the outcome in one line — *“And We certainly showed Pharaoh all of Our signs, but he denied them and refused ˹to believe˺”* (20:56) — and Sūrat al-Muʾmin's long narrative supplies the court's deliberations (40:23–46).

**Signs Addressed to Egypt's World**

The list works on the country's own terms. Flood and blood touch the Nile, the source of Egypt's life and of its cult; locusts and lice touch the fields and the household; frogs, the animal of the river's fertility, invade the palace. The Book's signs are not abstract demonstrations; they are the systematic dismantling of the natural order on which the kingdom's confidence rested.

**The Miracle That Was Also a Mercy**

The Book's narratives of the plagues are notably restrained about suffering: the signs are named in a word each, and the human cost is left to the reader's imagination. The purpose clause of the previous verse — that they might remember (7:130) — governs the list, and the Qur'an's framing of the sequence as a summons rather than a simple punishment is consistent across the accounts.

**Pharaoh's Own Appeal to Signs**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf had already recorded the court's demand for proof (7:106), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ shows the king being shown the staff and the hand and calling them magic (26:30–33). The plagues are the answer to a court that had asked for evidence and had never intended to treat it as evidence, and the sūrah's phrasing — "clear signs" — is the Book's verdict on the bad faith of the request.

**The Word for Arrogance**

*Istakbarū* — they grew arrogant — is the Book's diagnosis of the refusal rather than an accusation about intelligence. The Qur'an consistently connects the rejection of signs to the will's self-exaltation: *“I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant in the land without right”* (7:146), and Sūrat al-Muʾmin's account of the court's debate has a believer among Pharaoh's own people warning them against exactly this posture (40:28).

**The Nine Signs in the Book's Memory**

The Book's later reference to the nine signs keeps the sequence in the community's memory, and the tradition's lists identify them with the staff, the hand, the years of famine, the shortfall of crops, the flood, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood. The sūrah's own account mentions most of them, and it adds the court's response to each — which is the point the Book is preserving.

**Where the Signs Were Pointless**

The sūrah does not claim that the plagues produced belief in Egypt. The court's confession will come only at the sea and will be refused there (10:90–91), and the sūrah's own crowd had already declared its position on evidence in the previous verse. What the signs did produce was the release of Israel, and the Book treats that outcome as sufficient.

**The Wickedness Named in the Verse's Last Word**

*Mujrimīn* — wicked, criminal — is the Book's term for those whose conduct has passed from error into offense, and the sūrah uses it for the courts of every town it describes. The verse's final clause therefore records a legal as well as a moral verdict: the state that had enslaved a people, killed their sons, and refused their release is named with the Book's own category of guilt.

**The Plagues and the Contest**

Read in the architecture of the sūrah, the plagues are the continuation of the courtyard by other means. The contest had established that the throne's professionals could not match a sign; the plagues establish that the throne's country could not survive one. The narrative's movement from a magic show to a national emergency is the Book's measure of what the refusal of verse 132 cost.

**The Book's Refusal of Spectacle**

The verses describe no drama of negotiation, no scene in which the king is brought to his knees by a specific plague. The sūrah gives the list and the verdict, and it leaves the sequence's details to the other accounts. The Book's economy here is the same as at the courtyard: what matters is not the spectacle but the refusal that outlasts it.

**The Transition to the Promise**

The next verse records the court's plea for the removal of the torment, and the one after that records its betrayal. The list of plagues is therefore bracketed by an oath and a broken word, which is the sūrah's way of stating that the state's religion was a crisis behavior rather than a conviction.

**The Reader's Place in the Sequence**

For the sūrah's audience the signs had already been named as a lesson: the Book's recitation of the Egyptian plagues sits inside a sūrah whose readers are being asked whether they recognize the pattern in their own circumstances. The verse's last clause about the wicked applies to the court of Egypt, and the sūrah's rhetoric leaves the application open for every later generation.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:134

> **Then when the torment came down upon them, they said, “O Moses! Call upon your Lord for us by the covenant He has made with you. If you lift this torment from us, we shall surely believe in you, and we shall surely send forth the Children of Israel with you.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Pray to Your Lord for Us"**

*Wa-lammā waqaʿa ʿalayhimu al-rijzu qālū yā Mūsā udʿu lanā rabbaka bimā ʿahida ʿindaka la-in kashafta ʿannā al-rijza la-nuʾminanna laka wa-la-nursilanna maʿaka Banī Isrāʾīl* — when tormented, they pleaded: "O Moses! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. If you help remove this torment from us, we will certainly believe in you and let the Children of Israel go with you."

**The Court's Religion Under Pressure**

The men who had commissioned magicians, threatened crucifixion, and declared that no sign would ever be believed now request the prophet's intercession. The Book's analysis of this posture is given in several places: *“When one is touched with hardship, they cry out to Us ˹alone˺. Then when We shower Our blessings upon them, they say, ‘I have been granted all this only because of ˹my˺ knowledge’”* (39:49). The sūrah's court demonstrates the first half of the verse.

**The Same Plea in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Zukhruf preserves the scene with an added detail that must be read in full to be appreciated: *“˹Then˺ they pleaded, ‘O ˹mighty˺ magician! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. We will certainly accept guidance’”* (43:49) — followed immediately by the Book's report that they reneged (43:50). The court asks the man it calls a sorcerer to pray for it, and the Book lets the insult stand beside the request.

**The Sincere Supplication of the Desperate**

The Qur'an treats the cry of the distressed as evidence of something the prosperous person denies: the knowledge of where help actually comes from. Sūrat al-Anʿām states the fact without endorsing the speaker — *“When people are touched with hardship, they cry out to their Lord, turning to Him ˹alone˺. But as soon as He gives them a taste of His mercy, a group of them associates ˹others˺ with their Lord ˹in worship˺”* (30:33) — and Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt describes the same shipwreck scene: *“If they happen to be aboard a ship ˹caught in a storm˺, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But as soon as He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, they associate ˹others with Him once again˺”* (29:65).

**The Condition Attached to Belief**

The promise is transactional: remove the torment and we will believe. The Book's verdict on faith offered at the point of compulsion appears in two places near the end of this narrative. Sūrat Ghāfir states the rule for the nations: *“But their faith was of no benefit to them once they saw Our punishment”* (40:85), and Sūrat Yūnus reports the drowning king's confession and its refusal (10:90–91). The court's offer is the offer of a man negotiating, not of a man convinced.

**The Covenant They Invoke**

*Bimā ʿahida ʿindaka* — by what He has entrusted to you — is the court's appeal to the commission Moses claimed in verse 105. The Book's narratives of prophecy do not treat the prophetic office as a private privilege that can be borrowed, and the request's phrasing shows the court's grasp of the machinery it is trying to use: it wants a man with access to God, whoever that God may be.

**The Verb the Court Uses**

*Udʿu lanā rabbaka* — ask your Lord for us. The Book's instructions about prayer place intercession with the living and prayers of the community for one another, and it forbids reliance on intermediaries who cannot answer (6:51; 39:43–44). The magicians of the contest had asked nothing of the throne's gods when the sign arrived; the court asks everything of the prophet's Lord when the plagues arrive.

**The Promise Made Under Torment**

The Book records broken promises of this exact type throughout its histories: ʿĀd's leaders promising faith when the punishment appeared (7:70–71's context), the Sodomites' fear when the angels arrived, and Israel's own repeated pledges in the wilderness. The sūrah's narrative will state the outcome in the next verse in a single clause about their fate.

**The Magnitude of the Offer**

The court's promise is not trivial: it offers belief and the release of an enslaved people. The sūrah had opened the Egyptian narrative with the demand for that release (7:105), and the plagues bring the state to the point of conceding it. The Book's point is not that diplomacy failed but that the concession was a tactic, and the next verse's broken word is the proof.

**Why the Prophets Negotiate**

Moses prays for the torment's removal, and the Book does not criticize him for it. The Qur'an's prophets are described as men who intercede for their communities — Abraham for Sodom (11:74–76), Noah for his people (71:26–28's contrast), the Prophet for his — and the deliverance narrative requires the state's release of Israel to be given, however cynically, before the crossing of the sea.

**The Court's Last Instrument**

By this point in the story, the palace has tried spectacle, terror, religion, and denial. The plea to the prophet is its final instrument, and it is the one that most resembles faith: a state on its knees before the man it has sentenced. The Book allows the resemblance and then removes it in the next verse.

**The Verse's Function in the Narrative**

Every narrative of a hardened community in the Qur'an includes a moment like this, in which the pressure produces apparent submission. The sūrah places it directly before the drowning, which means the reader is meant to read the plea as the final data point in the state's moral record — not as the beginning of a change.

**The Book's Warning to Its Own Readers**

The verse's usefulness for later communities lies in its diagnosis: a religion practiced under the weight of an affliction is not yet a religion of the heart. Sūrat Yūnus's account of the one town whose faith was accepted — the people of Jonah — notes that their belief came while they could still see (10:98), a distinction the Qur'an uses consistently against deathbed conversions.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:135

> **But when We lifted the torment from them, for a term they were to fulfill, behold, they reneged.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"They Broke Their Promise"**

*Fa-lammā kashafnā ʿanhumu al-rijza ilā ajalin hum bālighūhu idhā hum yankuthūn* — but as soon as We removed the torment from them — until they met their inevitable fate — they broke their promise. The verse is the sūrah's verdict on the court's faith, and it records the interval between the relief and the reckoning.

**The Recurrence in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Zukhruf reports the same sequence in the same words: *“But as soon as We relieved their torment, they broke their word”* (43:50). The Book repeats the sentence because the pattern is the point: a promise made under affliction and broken under relief is one of the Qur'an's standard portraits of the unrepentant, and it appears in the sūrahs with a regularity that makes it a law.

**The Book's Psychology of Relief**

The Qur'an's descriptions of this pattern are numerous and precise. *“Then as soon as He removes the hardship from you, a group of you associates ˹others˺ with their Lord ˹in worship˺”* (16:54); *“But when He delivers you ˹safely˺ to shore, you turn away. Humankind is ever ungrateful”* (17:67); *“If We give people a taste of Our mercy then take it away from them, they become utterly desperate, ungrateful”* (11:9). The verses map a cycle the sūrah's court illustrates at the scale of a state.

**The Word for Breaking**

*Yankuthūn* is the tearing of a woven thing, and the Qur'an uses it for the violation of pledges in several places, including the instruction to Israel not to be like the woman who unravels her thread after spinning it (16:92). The Book's vocabulary for covenant-breaking is deliberately material: a promise in the Qur'an is something spun, and breaking it is an act of destruction.

**The Interval Before the End**

*Ilā ajalin hum bālighūhu* — until the term they were destined to reach. The sūrah's clause turns the plagues into a period with a terminus, and the Book's teaching about appointed terms is stated in the same sūrah: *“And for every community there is an appointed term; when their term comes, they will not delay it by a moment, nor will they advance it”* (7:34). Egypt's relief was an interval, not a reprieve.

**The Book's Warning About Deadlines**

The Qur'an's practical warning follows from the doctrine: a person who postpones repentance until the hardship ends may find that the end arrives first. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn states the tone of that warning in an address to the heedless — *“And spend from what We have provided you before death comes to one of you, and he says, ‘My Lord, if only You would delay me for a little while’”* (63:10) — and the sūrah's court receives its extension and spends it on further refusal.

**The Broken Word as a State Policy**

The verse describes the entire Egyptian state's behavior, not an individual lapse: the release of Israel was conceded and withdrawn as circumstances changed. The Book's histories treat this as a feature of tyrannies rather than a surprise, and the sūrah's own earlier warning about the towns' broken covenants (7:102) is fulfilled here at national scale.

**What the Plagues Accomplished**

The signs did not produce the believed promise, but they did produce the narrative's most important outcome: a state on record before its own people as a breaker of oaths. When the sea is reached and the crossing happens, the Book's readers have been given the whole record — the slaughtered children, the commissioned magicians, the crucifixion threat, the state's humiliation, the false plea, and now the broken word.

**Why the Sūrah Does Not Describe the Plagues Further**

The verse's brevity is part of its argument. The sūrah gives the court's plea in full, its removal of the torment in a clause, and its betrayal in three words, because what matters for the Book's case is the moral sequence and not the meteorological detail. The narrator's attention is on the promise and its fate.

**The Court's Own Account of Itself**

Between the plague cycles the sūrah had reported the palace's self-serving readings of events (7:131) and its absolute refusal of signs (7:132). Verse 135 supplies the missing piece: an admission extracted by pain and withdrawn at the first opportunity. Taken together, the three verses are the Book's complete anatomy of a hardened political class.

**The Pattern's Repetition Within the Story**

The plagues came in a series, and the sūrah's narrative implies that the plea and the betrayal happened more than once. Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's list of the signs (7:133) and Sūrat al-Zukhruf's note that each sign surpassed the last (43:48) leave no room for a single, isolated incident. The court's promise was broken as often as it was made, which is why the sūrah needs only one clause to dismiss the state's faith.

**The Doctrine of the Second Chance**

The Book grants relief repeatedly and does not treat the repetition as weakness in the divine government. Sūrat al-Fāṭir states the principle in terms of patience: *“If Allah were to punish people for what they do, He would not leave a single creature on earth; but He gives them respite until an appointed term”* (35:45). Egypt's plagues were that respite in operation, and the court used every day of it.

**The Transition to the Sea**

With this verse the plagues' cycle ends and the exodus begins. The narrative's next sentences move the story from a state that cannot keep a promise to a people that is about to be taken out of its hands, and the sūrah reports the crossing, the drowning, and the inheritance of Egypt's wealth in a single verse each.

**What the Reader Is Meant to Carry Forward**

The verse's lesson is stated for the Prophet's community through the story rather than in a command: relief is not an argument, and a promise made to God under pressure is tested by what happens after the pressure ends. The court of Egypt is the Book's case study, and the next verses give the verdict.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:136

> **So We took vengeance upon them and drowned them in the sea for their having denied Our signs and for having been heedless of them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Drowning Them in the Sea"**

*Fa-ntaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum fī al-yammi bi-annnahum kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-kānū ʿanhā ghāfilīn* — so We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them in the sea for denying Our signs and being heedless of them. The verse ends the Egyptian narrative's central conflict in a single clause.

**The Word for the Punishment**

*Intiqām* in the Qur'an is the recompense of a wrong that has been carried past warning, and the Book uses it for the sea in the other account as well: *“So when they enraged Us, We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all. And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them”* (43:55–56). The punishment's function in the Book is not only retribution; it is the production of a sign for later generations.

**The Same Event in Five Sūrahs**

Sūrat al-Baqarah records it as Israel's memory: *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh's people before your very eyes”* (2:50). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the sequence in four verses, ending with *“Then We drowned the others”* (26:66). Sūrat Ṭāhā narrates the pursuit and the waters (20:77–79), Sūrat al-Dukhān records the command to leave the sea parted because *“they are certainly an army bound to drown”* (44:24), and Sūrat al-Isrāʾ summarizes the whole: *“So Pharaoh wanted to scare the Israelites out of the land ˹of Egypt˺, but We drowned him and all of those with him”* (17:103).

**The Two Charges in the Verse**

The sūrah gives the reason for the drowning in two clauses: denial of the signs and heedlessness of them. The pairing is characteristic of the Book's moral analysis: the court had both refused the evidence and stopped attending to it. The Qur'an's reader has watched the sequence — the warning in verse 103, the courts' archetypes, the plagues, the broken oath — and the verse's verdict now names what all of it had been building.

**Heedlessness as a Category**

*Ghāfilīn* is the Book's word for the condition of being unbothered by what should command attention, and it appears throughout the Qur'an's diagnosis: the sūrah's own narrative censured the towns that were heedless (7:179), and the Prophet's community is repeatedly warned against it — *“And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget their own selves”* (59:19). The Egyptian state was not short of information; it was short of attention.

**The Nation That Was Not Destroyed Unwarned**

The Book is explicit that the pattern requires warning first: *“That is because your Lord would never destroy a town unjustly while its people were unaware”* (6:131). Egypt had been warned through a messenger, a contest, and a campaign of signs, and the sūrah's account of the plagues records the warnings precisely so that this verse's verdict can be read as the last step of a sequence rather than an arbitrary act of power.

**The Crossing and the Pursuit**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ supplies the human scene the sūrah only implies: the two groups face to face and the companions of Moses crying *“We are overtaken for sure”* (26:61), and Moses' answer — *“Absolutely not! My Lord is certainly with me — He will guide me”* (26:62). The drowning is the answer to the state's pursuit, and the Book gives the reassurance in the same breath as the event.

**The Corpse Preserved as a Sign**

Sūrat Yūnus adds the epilogue to the drowning that makes the Book's purpose explicit. When the king confessed as the water closed over him, the answer came: *“˹He was told,˺ ‘Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors. Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you’”* (10:91–92). The Book's own comment on the drowning is that it was staged for later readers.

**The Sea as Instrument**

The Book's narration gives the water both functions: it opens as a road for the oppressed and closes as a judgment on the pursuer (2:50; 44:24). The narrative's theology is that creation itself serves the messengers' mission, and the state's army is destroyed by the same element that had carried the people it enslaved.

**What the Verse Does Not Describe**

The sūrah reports no struggle in the water, no plea, and no detail of the deaths. Its account is a clause about a verdict, and the Book treats the event's terror with the same narrative restraint it used for the towns it destroyed earlier in the sūrah. What it registers instead is the reason, which is the part later generations were meant to learn.

**The State's Last Count**

By the time the sea closes, the throne of Egypt has been defeated in the contest, humiliated before its own people, subject to plagues, exposed as a breaker of oaths, and now destroyed with its army. The Book's narrative withheld nothing and added nothing; the verdict of verse 136 is the summary of a record that has been building since verse 103.

**The End of the Sūrah's Second Half**

The Egyptian narrative now turns from the state's destruction to the inheritance that follows it, and the sūrah's next verse reports both the succession and the ruin in one sentence. The Book's habit is to place the promise and the judgment side by side, and this transition is one of its clearest examples: Egypt's collapse is the frame around Israel's establishment.

**The Message for the Prophet's Community**

The sūrah's readers in Makkah were being told a story about a superpower's end, at a time when their own city's elite regarded them as an irritant. The Book's use of the drowning in its address is explicit elsewhere: *“Indeed, in that is a lesson for whoever fears”* (79:26), and the sūrah's narration leaves its audience to apply the lesson to their own situation.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:137

> **And We bequeathed unto the people who were oppressed the eastern and western parts of the land that We blessed. And the most beautiful Word of thy Lord was fulfilled for the Children of Israel because they were patient. And We demolished all that Pharaoh and his people had wrought and that which they used to build.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Successors of the Eastern and Western Lands"**

*Wa-awrathnā al-qawma alladhīna kānū yustadʿafūna mashāriqa al-arḍi wa-maghāribahā allatī bāraknā fīhā wa-tammat kalimatu rabbika al-ḥusnā ʿalā Banī Isrāʾīl bimā ṣabarū wa-dammarnā mā kāna yaṣnaʿu Firʿawnu wa-qawmuhu wa-mā kānū yaʿrishūn* — and ˹so˺ We made the oppressed people successors of the eastern and western lands, which We had showered with blessings. ˹In this way˺ the noble Word of your Lord was fulfilled for the Children of Israel for what they had endured. And We destroyed what Pharaoh and his people constructed and what they established.

**The Oppressed as Heirs**

The verse's subject is *the people who were oppressed* — the same phrase the sūrah has used for Israel since the beginning of the narrative (7:127's decree, 7:129's complaint). The Book states the divine intention behind the whole episode elsewhere in the same terms: *“But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land, making them models ˹of faith˺ as well as successors; and to establish them in the land; and through them show Pharaoh, Hamân, and their soldiers ˹the fulfilment of˺ what they feared”* (28:5–6).

**The East and the West**

*Mashāriqa al-arḍi wa-maghāribahā* — the eastern and western reaches — is the Book's way of describing a land whose extent is known to God rather than fixed on a map. The Qur'an uses the pair for the range of the earth and the created order generally (26:28; 55:17; 70:40), and here it measures the territory from the Nile valley to the lands beyond it, with the note that the region had been blessed.

**The Blessing of the Land**

The Qur'an refers to the region's blessing in several places: the land around the Sacred House (21:71), the Holy Land assigned to Israel (5:21), and the *“land which We have blessed for all peoples”* (21:71's context). The sūrah's own phrase — *which We had showered with blessings* — attributes the prosperity the Israelites were about to inherit to a prior divine act, not to Egypt's gods or the throne's management.

**The Noble Word Fulfilled**

*Wa-tammat kalimatu rabbika al-ḥusnā ʿalā Banī Isrāʾīl bimā ṣabarū* — the promise was completed for them because they endured. The Book's language of the "word" is the language of a decree that cannot fail, and the Qur'an applies it to this promise elsewhere: the deliverance narrative in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ was introduced as the realization of what the oppressors feared (28:6), and Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ states the transfer in a single line: *“So it was. And We awarded it ˹all˺ to the Children of Israel”* (26:59).

**Patience as the Stated Reason**

The clause *bimā ṣabarū* attaches the inheritance to endurance, and it looks back to the counsel Moses gave in verse 128: seek Allah's help and be patient. The Book's histories consistently make patience the condition on which its promises are realized: the sūrah's narrative of Israel's years of persecution and unbroken persistence is the Book's demonstration case.

**The Ruin of the Constructors**

*Wa-dammarnā mā kāna yaṣnaʿu Firʿawnu wa-qawmuhu wa-mā kānū yaʿrishūn* — We destroyed what Pharaoh and his people made and what they erected. The Book elsewhere describes the estate that was left behind: *“˹Imagine˺ how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind, and crops and fine dwellings, and luxuries they revelled in”* (44:25–27), followed by the verse that names the transfer of ownership (44:28). Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ states it as the dispossession of the gardens, springs, treasures, and noble stations (26:57–58) awarded to Israel (26:59).

**The Verb for Building Up**

*Yaʿrishūn* refers to raising structures — the Book's word for what a settled power erects. The sūrah's point is not that the buildings were demolished in the sea; it is that the system they represented, and the mastery they expressed, passed out of the hands of the state that had built them.

**A Pattern the Book Applies Repeatedly**

The Qur'an turns the inheritance of the arrogant into a general law. *“Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act”* (10:14); *“He can do away with you and replace you with whoever He wills”* (6:133); *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you”* (6:165). The Prophet's community receives the same law in its own promise of succession (24:55) and its own warning (7:129).

**The Test That Accompanies the Gift**

The sūrah does not treat Israel's inheritance as a charter. The narrative's next verses report the community's request for a visible god (7:138), and the sūrah's later chapters record the calf, the refusal to enter the Holy Land, and the breaking of the covenant (7:148–152; 5:20–26). The Book's gift and its test arrive together by design.

**Egypt's Monuments and the Book's Memory**

The Qur'an's references to the Egyptian state's constructions are consistently about their fate rather than their grandeur, and the later sūrahs treat the king's boast about the rivers beneath his palace (43:51) as the epitome of a confidence that did not survive him. The Book's histories of architecture are always histories of tenants.

**The Sūrah's Economic Theology**

Read with verse 130's famine and verse 133's plagues, verse 137 states the sūrah's economic theology: prosperity belongs to the One who grants it, is administered by whoever is given it, and changes hands when the administration turns to oppression. Egypt's abundance had been a loan; the verse records its recall.

**What Israel Was Given**

The verse does not describe the land's institutions or the terms of the transfer. The Book leaves the details to its narratives of the later history and states only the essential fact: a people that had been enslaved and bereaved became the owners of the land of their enslavers. For the sūrah's audience, the sentence was both a memory of a deliverance and a promise about the future of power.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:138

> **And We brought the Children of Israel across the sea, and they came upon a people clinging to their idols. They said, “O Moses! Make for us a god as they have gods.” He said, “Truly you are an ignorant people!**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Make for Us a God Like Their Gods"**

*Wa-jāwaznā bi-Banī Isrāʾīla al-baḥra fa-atā ʿalā qawmin yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmihim qālū yā Mūsā ijʿal lanā ilāhan ka-mā lahum ālihatun qāla innakum qawmun tajhalūn* — We brought the Children of Israel across the sea and they came upon a people devoted to idols. They demanded: "O Moses! Make for us a god like their gods." He replied: "Indeed, you are a people acting ignorantly!"

**The Scene Immediately After the Deliverance**

The verse's first word is the crossing, and its second is the request. The Book places the demand for an idol in the same breath as the rescue from Egypt, and the juxtaposition is the sūrah's whole judgment on the community's state of mind. The people who had watched the sea open and the army drown asked their prophet to manufacture a deity that afternoon.

**Devoted to Idols**

*Yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmihim* — the verb describes a settled attachment, a people stationed at their images. The translation's "devoted" catches the sense: the Book is describing a community's habitual posture, not an isolated ritual. The Qur'an's analysis of idolatry elsewhere treats it as an inherited practice with a social function, which is exactly what makes it attractive to a people in transition.

**The Request's Logic**

The Israelites had left a country of temples and arrived among a people with visible gods, and they wanted the visibility. The Book's narratives show this preference repeatedly: the demand to see Allah with their own eyes (2:55), the demand for a sign on paper (4:153), and the calf manufactured during Moses' absence (20:88). The request at the sea is the first entry in a pattern the sūrah will continue to document.

**Moses' Diagnosis**

*Innakum qawmun tajhalūn* — you are a people acting ignorantly. The prophet's answer is not a curse or a dismissal; it is a description. The Qur'an's vocabulary of ignorance (*jahl*) names a condition of acting without knowledge rather than a lack of intelligence, and the sūrah's sentence treats the request as a symptom to be treated rather than a crime to be punished.

**The Book's Critique of Inherited Religion**

The demand's premise is the practice it observed, and the Qur'an meets that premise head-on in sūrah after sūrah. *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing’”* (2:170). Sūrat al-Zukhruf reports the same formula in four consecutive verses: *“We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps”* (43:22), with the warner's question — *“Even if what I brought you is better guidance than what you found your forefathers practicing?”* — and the elite's answer that they reject it (43:23–24).

**Abraham's Argument Against the Ancestors**

The Qur'an's longest treatment of this logic is placed on Abraham's lips. The sūrah of al-Shuʿarāʾ records the question and the reply — *“They replied, ‘No! But we found our forefathers doing the same’”* (26:74) — followed by Abraham's demolition of the position: *“Have you ˹really˺ considered what you have been worshipping — you and your ancestors? They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds”* (26:75–77). The same scene in al-Anbiyāʾ has Abraham say to the people and their forefathers: *“Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray”* (21:54).

**The Footsteps Metaphor**

Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt uses the image of walking in another's tracks to describe the mechanism of inherited idolatry: *“Indeed, they found their forefathers astray, so they rushed in their footsteps!”* (37:69–70). The Book's complaint is not that tradition exists but that it is being used as a substitute for judgment, and its sentence about the majority is stated without qualification: *“If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's Way”* (6:116).

**The Favour They Had Just Received**

The verse's request is made by people who had been spared, fed, and freed. The Book recalls the sequence for them directly: *“O Children of Israel! We saved you from your enemy, and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and sent down to you manna and quails”* (20:80). Israel's pattern of response to blessing is one of the Qur'an's most persistent themes, and the sūrah's narrative does not soften it.

**The Refusal to Be Taught by Deliverance**

The request also shows what a deliverance cannot do. The Book's account of the exodus never suggests that the crossing produced a community of believers; it produced a freed people, whose formation would require law, testing, and time. Verse 138 is the earliest statement of that gap, and the sūrah's later narratives fill it with the golden calf, the spies, and the wilderness.

**Moses' Answer Begins a Sequence**

The prophet's response continues into the next two verses, and its structure is a lesson in how the Book deals with demands of this kind: the diagnosis of ignorance (7:138), the demonstration that the object of imitation is worthless (7:139), and the reminder of what the community already has (7:140). The sūrah gives the answer in three sentences, and each of them addresses a different level of the request.

**The Memory of the Idols' Fate**

The people the Israelites encountered were eventually overtaken by the same principle that destroyed the towns in the sūrah's earlier narratives, and the request's comparison therefore carries an unintentional prophecy. The Book's readers have just finished the story of Egypt's gods and their state; the scene of Israel asking for a version of the same thing is the sūrah's bitterest irony.

**The Warning for Later Communities**

The Book's placement of this scene immediately after the drowning makes it a permanent warning about what a community can do with its freedom. The Qur'an's readers were told the story of a people who were saved from a state and asked for its gods within sight of the sea, and the sūrah's narrative continues in the next verses to answer them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:139

> **As for these, what they practice shall perish, and vain is that which they used to do.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Doomed to Destruction"**

*Inna hāʾulāʾi mutabbarun mā hum fīhi wa-bāṭilun mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — what they follow is certainly doomed to destruction, and their deeds are in vain. The verse is Moses' second answer to the request for an idol, and it dismantles the model the Israelites wanted to imitate.

**The Word for Destruction**

*Mutabbar* is the Qur'an's term for complete ruin, and its root appears in the sūrah of al-Isrāʾ in the prediction about the temple's desecration: the enemy would enter and *“destroy utterly what they had conquered”* (17:7). Moses' sentence about the idolaters' religion uses the same vocabulary, and the Book's choice of so absolute a word is deliberate.

**"What They Are In"**

The phrase *mā hum fīhi* — what they are in — is not limited to the idols themselves; it covers the whole state of the people being imitated: their cult, their festivals, their attachment. Moses is telling the Israelites that the thing they admired was a system under sentence, and the fact that it looked settled to a traveler says nothing about its future.

**The Sūrah's Earlier Use of the Same Word**

*Bāṭil* — vain, void, false — is the exact term the sūrah used for the magicians' performance a few verses earlier: *“So the truth prevailed and their illusions failed”* (7:118). The Book repeats the word to connect the two scenes: Egypt's professional illusionists and the idolaters the Israelites admired were engaged in the same category of activity, and both were measured by the arrival of what is real.

**The Book's Doctrine of Falsehood's End**

The Qur'an states the principle in the form of a promise and an image: *“Say, ‘The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return’”* (34:49); *“In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes”* (21:18); and the sūrah of al-Ḥajj gives the doctrinal summary — *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood”* (22:62, repeated at 31:30). Moses' verdict on the idolaters' religion is the Book's general verdict applied to a particular people.

**Effort Without Weight**

The verse's second clause, *their deeds are in vain*, introduces a theme the Qur'an develops at length. Sūrat al-Kahf asks: *“Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds? ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!”* (18:103–104). Sūrat al-Furqān states what becomes of such labor — *“Then We will turn to whatever ˹good˺ deeds they did, reducing them to scattered dust”* (25:23) — and Sūrat al-Nūr compares it to a mirage in a desert (24:39).

**Work That Is Not Necessarily Evil**

The Book's verdict here is not that the idolaters did nothing; it is that their activity lacked a foundation. The Qur'an's phrase for the condition is that their deeds have no weight on the Day of Judgment because the deeds were performed for what does not exist (18:105). Moses' sentence therefore distinguishes between effort and achievement, a distinction the sūrah's readers would need in evaluating the impressive civilization they had just left behind.

**The Model's Worthlessness as an Argument**

The response's logic is worth noticing: Moses does not begin by forbidding the request, he begins by removing its attraction. The Qur'an's persuasion in these passages works by exposure rather than command, and the sūrah's narrative of Abraham destroyed the idols before arguing about them (21:57–58). The prophet's answer to the Israelites follows the same method in words.

**Idols and Their Worshippers in the Book**

The Qur'an's idols are consistently described as beings that cannot hear, see, or profit their worshippers: *“They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds”* (26:77) is Abraham's verdict, and Sūrat Maryam records his question to his father about worshipping what cannot hear or see (19:42). The sūrah's sentence about the adherents' state — what they are in is doomed — extends the verdict from the object to the practice.

**What the Worshippers Lose**

The Book's analysis is that the cost of idolatry is not a fine but a whole category of accomplishment: the deeds performed in its service are void, and the sūrah's readers are shown a people rebuilding their religious life on a foundation that cannot hold. The verse's two clauses therefore cover both the present state and the eventual accounting.

**The Verdict's Restraint**

The sentence contains no threat to the idolaters themselves and no command for the Israelites to attack them. The Book's Moses simply states the fact of the model's mortality, and the sūrah's narrative continues with the reminder of the community's own position in the next verse. The Book's treatment of foreign religions in this scene is a statement about futility rather than a call for action.

**The Sūrah's Refrain Repeated**

The theme of worthlessness runs through the sūrah's accounts of the destroyed towns: ʿĀd's strength, Thamūd's architecture, Sodom's license, Madyan's commerce — all described in the sūrah as substantial and all reported as gone. The verdict on the idolaters the Israelites admired is another instance of the sūrah's general rule about human constructions raised on false premises.

**The Third Answer**

The verse's last clause leads directly to Moses' third statement, which turns from what the Israelites wanted to what they already possessed (7:140). The sūrah's arrangement of the three answers — diagnosis, demolition, and reminder — is a compact model of prophetic teaching, and it is the answer the Book gives to every later community that asks for a visible substitute for what it has been given.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:140

> **He said, “Shall I seek for you a god other than God, when He has favored you above the worlds?”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"While He Has Honoured You Above the Others"**

*Qāla a-ghayra Allāhi abghīkum ilāhan wa-huwa faḍḍalakum ʿalā al-ʿālamīn* — he added: "Shall I seek for you a god other than Allah, while He has honoured you above the others?" The third and last of Moses' answers turns from the worthlessness of the model to the position of the people asking for it.

**The Rhetorical Question**

*A-ghayra Allāhi abghīkum ilāhan* repeats a construction the Qur'an puts in the Prophet's own mouth: *“Should I seek a lord other than Allah while He is the Lord of everything?”* (6:164). Sūrat al-Zumar states it with the Book's sharpest adjective — *“Are you urging me to worship ˹anyone˺ other than Allah, O ignorant ones?”* (39:64) — and Sūrat Ghāfir records the Prophet's declaration that he has been forbidden to worship what others invoke besides Allah (40:66).

**A Prophet Cannot Manufacture a God**

The request had asked Moses to be the author of a deity, and the Book's answer to that demand is stated categorically elsewhere: it is not for a person blessed with scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, "Worship me instead of Allah" (3:79). The verse's question therefore does more than reply to an insult; it defines the limit of the prophetic office. A messenger's task is to convey, not to invent.

**The Favour Recalled**

*Wa-huwa faḍḍalakum ʿalā al-ʿālamīn* — while He has honoured you above the others. The Qur'an repeats this reminder to Israel in several places as an argument against ingratitude: *“O Children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others”* (2:122, also 2:47), and Sūrat al-Jāthiyah lists the content of the favour — scripture, wisdom, prophethood, good provision, and precedence (45:16–17). Sūrat al-Dukhān states it as election: *“And indeed, We chose the Israelites knowingly above the others”* (44:32).

**What the Favour Consisted Of**

Sūrat al-Māʾidah's Moses speech names the elements: *“O my people! Remember Allah's favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign, and gave you what He had not given to others”* (5:20). The Book's account of Israel's distinction is therefore institutional and historical — messengers, kingship, scripture — rather than a claim about blood, and its purpose in the narrative is to show what the request for an idol would be throwing away.

**The Favour and the Covenant**

The same sūrah that records the honour records its condition. Abraham's elevation is followed by the sentence that the covenant does not extend to wrongdoers (2:124), and the Book's reminders to Israel always accompany a demand for loyalty: *“O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and fulfil My covenant, and I will fulfil your covenant”* (2:40). Verse 140's reminder is therefore an appeal to obligation as much as to privilege.

**The Meaning of "Above the Others"**

The Qur'an's own use of the phrase clarifies it: Israel was favoured among the peoples of its era and given a mission that the Book elsewhere transfers to the community of the Prophet — *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity”* (3:110) — on the same conditional terms. Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt states the criterion that governs the whole idea: the most honoured among people are the most mindful of Allah (49:13). The favour of verse 140 is a station held in trust.

**The Past Is Not an Inheritance**

The Surah's own narrative denies that the distinction can be inherited automatically: the community that crossed the sea will ask for an idol in this very verse, and later chapters record its failures. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the principle for the descendants of Israel and, by extension, for everyone: *“That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they did”* (2:134).

**Moses' Three Sentences**

Read together, the prophet's answer covers the whole ground of the request: the requesters are ignorant of what they are asking (7:138); what they want to imitate is doomed and its works are void (7:139); and what they already have is a favour that the imitation would abandon (7:140). The Book's method of answering a demand for a sign or a substitute is consistently this three-part structure, and the sūrah gives it here in its most compact form.

**The Book's Remedy for Idolatry**

The Qur'an's cure for the inclination the Israelites displayed is the rehearsal of what God has done — the sūrah's own repeated command: *“Remember Allah's favours so that you may succeed”* (7:69's context; 2:231). The sūrah's Moses turns the community's attention from the idols ahead of them to the history behind them, and the Book treats memory as the primary defense against imitation.

**The Scene's Warning**

The verse's question is addressed to a people who had just been saved, and the Book leaves the question standing for its readers. The Qur'an's narratives do not present idolatry as a foreign problem confined to ancient peoples; the request here comes from the community of the exodus at the beginning of its freedom.

**The Next Verse's Reminder**

The narrative immediately repeats the favour in the form of a command: *“And ˹remember˺ when We saved you from Pharaoh's people, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a tremendous test from your Lord”* (7:141). The sūrah's cure for the request of verse 138 is stated in the next chapter of its own story, and it is the same cure Moses offers here: remember.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:141

> **And when We saved you from the House of Pharaoh, who inflicted terrible punishment upon you, slaying your sons and sparing your women. And in this was a great trial from your Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Rescued From the People of Pharaoh"**

*Wa-idh anjaynākum min āli Firʿawna yasūmūnakum sūʾa al-ʿadhābi yuqattilūna abnāʾakum wa-yastaḥyūna nisāʾakum wa-fī dhālikum balāʾun min rabbikum ʿaẓīm* — and ˹remember˺ when We rescued you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment — killing your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord.

**The Verse's Position**

The narrative has just reported the drowning and the inheritance of the land (7:136–137), and it now returns to Israel's memory with a command to remember. The sūrah's placement is deliberate: the reminder of the persecution is given at the moment the community is about to be summoned to the mountain and given the law, so that the covenant to come is grounded in what has been done for them.

**The Liturgy of Remembering**

The Qur'an's address to Israel is built on the verb of remembrance: *“O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you”* (2:40); *“Remember My favours upon you and how I honoured you above the others”* (2:122); *“Remember Allah's favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you”* (5:20). The sūrah's verse belongs to that series, and the Book treats memory as the discipline that steadies a community between deliverance and obedience.

**The Same Sentence in Al-Baqarah**

Sūrat al-Baqarah preserves the identical description of Egypt's policy: *“And ˹remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a tremendous test from your Lord”* (2:49). The Book's repetition of the sentence at the head of its longest sūrah and again here, at the foot of the mountain, shows what work it is doing: the memory of the oppression is part of the evidence for the covenant.

**The Detail That Will Not Be Softened**

The verse names the killing of sons and the keeping of women, and it does not substitute a general word for suffering. The Book's narratives of oppression are concrete in this way, and the sūrah will later show the same state's policy renewed in verse 127 and its enforcers drowned at the sea. Israel's memory is being given a factual shape rather than a moral one.

**The Test Named**

*Balāʾun min rabbikum ʿaẓīm* — a severe test from your Lord. The Book calls the persecution a test, which relocates the question of theodicy: what a community does under oppression is part of its formation, and the Qur'an's narratives of Israel uniformly treat the years in Egypt as the making of a people. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same verdict in the same words (2:49), and Sūrat Ibrāhīm's Moses prayer adds the words of the community itself: *“Remember Allah's favour upon you when He rescued you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment”* (14:6).

**The Delivered and Their Deliverer**

The verse's first word is *We*: the rescue is attributed to God, and Moses appears only as the messenger through whom it happened. The Book's exodus narrative consistently presents the crossing as a divine act performed on behalf of an enslaved people, and the narrative's later episodes — the manna, the quails, the water from the rock (2:60) — continue the attribution.

**What the Reminder Is For**

The command to remember is given in the middle of the sūrah's narrative about the building of a community. The generation that had been freed now had to be formed, and the Qur'an's account of the mountain, the tablets, and the covenant (7:142–145) is the institution of that formation. The Book's therapeutic arrangement is memory first, then law.

**The Book's Use of Political Memory**

The Qur'an's narratives of liberation do not present freedom as an end in itself; the freed are immediately addressed as people with obligations. The sūrah's Israel is rescued at the sea, reminded of the torment, appointed to the mountain, and given commandments — all within a few verses. The Book's political theology is stated by that sequence: a people's independence exists to be filled with worship and justice.

**The Sūrah's Contrast With Its Own Towns**

Set beside the sūrah's earlier narratives, verse 141 completes the picture the reader has been assembling: the towns that refused the messengers were destroyed, the Egyptian state that persecuted Israel was drowned, and Israel was preserved. The sūrah's long narrative of the towns and Egypt is thus a single demonstration of the rule that Moses would state a few verses earlier — the earth belongs to Allah, who gives it to whoever He wills of His servants (7:128).

**The Same Reminder in the Prophet's Community**

The Qur'an's method with Israel became its method with the Muslims: *“So remember Me, and I will remember you; and be grateful to Me and never deny Me”* (2:152). The sūrah's readers in Makkah were being told to do with their own history what Israel was told to do with Egypt's — to treat the record of deliverance as the ground of loyalty.

**The Verse's Historical Reach**

The reminder is also a warning to a community that had not yet entered its own tests. The generation that sang at the sea would manufacture a calf within weeks (20:85–88), and the sūrah's reminder of the persecution is placed where the reader knows what is coming. The Book's narrative gives the memory of oppression to a community in order to make its later failures legible rather than excusable.

**The Test and Its Answer**

The verse names the persecution a severe test, and the sūrah's narrative will shortly name a second test at the mountain (7:143's vision) and a third in the wilderness (7:148's calf). The Book's account of Israel is a series of examinations, and the answer to each is recorded — including the answers that failed. The sūrah's honesty about that record is its own form of instruction for the community receiving the recitation.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:142

> **And We appointed for Moses thirty nights, and We completed them with ten [more]; thus was completed the appointed term of his Lord: forty nights. And Moses said unto his brother, Aaron, “Take my place among my people, set matters aright, and follow not the way of those who work corruption.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Thirty Nights and Ten More"**

*Wa-wāʿadnā Mūsā thalāthīna laylatan wa-atmamnāhā bi-ʿashrin fa-tamma mīqātu rabbihi arbaʿīna laylatan wa-qāla Mūsā li-akhīhi Hārūna akhlufnī fī qawmī wa-aṣliḥ wa-lā tattabiʿ sabīla al-mufsidīn* — We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten — completing his Lord's term of forty nights. Moses commanded his brother Aaron, "Take my place among my people, do what is right, and do not follow the way of the corruptors."

**The Appointment's Arithmetic**

The sūrah states the two figures and their total, and Sūrat al-Baqarah refers to the whole as *“forty nights”* (2:51). The Book's care with numbers here is characteristic of its legal-historical narration: the appointment (*mīqāt*) is a period of preparation before the giving of the Book, and the two stages — thirty and ten — mark a progress in readiness rather than a simple calendar.

**The Meeting to Come**

Sūrat Ṭāhā supplies the dialogue the sūrah compresses. Moses arrives ahead of his people and is asked why: *“˹Allah asked,˺ ‘Why have you come with such haste ahead of your people, O Moses?’ He replied, ‘They are close on my tracks. And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased’”* (20:83–84). The sūrah's phrase *the term of his Lord* is the appointment of that meeting, and the Book's narrative of it is one of its most intimate.

**The Command to Aaron**

*Akhlufnī fī qawmī* — take my place among my people. The Qur'an's Moses had asked for his brother from the beginning, and Sūrat Ṭāhā records the request and its answer: *“And grant me a helper from my family — Aaron, my brother”* (20:29–30), granted in 20:36. The sūrah's narrative now shows the deputy in office, which makes what follows during the absence a story about delegated responsibility as well as about a community's weakness.

**"Do What Is Right"**

*Wa-aṣliḥ* — the command's verb is the Book's term for setting a matter right, and it establishes Aaron's mandate as reform rather than mere maintenance. The Qur'an's vocabulary opposes *iṣlāḥ* to *ifsād*, and the same sūrah uses both terms in its narratives: Shuʿayb's counsel in Madyan (7:85's context), and the destroyers' label for their crimes (7:103). Moses' charge to Aaron is therefore religious and political at once.

**"Do Not Follow the Way of the Corruptors"**

*Wa-lā tattabiʿ sabīla al-mufsidīn* — the prohibition names the sūrah's recurring category. The corruptors (*mufsidūn*) are those whose activity degrades the moral order of a community, and the sūrah has just recorded the destruction of the towns for exactly this (7:103). Moses' instruction anticipates the specific failure that will occur: a population ready to follow a maker of idols (20:88).

**The Deputy's Record**

The Qur'an's account of Aaron during the absence is unsparing and sympathetic at once. He warned the people: *“O my people! You are only being tested by this, for indeed your ˹one true˺ Lord is the Most Compassionate. So follow me and obey my orders”* (20:90); they answered that they would persist until Moses returned (20:91). The Book's verdict on his performance arrives when Moses questions him, and Aaron answers, *“O son of my mother! Do not seize me by my beard or ˹the hair of˺ my head. I really feared that you would say, ‘You have caused division among the Children of Israel and did not wait for my word’”* (20:94).

**The Test That Was Coming**

The sūrah places the appointment and the deputation immediately before the community's fall, and the arrangement is the Book's method: the warning is given in advance and the failure recorded without excuses. Sūrat Ṭāhā's narration of the same events makes the test explicit — the calf was a trial, and the people's response to it was measured against the instruction that had been left with them.

**The Mountain's Place in the Book**

The appointment's location is fixed in several sūrahs: Moses is called at the sacred valley of Ṭuwā (20:12), from the bush on the right side of the valley (28:30), and blessed is the fire and those around it (27:8). The forty nights therefore lead to a meeting whose setting the Book treats with reverence, and the sūrah's wording — the term of his Lord — keeps the reader's attention on who set it.

**The Contrast With the Contest**

Earlier in the sūrah, Moses faced a state that demanded proofs and paid its experts. Here he leaves a community with a deputy and an instruction, and the narrative's contrast is instructive: Egypt's resistance was defeated by a sign in a day, and Israel's weakness required a whole book and forty nights of preparation.

**What the Book Says About Leadership**

The verse's two elements — a leader's private appointment with God and his public arrangements for the community — describe the Book's model of prophetic leadership. Moses' absence is planned, his brother's authority is specified, and the criterion given is reform. The Qur'an's political instruction consistently ties the legitimacy of a leader's deputy to conduct rather than to lineage or claim.

**The Fulfilment of the Term**

*Fa-tamma mīqātu rabbihi arbaʿīna laylatan* — the term was completed at forty nights. The Book's use of *tamma*, completed, echoes the language it uses for the fulfilment of the promise to Israel at 7:137 (*wa-tammat kalimatu rabbika*). The two completions frame the sūrah's narrative of the sea and the mountain: a deliverance and the constitution that was to follow it.

**What Comes to the Mountain's Foot**

The next verse reports the meeting itself, and its content is the Book's most celebrated dialogue about the vision of God. The reader of the sūrah arrives there having just seen Israel instructed, deputized, and warned, and with the knowledge that the community was already preparing the golden calf in its camp.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:143

> **And when Moses came to Our appointed meeting and his Lord spoke unto him, he said, “My Lord, show me, that I might look upon Thee.” He said, “Thou shalt not see Me; but look upon the mountain: if it remains firm in its place, then thou wilt see Me.” And when his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down in a swoon. And when he recovered, he said, “Glory be to Thee! I turn unto Thee in repentance, and I am the first of the believers.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"My Lord! Reveal Yourself to Me"**

*Wa-lammā jāʾa Mūsā li-mīqātinā wa-kallamahu rabbuhu qāla rabbi arinī anẓur ilayka qāla lan tarānī wa-lākini anẓur ilā al-jabali fa-in istaqarra makānahu fa-sawfa tarānī fa-lammā tajallā rabbuhu li-l-jabali jaʿalahu dakkan wa-kharra Mūsā ṣaʿiqan* — when Moses came at the appointed time and his Lord spoke to him, he asked: "My Lord! Reveal Yourself to me so I may see You." Allah answered: "You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me." When his Lord appeared to the mountain, He levelled it to dust and Moses collapsed unconscious.

**The Request**

The Book places the request at the summit of Moses' career: after forty nights, at the appointed meeting, in the middle of a conversation. Moses asks for the vision of God, and the sūrah treats the request as the overflow of an intimacy rather than an act of insolence. The Qur'an's other requests of this kind — the people's demand to see Allah with their own eyes (2:55) and the challenge of those who do not expect to meet Him (25:21) — are answered differently, and the difference lies in who is asking and how.

**The Answer's Absolute Form**

*Lan tarānī* — you will never see Me — is the Book's categorical statement about the vision of God in this world, and the Qur'an supports it elsewhere: *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware”* (6:103), and the declaration that nothing is like Him (42:11). Islamic theology has read verse 143 as its foundational text on the subject, and the sūrah's context — the mountain reduced to dust — supplies the demonstration.

**The Mountain as the Proof**

The answer is not merely a refusal; it contains a test the requested party can evaluate. *If it remains firm in its place* — and the Book reports what happened: *he levelled it to dust*. The Qur'an's argument is thus empirical within the narrative: a created thing cannot endure the manifestation of the Creator, and therefore the request's own terms establish its impossibility.

**Moses' Collapse and Confession**

*Kharra Mūsā ṣaʿiqan* — Moses fell unconscious — is followed by the recovery and one of the Book's most complete statements of repentance: *“Glory be to You! I turn to You in repentance and I am the first of the believers”* (7:143). The sequence of vision refused, creature undone, and prophet prostrate is the Qur'an's answer to every later claim of having seen God in this world.

**"The First of the Believers"**

The clause has been read in several ways, and the readings agree on its humility: the first among his people to believe, or the first to believe the truth of what he had just learned about God's transcendence. The sūrah's narrative gives the same phrase to the Egyptian magicians after the contest (26:51) and to Abraham in the matter of pure monotheism (6:79's sense), and the Book consistently places it on the lips of those who have just been corrected.

**The Earlier Encounters at the Mountain**

The sūrah's readers have been given the mountain's history: the call from the fire at the sacred valley (20:11–14), the blessing pronounced at the bush (27:8–9), and the direct speech the Book names as Moses' distinction — *“And to Moses Allah spoke directly”* (4:164). Verse 143 is the culmination of that series: the man who had been addressed by God asks to see Him and is shown the limit of the creature.

**The Distinction of Speech and the Refusal of Sight**

The two revelations are placed side by side in this passage: God speaks to Moses, and God refuses to be seen by him. The Book thereby establishes a doctrine of revelation as address rather than appearance, and the next verse names the honour that remains — messages and speech (7:144). The Qur'an's theology of the divine-human relation runs through that distinction.

**What the Scene Teaches the Community**

Israel had asked for direct vision at Sinai (2:55; 4:153), and Moses' experience answered the request before it was made again. The sūrah's placement of this scene immediately after the reminder of the deliverance and the appointment of Aaron makes it part of the community's formation: a people being given a book is being taught, at the same time, the limits of what its prophet may ask.

**The Prophets and Their Answers**

The Book's narratives of prophetic requests do not present them as failures: Abraham's plea for his father (9:114's account), Noah's question about his son (11:45–47), and Moses' request for vision are reported with their corrections. The Qur'an's model of prophethood includes the correction of the messenger, and the sūrah's report of Moses' repentance is one of its clearest instances.

**The Scene's Aftermath**

The narrative continues with God's address to Moses in the next verse, which names what has been granted in place of the vision: elevation by messages and speech, and the command to hold firmly and be grateful (7:144). The Book's sequence is deliberate: the refusal of sight is followed immediately by the grant of guidance, and the sūrah treats the second as the greater gift.

**The Contrast With the Idol**

While Moses was at the mountain receiving these answers, his people were manufacturing a calf to look at (20:85–88). The sūrah's arrangement of the two scenes is the Book's sharpest illustration of what the human being wants as opposed to what the human being is given: a visible object on one side, and speech that cannot be seen on the other.

**What the Reader Is Left With**

The verse ends with a prophet prostrate and a mountain reduced to dust, and the Book does not add a commentary. The reader is left with the sentence that has shaped the discussion of God's nature ever since — *you will never see Me* — and with the next verse's reminder that Moses was nevertheless spoken to.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:144

> **He said, “O Moses! Verily I have chosen thee above mankind through My messages and My speaking [unto thee]. So take that which I have given thee, and be among the thankful.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Elevated You Above All Others by My Messages and Speech"**

*Qāla yā Mūsā innī iṣṭafaytuka ʿalā al-nāsi bi-risālātī wa-bi-kalāmī fa-khudh mā ātaytuka wa-kun mina al-shākirīn* — He said: "O Moses! I have ˹already˺ elevated you above all others by My messages and speech. So hold firmly to what I have given you and be grateful."

**The Basis of the Honour**

The verse names the two things that constitute Moses' distinction: *risālātī* and *kalāmī*, the messages and the direct speech. The Qur'an states the second elsewhere in a single clause — *“And to Moses Allah spoke directly”* (4:164) — and it is this, not sight, that the Book treats as the summit of Moses' experience at the mountain.

**The Intimacy of the Call**

The sūrahs that narrate the call supply the atmosphere the abbreviation retains: the voice from the fire in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā (20:12), the blessing at the bush on the right side of the valley (27:8), and the nearness the Book states in its own words: *“We called him from the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and drew him near, speaking ˹with him˺ directly”* (19:52). The verse's *speech* is the content of those nights and this appointment.

**The Choice of a Messenger**

*Iṣṭafaytuka* — I have chosen you — is the Book's verb for the selection of messengers, and the Qur'an uses it of the families chosen above their peoples: *“Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ʾImrān above all people ˹of their time˺”* (3:33). The honour of verse 144 is therefore not personal superiority but commission: Moses had been selected for a task, and the sūrah's earlier chapters have shown the scale of it — a court, a kingdom, a sea, and a community to be formed.

**The Command to Hold Firm**

*Khudh mā ātaytuka* — take what I have given you — is the same charge given to Israel at the mountain: *“Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”* (2:63; 2:93), and it recurs in the sūrah's own instruction about the tablets (7:145). The Book's reception of revelation is consistently described as an act of grasping, and obedience is presented as the strength to hold.

**Gratitude as the Response to Elevation**

*Wa-kun mina al-shākirīn* — and be among the grateful. The Qur'an's treatment of gratitude is a whole theology: *“If you are grateful, I will surely increase you ˹in favour˺”* (14:7), *“whoever is grateful, it is only for their own good”* (31:12), and Solomon's court hears the same principle stated in the middle of a miracle: *“whoever is grateful is grateful for ˹the good of˺ his own soul”* (27:40), and the Book's summary command to the believers — *“remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful”* (2:152). The honour of verse 144 arrives with an obligation to acknowledge its source.

**The Contrast With the Request**

Read after verses 138–140 and 143, the verse completes a sequence of contrasts: the people asked for an idol and were told the model was doomed; Moses asked to see God and was shown a levelled mountain; and the gift actually granted was speech, with the command to hold and thank. The Book's arrangement is a lesson in the difference between what people want from revelation and what revelation is.

**The Rank of Moses in the Book**

The Qur'an names Moses more often than any other prophet, and the sūrahs that tell his story assign him the kinds of signs given to no one else: the staff, the hand, the sea, and the speech. Yet the Book's account of his elevation keeps the gift tied to a mission, and his failure to enter the Holy Land (5:23–26's context) is reported without exemption. The Qur'an's honour is never a claim about a person's essence.

**The Prophet's Own Comparison**

The Book tells the Prophet to follow the guidance given to the earlier messengers — *“They are the ones whom Allah has guided, so follow their guidance”* (6:90) — and it records his instruction to say that he is a human being receiving revelation (18:110). The honour of verse 144 is thus not a model for the comparison of prophets but for the relationship between a messenger and his commission.

**The Two Verbs of the Verse**

The command's verbs — hold and be grateful — define the whole of the religious response as the Book describes it: reception and acknowledgment. The sūrah's narratives of communities that failed at either are the surrounding argument. Israel will break both halves of the charge within a few verses, and the sūrah records it.

**The Mountain's Legacy**

The encounter of verse 143 and the commission of verse 144 are the origin of the tradition's description of Moses as the one who spoke with God, and of the Qur'an's use of Sinai as a place of covenant. The Book refers to the mountain by name and by oath (95:2; 52:1's Mount), and the sūrah's account of this appointment is the reason.

**The Verse's Practical Instruction**

For the sūrah's readers, the sentence has a portable shape: what God has given is to be held, and the response to being given anything is gratitude. The Book's ethical vocabulary runs from that base to the details of law, and the sūrah's next verses supply the written form of the guidance that Moses was told to grasp.

The Book never separates the gift from the giver in this way. The community that receives a scripture receives a trust, and the sūrah's own later verses report what happened when the generation at the mountain treated the trust as a possession: they were succeeded by generations who inherited the Book and used it as a cover for unlawful gain (7:169). Gratitude, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, is the acknowledgment that nothing received is owned.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:145

> **And We wrote for him upon the Tablets an exhortation concerning all things, and an elaboration of all things. “Take hold of them with strength, and command thy people to hold to the best of them. Soon I shall show thee the abode of the iniquitous.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"We Wrote for Him on the Tablets"**

*Wa-katabnā lahu fī al-alwāḥi min kulli shayʾin mawʿiẓatan wa-tafṣīlan li-kulli shayʾin khudhhā bi-quwwatin wa-mur qawmaka yaʾkhudhū bi-aḥsanihā sa-urīkum dāra al-fāsiqīn* — We wrote for him on the Tablets ˹the fundamentals˺ of everything; commandments and explanations of all things. ˹We commanded,˺ "Hold to this firmly and ask your people to take the best of it. I will soon show ˹all of˺ you the home of the rebellious."

**The Written Form of Revelation**

The verse is the Qur'an's most concrete statement about the material transmission of revelation: writing, tablets, and a text. The Book elsewhere names the scripture given to Moses as a guide and a mercy (17:2), a completion of the favour detailing everything (6:154), and *“the Book of Moses... a guide and mercy”* (46:12). The sūrah's readers are being told that the guidance which could not be seen on the mountain had been written down instead.

**"The Fundamentals of Everything"**

The phrase claims comprehensiveness for the revelation given to Moses: commandments, explanations, and the principles of conduct. The Qur'an makes a similar claim for its own book — *“We have sent down to you the Book as an explanation of everything”* (16:89) — and the sūrah's formulation belongs to the Book's consistent doctrine that divine guidance is sufficient for the life of a community. What is given is not a set of hints but a constitution.

**The Command to Hold Firmly**

*Khudhhā bi-quwwatin* — take it with strength — is addressed to Moses, and it repeats the charge of the previous verse (*hold firmly to what I have given you*, 7:144). The Book's language for receiving revelation is physical and demanding, and the same verb is used for Israel at the mountain: *“Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”* (2:63; 2:93).

**"Ask Your People to Take the Best of It"**

The command extends the obligation to the community: the prophet is to require his people to adopt the best of what has been revealed. The Qur'an's description of the guided uses the same idea — *“those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it. These are the ones ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah”* (39:18) — and the sūrah's phrasing makes the community's reception of the law a matter of deliberate choice rather than inheritance.

**The Warning That Accompanies the Gift**

*Sa-urīkum dāra al-fāsiqīn* — I will soon show you the home of the rebellious. The sūrah has used the word *fāsiqīn* for those who broke their covenants (7:102) and it will use it for the dwellings of the disobedient, and the Book's later verses supply the meaning: *“But as for those who are rebellious, the Fire will be their home”* (32:20). The warning is attached to the gift, so that the giving of the tablets is accompanied by the statement of what refusal costs.

**The Tablets in the Narrative**

The sūrah's readers will shortly see the tablets thrown down and then taken up again. When Moses returns to find the calf, the Book says he was *“totally furious and sorrowful”* (7:150), and the tablets are cast aside in his grief; verse 154 reports the sequel: *“When Moses' anger subsided, he took up the Tablets whose text contained guidance and mercy for those who stand in awe of their Lord.”* The written revelation survives the leader's shock and the community's failure.

**The Law and the Community's Formation**

The tablets' function in the sūrah is to constitute a people. Israel had been delivered from a state, reminded of the deliverance, and given a deputy; now it receives a written law, and the narrative's later chapters record its repeated failures to keep it. The Book's account of the law at Sinai is thus not the end of the story but the beginning of a long examination.

**The Book's Continuity of Scripture**

The Qur'an consistently presents itself as the continuation of the revelation written for Moses: *“And before this ˹Quran˺ the Book of Moses was ˹revealed as˺ a guide and mercy. And this Book is a confirmation”* (46:12). The sūrah's tablets are therefore part of a single tradition of divine writing, and the Muslim community is told that it has received the same kind of trust — the sūrah of al-Baqarah's charge to hold and remember (2:63) applies to it in its own terms.

**What the Verse's Comprehensiveness Excludes**

The claim that the revelation contains commandments and explanations for everything is followed in the sūrah's history by the community's manufacture of a calf, and by Moses' own need for guidance in handling them. The Book's argument is not that the law answers every question but that it states the terms on which the community is to live, and that departure from it is a choice rather than a consequence of obscurity.

**The Faithful and the Rebellious**

The verse sets the two categories side by side: those who take the best of what is revealed, and the home of the rebellious that will be shown to them. The Qur'an's moral universe is built on that bifurcation, and the sūrah's narrative will supply both examples within a few verses — the calf-worshippers and those who repented (7:148–153).

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Structure**

With the tablets' writing, the sūrah completes the sequence that began with the deliverance at the sea: a people freed, a prophet summoned, a law written, and a warning issued. The narrative's next movement is the collapse of the community while its prophet is still on the mountain, and the Book reports it without adjusting the honour of what had been given.

**The Tablet's Afterlife in the Book's Memory**

The Qur'an's readers are told that the written guidance outlasted the crisis, and the Book's own claim for its own revelation is that it confirms what was written before while guarding it (5:48's framework). The sūrah's handling of the tablets is the origin of that idea: what God writes remains, and it is the community's business to hold it with strength.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:146

> **I shall turn away from My signs those who wax arrogant upon the earth without right. Even if they were to see every sign, they would not believe in them. And if they were to see the way of sound judgment, they would not take it as a way, but if they were to see the way of error, they would take it as a way. That is because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"I Will Turn Away From My Signs"**

*Sa-aṣrifu ʿan āyātīya alladhīna yatakabbarūna fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi wa-in yaraw kulla āyatin lā yuʾminū bihā wa-in yaraw sabīla al-rushdi lā yattakhidhūhu sabīlan wa-in yaraw sabīla al-ghayyi yattakhidhūhu sabīlan dhālika bi-annahum kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-kānū ʿanhā ghāfilīn* — I will turn away from My signs those who act unjustly with arrogance in the land. And even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them. If they see the Right Path, they will not take it. But if they see a crooked path, they will follow it. This is because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them.

**The Correspondence of Turning**

The verse states the Book's regular correspondence between a person's orientation and his capacity for evidence: those who turn away are turned away. The Qur'an says the same thing in the Makkan sūrahs about the Prophet's opponents: *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance”* (6:110), and *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered”* (2:7). What the sūrah's Moses is being told is that the audience for his signs is conditioned by what it has already chosen.

**The Arrogance Without Right**

The verse's condition for the blindness is a specific one: *those who act unjustly with arrogance in the land*, without any legitimate claim. The Book uses the phrase for Pharaoh and his army — *“he and his soldiers were arrogant in the land without right”* (28:39) — and it connects the posture to the reward that is excluded from the arrogant: *“That ˹eternal˺ Home in the Hereafter We reserve ˹only˺ for those who seek neither tyranny nor corruption on the earth”* (28:83). What the sūrah's verse describes is a self-elevation that has no basis in anything but the self.

**The Anatomy of a Settled Refusal**

The verse gives three symptoms of a refused revelation, and they form a sequence: signs seen and not believed; guidance recognized and not taken; error chosen as a road. The Qur'an states the same triple incapacity elsewhere — *“Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain…”* (59:21) is the counter-image; the sealed heart is the same phenomenon in different words (7:100–101; 30:59). The Book's grammar of unbelief is consistently about direction rather than information.

**The Crooked Path**

*Sabīl al-ghayy* — the road of error — is contrasted with *sabīl al-rushd*, the path of right judgment. The Qur'an uses the language of crookedness for the deliberate distortion of guidance: the sūrah's Shuʿayb warned Madyan against lying in ambush on every road and *“striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked”* (7:86), and Sūrat Āl ʿImrān asks the People of the Book why they turn believers away from the Way of Allah, *“striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, while you are witnesses ˹to it˺”* (3:99). The verse's travelers who prefer the bent road are the Book's image of a will that has stopped looking for the straight one.

**The Sūrah's Own Diagnosis Repeated**

The verse's final clause is the exact charge that closed the Egyptian narrative: *“so We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them in the sea for denying Our signs and being heedless of them”* (7:136). The sūrah is telling Israel, at the mountain, that the sentence which destroyed the state that had enslaved them describes a conduct rather than a nation, and that the conduct is available to anyone — including the people who had just been delivered.

**The Human Side and the Divine Side**

The Book presents the sealing from both directions without embarrassment. Verse 146 is spoken by God about those who behave in a certain way; the sections preceding it (7:100–102) stated the law in general terms about generations that inherit a land; and the Book's other statements place the same reality in the language of abandonment — *“Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts”* (45:23). The Qur'an's theology treats human choice as real and its consequences as authoritative.

**The Book's Warning Against Self-Confidence**

The verses around 146 explain themselves: the community that asked for an idol (7:138) is being told, in advance, what the end of that request looks like. The Qur'an's use of the teaching in its own address to the Muslims is explicit — *“And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord's revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done?”* (18:57) — and the sūrah's readers are meant to hear the caution in their own case.

**The Refusal's Cost Stated Positively**

The verse's prohibitions are completed in the sūrah's later scenes: the calf-worshippers, the shattered tablets, the generations punished in the wilderness. The Book does not leave the doctrine abstract; the very next chapter of the narrative supplies the demonstration that a community with the clearest signs in history could still see nothing.

**The Sealed Heart in the Sūrah's Vocabulary**

The sūrah had already used the vocabulary of the seal — *“Thus Allah seals the hearts of the disbelievers”* (7:101) — and the verse's *I will turn away* is the same operation described from the other side. The Qur'an's readers are being given, in the space of a few verses, both halves of a doctrine that later theology would call divine abandonment: the human choice, and the withdrawal of the light by which the choice would have been informed.

**The Transition to the Calf**

The narrative now leaves the mountain and returns to the camp, and the sūrah's next verse reports what the community had been doing while Moses received these instructions. The juxtaposition — the law written on tablets and an idol made of jewellery — is the Book's arrangement, and it gives verse 146 its immediate meaning.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:147

> **As for those who deny Our signs and the meeting of the Hereafter, their deeds have come to naught. Are they recompensed for aught save that which they used to do?**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Their Deeds Will Be in Vain"**

*Alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-liqāʾi al-ākhirati ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — the deeds of those who deny Our signs and the meeting ˹with Allah˺ in the Hereafter will be in vain. Will they be rewarded except for what they have done?

**The Verb for Voidness**

*Ḥabiṭat* — the deeds have become worthless — is the Qur'an's technical term for the collapse of human effort, and the Book illustrates it with a series of images. Sūrat Ibrāhīm compares such deeds to ashes on a stormy day, blown away with nothing gained (14:18); Sūrat al-Nūr compares them to a mirage that a thirsty traveler takes for water (24:39); Sūrat Āl ʿImrān compares them to a harvest struck by a bitter wind that destroys it completely (3:117); and Sūrat al-Baqarah compares them to a smooth rock with dust on it which heavy rain leaves bare (2:264).

**What Makes Deeds Void**

The verse names two denials: the signs and the meeting in the Hereafter. The Qur'an's pairing is consistent — the moral quality of an act depends on the horizon against which it is performed — and the Book states the consequence in closely parallel verses: *“Losers indeed are those who deny the meeting with Allah until the Hour takes them by surprise”* (6:31); *“Indeed, those who do not expect to meet Us, being pleased and content with this worldly life, and who are heedless of Our signs”* (10:7). The sūrah's sentence is the doctrinal summary of that pairing.

**The Book's Word for the Meeting**

*Liqāʾ Allāh* is the Qur'an's term for the encounter with the Lord that gives life its accountability, and the Book's warnings about its denial are among its most vivid. Sūrat al-Jāthiyah reports the verdict spoken to the negligent: *“This Day We will neglect you as you neglected the meeting of this Day of yours! Your home will be the Fire”* (45:34). The sūrah's verse states the same law in the register of a general observation about deeds.

**The Justice Formula**

*Hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — will they be rewarded except for what they have done? The Qur'an repeats this principle in several forms: *“Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss”* (45:15; 41:46), and *“Is there any reward for goodness except goodness?”* (55:60). The Book's doctrine of recompense is consistently proportionate, and it sets limits that the sūrah's readers are meant to notice: *“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold. But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one”* (6:160).

**The Contrast With the Believers' Record**

Sūrat al-Kahf states the other half of the ledger immediately after describing the futile: *“As for those who believe and do good, We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds”* (18:30). The Qur'an's God is described as neither wasting the effort of the good nor allowing the deeds of the heedless to provide them with standing. Verse 147 is the second clause of that pairing in the sūrah's own words.

**The Record That Cannot Be Lost**

The Book's description of the final accounting makes the verse's premise concrete: the record will be laid open, and the wicked will be shown what is written in it (18:49); *“whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it”* (99:7); and the one whose scale is light *“will have doomed themselves”* (7:9). The sūrah's declaration that deeds become worthless therefore means worthless for reward, not that they go unrecorded.

**The Sūrah's Own Warning**

The sūrah has already stated the criterion in its account of the scales: *“But those whose scale is light, they have doomed themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs”* (7:9). Verse 147 repeats the diagnosis with the addition of the Hereafter, and its placement in the narrative — after the calf-worshippers' remorse and before Moses' return — makes it a comment on the community whose deeds had just failed the standard.

**The Denial of the Meeting as a Way of Life**

The Qur'an's analysis of those who deny the encounter is not that they hold a mistaken opinion but that they organize their conduct accordingly. The Book describes them as those who *“do not expect to meet Us, being pleased and content with this worldly life, and who are heedless of Our signs”* (10:7), and the verses that follow report their home as the Fire. The sūrah's verse states the consequence in a sentence, and the Book's narratives supply the demonstration.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The verse does not state that the deniers' deeds were evil in themselves, and the Books' images of ashes and mirages make the point: effort can be considerable and produce nothing. Sūrat al-Kahf's account of those *“whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good”* (18:104) is the sūrah's companion text, and the sūrah's own calf narrative had shown a community engaged in elaborate religious activity.

**The Address to Israel**

Spoken in the context of the Sinai narrative, the verse is directed at a community with a history of signs and a repeated failure to hold them. The Book's reproach uses the plural of deeds — what they used to do — and it treats the calf episode as one instance within a record rather than a single lapse. The sūrah's account of that record continues with Moses' return in the next verse.

**The Doctrine's Use in the Sūrah's Address**

The Prophet's community receives the same lesson through the Book's recitation of the history, and Sūrat al-Kahf's question — shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds? (18:103) — is addressed to the Book's own audience. The sūrah's verse therefore serves both as a comment on Israel's past and as a warning to the people hearing it recited.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

The sūrah's account of the calf has reported the sin (7:148) and the remorse (7:149); the general law of verse 147 intervenes before the narrative resumes with the leader's return and the punishment. The Book's arrangement places doctrine inside story, and the reader is meant to carry the principle into the scene that follows — in which mercy and judgment are both administered.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:148

> **And while he was away, the people of Moses took a calf [made] from their ornaments—a body that lowed. Did they not consider that it spoke not unto them, nor guided them to any way? They took it up, and they were wrongdoers.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"An Idol of a Calf That Lowed"**

*Wa-ittakhadha qawmu Mūsā min baʿdihi min ḥuliyyihim ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun a-lam yaraw annahu lā yukallimuhum wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan ittakhadhūhu wa-kānū ẓālimīn* — in the absence of Moses, his people made from their ˹golden˺ jewellery an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. Did they not see that it could neither speak to them nor guide them to the right path? Still they worshipped it, and were unjust.

**The Comparison With the Mountain**

The verse's first words place it in time: *in his absence*. While Moses was receiving the tablets and being told to hold them with strength (7:145), the community was melting down its jewellery. The sūrah's arrangement of the two scenes gives the reader the contrast the narrative has been building since the request for a visible god at 7:138.

**The Raw Material of the Idol**

*Min ḥuliyyihim* — from their jewellery. The Book's detail is not incidental: the gold had come out of Egypt, and the sūrah's narrative had reported the plagues and the deliverance. The metal the people used to make a god was the residue of a state's wealth, and the Qur'an's narratives of idolatry consistently connect the object of worship to the economy that produced it.

**The Sound and the Silence**

The calf's single feature is *khuwār* — a lowing sound — and the verse's question is built on the contrast between noise and speech: *could it neither speak to them nor guide them?* The Book's critique of idols is repeated in this form across the sūrahs: Abraham asks his father about a being that cannot hear or see (19:42), and Sūrat Ṭāhā's account of the same calf asks whether it could return a word to them or had any power to benefit or harm (20:89).

**The Rubric the Book Uses for Idols**

The Qur'an's tests for divinity are consistently functional: speech, guidance, benefit, harm, creation, provision. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ's Abraham states the standard and then applies it — *“They are ˹all˺ enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds”* (26:77) — after questioning the worship of what his ancestors had served. The sūrah's question about the calf is that rubric in a single sentence, and it is framed as something the people could have checked for themselves.

**The Failure to Check**

*Did they not see* — the question identifies the moment of refusal as one of attention rather than of evidence. The people had the calf in front of them and could observe that it did nothing; the Book's verdict on the episode is that they were *ẓālimīn*, unjust, which in the Qur'an's usage means placing something where it does not belong. The sūrah's earlier verdict on the idolaters the Israelites admired — *doomed to destruction* (7:139) — now applies inside the camp.

**The Samaritan's Role and the Community's Consent**

Sūrat Ṭāhā names the maker and the community's excuse: *“We did not break our promise to you of our own free will, but we were made to carry the burden of the people's ˹golden˺ jewellery, then we threw it in ˹the fire˺ — and so did the Samaritan”* (20:87), followed by the molding of the calf and the claim that it was the god of Moses (20:88). The sūrah's account here compresses all of it into one clause, and its verb — *they made* — assigns the deed to the community rather than to its instigator.

**Aaron's Warning Ignored**

Sūrat Ṭāhā records that the deputy did his duty: *“Aaron had already warned them beforehand, ‘O my people! You are only being tested by this, for indeed your ˹one true˺ Lord is the Most Compassionate. So follow me and obey my orders’”* (20:90). The people's answer was to wait for Moses (20:91). The sūrah's narrative of the episode is thus a study in collective responsibility: a warning given, a community that preferred to wait for a leader, and an offense committed in the meantime.

**The Book's Repetition of the Episode**

The Qur'an returns to the calf in sūrat after sūrah, because the episode became the archetype of Israel's covenant-breaking: *“And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully”* (2:51). The repetition is the Book's method of training its own community to recognize the pattern — an idol made while the guidance is being received.

**What the Calf Scene Says About Leadership**

The episode's real drama is about the interval between a leader's departure and his return. Moses had provided a deputy, a charge, and a criterion (7:142); the community had the guidance and asked for a spectacle. The sūrah's readers are being shown that the strength of a community's institutions is tested precisely when leadership is absent.

**The Word for the Community's Position**

*Ẓālimīn* — unjust — is the Book's most general term of condemnation, and the verse's use of it here is deliberate: the offense is not primarily intellectual error but misplacement of worship. The Qur'an's narratives of idolatry treat the practice as an injustice against God, against the self, and against the community's own constitution.

**The Narrative's Next Move**

The report of the calf is followed by the people's remorse and prayer (7:149), by Moses' furious return (7:150), and by the punishment and forgiveness that follow (7:152–153). The sūrah treats the sin as a real offense with real consequences and real repentance, and it records all three rather than allowing the episode to end in either despair or indulgence.

**The Episode's Lesson for the Sūrah's Readers**

The Qur'an's address to the Prophet's community used this history as a warning about their own loyalty: the Book's refrain about the people who saw the sea and asked for an idol within weeks of their deliverance is not a story about antique foreigners. It is a diagnosis of what a community can do with a freedom it has not yet learned to use.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:149

> **And when they wrung their hands and saw that they had gone astray, they said, “If our Lord does not have mercy upon us and forgive us, we shall surely be among the losers!”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Our Lord! We Have Wronged Ourselves"**

*Wa-lammā nadimū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-raʾaw annahum qad ḍallū qālū la-in lam yarḥamnā rabbunā wa-yaghfir lanā la-nakūnanna mina al-khāsirīn* — later, when they were filled with remorse and realized they had gone astray, they cried: "If our Lord does not have mercy on us and forgive us, we will surely be among the losers."

**The Same Prayer Earlier in the Sūrah**

The sūrah has recorded this formula before, and its first appearance was on the lips of Adam and Eve after the fall: *“They replied, ‘Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers’”* (7:23). The calf-worshippers' prayer is thus placed inside a pattern established at the beginning of the sūrah: the Book's central account of repentance is the one the first human beings spoke, and Israel's idolaters arrive at it after their own failure.

**The Two Elements of the Sentence**

The verse's opening clauses are a diagnosis that precedes the plea. *Wa-lammā nadimū* — when they regretted — is the emotional acknowledgment; *wa-raʾaw annahum qad ḍallū* — when they saw that they had strayed — is the intellectual one. The Qur'an's repentance scenes consistently join the two, and the Book's vocabulary for moral failure includes seeing it as clearly as it does feeling it.

**The Admission of Wrong**

*Rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā* in Adam's words, and the sūrah's repetition of the same construction in verse 149, are the Book's model of confession. The Qur'an's sinners are given sentences that name the fault, and the Book's doctrine of repentance begins with that naming. The opposite of the practice is the posture of verse 146's arrogant, who see signs and take no notice of their own condition.

**The Condition's Grammar**

*La-in lam yarḥamnā rabbunā wa-yaghfir lanā* — if our Lord does not have mercy on us and forgive us. The sentence hangs the outcome entirely on God's action, and the Book's repentance formulas are built that way: the supplicant contributes the acknowledgment, and the remedy belongs to God. The Qur'an's answer to despair is stated in the same register: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins”* (39:53).

**The Book's Promise of Acceptance**

The Qur'an treats repentance as a standing offer with stated conditions, and the sūrah itself supplies them in the next verse: *“But those who commit evil, then repent and become ˹true˺ believers, your Lord will certainly be All-Forgiving, Most-Merciful”* (7:153). The Book's other formulations add detail: *“Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance”* (2:37); *“I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on ˹true˺ guidance”* (20:82); *“He is the One Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons ˹their˺ sins”* (42:25).

**The Scope of the Remedy**

The sūrah's promises are not limited to forgiveness. Sūrat al-Furqān states the exchange that the Book offers the repentant — *“they are the ones whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds”* (25:70) — and the sūrah's own narrative reports that Moses' prayer for the community included both forgiveness and mercy (7:151). The Book's treatment of repentance is architectural rather than cosmetic: the structure of the person's record is rebuilt.

**The Word for the Losers**

*Mina al-khāsirīn* is the Book's term for those whose loss is total, and it appears in its own distinct contexts: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, they are the ˹true˺ losers”* (7:178); *“those who doomed themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs”* (7:9); and the sūrah of al-Kahf's account of those whose efforts are in vain (18:103–105). The calf-worshippers' fear is therefore precisely stated: the loss they dread is not the loss of a battle but of the self.

**The Community's Remorse and Moses' Return**

The verse is placed directly before Moses' furious arrival, and the sequence matters: the people had repented before their leader returned, and the sūrah's narrative reports their prayer and the leader's anger as consecutive facts. The Book does not treat the remorse as cancelling the punishment; the sūrah's next verses report Moses' grief, the shattering of the tablets, and the retribution and forgiveness that follow.

**The Tablets and the Repentance**

The tablets' fate is the narrative's symbol for what sin does to guidance: cast down in the leader's fury (7:150) and taken up again after the anger subsides, *“whose text contained guidance and mercy for those who stand in awe of their Lord”* (7:154). The Book's arrangement places the recovery of the law after the community's remorse, and the sūrah's readers are shown a community that broke its covenant and was given the law back.

**The Book's Rejection of Despair**

The Qur'an's teaching about repentance is stated most directly in the call not to despair: the sūrah of al-Zumar's verse is addressed to the ones who have exceeded every limit, and it forbids hopelessness on the ground that God forgives. The calf episode's function in the sūrah is to demonstrate the doctrine within a narrative: an offense as serious as idolatry is followed by a prayer and a promise.

**The Model That Outlives the Episode**

The vocabulary of verse 149 reappears throughout the Qur'an's exhortations, and Muslim devotional practice has preserved these sentences among its most familiar supplications. The sūrah's readers are given a community's failure and its prayer in the same passage, which is the Book's way of teaching that the remedy is not the avoidance of the story but the repetition of the words.

**What the Next Verses Report**

Moses' anger, Aaron's defense, the punishment of the calf-makers, and the forgiveness extended to the repentant are all reported in the verses that follow (7:150–153). The sūrah treats the episode as closed by justice and mercy rather than by amnesia: the offense is named, the punishment stated, and the door of return held open in the same passage.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:150

> **And when Moses returned unto his people angry and aggrieved, he said, “How evil is the course you have followed after me! Would you hasten the Command of your Lord?” And he cast down the Tablets and seized his brother by the head, dragging him toward himself. He said, “Son of my mother! Truly the people deemed me weak, and they were about to kill me. So let not the enemies rejoice in my misfortune, and place me not with the wrongdoing people.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"What an Evil Thing You Committed in My Absence"**

*Wa-lammā rajaʿa Mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayh* — upon Moses' return to his people, ˹totally˺ furious and sorrowful, he said: "What an evil thing you committed in my absence! Did you want to hasten your Lord's torment?" Then he threw down the Tablets and grabbed his brother by the hair, dragging him closer.

**The Return and the Anger**

The Book's Moses comes down the mountain with the law in his hands and finds his community worshipping a calf. The verse names his state with two words — *ghaḍbān asifan*, furious and sorrowful — and the Book's prophets are consistently portrayed as men whose anger and grief are real. The Qur'an does not sanitize the scene, and its honesty here is part of what makes the narrative's reconciliation credible.

**The Question**

*What an evil thing you committed in my absence* is an indictment, and the second clause asks whether they had meant to bring their Lord's punishment early. The Book's rebuke of hastiness runs through its narratives of Israel: the community that asked to see God (2:55), the demand for a sign on paper (4:153), and the readiness to trade a prophet for a visible object. Moses' question identifies the calf's real offense as impatience with the terms of their own covenant.

**Throwing Down the Tablets**

The Book reports the gesture and does not comment on it. The written law — which Moses had been commanded to hold firmly (7:145) — is cast down in the leader's grief, and the sūrah's later verse reports its recovery (7:154). The narrative's symbol is exact: the guidance given for a community is knocked from its hands by that community's failure.

**Seizing Aaron**

The verse's next clause is one of the Book's most human scenes: Moses *grabbed his brother by the hair, dragging him closer*. Sūrat Ṭāhā's account gives the same detail with both hands involved — *“Do not seize me by my beard or ˹the hair of˺ my head”* (20:94) — and Aaron's plea explains his conduct: he had feared that resistance would divide the Children of Israel and that Moses would blame him for causing the split.

**The Deputy's Account of Himself**

The Qur'an's version of Aaron's defense is a statement of political judgment rather than an excuse: *“I really feared that you would say, ‘You have caused division among the Children of Israel and did not wait for my word’”* (20:94). The Book's readers had already seen Aaron warn the people that the calf was a test and command them to follow him (20:90); the community had refused and waited for Moses (20:91). The scene's allocation of blame is therefore more complicated than the surface quarrel suggests.

**What the Book Says About Leadership and Anger**

The episode's realism is deliberate. The Qur'an reports the fury of its own prophet toward his appointed deputy, and it reports the prayer that follows: *“My Lord! Forgive me and my brother! And admit us into Your mercy”* (7:151). The Book's model of leadership includes accountability, error, and repair, and it never presents its messengers as exempt from the emotions that make the repair necessary.

**The Prophets' Displays of Temper in the Book**

The Qur'an's narratives contain other such scenes: Noah's plea about his son and the correction it received (11:45–47), Jonah's departure and the lesson of the fish (37:139–144), and David's judgment in the case of the two litigants (38:21–25). The Book's consistent practice is to record the human moment and the divine instruction together, and the sūrah's Moses follows that pattern.

**The Community's Other Account**

Sūrat Ṭāhā records what Moses said to the people themselves, and it supplies the context for the question of verse 150: *“O my people! Had your Lord not made you a good promise? Has my absence been too long for you, or did you want your Lord's wrath to befall you?”* (20:86). The sūrah's compressed phrase — did you want to hasten your Lord's torment — is the same accusation in a clause.

**The Samaritan and the Calf's End**

The fuller narrative names the man who made the calf and reports his sentence: the idol was burned and its ashes scattered into the sea (20:97). The sūrah's own account of the punishment belongs to the next verse (7:152), and the community's penance is reported in Sūrat al-Baqarah's charge: *“turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves”* (2:54).

**The Tablets' Fate as the Scene's Real Subject**

The Book's placement of the tablets in the middle of this quarrel is not incidental. The community had been given a written law, and the narrative's next movement — the leader's rage, the deputy's seizing, the punishment, and the restoration of the text — is a study in what happens to guidance when a people abandons it. Verse 154 will report the text's return to Moses' hands.

**The Scene's Place in Israel's Record**

The sūrah's account of the calf sits inside a longer record that the Book recites to the Prophet's community: the sea, the manna, the water from the rock, the mountain, the calf, the spies, and the forty years of wandering (2:57–61; 5:20–26).

**The Anger's Resolution**

The next verse reports what Moses did with his fury: he prayed for himself and his brother. The sūrah therefore gives the reader a leader who acts on his anger and then repairs the damage in the same scene, and the Book's use of the episode in its teaching about repentance depends on both halves being told.

**The Narrative's Next Movement**

With the confrontation completed, the sūrah turns to the judgment on the calf-worshippers (7:152), the offer of forgiveness to those who repent (7:153), and the recovery of the tablets (7:154). The reader of the sūrah is given the fall, the quarrel, the verdict, the mercy, and the law in the space of five verses.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:151

> **He said, “My Lord, forgive me and my brother and bring us into Thy Mercy, for Thou art the most Merciful of the merciful.”**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Forgive Me and My Brother"**

*Qāla rabbi ighfir lī wa-li-akhī wa-adkhilnā fī raḥmatika wa-anta arḥamu al-rāḥimīn* — Moses prayed: "My Lord! Forgive me and my brother! And admit us into Your mercy. You are the Most Merciful of the merciful."

**The Prayer That Ends the Quarrel**

The sūrah's previous verse reported Moses' fury and the seizing of Aaron; this one reports the prayer that closed the scene. The Book's narrative gives the two brothers a shared request — forgiveness for both — and thereby settles the question of responsibility that the quarrel had raised. Aaron had explained his fear of division (20:94), and Moses' answer is to place them both in the same plea.

**The Leader's Own Need**

*Ighfir lī* — forgive me — is spoken by the man who had spoken with God and received the tablets. The Qur'an's prophets are consistently depicted as supplicants: Moses himself prays elsewhere, *“My Lord! I have surely wronged myself, so forgive me”* (28:16), David asks forgiveness after his judgment (38:24), and Abraham includes his own errors in his prayer (26:82). The Book's doctrine of prophecy never removes the messenger's need for mercy.

**A Prayer for a Brother**

The petition's second half is a model of reconciliation, and the Qur'an's narratives of brothers supply its context. Joseph's treatment of his brothers ends in the same register — *“There is no blame on you today. May Allah forgive you! He is the Most Merciful of the merciful!”* (12:92) — and the Book's instruction to the believers addresses divisions directly: *“The believers are brothers, so make peace between your brothers”* (49:10). The sūrah's Moses prays for the deputy he had just dragged by the hair.

**The Request to Enter Mercy**

*Wa-adkhilnā fī raḥmatika* — admit us into Your mercy — uses the Book's language of entry rather than of gift, and the sūrah itself states the widest form of the mercy a few verses later: *“My mercy encompasses everything”* (7:156). The Qur'an's other prayers use the same spatial vocabulary — *“Our Lord, You encompass everything in mercy and knowledge”* (40:7) — and the request places the two brothers inside a mercy that is larger than their dispute.

**The Superlative Name**

*Wa-anta arḥamu al-rāḥimīn* — You are the Most Merciful of the merciful. The phrase is one of the Qur'an's recurring supplications: Joseph's father says it when he fears for Benjamin (12:64), Ayyūb prays it in his illness (21:83), and the believers' prayer in Sūrat al-Muʾminūn closes with the same affirmation (23:109, 23:118). The Book gives this superlative to the human beings asking for mercy, which is its own statement about how the Qur'an's supplicants address God.

**The Community's Own Reconciliation**

The prayer's usefulness for the community is not limited to its two subjects. Israel's commander had publicly blamed his deputy; the next sentence of the story is a prayer offered for them both, and the sūrah's subsequent verses turn to the community's repentance and forgiveness (7:153). The Book's narrative treats the healing of leadership as the precondition for the healing of the people.

**The Recovered Tablets**

The verse that immediately follows the prayer reports the restoration of the text: *“When Moses' anger subsided, he took up the Tablets whose text contained guidance and mercy for those who stand in awe of their Lord”* (7:154). The Book's arrangement places the leader's reconciliation before the law's return to his hands, and the sequence is the sūrah's statement about the order in which a broken community is rebuilt.

**What the Book Teaches About Conflict**

The Qur'an's instructions for settling disputes are stated in its legal verses — arbitration, waiting, and the duty to make peace (49:9–10) — and this passage supplies a prophetic illustration of the same ethic. The Book's Moses does not defend his anger, justify the seizure, or assign the blame to his deputy in the prayer; he asks forgiveness for the pair and then continues his mission.

**Moses' Own Standing**

The prayer for himself is not a formality. Moses had asked for his brother's assistance (20:29–36), had left him in charge (7:142), and had returned to find the community in the worst condition imaginable. His plea for forgiveness suggests the Book's awareness that a leader's arrangements may have contributed to the outcome, and the Qur'an's narratives elsewhere state that no one is exempt from accounting — *“the covenant will be questioned”* (17:34's principle).

**The Book's Vocabulary of Mercy**

The sūrah's readers meet the mercy language repeatedly: the messengers are sent as mercy (21:107), the Book is guidance and mercy (7:154), and the divine names in the prayer open the sūrah's own account of God's letters to His servants (7:156). Verse 151 therefore sits inside the sūrah's most sustained treatment of mercy, and it uses the vocabulary that treatment establishes.

**The Scene's Instruction for Leaders**

The episode gives leaders three things in three verses: the accountability of a deputy (7:150), the reconciliation of those who lead (7:151), and the community's punishment and forgiveness (7:152–153). The Book's editorial arrangement of the scene turns a family quarrel into a paradigm, and the sūrah's next chapter of Israel's history begins from the repaired relationship between the two brothers.

**The Prayer's Continued Use**

The sentence has remained part of Muslim supplication because of its shape: a request for forgiveness for oneself and another, a request for inclusion in mercy, and an affirmation of God's superlative mercy. The sūrah presents the words in a moment of acute conflict, and the Book's placement of them there is part of its teaching about what should be said when a community's leaders have failed together.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:152

> **As for those who took up the calf, anger from their Lord shall seize them, and abasement in the life of this world. Thus do We recompense those who fabricate.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Wrath and Disgrace in This World"**

*Inna alladhīna ittakhadhū al-ʿijla sa-yanāluhum ghaḍabun min rabbihim wa-dhillatun fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-kadhālika najzī al-muftarīn* — those who worshipped the calf will certainly be afflicted with Allah's wrath as well as disgrace in the life of this world. This is how We reward those who invent falsehood.

**The Punishment Stated Before It Is Described**

The sūrah announces the penalty for the calf-worshippers in a sentence, and the Book's other accounts supply the details: Moses commanded the community to carry out the execution of the offenders among themselves (2:54), and the maker of the calf received his own sentence — his idol was burned and its ashes scattered into the sea (20:97). The sūrah's verse states the verdict that governs all of it: wrath and humiliation in this world.

**The Pairing of Both Worlds**

The Qur'an's warnings frequently name the double cost — disgrace here and torment later — and the Book treats the first installment as evidence for the second. Sūrat al-Ḥajj describes those who turn away in pride: *“They will suffer disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Judgment We will make them taste the torment of burning”* (22:9); Sūrat al-Aḥzāb states the general rule about those who offend God and His Messenger: *“condemned by Allah in this world and the Hereafter”* (33:57). Verse 152 is the sūrah's instance of the pattern.

**"Those Who Invent Falsehood"**

*Al-muftarīn* — the fabricators — is the Book's term for those who attribute to God what is not His, and the Qur'an's challenge to them is repeated in almost identical words across the sūrahs: *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah?”* (6:21; 11:18; 29:68; 39:32). The calf was not merely an object of bad taste; the sūrah names it as a fabrication, an invention presented as the god of Moses (20:88).

**The Sin's Shape**

The Book's designation of the offense clarifies what the sūrah considers the gravest element of the episode: the community did not simply lapse into an old habit; it manufactured a claim about God. Sūrat al-Nisāʾ states the act's character in a single clause — *“See how they fabricate lies against Allah — this alone is a blatant sin”* (4:50) — and the Qur'an's strong reproach to whoever persists in such claims closes with, *“they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers”* (3:94).

**The Penance the Book Records**

Sūrat al-Baqarah gives the remedy Moses prescribed and the outcome: *“O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator”* — and the verse ends with the sentence that governs the whole episode — *“Then He accepted your repentance”* (2:54). The Book's sternest community sanction is reported together with its acceptance.

**The Maker's Own Sentence**

The fuller narrative distinguishes between the community and the man who built the idol: Sūrat Ṭāhā reports Moses' sentence on the Samaritan — a lifetime of exclusion and a fate he cannot escape (20:97) — and the destruction of the calf itself. The Book's judicial realism assigns responsibility at both levels, and the sūrah's verse states the outcome for those who worshipped it.

**The Difference From the Sūrah's Other Destroyed Communities**

The sūrah's earlier narratives end in extinction: the towns were cut off, the Egyptians drowned, and no repentance was reported. The calf episode ends with forgiveness, and the difference lies in what the people did after their sin. The sūrah's placement of verse 152 immediately before the promise of verse 153 is the Book's way of marking the distinction between a ruined community and a corrected one.

**The Verdict and the Warning to Later Generations**

The clause *this is how We reward those who invent falsehood* makes the sentence a rule rather than a report. The Prophet's community heard it as a warning about the claims people make on God's behalf, and the Book's narratives of the fabricators — from the calf to the speculations of the later scripture-holders — are all gathered under the same rubric. The verse's grammar is general, and the sūrah intends it to be.

**The Book's Proportionality**

The verses around the episode keep the punishment in proportion to the crime in the Book's own terms: the community's offenses had been corporate and public, its correction was corporate and public, and its acceptance was announced by God. The sūrah's treatment of the calf-worshippers is thus a case study in the Qur'an's conviction that communities, like individuals, are accountable for what they construct.

**What the Verse Does Not Authorize**

The Book's report of Israel's internal execution is a specific command given to a specific community, and the sūrah presents it as a penance ordered by God through His messenger. Later Muslim jurisprudence developed its own rules for apostasy and public order; the sūrah's readers are not given the episode as a license for private violence. The Book's narrative context is the key to the verse's meaning, and the context is divine instruction.

**The Mercy That Follows the Wrath**

The Qur'an's habit is to place the promise of forgiveness next to the announcement of punishment, and the sūrah does it here in consecutive verses. The sequence is the Book's pastoral arrangement: the reader is not allowed to conclude that the offense was trivial, and is not allowed to conclude that it was unforgivable.

**The Episode's Legacy in the Book's Address**

The Qur'an recalls the calf in sūrah after sūrah as the emblem of Israel's weakness, and it uses the memory to warn the Prophet's community about its own loyalty to revelation. Verse 152 supplies the verdict that accompanies the memory everywhere it appears, and the Book's readers are meant to hear in it both a judgment on the past and a caution about the present.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:153

> **But as for those who commit evil deeds and then repent thereafter and believe, surely, thereafter, thy Lord is Forgiving, Merciful.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"But Those Who Repent and Believe"**

*Wa-alladhīna ʿamilū al-sayyiʾāti thumma tābū min baʿdihā wa-āmanū inna rabbaka min baʿdihā la-ghafūrun raḥīm* — but those who commit evil, then repent and become ˹true˺ believers, your Lord will certainly be All-Forgiving, Most-Merciful.

**The Verse's Position**

The sūrah has just announced the punishment of the calf-worshippers (7:152), and it now states the exception. The Book's habit of placing mercy after warning is consistent across the Qur'an, and its purpose is stated in the sūrah of al-Zumar: *“Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins”* (39:53). Verse 153 is the sūrah's own version of that door.

**The Order of the Conditions**

The verse names evil, repentance, and faith in sequence, and the Book's other formulations expand the list. Sūrat al-Furqān states the exchange offered to those who meet the conditions — *“As for those who repent, believe, and do good deeds, they are the ones whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds”* (25:70) — and Sūrat Ṭāhā's promise includes persistence in guidance: *“I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on ˹true˺ guidance”* (20:82). The Qur'an's repentance is consistently a program rather than a sentiment.

**Repentance That Repairs**

The Book's other statements of the same promise emphasize amendment of conduct. *“As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺”* (2:160); *“As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (3:89). The vocabulary of mending in these verses shows that the Qur'an's door is open to the person who changes course, not to the person who merely regrets the consequences.

**The Two Names**

*Ghafūr* and *Raḥīm* — All-Forgiving, Most-Merciful — are the Book's most frequently paired divine names, and the sūrah has used them for the remedy throughout: the sūrah's own statement of the widest mercy, *“My mercy encompasses everything”* (7:156), stands a few verses later, and the promise attached to it names those who shun evil, pay alms, and believe. The sūrah's God is described neither as indifferent nor as unforgiving, and the pairing is the Book's way of holding judgment and mercy together.

**The Community That Received the Promise**

The verse's subject is Israel at the foot of the mountain, and the Book's later reports show the promise was taken up: Sūrat al-Baqarah's account of the penance ends with God's acceptance of the community's repentance (2:54), and the sūrah's own narrative continues with the seventy chosen for the appointment and the grant of mercy that followed (7:155–156). The Book treats the calf episode as closed by both judgment and forgiveness.

**The Contrast With the Book's Other Cases**

The Qur'an's warnings about repentance arriving too late are as clear as its promises: Sūrat al-Nisāʾ distinguishes the repentance of those who sin in ignorance and turn quickly from the repentance of those who persist until death (4:17–18), and Sūrat Āl ʿImrān states that those who disbelieve after believing and increase in disbelief will not have their repentance accepted (3:90). The sūrah's verse is the promise offered while there is still time, and the Book's narratives supply the cases where the time ran out.

**The Believer's Balance of Fear and Hope**

The Qur'an instructs its readers to hold the two together: *“Tell My servants that I am indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and that My torment is indeed the most painful torment”* (15:49–50's pairing). The sūrah's sequence — a punishment described and a forgiveness promised in the next verse — is the Book's most economical statement of that balance, and its readers are given both halves in the space of a breath.

**The Sūrah's Larger Argument**

Read with the sūrah's earlier narratives, verse 153 explains why the towns were destroyed and Israel was not. The sūrah's account of the communities that rejected their messengers contains no report of remorse, and its account of the calf does. The Book's narratives of punishment consistently locate the decisive difference in what people do after they have been warned, and the sūrah's structure makes that difference visible.

**The Verse's Use for the Prophet's Community**

The Qur'an applies the same promise to the Prophet's audience in its own voice: the Book's call not to despair, the promise of exchanged deeds, and the command to seek forgiveness describe the community's path in identical terms. The sūrah's history is thus a case law for the Muslims, and its placement in the middle of Israel's narrative gives the community a pattern for reading its own failures.

**What the Verse Requires of the Community**

The verse's conditions carry institutional consequences: faith and amendment of conduct are public facts, and the Book's legal material elsewhere works out what they mean for a community's order. The sūrah's own next chapter reports the covenant's renewal, and the sequence — offense, punishment, repentance, forgiveness, restoration — is the Book's complete account of how a community recovers.

**The Mercy's Address**

The verse's phrase *your Lord* is spoken to Moses about his people, and the possessive form is characteristic of the Book's consolation: the attribute is not abstract but attached to the community being addressed. The sūrah's readers, receiving the verse in their own recitation, are addressed by the same construction, and the Book's pastoral intention in the passage is stated in its last two words.

The sūrah's readers are also being told something about how a community should speak about its own past: Israel's worst episode is recited by the Book in full, including the forgiveness that followed, and the recitation itself becomes part of the community's instruction. A community that cannot bear to narrate its failures cannot learn from them.

## Sūrah al-Aʿrāf 7:154

> **And when the anger abated from Moses, he took up the Tablets; and in their inscription lay a guidance and a mercy for those who are in awe of their Lord.**

**Expanded Commentary**

**"He Took Up the Tablets"**

*Wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍabu akhadha al-alwāḥa wa-fī nuskhatihā hudan wa-raḥmatun li-lladhīna hum li-rabbihim yarhabūn* — when Moses' anger subsided, he took up the Tablets whose text contained guidance and mercy for those who stand in awe of their Lord.

**The Anger Subsides**

*Wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍab* — the Book's verb for the settling of Moses' fury closes the scene that began with his return from the mountain. The narrative has reported the calf, the remorse, the confrontation, the seizing of Aaron, the prayer for both brothers, the penalty on the worshippers, and the promise of forgiveness (7:148–153); the verse that concludes it reports only the leader's calm and the retrieval of the law.

**The Tablets Recovered**

The sūrah had reported the tablets' casting down in the moment of grief (7:150), and it now reports their recovery. The Book's symbol is exact and its sequence deliberate: the written guidance is knocked from a community's hands by that community's failure, and it is taken up again after the punishment and the repentance. What survives the crisis is the text.

**"Guidance and Mercy"**

The verse describes the tablets' contents with the pairing the Qur'an uses for revelation throughout: *“This is an insight to humanity — a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing”* (3:138); *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers”* (17:82); *“a warning from your Lord, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for the believers”* (10:57); *“This ˹Quran˺ is an insight for humanity — a guide and mercy for people of sure faith”* (45:20). The sūrah's own earlier statement — the Book is *“a guide and mercy for those who believe”* (7:52) — belongs to the same series.

**For Those Who Stand in Awe**

The verse's final clause restricts the benefit: *li-lladhīna hum li-rabbihim yarhabūn*, for those who are in awe of their Lord. The Book's statements about its own guidance are consistently conditioned in this way — *“a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”* (2:2); *“˹It is˺ a guide and mercy for the good-doers”* (31:3); *“it is truly a guide and mercy for the believers”* (27:77) — and the Qur'an states the reason elsewhere: *“Of all of Allah's servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him”* (35:28). The tablets guide the people disposed to be guided.

**The Fear That Receives Guidance**

The sūrah's use of *rahba* rather than a milder term for reverence sets the tone for the passage's conclusion, and the Qur'an attaches forgiveness and reward to the same disposition: *“Indeed, those in awe of their Lord without seeing Him will have forgiveness and a mighty reward”* (67:12). The community that made a calf out of its jewellery is told, at the end of the episode, that the law it had dropped was mercy for those who approach God with awe.

**The Law's Continuity**

The recovery of the tablets is also the Book's claim about the survival of revelation. The sūrah's narrative does not say the text was rewritten; it says Moses picked it up, and the guidance it contained remained the community's. The Qur'an's presentation of its own book in relation to earlier scripture follows the same logic: *“We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them”* (5:48).

**What the Community Had Left**

The episode's sequence is the sūrah's most complete account of how a religious community fails and recovers: a leader's absence, a deputy's warning ignored, an idol manufactured from the wealth of a former empire, a community's remorse, a leader's anger and reconciliation, a punishment accepted, a forgiveness promised, and a law taken up again. The sūrah's arrangement of the material is itself the lesson, and the verse's quiet sentence about the tablets is its last note.

**The Contrast With the Sūrah's Earlier Communities**

The towns whose stories fill the earlier part of the sūrah received messengers and destroyed them; Israel received a messenger, made a calf, repented, and was forgiven. The Book's placement of the two kinds of narrative in one sūrah shows what the difference consists in: not in the severity of the offense, but in what the offenders did next. The tablets' recovery is the visible form of that difference.

**The Messenger's Own Position at the End**

The episode's final image is of a prophet holding the revelation again, with his brother at his side and his community under a promise of forgiveness. The Book's account of Moses in this sūrah moves from a staff in the desert to a court in Egypt, a sea, a mountain, a shattered and recovered law, and it ends the episode with the messenger doing what he had been told on the mountain: *hold firmly to what I have given you* (7:144).

**What Comes After**

The sūrah's narrative continues immediately with the seventy men Moses chose for the appointment, the earthquake that seized them, and his plea that ends in the declaration that God's mercy encompasses everything and is ordained for those who shun evil, pay alms, and believe (7:155–156). The verse about the tablets is therefore a hinge: the episode of the calf closes, and the sūrah turns to the community's covenant and the favours it was given.

**The Verse's Instruction for Later Communities**

The Book's readers are given the sequence as a model, and the sūrah's own closing verses about the law's guidance states the pattern in its most general form: the text remains, and its benefit depends on the disposition of the people who approach it. The sūrah's narrative leaves the tablets in Moses' hands and the doctrine in the reader's: guidance and mercy are available, and they belong to those who stand in awe.
