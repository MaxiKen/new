**"Do They Not See Who Inherits the Land?"**

*A-wa-lam yabīn li-lladhīna yarithūna al-arḍa min baʿdi ahlihā an law nashāʾu aṣabnāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-naṭbaʿu ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yasmaʿūn* — is it not clear to those who inherit the land after its former people that, if We willed, We could strike them for their sins and seal their hearts so that they would not hear?

The verse closes the sūrah's meditation on the towns by turning from the dead to the survivors. Its addressees are the people who moved into the abandoned houses, irrigated the abandoned fields, and took over the caravan stops whose former owners the Book has just described being shaken out of them. The Book's question to them is a question about evidence: the ruins are visible, the story is public, the mechanism is documented — is all of that not plain?

**The Book's Doctrine of Succession**

The Qur'an consistently treats the succession of peoples as an office and not an accident. *“Then We made you successors in the land after them, to see how you would behave”* (Yūnus 10:14). *“Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? … We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins …”* (Anʿām 6:6). The Book's formula — establishment, abundance, destruction, replacement — is the same sequence the sūrah has just narrated in five instalments, and 6:6 states it as a general law addressed to the Prophet's contemporaries.

The doctrine cuts the other way as well. Sūrat al-Nūr makes the succession a promise to the faithful — *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good deeds that He will surely make them successors in the earth as He made successors of those before them”* (24:55) — and Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ states the condition plainly: *“Surely, following the ˹heavenly˺ Record, We decreed in the Scriptures: ‘My righteous servants shall inherit the land.’”* (21:105). The verse's warning and the Book's promise are the two faces of one law: the land changes hands, and the tenants' conduct decides the tenancy.

**The Seal on the Heart**

*Wa-naṭbaʿu ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yasmaʿūn.* The verse's second threat is graver than the first, and it names the penalty that makes the whole meditation on the towns intelligible. The Book's vocabulary of the sealed heart appears in several forms — *khatama* (2:7), *ṭabaʿa* (7:101, 10:74, 40:35), *rān* (83:14), *ghulf* (4:155) — and in each place it is the settled result of a person's own persistence rather than a sentence imposed from outside. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn states the mechanism with the most precision: *“But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14).

The Prophet's teaching preserved the same image in one of the best-known explanations of that verse. Abū Hurayrah reported him saying: *“When the servant commits a sin, a black spot appears in his heart; if he repents and desists, his heart is polished clean; but if he returns, it increases until it covers his heart — and that is the covering (rān) which Allah mentions: 'No indeed, what they used to earn has covered their hearts.'”* (al-Tirmidhī 3334, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ).

**"So They Will Not Hear"**

The verse's consequence is stated in terms of hearing rather than punishment, and the choice is deliberate in a sūrah that has been about speech and its reception from its first page. The Book's own description of the loss appears at 7:179, where the condemned have eyes that do not see and ears that do not hear, and at 7:198, where the idols' claimants are called and do not hear the call. Sūrat Yā Sīn states the same condition in the form of a barrier: *“We have placed before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them, so they do not see”* (36:9), followed immediately by the Book's own summary — *“and whether you warn them or do not warn them, they will not believe”* (36:10).

Sūrat al-Anʿām gives the same loss a first-person form: *“We shall turn away their hearts and their sight as they did not believe in it the first time”* (6:110). The sūrah's 7:100 therefore states, in one conditional sentence, the whole arithmetic of the Book's warnings: the punishment a nation fears may take away its cities, but the punishment that ends its possibility is the closing of the organ by which the message is received.

**What the Ruins Are For**

 The Book's recitation of the towns' ends is not an attempt to prove God's power in the abstract; it is a demonstration arranged for tenants who believe themselves permanent. Sūrat Ṭāhā's formulation is the closest parallel: *“Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment.”* (20:128). Sūrat al-Sajdah's version adds the Book's own lament: *“Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs. Will they not then listen?”* (32:26) — and the verse ends with the very faculty that 7:100's seal removes.

**The Warning the Sūrah Leaves Open**

The verse's grammar leaves the reader inside the condition: *if We willed* — the strike is available, the seal is available, and the only thing standing between the inheritors and the towns' fate is not an absence of precedent but the interval described at 7:97–99. The sūrah has already given the reader the remedy in the same passage: the towns would have been opened upon with blessings had they believed and been mindful. The verse extends the option to the new tenants: the sealed heart of 7:100 is stated as a possibility precisely because it can still be avoided.

The sūrah's next verse will explain that the seal has a history in every people the Book has addressed, and that the recounting of the towns is itself the instrument by which the door is kept open. Between the law of the ruins and the doctrine of the seal, the sūrah's reader is being taught to hear — the one verb the verses keep pressing on him.
