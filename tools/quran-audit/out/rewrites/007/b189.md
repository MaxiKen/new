**"Created You From a Single Soul"**

*Huwa alladhī khalaqakum min nafsin wāḥidatin wa-jaʿala minhā zawjahā li-yaskuna ilayhā fa-lammā taghashshāhā ḥamalat ḥamlan khafīfan fa-marrat bihi fa-lammā athqalat daʿawā Allāha rabbahumā la-in ātaytanā ṣāliḥan la-nakūnanna mina al-shākirīn* — He is the One Who created you from a single soul, then from it made its spouse so he may find comfort in her. After he had been united with her, she carried a light burden and went about with it. Then when it grew heavy, they both prayed to Allah, their Lord: "If You grant us a good child, we will surely be grateful."

**The Single Soul**

The Book's account of human origins begins with the one soul and the pair made from it, and the sūrah states the same fact in the same words as Sūrat al-Nisāʾ (4:1) and Sūrat al-Zumar (39:6). The phrase's function in the sūrah is to establish the unity of the human family before the narrative turns to the family's own prayers and, in the next verse, to its failures.

**"So He May Find Comfort in Her"**

The verse gives the purpose of the pairing in a single word: *li-yaskuna ilayhā*, so that he may find rest in her. The Qur'an's statements about marriage use the vocabulary of tranquility and companionship — *“He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them”* (30:21) — and the sūrah's readers are shown the institution as a provision rather than a transaction.

**The Spouses as a Sign**

The Qur'an's treatment of the pair belongs to its catalogues of signs: Sūrat al-Rūm names the creation of spouses among the signs and adds the compassion and mercy placed between them (30:21), and Sūrat al-Naḥl lists spouses, children, and grandchildren among God's gifts (16:72). The sūrah's verse is the same teaching in narrative form, and its placement in the sūrah's closing movement gives it the weight of a summary.

**The Light Burden**

The verse's description of the pregnancy is deliberately gentle: a light burden that the woman carries about without difficulty. The Book's narratives of human life attend to the ordinary course of things, and the sūrah's readers are shown the beginning of a family in the plainest terms before the verse reports the prayer that came with its completion.

**When the Burden Grew Heavy**

*Fa-lammā athqalat* — when she grew heavy — the verse marks the point at which the couple's dependence becomes conscious, and their prayer follows. The Qur'an's presentation of human need is that it becomes articulate in extremity, and the sūrah's readers are given the moment of prayer in the natural sequence of a pregnancy.

**"They Both Prayed to Allah, Their Lord"**

The verse's *daʿawā Allāha rabbahumā* — the dual verb — makes the prayer a joint act, and the appeal to God as *their Lord* states the relationship on which the petition rests. The Book's prayers are consistently addressed by its own vocabulary, and the sūrah's readers are shown a couple beginning their parenthood with a supplication rather than with an assumption.

**"If You Grant Us a Good Child"**

*La-in ātaytanā ṣāliḥan* — if You grant us a good, righteous child — the condition is stated as the content of the request, and the promise that follows is gratitude. The Qur'an's prayers for offspring recur in its narratives — Zakariyyā's plea, Abraham's supplication, and the servants of the Most Compassionate who ask for comfort from spouses and children (25:74) — and the sūrah's verse belongs to that family of passages.

**The Gratitude Promised**

The couple's promise is to be among the grateful, and the Book's readers have already met the theme in the sūrah's warnings against the nation that was not grateful (7:130's provision misread, and 34:13's few grateful servants). The verse thus states the right response to a gift before the next verse reports the response that was actually made.

**The Book's Family Theology**

The Qur'an treats the family as the primary school of faith and the primary field of its testing, and the sūrah's verse stands near the beginning of that treatment. The Book's readers are shown a couple whose first act as parents is a prayer, and the tradition drew from the passage its teaching about supplication for children and about the gratitude owed for them.

**The Verse in the Sūrah's Sequence**

The sūrah has moved from the created order and the Hour to the human being's own origin and family, and the movement continues into the verses about the idols that follow. The Book's arrangement is characteristic: the reader is taken from the widest frame to the most intimate, and the charge that follows is addressed to the intimacy he has just been shown.

**The Shadow of the Next Verse**

The verse ends with the couple's promise, and the next verse reports what their descendants did with the gift: *“But when He granted their descendants good offspring, they associated false gods in what He has given them”* (7:190). The sūrah's readers are thus given the prayer and its outcome in consecutive sentences, and the passage becomes a study of how a gift is received.

The Book's narratives of the patriarchs are full of such prayers for children — Zakariyyā's plea behind the sanctuary, Abraham's supplication for righteous offspring, and the believers described in Sūrat al-Furqān who ask for comfort in their spouses and offspring (25:74) — and the sūrah's verse gives the same petition in its simplest form: a good child and the gratitude that should follow.

The tradition read the verse's couple as the beginning of the human family and drew from the passage its teaching that the first response to a child should be a prayer and a promise rather than an assumption of possession. The Book's readers are shown the gift being asked for and the vow attached to it, and the next verse shows what happened when the gift was given. Nothing in the passage suggests that the couple's gratitude was universal in their descendants, and the sūrah's critique falls on those who received without returning thanks.
