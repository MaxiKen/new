**The Winds That Herald the Mercy**

*Huwa alladhī yursilu al-riyāḥa bushrā bayna yaday raḥmatihi* — He is the One Who sends the winds as good tidings ahead of His mercy. The sūrah has just asked its reader to call upon God with hope, and it now supplies one of the Book's most repeated arguments for hope: the ordinary weather. The winds go before the rain the way a messenger goes before a king, and the Book adopts the imagery in several places with the same term: *“He is the One Who sends the winds as good tidings ahead of His mercy, and We send down pure water from the sky”* (25:48); *“Among His signs is that He sends the winds as bearers of glad tidings, and to let you taste His mercy”* (30:46); *“It is He Who shows you the lightning, causing fear and hope, and produces the heavy clouds”* (13:12).

The vocabulary is deliberately administrative. *Bushrā* is the announcement given to a person awaiting news; *raḥma* here is specifically rain, the sustenance that keeps a desert population alive from one season to the next. The verse is telling its reader that the mercy he is asking for in hidden, quiet prayer is the same thing he watches arrive overhead every winter with a herder's anxiety — the Book is not introducing a new category of event, only naming the author of a familiar one.

**The Argument From the Dead Land**

*Ḥattā idhā aqallat saḥāban thiqālan suqnāhu li-baladin mayyitin* — until, when they carry heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land. The progression is careful: the winds, the clouds, the journey of the clouds by a movement the verse attributes to God (*suqnāhu*, We drove it), the rainfall, and then the fruit. What is emphasized is the destination. The clouds are not sent to the sea, where the rain would be wasted, or to the fertile mountains; they are driven to the *mayyit* — the corpse-land, the dust that has no green on it.

Then comes the application: *kadhālika nukhriju al-mawtā laʿallakum tadhakkarūn* — that is how We will bring forth the dead, so that you may remember. The comparison is drawn in the Book repeatedly and with a consistent structure: rain revives a dead land, and that revival is the closest thing in the experience of the Book's audience to the resurrection. *“And We send down blessed water from the sky, producing gardens and grain from it”* (50:9), followed a verse later by *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). Sūrat al-Rūm makes the inference explicit: *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead” (30:50)* (30:50; cf. 16:65; 45:5; 22:5).

The argument works because it is inductive, and the sūrah trusts its audience to complete it. Nobody in the audience doubted that last year's rain produced last year's pasture; the only question the verse asks them to face is whether the power that did that is short of the power required for a different kind of spring.

**The Resurrection Argument in Its Own Register**

The Book's readers have often noted that its proofs of the resurrection are drawn overwhelmingly from agriculture and gestation rather than from philosophy. *“Consider the seed you sow: is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who cause it to grow?”* (56:63–64). *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—in order to …”* (22:5). *“It is Allah Who sends the winds, which raise the clouds, and We drive them to a dead land and revive the earth after its death — such is the resurrection”* (35:9).

The choice is a claim about accessibility. Arguments from first principles require learning, and the Book is addressed to shepherds and weavers as much as to the learned; its demonstration is a process every farmer performs annually without noticing that he is performing it. The dead land, the rain, the green shoot, and the fruit are, in the logic of these verses, a rehearsal of the Day performed in front of the whole human race on an annual basis — and the final phrase of verse 57, *laʿallakum tadhakkarūn*, is the Book's sigh at the fact that they still forget.

**Who Is Being Addressed**

The verse's closing words matter: *so that you may remember*. The Qur'an's reminders are always aimed at a faculty rather than at an ignorance, and the here-and-now setting of the sūrah — its Makkan audience watching the winter rains and the caravans — makes verse 57 one of the most concrete verses in the chapter. The Book does not tell them that they have never encountered mercy; it tells them that they have encountered it every year under a different name and have not drawn the conclusion. The cultivation of the earth that verse 56 commanded and the rain that verse 57 describes are the same story told twice: the world is maintained by a mercy that keeps arriving, and the religion being preached in Makkah is the act of noticing where it comes from.
