**Rejection With Contempt**

The previous verse described the reverent who mend; this one describes the opposite, and it does not simply say that they disbelieved. The Arabic is *alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-stakbarū ʿanhā* — those who denied Our signs *and treated them with disdain*. Two acts, joined by *wa*: denial, and then the arrogance that refuses to take them seriously.

The distinction is not a redundancy. A person may deny a claim and still treat it with the respect due to a serious proposition; he may weigh it, reject it on grounds, and remain answerable for his reasoning. What this verse names is the other posture: the refusal to grant the claim a hearing, the turning away of the proud man who is offended by the thought that he might be addressed. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn's image of the arrogant is that they turn their heads aside (63:5); Sūrat al-Aʿrāf's own image, six verses later, will be the camel and the needle's eye.

**The Echo of Iblīs**

The word *istakbara* is the same word the sūrah used for Iblīs in verse 12 — *abā wa-stakbara*, he refused and was arrogant — and in al-Baqarah's account of the same scene, *“he refused and acted arrogantly, becoming one of the disbelievers”* (2:34). The echo is structural. The sūrah opened its narrative with a creature destroyed by arrogance against a command; here, at the end of the passage addressed to the children of Adam, the same word describes the fate of those who meet the signs of God with disdain. The chapter's answer to its own opening story is that arrogance is not a historical curiosity in the biography of one jinn. It is the recurring disposition that turns rejection into final loss.

Narrative aside, the Book treats *istikbār* as a category of its own with a specific destination. *“Your Lord has proclaimed: Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). *“On the Day of Judgment you will see those who lied about Allah with their faces gloomy. Is Hell not a fitting home for the arrogant?”* (39:60). *“The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels who are near. And whoever is too proud to worship Him and is arrogant — He will gather them all to Himself”* (4:172). The last verse is notable for who is included: even the highest created beings are said never to be too proud, which leaves the human arrogance of 7:36 with no model to imitate and no company.

**The Definition the Prophet Gave**

The sūrah's readers would have understood the word, but the Prophet ﷺ defined it, and the definition is a small masterpiece of precision: *“Arrogance is rejecting the truth and belittling people”* (Muslim 91, from ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd). A single sentence, two halves, and both are present in this verse.

*Rejecting the truth* is the denial: *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā*. *Belittling people* is what the arrogant posture does to the messenger and to the community of the weak whom the message gathers — and the sūrah's own account of the Makkan leadership is a study in exactly that (see 7:75–76, where the proud ask whether they should believe as the poor do). The Prophet's definition leaves no room for the self-flattering interpretation of arrogance as dignity. In his sentence, it is not a feeling about oneself at all; it is a pair of actions, one toward God's truth and one toward His creatures.

He also stated the consequence in a form this verse's audience would have grasped immediately: *“No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise”* (Muslim 91). The sūrah will shortly translate the same verdict into the image of the gates and the needle.

**"The Inhabitants of the Fire"**

The sentence is stated flatly: *fa-ulāʾika aṣḥāb al-nār*, they are the companions of the Fire; *hum fīhā khālidūn*, they will abide there. Two words carry the theology.

*Aṣḥāb* is the vocabulary of companionship and ownership — the term Arabic uses for the owner of a thing, the companion of a person, the people of a place. The Qur'an could have said, "they will be put in the fire"; it says they are *its people*. The relationship is one of settled residence, which is what the word *khālidūn*, abiding forever, then states explicitly.

The Messenger of God's description of the same place preserves the sūrah's insistence that what ends there began as a manner of dealing with the truth. The Prophet ﷺ said that the Fire will be brought forward on the Day with seventy thousand bridles, each held by seventy thousand angels, and that it will arrive with its people. What the verse establishes is not an arbitrary sentence but the completion of a relationship: the servant who despised the signs spent his life elsewhere, and is placed with the objects of his attention.

**The Contempt That Costs Nothing Until It Costs Everything**

One final observation belongs to the psychology of the verse, and the sūrah has prepared for it. *Istikbār* was the cheapest option available to Iblīs: a feeling, requiring no act, expressed in nothing but a refusal to bend. It was, by any worldly measure, a low-cost choice. That is what makes it so dangerous, because it is the reason the proud do not recognize the moment of their loss. Nothing happened; no deed was performed; no harm was done to another; and a creature's destiny quietly closed the other way.

The children of Adam are warned at the end of a passage about clothing, food, and worship — all of it tangible, all of it actionable — and this verse names the one thing an otherwise decent life can carry into damnation. Disdain does not look like sin. It looks like standards.
