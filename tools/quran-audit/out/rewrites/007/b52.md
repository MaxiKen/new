**After the Verdict, the Evidence**

The sūrah has just shown the condemned asking for water and listening to a prohibition. It now steps out of the Hereafter and back to the thing that decided the case: *wa-laqad jiʾnāhum bi-kitābin faṣṣalnāhu ʿalā ʿilmin* — and We brought them a Book which We explained in detail, with knowledge.

The placement is the argument. The Book is not introduced as a book that *should* have been followed; it is introduced as the exhibit filed after the sentence has been pronounced and the reason the sentence is just. The Qur'an's habit throughout is to state its own nature as a response to the accusations brought against it, and this verse summarizes the response in four words: *brought*, *explained*, *with knowledge*, and — further on — *guidance and mercy*.

**"We Explained It in Detail"**

The verb *faṣṣala* means to separate a thing into its parts so that each one is distinct: the cutting of a text into clear statements, the articulation of a body of teaching into chapters, commands, stories, warnings, and promises. The Qur'an uses it to describe its own composition at its opening pages: *“a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained. It is from the One Who is All-Wise, All-Aware”* (11:1); *“a Book whose verses are perfectly explained”* (41:3). And it uses the same verb for what the Book's opponents demand: *“Why is the Book not explained in detail?”* — the sūrah of Fuṣṣilat records the objection and answers it (41:44).

The claim *ʿalā ʿilm*, with knowledge, is the one that distinguishes this Book from the other things the Makkans classed it with. The audience of 7:52 had poets, soothsayers, genealogists, and story-tellers, and all of them produced texts out of talent and memory. The Qur'an asserts a different provenance: what is in it was put there knowingly, by the One who knows the human being from the inside. Sūrat Yā-Sīn denies the poet label outright — *“We have not taught him poetry, nor would it befit him”* (36:69) — and Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah lists the alternatives and rejects them one by one: it is not the word of a poet, not the word of a soothsayer, not the word of a soothsayer-like jinn (69:40–42).

**"Guidance and Mercy for a People Who Believe"**

*Hudan wa-raḥmatun li-qawmin yuʾminūn* — guidance and mercy for those who have faith. The pair is one of the Book's standing self-descriptions: *“O humanity! There has come to you a good admonition from your Lord, a healing for what is in the breasts, and a guidance and mercy for the believers”* (10:57). *“We send down the Qur'an as a healing and mercy for the believers, though it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”* (17:82). *“This Qur'an is a guidance and mercy for those who believe”* (27:77). *“A guidance and mercy for the doers of good”* (31:3).

The promise is generous and the qualifier is exact. The Book is not described as a source of mercy in the abstract; it is *mercy for a people who believe*, and Sūrat Fuṣṣilat explains what it does to those who will not: *“for those who do not believe, there is deafness in their ears, and it is blindness to them”* (41:44). The same revelation, in the same words, is a cure for one listener and an aggravation for another, and the difference is described by the Book as a condition of the heart rather than a quality of the text. The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf states the law bluntly a few verses later: God will turn away from His signs those who treat them with arrogance (7:146).

**The Explanation Requires Students**

If a Book is explained in detail, its hearers have an obligation that those who receive oracles do not have: the obligation to study. The Qur'an expects its audience to look things up — *“Had they referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, those who can deduce would have known it”* (4:83) — and the sūrah of Muḥammad instructs them to reflect on it directly: *“Will they not contemplate the Qur'an, or are there locks upon their hearts?”* (47:24). Sūrat al-Nisāʾ goes further and makes recitation, memorization, and teaching the identifying work of a community (4:82; 9:122).

The Prophet ﷺ summarized the resulting status in a sentence the tradition has never stopped repeating: *“The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it”* (al-Bukhārī 5027, from ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān). Verse 52 is the doctrinal ground under that hadith. The Book that was explained in detail can only accomplish what it was sent to accomplish through people who take the explanation seriously enough to pass it on.

**What the Sūrah Does With the Book**

It is worth noting how consistently al-Aʿrāf returns to revelation as its anchor. It opened with the letters *alif lām mīm ṣād* and the announcement of a Book revealed so that its Prophet might warn (7:2). It told its readers, *“Follow what has been sent down to you from your Lord, and do not take others as guardians besides Him”* (7:3). It will say of itself that it is *“a Book which We have revealed to you so that you may lead people out of darkness and into light”* (14:1), and it will close the section on the people of the Heights with the reminder that those who hold fast to the Book and establish prayer are not forgotten (7:170).

Verse 52 therefore sits in the sūrah as the answer to every scene that has preceded it. The mockers of 7:51 treated the revelation as entertainment; the condemned of 7:50 had nothing to drink; the people of the Heights recognized everyone by their marks. And the reason none of that had to happen — the whole weight of the sūrah's grief — is contained in the phrase *We brought them a Book*. It was not hidden, not withheld, and not vague. It was explained, in detail, with knowledge, and it was offered as mercy to whoever would believe.
