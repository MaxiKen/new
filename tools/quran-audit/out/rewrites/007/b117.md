**"It Devoured the Objects of Their Illusion"**

*Thumma awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an alqi ʿaṣāka fa-idhā hiya talqafu mā yaʾfikūn* — then We inspired Moses, "Throw down your staff," and behold, it devoured the objects of their illusion. The verse answers the display of the previous one, and it does so with a verb that names the difference between the two performances.

**The Command Comes as Revelation**

The verse opens with *awḥaynā*, We inspired: the act that decides the contest is not attributed to Moses' skill, courage, or timing. The command is given, the staff is thrown, and the result follows in the same sentence. Throughout the sūrah's Moses narrative, the signs are identified as God's work performed through a servant — the hand, the staff, the plagues, and the sea are all described this way.

**The Object That Was Already in His Hand**

The staff had been established as an ordinary possession before it became an instrument. Sūrat Ṭāhā's dialogue gives it its first biography — *“It is my staff! I lean on it, and with it I beat down ˹branches˺ for my sheep”* (20:18) — and the command to throw it down had already produced a serpent once before (20:19–20). Verse 117 is therefore the second appearance of the same sign, this time in public and against Egypt's professionals.

**The Verb: Talqaf**

*Talqafu* is the swallowing of something solid, and the Book uses it for what the staff does: it devours *mā yaʾfikūn*, what they were fabricating. Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ gives the same verb with the same object — *“it devoured the objects of their illusion”* (26:45) — and Sūrat Ṭāhā states the mechanism in God's instruction to Moses: *“it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick”* (20:69).

The precision is worth pausing over. What disappears down the serpent's throat is not ropes and sticks but an illusion's output. The Book is saying that the thing being destroyed had no substance to begin with; it is removed as appearance is removed, by the arrival of what is real.

**The Instant of Reversal**

*Fa-idhā hiya* — and behold it — the construction the sūrah has used for the staff's first transformation and for the world's signs elsewhere. The Book's narratives of divine intervention characteristically compress the decisive moment into a particle and a pronoun, and the compression is a claim about scale: what changes the situation is not a process but an event.

Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ gives the same act a cosmological form: *“In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes”* (21:18). The staff against the ropes is that verse's image made concrete in a courtyard.

**The Crowd That Had Been Brought to See**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ had reported the public being organized to follow the magicians if they prevailed (26:39–40). The same crowd is present when the illusion vanishes, which means the state's own instrument of legitimacy — the assembled people — became the witness of the reversal. The Book's narratives repeatedly stage the humiliation of power in front of the audience power had gathered.

**What Truth Did Not Need**

The verse contains no counter-illusion. Moses does not produce a better trick, a larger serpent, or a greater spectacle; he throws what he is holding, and the false is gone. Sūrat Sabaʾ states the principle as a sentence God gives the Prophet to speak: *“The truth has come, and falsehood will vanish, never to return”* (34:49).

Sūrat al-Raʿd's parable of the valleys adds the image of what remains: the flood carries foam on its surface, and the foam passes while the water that does the work continues (13:17). The Qur'an's thinking about illusion is agricultural and hydraulic — surface versus substance — and its verdict is always the same.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Hinge**

Everything before verse 117 in the Egyptian narrative had belonged to the court's initiative: the accusation, the mobilization, the contract, the terms, the order of casting, and the display. From verse 117 the initiative passes, and the remaining verses of the contest report the consequences in the passive voice of events that no one can stop.

The Book treats the question of signs as settled by its own record of them. *“Nothing keeps Us from sending the ˹demanded˺ signs except that they had ˹already˺ been denied by earlier peoples. And We gave Thamûd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they wronged her”* (17:59), and Sūrat al-Baqarah asks the Children of Israel to count what they had been given: *“Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We have given them. And whoever trades Allah's favour — after receiving it — ˹for disbelief˺ should know that Allah is severe in punishment”* (2:211). Egypt's contest is one more entry in that ledger.

What the verse establishes has a name in the Book's vocabulary: the truth is established by God's words. Sūrat al-Shūrā states it against the accusation that the Prophet had fabricated the revelation — *“And Allah wipes out falsehood and establishes the truth by His words”* (42:24) — and the staff that swallowed the illusions is the same principle enacted in front of a crowd. The instrument is a piece of wood; the agent is the word that made the wood.

The next verse names what happened in the courtyard in four words, and the one after it names the state of the men who had arranged the day.
