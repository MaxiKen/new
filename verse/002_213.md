## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:213

> **Humanity had once been one community ˹of believers before they lost faith˺. Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners, and revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes. And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them—out of jealousy. Then Allah, by His grace, has guided the believers to the truth regarding those disputes. And Allah guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The One Community — Before the rift**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between humanity and belief. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:213. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:213. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:213.

**Prophets and the Judging Book**

That mankind was one community refers, for some, to the period between Adam and Noah; al-Ṭabarī states that the exact dates are not significant to the meaning of the verse. Some believe that people were unified in disbelief and degradation, which was then corrected by Noah and later Abraham; others describe a state of unity in submission (islām) to Allah when all human beings followed one religion . Another interpretation attributes this oneness to the person of Adam, meaning that humanity began as one individual, but then became many. Such a usage of community (ummah) referring to a single person is found in 16:120: Truly Abraham was a community. Others understand one community as an allusion to the pretemporal covenant all human beings made with Allah, described in 7:172 . See also 10:19, which mentions the one community of the past; other verses (5:48; 11:118; 16:93; 42:8) mention that had Allah willed, humanity would be one community.

**Envy After Evidences**

Many understand an implied phrase in this first sentence, so that it would read Mankind was one community (and then they differed); then Allah sent the prophets (al-Rāzī). This reading would explain why Allah would then send the prophets to judge concerning that wherein they differed. Most commentators reason that the purpose of sending prophets is to correct error and sin, and they would be superfluous if human beings were united in one true religion (al-Rāzī).

**"And Allah Guides Whom He Wills Unto a Straight Path"**

Another interpretation is that the initial history of humanity was one without the need for the commands and prohibitions of religion, and the sending of prophets mentioned in this verse refers to a later time when prophecy became necessary . Al-Rāzī disapprovingly relates an opinion he attributes to Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ (d. 544 /1149), a prominent Mālikī jurist and theologian, that the first period was one in which human beings acted according to the intellect (both theoretical and practical); that is, they knew that there was a Creator, that one should be good to others, that lying and oppression are bad, that one should be just, and so forth. Pride, envy, and other vices eventually crept in and led to division, which then necessitated the sending of prophets.

**The first movement of a guided life**

But such a view would have to take into account that Adam was the first prophet according to Islamic belief, and the clear proofs would have begun with him and continued throughout history. It is useful to recall here that in discussing 3:19 (Those who were given the Book differed not until after knowledge had come to them), many commentators point out that after several generations the Jews and Christians came to differ each within their own group, and an analogy can be made with Adam and the initial one community, whose members began as one, but then came to differ among themselves over the truth.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The Israelite prophets, for example, were sent to one people with one religion, who nevertheless differed among themselves. See 3:19c (and also 98:4c), where the apparent paradox of differences arising after the coming of revelation is discussed further. Here, as in 3:19 and 98:4, the passage suggests that it is upon receiving truth and guidance that people enter into differences over questions of belief and practice, and that there is something almost inevitable about this occurrence given the tendencies of human nature.

Here the Book is taken as the general name for all the revealed books (Sh), or it is simply a specific book, namely, the Torah, if this verse is read in connection with only the Jews and Christians (al-Ṭabarī). Clear proofs (bayyināt) refers not only to revealed books, but also to any reality that demonstrates the Truth of Allah. Some commentators variously identify that wherein they differed to be the Truth as such, the revealed Book (including their own), or Islam and/or the Prophet (Sh).

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The verse describes humanity not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:213, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:213, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

The standard in 2:213 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from humanity: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:213 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands belief, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:213.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:213 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving humanity; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating humanity from the rest of life. In 2:213, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about humanity. They are the place in which assent to 2:213 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:213 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: humanity is joined to belief, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:213 gives the reader a useful order of questions about humanity. What does the term humanity mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:213? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:213 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:213.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:213. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:213 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between humanity and belief.

In 2:213, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of humanity while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:213 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
