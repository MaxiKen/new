## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:270

> **Whatever charities you give or vows you make are surely known to Allah. And the wrongdoers will have no helpers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Not a Grain Without Witness**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings charities and helpers into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:270 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:270 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Allah knows it means that He keeps an account of it (al-Ṭabarī) and knows what the intention was behind the act, great or small (al-Rāzī). The wrongdoers here are understood to be those who give for hypocritical reasons, as described in v. 264.

**The Wrongdoers' Choice — No Helpers**

The verse's reference to charities widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:270 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:270: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if charities were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:270 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:270 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:270.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The relation between charities and helpers also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:270 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:270 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:270 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:270 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:270 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving charities; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating charities from the rest of life. In 2:270, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about charities. They are the place in which assent to 2:270 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:270 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: charities is joined to helpers, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The wording of 2:270 gives the reader a useful order of questions about charities. What does the term charities mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:270? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:270 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:270.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:270. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:270 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between charities and helpers.

**The deed before its final account**

In 2:270, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of charities while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:270 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:270 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for charities, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:270, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:270, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:270, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:270 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:270 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:270, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine charities until the demand disappears.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The final emphasis of 2:270 returns to responsibility. No explanation of charities is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:270 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word charities in 2:270 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:270 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:270, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A report preserved by the tradition**

The exegetical tradition approaches charities from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:270 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:270 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding charities and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:270. The verse 2:270 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:270 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands helpers, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:270.
