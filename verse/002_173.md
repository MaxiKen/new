## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:173

> **He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity —neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Four-Chambered Prohibition**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between forbidden and swine. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:173. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:173. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:173.

**The Emergency Clause**

The good things (Ṭayyibāt) are those things that Allah has not forbidden or singled out as being sinful (al-Ṭabarī). See also 2:57c. In a ḥadīth the Prophet said, Allah is good (ṭayyib), and only accepts that which is good. Offered to other than Allah means food that is sacrificed to an idol, thus excluding the meat eaten by the idolaters (but not that of the People of the Book according to many jurists). In Islamic practice, an animal is slaughtered with the words, In the Name of Allah. It can occur that one is compelled by necessity, meaning by hunger, to eat under certain circumstances things that are forbidden in order to survive and protect ones life, but this should be done neither coveting nor transgressing, meaning not with the intention of bypassing Allahs Commandments in order to enjoy something that is forbidden (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). In such cases of necessity one is therefore permitted to eat what in normal circumstances would be forbidden.

**Divine Economy as Virtue**

Coveting renders bāghin, which has the sense of rebellion. For some the idea of transgressing (ʿādin) also refers to the practice of highway robbery, going against the authorities, or setting out to disobey Allah, presumably meaning that it is not permissible to pursue food in these ways even in cases of need (al-Ṭabarī). Regarding the legal and ritual issues of carrion, blood, and the flesh of swine, see 5:3c.

The verse's reference to forbidden widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:173 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:173: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The standard in 2:173 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from forbidden: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between forbidden and swine also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:173 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:173 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:173 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:173 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating forbidden from the rest of life. In 2:173, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about forbidden. They are the place in which assent to 2:173 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:173 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: forbidden is joined to swine, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The deed before its final account**

The wording of 2:173 gives the reader a useful order of questions about forbidden. What does the term forbidden mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:173? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:173 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:173.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:173. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:173 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between forbidden and swine.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:173, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of forbidden while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:173 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:173 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for forbidden, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:173, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

In 2:173, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:173, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:173 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:173 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:173, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine forbidden until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:173 returns to responsibility. No explanation of forbidden is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:173 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word forbidden in 2:173 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:173 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:173, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches forbidden from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:173 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:173 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
