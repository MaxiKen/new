## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:214

> **Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, “When will Allah’s help come?” Indeed, Allah’s help is ˹always˺ near.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Did You Suppose?" — The Wrong Assumption**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings think and without into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:214 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:214 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Shaken Until the Prophet Himself Cried**

Similar verses mentioning the testing of faith include 3:142 and 29:2–3. According to some this verse was revealed in connection with the Battle of the Trench (al-Khandaq, 5/627), also called the Battle of the Parties (al-Aḥzāb), which is discussed in 33:9–20; others connect it to the Battle of Uḥud (3/625; Th). It is related that the burden of being exiled and living without their property was a hardship for those who had left it behind in Makkah to migrate to Madinah, and they suffered the taunts and insults of the locals and hypocrites who asked them how long they would sacrifice themselves and their property for a seemingly losing cause .

**This World Is a Test-Garland**

The Messenger is not identified, but some interpret it to mean Muhammad; others say that, because all prophets encounter severe trials, it could be a general description of all of them . Perhaps to protect the idea of the perfect faith of the prophets, some interpret the question When will Allahs Help come? to mean that, although the Messenger knew the help was coming, he was seeking to hasten it (al-Qurṭubī). Examples of prophets being troubled include 12:110: The messengers despaired and thought that they were deemed liars; 15:97: We know that thy breast is straitened; and 26:3: Perhaps thou dost torment thyself that they are not believers. Indeed, the station of prophethood in the Qur'an does not preclude the prophets human vulnerability to fear, sadness, and discouragement. See 21:83, which mentions the story of Job.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to think widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:214 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:214: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if think were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:214 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:214 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:214.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between think and without also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:214 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:214 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:214 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:214 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:214 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving think; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating think from the rest of life. In 2:214, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about think. They are the place in which assent to 2:214 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:214 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: think is joined to without, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The deed before its final account**

The wording of 2:214 gives the reader a useful order of questions about think. What does the term think mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:214? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:214 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:214.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:214. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:214 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between think and without.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:214, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of think while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:214 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:214 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for think, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:214, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

In 2:214, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:214, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:214 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:214 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:214, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine think until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:214 returns to responsibility. No explanation of think is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:214 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word think in 2:214 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:214 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:214, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
