## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:271

> **To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Charity Shown or Hidden**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between charity and poor. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:271. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:271. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:271.

**Acquitting of Evil Deeds — the Erasing Charity**

Many aḥādīth speak to the virtue of giving secretly, often including a variation of the saying, “Let the left hand know not what the right hand is doing.” In an opinion attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, when it comes to obligatory actions such as the institutional alms (zakāh), performing them openly is twenty-five times better, while voluntary or supererogatory giving is seventy times better when done privately or secretly . Open charity, it is reasoned, provides a good example for others, shows the poor that there is recourse in their neighbors, and creates an open bond between people in a community (al-Rāzī). Secret charity is a test of sincerity, since one gives but garners no prestige in the eyes of others for one’s generosity.

**Allah Is Aware — khabīr**

Acquit you is related to expiation (kaffārah), meaning that the good deeds are a kind of atonement for the evil ones. The message of this verse is also present in 11:114: Truly good deeds remove those that are evil. Some commentators say, Charity puts out sin like water puts out a fire (al-Ṭabarī), as in the ḥadīth, When you perform an evil deed, follow it with a good deed and it will wipe it away. When asked, Is [remembrance of] There is no god but Allah [lā ilāha illaLlāh] a good deed? the Prophet said, It is the best of good deeds. Instead of that is better for you, and will acquit you of some of your evil deeds, some read here a first-person plural pronoun rather than the third-person singular, giving the translation, that is better for you, and We will acquit you of some of your evil deeds (al-Ṭabarī).

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The subject of charity joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:271, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:271 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:271 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from charity: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The relation between charity and poor also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:271 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:271 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:271 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:271 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating charity from the rest of life. In 2:271, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about charity. They are the place in which assent to 2:271 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The most balanced response to 2:271 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: charity is joined to poor, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:271 gives the reader a useful order of questions about charity. What does the term charity mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:271? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:271 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:271.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:271. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:271 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between charity and poor.

In 2:271, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of charity while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:271 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The poor person’s dignity**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:271 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for charity, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:271, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:271, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:271, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:271 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**A community shaped by obedience**

A reader who finds the command in 2:271 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:271, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine charity until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:271 returns to responsibility. No explanation of charity is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:271 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word charity in 2:271 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:271 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:271, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches charity from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:271 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:271 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
