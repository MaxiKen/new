## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:113

> **The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Mutual Annulment Between Reciters of One Book**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between jews and stand. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:113. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:113. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:113.

**Allah Will Judge Between Them**

This verse may have been revealed after a group of Jews argued with the Christian delegation from Najrān (see the introduction to Sūrah 3; IK, Ṭ), though other commentators think that it can apply to Jews and Christians generally and does not require them to be present before each other or even to be contemporaries of the Prophet. Stand on nothing could also be rendered “follow nothing,” and in this sense some commentators suggest that in the beginning of their respective religious histories the Jews and Christians did indeed “follow something” true, but then differed and altered their religion (al-Ṭabarī).

**The Mirror of Negatives**

But, as others point out, this would negate the obvious intent of censure in the verse, which indicates that the Jews and Christians are wrong to make this accusation against each other (Ibn Kathīr). They make these claims even though they recite the Book, which means that in what they espouse they should affirm rather than deny the truths in the other religion; in the case of the Jews, the Torah speaks to the truth of Jesus, and in the case of the Christians, the Gospel affirms Moses and the Torah (al-Ṭabarī).

The people who spoke words like theirs are understood to be peoples before the Jews and Christians (Ibn Kathīr) or possibly the idolaters who opposed Islam (al-Rāzī). The commentator al-Kāshānī notes that the Christians are veiled from the outward by their attachment to the inward, while the Jews are veiled from the inward by their attachment to the outward, and that similar problems beset certain schools of thought in Islam (K).

**The first movement of a guided life**

The suspension of a final verdict regarding religious differences is a consistent theme in the Qur'an. Allahs Judgment or disclosure of the truth in matters of religious disagreement on the Day of Judgment is also mentioned in 3:55; 5:48; 6:164; 10:93; 16:92; 16:124; 22:69; 32:25; 39:3, 46. The Qur'an states that at one time mankind was but one community (10:19), and that even if all were made one community again, they did not cease to differ (11:118). In other instances, the prophets are given the mission of making clear these differences (16:64, in the case of Muhammad; 43:63, in the case of Jesus).

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The verse describes jews not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:113, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:113, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

The standard in 2:113 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from jews: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The relation between jews and stand also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:113 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:113 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:113 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:113 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The exegetical tradition approaches jews from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:113 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:113 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

There is also a distinction between understanding jews and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:113. The verse 2:113 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:113 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands stand, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:113.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:113 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving jews; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating jews from the rest of life. In 2:113, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about jews. They are the place in which assent to 2:113 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The most balanced response to 2:113 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: jews is joined to stand, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:113 gives the reader a useful order of questions about jews. What does the term jews mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:113? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:113 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:113.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:113. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:113 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between jews and stand.

In 2:113, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of jews while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:113 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
