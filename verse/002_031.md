## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:31

> **He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, “Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true? ”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Curriculum of Names**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between taught and presented. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:31. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:31. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:31.

Some derive Adam from adīm, meaning the surface of the earth, in keeping with the ḥadīth, “Truly Allah created Adam from a handful taken from the entire earth. The Children of Adam thus correspond to the earth, some being red or black or white, or in between, and [they experience] ease and sorrow, bad and good.”

**Names Displayed Before the Angels**

The names Adam is taught are considered by some, such as Ibn ʿAbbās, to be those that people use in discourse with one another, such as man, sea, mountain, and even kettle. To others, such as Mujāhid, they are the names of everything. Still others say he was taught the names of angels; or that he was taught the names of all his progeny, which the commentator al-Ṭabarī prefers, in part because the pronoun hum is used rather than hāʾ, indicating human or angelic named objects. The plural of persons (human beings and angels) is the masculine plural, but the plural of other things (animals, inanimate objects) is usually expressed by the feminine singular pronoun, though this rule does not always hold (e.g., 24:45, where hum refers to beasts).

**Knowing, Address, and Prayer**

For al-Rāzī, this means that Adam indeed was taught all the languages of the earth, and his descendants came to prefer one over the others in the course of time. The commentator al-Ṭabrisī adds that a similar process would have taken place after the flood. Laid them before refers either to the names themselves or to the objects named.

For some, to be taught the names of things means to be given knowledge of all things. Ibn ʿArabī’s interpretation of this passage begins by pointing out that in relation to the world angels “are like the spiritual and sensory faculties” in human beings. As the faculties of men and women are diverse, so are the angels in their myriad functions arrayed in a hierarchy. Each human faculty or power is by definition limited to what it is in itself, and each angel, by Ibn ʿArabī’s analogy, is limited to what it is; that is, in its knowledge and function.

**The first scene of human responsibility**

Thus each of these faculties “is veiled and cannot see anything superior to itself.” Adam was capable of knowing all the Divine Names, unlike the angels who “did not possess the synthesis possessed by Adam, and were not aware of the Divine Names by which it [namely, Adam’s synthetic reality] is set apart such that they could glorify the Real and proclaim Him holy through them. Nor did they know that Allah possesses Names to whose knowledge they did not attain, therefore not glorifying Him with them nor proclaiming Him holy as did Adam.” Thus, Adam could know Allah in ways the angels could not.

The theme of taught places human weakness beside human responsibility. The account does not require a fantasy of moral perfection; it asks what a person does after knowledge, temptation, and failure have entered the scene. The important distinction is between being tested and choosing the test's wrong answer, then refusing the return that Allah makes possible.

**What the command reveals about the human being**

The standard in 2:31 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from taught: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The adversary’s method**

The relation between taught and presented also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:31 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:31 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:31 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:31 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:31 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands presented, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:31.

**Knowledge, temptation, and choice**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:31 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving taught; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating taught from the rest of life. In 2:31, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about taught. They are the place in which assent to 2:31 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Theological questions around the account**

The most balanced response to 2:31 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: taught is joined to presented, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:31 gives the reader a useful order of questions about taught. What does the term taught mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:31? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:31 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:31.

**The mercy that follows failure**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:31. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:31 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between taught and presented.

In 2:31, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of taught while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:31 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:31 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for taught, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:31, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
