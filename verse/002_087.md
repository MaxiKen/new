## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:87

> **Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Unbroken Chain**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between indeed and sent. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:87. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:87. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:87.

**"Whenever a Messenger Brought What Your Souls Did Not Desire"**

Here the Book refers to the Torah. The succession of messengers refers to those Biblical figures mentioned in the Torah and the Gospel whom the Qur'an considers to have been prophets sent to the Children of Israel, including David, Solomon, Elias, John, and others (al-Rāzī). They were sent by Allah to preach the Oneness of Allah, but in accordance with the religious law of Moses and not to bring a new religion. Clear proofs renders bayyināt, meaning something that makes plain, shows clearly, or demonstrates (see also the introduction to Sūrah 98). In the case of Jesus, clear proofs may consist of the Gospel (al-Ṭabarī) or may be a reference to his miracles, such as healing the sick and raising the dead, giving life to the figure of a bird (3:49), and telling of realities from the unseen world, though some say it can include both the miracles and the Gospel (al-Rāzī).

**The Sufi Mirror and the Lizard's Hole**

The Holy Spirit in the present verse is usually identified as the Archangel Gabriel (Ibn Kathīr); others have said it was a name by which Jesus revived the dead or that it refers to the Gospel itself (al-Ṭabarī), although this latter opinion goes against the juxtaposition of the Gospel and the Holy Spirit in 5:110. The interpretations have in common the sense of giving life, whether it is bodily quickening or the nourishment of the heart and intelligence (al-Rāzī). The place of the spirit in Islamic metaphysics and epistemology is essential and profound and is, In one respect, inexhaustible as a theological category; see 17:85c; 78:38c, where the spirit is discussed further.

**The Book as an address**

The word Holy, qudus, is defined by some as blessing (barakah) or purity (ṭuhr), while others say qudus is synonymous with the Divine Name al-Quddūs (the Holy; see 59:23; 62:1). In this latter sense the Holy Spirit means the spirit of (Allah) the Holy and refers then to the same Spirit that was breathed into Adam in 15:29: I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My Spirit. Jesus was strengthened in the sense of being supported, helped, and reinforced.

**The word that carries the argument**

For most Sufis, what this passage says outwardly to the Jews it communicates inwardly to the Muslims: Allah caused a succession of saints and sages to show right from wrong and guide them to the straight path, but these luminaries are often called liars or are opposed because their accusers are ruled by their passions (K). In a ḥadīth the Prophet said, “You will follow the ways of those who went before you, span by span, cubit by cubit, so much so that, were they to enter a lizard’s hole, you too would enter it.”

The wording around indeed assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

The standard in 2:87 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from indeed: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The reader’s responsibility**

The relation between indeed and sent also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:87 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:87 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:87 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:87 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:87 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands sent, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:87.

**Why the wording is exact**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:87 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving indeed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating indeed from the rest of life. In 2:87, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about indeed. They are the place in which assent to 2:87 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**A question answered by the surrounding verses**

The most balanced response to 2:87 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: indeed is joined to sent, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:87 gives the reader a useful order of questions about indeed. What does the term indeed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:87? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:87 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:87.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:87. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:87 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between indeed and sent.

In 2:87, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of indeed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:87 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:87 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for indeed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:87, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
