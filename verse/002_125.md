## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:125

> **And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.” And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The House: Rendezvous and Sanctuary**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between sacred and sanctuary. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:125. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:125. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:125.

This verse refers to the Kaʿbah, also called the House of Allah or the Sacred Mosque, which was built by Abraham and Ishmael (see v. 127). In pre-Islamic times, the Kaʿbah and the precinct surrounding it was a sanctuary, in that a murderer, for example, would be safe within it (al-Ṭabarī), which would have been seen as a continuation in some sense of its original status as established by Abraham. Some read the verb take to mean “they took,” referring to past people who took the station as a place of prayer (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī).

**"Take the Station of Abraham as a Place of Prayer"**

The station of Abraham is a place in the close vicinity of the Kaʿbah marked by a stone that is said to have borne the footprint of Abraham. However, some consider the station of Abraham to refer to the ḥajj rites as a whole. Others consider it to be another location important in the ḥajj, ʿArafah, Minā, or Muzdalifah (al-Ṭabarī). Circumambulation is the practice of walking around the Kaʿbah and is one of the major ritual actions of the ḥajj, while bowing and prostration are movements in the canonical prayer.

**"Purify My House" — Abraham and Ishmael at Work Together**

According to a ḥadīth the Prophet took ʿUmar by the hand and said, “This is the station of Abraham,” upon which ʿUmar said, “Shall we take it as a place of prayer?” The Prophet said he was not commanded to do so, and some commentators say that before sunset of that day this verse was revealed. Abraham was to purify it, that is, cleanse it from idolatry and doubt (al-Ṭabarī), which in Abraham’s case would mean to maintain it in that state, a state to which the Prophet Muhammad would return it later.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Those who make retreat means those who stay there without moving away; it derives from the root ʿ-k-f, which depending on context can mean “devoted to” or “resident in.” The supererogatory Islamic practice of iʿtikāf (from the same root), which consists of spending a period of time, usually a number of days, within the mosque in prayer and remembrance, is based upon the Prophet’s practice of doing so during the last ten days of Ramadan. The practice of seclusion (khalwah), or “spiritual retreat,” in Sufism is also based ultimately upon iʿtikāf.

The subject of sacred joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:125, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:125 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The standard in 2:125 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from sacred: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between sacred and sanctuary also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:125 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:125 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:125 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:125 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The final emphasis of 2:125 returns to responsibility. No explanation of sacred is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:125 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word sacred in 2:125 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:125 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:125, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The exegetical tradition approaches sacred from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:125 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:125 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding sacred and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:125. The verse 2:125 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:125 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands sanctuary, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:125.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:125 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving sacred; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating sacred from the rest of life. In 2:125, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about sacred. They are the place in which assent to 2:125 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:125 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: sacred is joined to sanctuary, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:125 gives the reader a useful order of questions about sacred. What does the term sacred mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:125? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:125 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:125.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:125. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:125 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between sacred and sanctuary.
