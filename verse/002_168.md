## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:168

> **O humanity! Eat from what is lawful and good on the earth and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. He is truly your sworn enemy.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Universal Table Spread**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings humanity and earth into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:168 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:168 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

It is reported that this verse was revealed in connection with the tribes of Thaqīf, Khuzāʿah, and Banū Mudlij, who had decided that the eating of certain kinds of animals was forbidden, although the plain sense of the verse shows that it is of universal import (al-Qurṭubī). Good (ṭayyib) also has the sense of being pleasant, agreeable, and delicious. Lawful (ḥalāl) is what in Islamic Law is deemed permissible and derives from a root with a range of meanings including “untie,” “set free,” and “unbind.” In the categorization of actions in Islamic Law, it is the opposite of ḥarām, or “forbidden.”

**Occasion and Universal Rollout**

The image of following in the footsteps of Satan is mentioned elsewhere (2:208; 6:142; 24:21) and is often described by commentators as an idiomatic way of saying that a person submits to temptation and commits evil. Satan is often called a manifest enemy in the Qur'an (7:22; 12:5; 17:53; 28:15; 36:60; 43:62).

Some relate this verse to the practice of taking oaths that one will refrain from something or other, but oaths cannot be taken in a state of anger. When they are taken in such a state, one can pay an expiation. On carelessness in oaths, see 2:225. Ibn Kathīr mentions several incidents where some people made oaths that they would do thus and so, but were reprimanded by other Companions for following in the footsteps of Satan. A ḥadīth states, Allah said, I created My servants as ḥanīfs. Then the satans came to them, and diverted them from their religion, and forbade for them what I had made lawful.

**Food and the Answering of Prayer**

A Companion asked the Prophet how he could become someone whose supplications were accepted. The Prophet said, “Make wholesome what you eat, and your supplications will be accepted by Him in Whose Hand is the soul of Muhammad. Let a man put a forbidden morsel in his mouth and [his supplications] will not be accepted for forty days. Any man who raises his meat from ill-gotten gains is most deserving of the Fire.”

The mention of humanity must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if humanity were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:168 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:168 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:168.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

The relation between humanity and earth also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:168 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:168 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:168 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:168 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

The choice of the word humanity in 2:168 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:168 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:168, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches humanity from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:168 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:168 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

There is also a distinction between understanding humanity and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:168. The verse 2:168 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:168 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands earth, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:168.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:168 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving humanity; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**How the early community understood it**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating humanity from the rest of life. In 2:168, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about humanity. They are the place in which assent to 2:168 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:168 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: humanity is joined to earth, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Power restrained by accountability**

The wording of 2:168 gives the reader a useful order of questions about humanity. What does the term humanity mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:168? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:168 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:168.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:168. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:168 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between humanity and earth.

In 2:168, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of humanity while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:168 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
