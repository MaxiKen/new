## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:37

> **Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord, so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Then Adam Received Words": The First Gift of Repentance**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between adam and prayer. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:37. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:37. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:37.

**Wa-He Relented: the Tawbah of Allah**

Some understand words as a reference to the supplication of Adam and Eve: Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If Thou dost not forgive us and have Mercy upon us, we shall surely be among the losers (7:23). Another account mentions that Adam beseeched Allah, Did you not make me vicegerent? Did you not breathe into me of Thy Spirit? Does Thy Compassion not outstrip Thy Wrath? Allahs response of Yea to each of these questions and His informing Adam that he was forgiven constitute the words that Adam received. Others say that Allah taught Adam and Eve the pilgrimage, and the pilgrimage was the words. Still others claim it was a prayer (presumably given to Adam by Allah to recite), There is no god but Thee! Glorified be Thee, praised be Thee! Thou knowest evil, and I have wronged myself. So forgive me! Thou art the best of forgivers! This is repeated again, substituting mercy and repentance for forgiveness in the formulation (al-Rāzī).

**The Covenant of Tawbah: Do Not Despair**

In Arabic the verb for repent and relent (tāba/yatūbu) is the same; its meaning changes only with the addition of a preposition. Tawbah, the noun derived from the verb, means literally a turning or a return: we turn or return to Allah in repentance, and He turns or returns to us. Similar passages in the Bible (Jonah 3:9; Jeremiah 15:6; Genesis 6:6) predicate a kind of repentance of Allah. Al-Tawwāb (the Relenting) is the intensive participle of tawbah, meaning repentance; see also 4:17–18c; 4:147c.

The subject of adam joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:37, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:37 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The standard in 2:37 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from adam: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between adam and prayer also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:37 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:37 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:37 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:37 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

In 2:37, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of adam while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:37 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:37 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for adam, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:37, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

In 2:37, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:37, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:37 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:37 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:37, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine adam until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:37 returns to responsibility. No explanation of adam is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:37 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The choice of the word adam in 2:37 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:37 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:37, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches adam from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:37 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:37 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The poor person’s dignity**

There is also a distinction between understanding adam and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:37. The verse 2:37 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:37 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands prayer, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:37.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:37 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving adam; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating adam from the rest of life. In 2:37, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about adam. They are the place in which assent to 2:37 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:37 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: adam is joined to prayer, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
