## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:206

> **When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Admonition That Backfires**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings fear and hell into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:206 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:206 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The concept of sufficiency (suffices) is used both in a positive and in a negative sense in the Qur'an, often in the sense of serving the purpose of something, in this case punishing the misdeeds of the worker of corruption; see 2:137c.

**"Hell Suffices Him" — The Completeness of Fit**

The verse's reference to fear widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:206 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:206: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if fear were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:206 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:206 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:206.

**The Medicine of Reminding**

The relation between fear and hell also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:206 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:206 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:206 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:206 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:206 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for fear, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:206, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:206, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:206, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:206 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

A reader who finds the command in 2:206 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:206, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine fear until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:206 returns to responsibility. No explanation of fear is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:206 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The choice of the word fear in 2:206 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:206 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:206, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches fear from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:206 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:206 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The deed before its final account**

There is also a distinction between understanding fear and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:206. The verse 2:206 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:206 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands hell, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:206.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:206 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving fear; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating fear from the rest of life. In 2:206, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about fear. They are the place in which assent to 2:206 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The most balanced response to 2:206 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: fear is joined to hell, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:206 gives the reader a useful order of questions about fear. What does the term fear mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:206? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:206 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:206.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:206. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:206 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between fear and hell.

In 2:206, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of fear while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:206 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
