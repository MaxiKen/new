## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:142

> **The foolish among the people will ask, “Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The east and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**"What Has Turned Them?" — The Taunt Arrives**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings foolish and away into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:142 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:142 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

See 2:115c, which discusses the qiblah and how the East and the West belong to Allah. The use of the future will say in this verse does not exclude its referring to the past, as a kind of trait, and not only a prediction (al-Rāzī). The verse is meant partly to remind one that it is not the place itself (Jerusalem or Makkah) that merits being the direction of prayer or qiblah, but the fact that Allah has commanded it to be so (al-Rāzī).

**"To Allah Belong the East and the West"**

The Prophet prayed toward Jerusalem for a certain number of months after arriving in Madinah after the hijrah (the emigration from Makkah); in the reports the number of months varies between thirteen and twenty, but most mention sixteen or seventeen . Some accounts state that, initially, the Prophet and his followers, while still in Makkah, prayed in the direction of the Kaʿbah, then faced Jerusalem for a brief period after migrating to Madinah, and then faced the Kaʿbah once more. Others aver, however, that even while in Makkah they prayed toward Jerusalem . Some commentators believe that it was the hypocrites who asked this question regarding the change in direction and that, as such, they were not sincere but cynical in their intentions (al-Ṭabarī).

**Obligations and Their Changes**

This question of the change of qiblah is also significant for the issue of the abrogation (naskh) of legal rulings in the Qur'an. It is the first instance of abrogation in the chronological order of revelation and is often cited as a paradigmatic case, though it is an instance of the Qur'an abrogating the Sunnah (al-Qurṭubī); on naskh, see 2:106c. This verse is also associated with masjid al-qiblatayn, or the Mosque of the Two Qiblahs, which still stands, although recently rebuilt, and is a few kilometers from the Prophets mosque in Madinah, because it was reported that in the midst of a prayer, a caller came to announce the change in direction, at which the congregation turned, mid-prayer, and faced the new qiblah .

**"He Guides Whom He Wills"**

Determining the qiblah in locales outside of Makkah has been and continues to be a subject of lively debate. Several different methods have been employed throughout the centuries wherever Muslims have lived or traveled. One early method was to stand as though one were facing a wall of the Kaʿbah that was extended far enough horizontally; in this system the Islamic world was divided into geographic sectors, each praying toward a different wall of the Kaʿbah. Since the corners of Kaʿbah are oriented toward the cardinal directions and its sides face the summer sunrise and Canopus (the southern pole star frequently used for navigation), the direction of prayer was often determined by these celestial markers.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Later, increasingly sophisticated mathematical methods were developed and continue to be used to calculate the qiblah, among them the great circle, which is the shortest surface line between Makkah and any spot on the earth. In North America, for example, the great circle to Makkah begins in a northeasterly direction, constantly changing compass bearing toward the southeast until it reaches Makkah. The older method for calculating the qiblah direction for North America would give a southeasterly bearing, since North America would be part of the sector of the world corresponding to the Kaʿbah wall that is perpendicular to that direction. Both are used today. Another method is to face the direction that, if one kept a constant compass bearing or “rhumb line,” would bring one to Makkah.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

In some old Islamic cities the qiblah of various mosques was not always uniformly oriented, because it was calculated using the nonmathematical means available to their inhabitants. Islamic mathematics developed sophisticated methods for determining the direction of the qiblah before modern times, including the use of astronomical instruments such as astrolabes and armillary spheres. Sometimes later calculations would result in a physical adjustment of the qiblah within a mosque, often with the modification of an inner wall. A ḥadith states, “Whatever is between the east and the west is a qiblah” , which has been interpreted as providing a certain latitude when it comes to matters of exact direction, not only in the absence of mathematical calculation, but also in consideration of local conditions that might make the determination of the qiblah difficult or even impossible.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The subject of foolish joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:142, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:142 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if foolish were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:142 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:142 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:142.

**The measure of sincere giving**

There is also a distinction between understanding foolish and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:142. The verse 2:142 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:142 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands away, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:142.

**The poor person’s dignity**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:142 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving foolish; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating foolish from the rest of life. In 2:142, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about foolish. They are the place in which assent to 2:142 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:142 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: foolish is joined to away, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:142 gives the reader a useful order of questions about foolish. What does the term foolish mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:142? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:142 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:142.
