## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:49

> **˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Command to Remember Deliverance**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between delivered and dreadful. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:49. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:49. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:49.

The use of when (idh) links this and subsequent verses to v. 47 in the sense of “remember when,” as idh can mean “remember when” independently of the explicit use of a word such as udhkur (“remember”). Idh can also carry the sense of “Lo!” or “See!” or “Behold!”

**Salvation as Sheer Unmerited Grace**

The house (āl) refers to the family or people of Pharaoh (which term itself means great house). Other than describing it as a punishment or suffering (ʿadhāb), the Qur'an does not give a specific reason for the killing mentioned in this verse. In light of the fact that Pharaoh repeats the same threat later when Moses is an adult (7:127; 40:25), it would indicate that the murder of males was a form of collective punishment or control (R; cf. Exodus 1), rather than the result of a foretelling of future events, as is sometimes mentioned in the commentaries , by soothsayers or astrologers (or even through a dream of Pharaoh) warning that a child would be born to the Israelites who would rise up and destroy Pharaohs kingdom; see 20:25–28c. See also 28:4.

**"A Great Trial from Your Lord"**

The trial (balāʾ) is lexically a testing or finding out, here usually connected to the sufferings, but understood by some to be a niʿmah, or blessing, which appears in 2:47 (al-Ṭabarī). In the Qur'an a good can also be a trial, as in 21:35: We try you with evil and with good; and 7:168: And We tried them with good things and with evil things.

The reference to delivered turns memory into a demand. A community can recount a rescue, a miracle, or a failure and still repeat the same error if it treats history as the property of ancestors. In this passage the earlier event becomes evidence addressed to the present: blessing creates a duty, and a broken promise leaves a recognizable pattern.

**The Verse at Your Table and in Your Garden**

The standard in 2:49 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from delivered: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**History as a present warning**

The relation between delivered and dreadful also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:49 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:49 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:49 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:49 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The covenant behind the event**

The wording of 2:49 gives the reader a useful order of questions about delivered. What does the term delivered mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:49? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:49 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:49.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:49. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:49 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between delivered and dreadful.

**Memory turned into responsibility**

In 2:49, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of delivered while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:49 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:49 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for delivered, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:49, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The people and the command**

In 2:49, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:49, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:49 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:49 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:49, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine delivered until the demand disappears.

**What the exegetes disputed**

The final emphasis of 2:49 returns to responsibility. No explanation of delivered is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:49 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word delivered in 2:49 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:49 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:49, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches delivered from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:49 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:49 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding delivered and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:49. The verse 2:49 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:49 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands dreadful, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:49.
