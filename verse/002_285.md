## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:285

> **The Messenger ˹firmly˺ believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord, and so do the believers. They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers. ˹They proclaim,˺ “We make no distinction between any of His messengers.” And they say, “We hear and obey. ˹We seek˺ Your forgiveness, our Lord! And to You ˹alone˺ is the final return.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Creed's Diamond — Two Verses from Beneath the Throne**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between messenger and belief. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:285. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:285. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:285.

**"We Hear and Obey"**

These final two verses constitute some of the most often recited and memorized passages in the Qur'an, encompassing both a statement of faith and true belief in v. 285 and a supplicatory prayer to Allah in v. 286. They are in fact often recited together. It is reported that the Prophet said of these two verses, Whosoever recites these two verses during the night, they will suffice him, and also, I was given the two verses that seal [the sūrah] The Cow from a storehouse beneath the Throne (min kanzin taḥtal-ʿarsh). In another ḥadīth it is said that during his Night Journey and Ascension (al-isrā and al-miʿrāj; see 17:1; 53:1–18) the Prophet was given three things: the five canonical prayers, the final two verses of The Cow, and forgiveness of sins for any member of his community who does not ascribe partners to Allah.

**Thy Forgiveness, Our Lord — Unto Thee the Journey's End**

The five fundamental articles of Islamic faith—namely, belief in Allah, angels, books, messengers, and the return to Allah (and unto Thee is the journeys end)—are summarized here; they are also listed in v. 177 and 4:136. We make no distinction is spoken by the believers and appears also in v. 136; 3:84; 4:152; the ranking of prophets is discussed in v. 253 and 3:163. Beyond the question of ranking and equality, the statement of the believers that they make no distinction between the prophets is also meant to distinguish them from the Jews, who rejected Jesus and Muhammad, and from the Christians, who rejected Muhammad. Thy forgiveness is understood as an entreaty, with an implied verbal imperative, meaning Grant us Thy forgiveness.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The message that Allah tasks no soul beyond its capacity is also found in 2:233; 6:152; 7:42; 23:62. The verbs earned and perpetrated are closely related by root and can be synonymous depending on context. It shall have what it has earned means that what it earned is added to its merit, while subject to what it has perpetrated refers to sins for which it is held responsible (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī).

**Belief made visible in conduct**

Take us not to task if we forget or err can refer to sins of omission, such as forgetting to offer the canonical prayers, or of commission, such as adultery (Ibn Kathīr). Lay not upon us a burden is understood to refer to the covenant or pact that was also made with different conditions with other religious communities, such as the Jews and Christians (al-Ṭabarī), and is usually interpreted to mean that in many ways Islam represented an alleviation and reduction of the ritual and legal obligations placed on people’s past (Ibn Kathīr), though it may also refer to other burdens. Impose not upon us that which we have not the strength to bear refers to commands and prohibitions that would be too difficult to uphold (al-Rāzī). Some commentators mention in this context the ḥadīth, “Truly Allah has absolved my community of mistakes, forgetfulness, and that which they were compelled to do.”

**The choice hidden inside the command**

Various aḥādīth describe how, after each of the phrases of supplication uttered by the Prophet or the believers in this verse (take us not to task, lay not upon us, impose not upon us, pardon us, forgive us, have mercy upon us) Allah says, “I have done so” or “Yes,” or the Prophet is told by Gabriel, “He has done so” . It is said that, upon completing this sūrah, the prominent Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal would say āmīn, or “amen,” which is also usually said at the end of the recitation of the Fātiḥah, the opening sūrah of the Qur'an (Ibn Kathīr).

The verse describes messenger not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:285, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:285, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The standard in 2:285 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from messenger: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating messenger from the rest of life. In 2:285, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about messenger. They are the place in which assent to 2:285 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The most balanced response to 2:285 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: messenger is joined to belief, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:285 gives the reader a useful order of questions about messenger. What does the term messenger mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:285? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:285 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:285.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:285. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:285 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between messenger and belief.

In 2:285, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of messenger while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:285 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:285 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for messenger, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:285, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:285, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:285, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:285 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
