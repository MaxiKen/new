## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:224

> **Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good, not guarding against evil, or not making peace between people. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Oaths That Block Goodness**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings allahs and excuse into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:224 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:224 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Against Pious Deeds, Cross-Croyers, and Peace-Making**

Rules regarding oaths are also discussed in 5:89: Allah will not take you to task for that which is frivolous in your oaths, but He will take you to task for the oaths you have pledged in earnest; and 68:10: So obey not any vile oath-monger. These verses all convey the message against making excessive oaths and emphasize the seriousness of taking an oath; even in pre-Islamic Arabia it was considered a vice to be immoderate in oath taking. The present verse condemns oaths against righteous behavior, such as swearing never to speak to relatives, to give charity, to make peace between two people, to reverence Allah, or, in general, to perform some good deed (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī).

**The Prophet's Own Sunnah of Breaking the Oath**

The verse's reference to allahs widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:224 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:224: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if allahs were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:224 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:224 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:224.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The relation between allah’s and excuse also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:224 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:224 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:224 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:224 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The choice of the word allahs in 2:224 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:224 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:224, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches allahs from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:224 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:224 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Mercy and warning held together**

There is also a distinction between understanding allahs and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:224. The verse 2:224 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:224 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands excuse, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:224.

**The deed before its final account**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:224 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving allahs; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating allahs from the rest of life. In 2:224, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about allahs. They are the place in which assent to 2:224 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The most balanced response to 2:224 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: allahs is joined to excuse, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:224 gives the reader a useful order of questions about allahs. What does the term allahs mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:224? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:224 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:224.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:224. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:224 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between allahs and excuse.

In 2:224, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of allahs while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:224 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:224 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for allahs, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:224, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:224, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:224, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:224 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:224 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:224, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine allahs until the demand disappears.
