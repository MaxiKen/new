## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:61

> **And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺. So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils, and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village and you will find what you have asked for.” They were stricken with disgrace and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah for rejecting Allah’s signs and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Complaint of the Well-Fed**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between moses and same. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:61. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:61. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:61.

A Biblical parallel to this verses content is found in Numbers 11:4–5, where the Israelites crave meat and wish for the fish . . . the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic they knew in Egypt. In Numbers 11:31–35, they are given quails, only to have those who craved them struck down for their greediness in relation to Allahs provision. Here garlic (fūm) may also mean wheat.

**From Craving Onions to Killing Prophets: The Thread**

Go down (habaṭa) need not be a change in elevation; analogously, in English one might say that one descends upon a place; one can go down (habaṭa) into or out of a valley, for example (al-Zamakhsharī). Better is usually understood here to refer to the manna and quails, while the lesser refers to the other foods they were requesting. A town renders miṣr, which can also mean a city, and used as a proper name or foreign word it means Egypt. The orthography (which requires it be read as miṣran and not miṣra) indicates that it could not refer to Egypt itself (al-Ṭabarī), though some mention is made of early commentators understanding this to be the miṣr of Pharaoh. The killing of the prophets (2:61, 87, 91; 3:21, 112, 181; 4:155; 5:70) is also mentioned several times in the Bible (Luke 13:34; Matthew 23:37; Romans 11:3; 1 Thessalonians 2:14–15; 1 Kings 19:10).

**Abasement and Poverty as Divine Sentence**

The reference to moses turns memory into a demand. A community can recount a rescue, a miracle, or a failure and still repeat the same error if it treats history as the property of ancestors. In this passage the earlier event becomes evidence addressed to the present: blessing creates a duty, and a broken promise leaves a recognizable pattern.

The standard in 2:61 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from moses: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The Verse's Operating Instruction for the Believer**

The relation between moses and same also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:61 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:61 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:61 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:61 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**History as a present warning**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating moses from the rest of life. In 2:61, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about moses. They are the place in which assent to 2:61 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:61 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: moses is joined to same, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The covenant behind the event**

The wording of 2:61 gives the reader a useful order of questions about moses. What does the term moses mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:61? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:61 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:61.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:61. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:61 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between moses and same.

**Memory turned into responsibility**

In 2:61, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of moses while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:61 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:61 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for moses, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:61, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The people and the command**

In 2:61, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:61, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:61 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:61 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:61, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine moses until the demand disappears.

**What the exegetes disputed**

The final emphasis of 2:61 returns to responsibility. No explanation of moses is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:61 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word moses in 2:61 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:61 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:61, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches moses from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:61 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:61 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
