## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:4

> **and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what was revealed before you, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Faith Across the Whole Chain of Revelation**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings revealed and faith into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:4 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:4 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Sent Down: The Verticality of Scripture**

In this verse it is the Prophet who is addressed in the second-person singular, always rendered thou or thee in this translation. A question arises: does the and at the start of the verse continue the description of the believers from v. 3, or does v. 4 refer to a different group of people, requiring a translation of And those who believe . . . such that v. 5 then describes the status of the believers of v. 4, not v. 3? One understanding of this passage is that v. 3 refers to the believers among the Arabs (presumably specifically Muslim believers who are neither Christian nor Jewish), while v. 4 refers to the believers among the People of the Book who had previously believed in earlier revelations and now believe in the Prophet (Ṭ; see also 98:1–8, where a similar distinction is made).

**Certainty, Not Suspicion: The Grammar of Yūqīnūn**

But v. 3 and v. 4 can also be understood more generally as two descriptions of one group; namely, the reverent. See also 3:199, which mentions People of the Book who believe in what was sent down to them and to the Prophet.

For a discussion of the Islamic view of different revelations, see 2:62c, 5:69c, and the essay The Qur'anic View of Sacred History and Other Religions. For the Hereafter, see the essay Death, Dying, and the Afterlife in the Qur'an.

The verse describes revealed not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:4, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:4, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**Why the Portrait Ends at the Hereafter**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if revealed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:4 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:4 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:4.

**The Skeptic's Age and the Anatomy of Sureness**

The relation between revealed and faith also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:4 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:4 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:4 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:4 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:4 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving revealed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating revealed from the rest of life. In 2:4, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about revealed. They are the place in which assent to 2:4 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:4 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: revealed is joined to faith, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The wording of 2:4 gives the reader a useful order of questions about revealed. What does the term revealed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:4? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:4 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:4.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:4. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:4 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between revealed and faith.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

In 2:4, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of revealed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:4 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:4 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for revealed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:4, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

In 2:4, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:4, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:4 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:4 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:4, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine revealed until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:4 returns to responsibility. No explanation of revealed is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:4 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word revealed in 2:4 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:4 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:4, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
