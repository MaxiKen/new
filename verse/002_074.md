## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:74

> **Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Then Your Hearts Hardened" — After All That?**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings even and hardened into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:74 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:74 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Like Stones — Or Harder**

This verse can be understood to describe those present at the incident of the cow or the Jewish contemporaries of the Prophet insofar as they were members of the same group by affiliation and descent (al-Rāzī). In the first case they are rebuked for being unmoved by so obvious a sign as the raising of the dead, and in the latter case for turning away from the Qur'anic teaching about this incident. Even rock can be worn down and split open by the force of water. For the symbolism of water, also see 2:60. Water is a symbol of life and that by which life is made possible, whether it comes from the sky (22:63) or comes from beneath the ground (39:21) or is figured in our creation (21:30); here the image of the rock from which water comes forth is powerfully contrasted to the lifelessness of the hardened heart.

**"Allah Is Not Heedless of What You Do"**

Stones crashing down from the fear of Allah is connected by some commentators with the incident of Allahs disclosing Himself to the mountain in the presence of Moses, on the occasion of which the mountain crumbled (7:143), and with 59:21: Had We made this Qur'an descend upon a mountain, thou wouldst have seen it humbled, rent asunder by the fear of Allah. Some commentators think that all the types of rock in this verse behave as they do because of the fear of Allah (al-Ṭabarī). The living consciousness of Allah by all creation is also mentioned in 17:44: And there is no thing, save that it hymns His praise, though you do not understand their praise; and 34:10: O you mountains! Echo Allahs praises with him, likewise you birds!

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Some aḥādīth mentioned in connection with this verse describe the Prophet as saying, “I know a stone in Makkah that used to greet me before I was made a prophet, and I know it even now.” Regarding Mt. Uḥud, he said, “This is a mountain that loves us, and we love it.” In another related ḥadīth that describes the behavior of apparently insensate objects, the Companions made a pulpit for the Prophet, but when he ascended it, the palm-tree stump beside which he used to deliver his sermons began to cry, at which the Prophet descended, embraced it, and said, “It weeps for the remembrance it used to hear.” In one interpretation, the three kind of stones refer, respectively, to those human beings who weep much, those who weep little, and those who weep in their hearts without showing it outwardly (Ibn Kathīr).

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to even widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:74 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:74: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if even were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:74 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:74 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:74.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between even and hardened also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:74 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:74 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:74 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:74 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:74 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving even; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The deed before its final account**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating even from the rest of life. In 2:74, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about even. They are the place in which assent to 2:74 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:74 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: even is joined to hardened, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The wording of 2:74 gives the reader a useful order of questions about even. What does the term even mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:74? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:74 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:74.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:74. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:74 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between even and hardened.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

In 2:74, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of even while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:74 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:74 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for even, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:74, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:74, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:74, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:74 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
