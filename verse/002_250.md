## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:250

> **When they advanced to face Goliath and his warriors, they prayed, “Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, make our steps firm, and give us victory over the disbelieving people.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Prayer of the Few**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings advanced and warriors into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:250 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:250 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The Qur'anic account here assumes a general knowledge of the story of Saul, David, and Goliath: though a young boy, David is appointed by Saul to face Goliath as the Israelites champion and fells the huge warrior with a stone from his sling (cf. 1 Samuel 17). He was given wisdom, meaning that, as a major prophet in the Islamic tradition, he inherited the prophethood of Samuel, and sovereignty, meaning he inherited the kingship of Saul (al-Ṭabarī). For further description of David and his prophethood and reign, see 38:17–29.

**A Duʿāʾ to Keep in Every Throat**

Were it not for Allahs repelling people, some by means of others is similar to the message of 22:40, where the destruction of houses of worship is thwarted by this repelling. Here it is the corruption of the earth as such that is averted through this repelling of some by others. Commentators state that Allah uses good people to repel the effect of evil people, but offer different interpretations as to who precisely repels whom. Some say this refers to the Substitutes (abdāl), human beings who are inwardly members of a spiritual hierarchy through which Allah dispenses grace and protection to the world; they are forty in number, and when one dies, that one is replaced by another person (al-Qurṭubī).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Others interpret it to mean that the prayerful and the pious are used to repel those who do not pray and are impious, whose sins would otherwise destroy humanity, or that the obedient repel the evil of the disobedient (al-Ṭabarī). In a ḥadīth the Prophet says, Allah repels the punishment of the one who does not pray by means of the one who prays, and of the one who does not give alms by means of the one who gives alms, and of the one who does not fast by means of the one who fasts, and of the one who does not make the ḥajj by means of the one who makes ḥajj, and of the one who does not struggle by means of the one who struggles.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to advanced widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:250 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:250: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if advanced were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:250 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:250 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:250.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between advanced and warriors also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:250 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:250 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:250 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:250 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:250 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:250, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine advanced until the demand disappears.

**The deed before its final account**

The final emphasis of 2:250 returns to responsibility. No explanation of advanced is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:250 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word advanced in 2:250 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:250 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:250, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The exegetical tradition approaches advanced from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:250 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:250 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding advanced and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:250. The verse 2:250 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:250 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands warriors, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:250.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:250 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving advanced; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A report preserved by the tradition**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating advanced from the rest of life. In 2:250, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about advanced. They are the place in which assent to 2:250 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:250 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: advanced is joined to warriors, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:250 gives the reader a useful order of questions about advanced. What does the term advanced mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:250? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:250 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:250.
