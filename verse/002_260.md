## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:260

> **And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.” Allah responded, “Do you not believe?” Abraham replied, “Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured.” Allah said, “Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops. Then call them back, they will fly to you in haste. And ˹so you will˺ know that Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Show Me How" — The Friend Asks His Friend**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings abraham and dead into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:260 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:260 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Four Birds, Every Mountain**

The verse implies no context for Abrahams question, but commentators nevertheless record several possibilities. Abraham saw a scene on a coast where creatures of the sea, land, and air were all eating other creatures and wondered how resurrection could bring all the pieces of these animals together; or it was part of his exchange with his antagonist in v. 258, where the sign would have given Abraham a stronger hand or lent support to his message to other people; or it may have been in connection with the sacrifice of his son or that Abraham wanted confirmation of his special status as prophet.

**Mighty, Wise — The Twin Seals**

Many interpret it spiritually to mean the revival of dead hearts through the Spirit (al-Rāzī). Dost thou not believe? is understood to be a kind of implicit affirmation of Abrahams faith, as if to say, You believe, so why are you asking? That my heart may be at peace evokes other Qur'anic references, such as 89:27, where the soul at peace is invited to enter Paradise, and 13:28, where souls are at peace in the remembrance of Allah. Here commentators grapple with the idea that Abraham would be out of this state of peace.

**The first movement of a guided life**

Some suggest that there are degrees of spiritual knowledge, and that Abraham is speaking of going from one depth of understanding to another, not that he is expressing doubt about Allahs existence or His Power (al-Rāzī). Since believers acknowledge no end to the Glories of Allah, Abrahams question can be seen as an expression of a desire to witness ever more of Allahs Self-Disclosure, to use the language often employed by Sufis. Regarding this verse a ḥadīth says, We have more right to doubt than did Abraham. Abrahams doubt was an entreaty for more knowledge, not a hesitation about Allahs existence.

Even the Prophet Muhammad is commanded to say, My Lord! Increase me in knowledge! (20:114), which is not meant to highlight the Prophets ignorance, but to point to Allahs Limitlessness and His infinite Reality.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The verb in make them be drawn to thee is understood by many, perhaps even a majority, to mean “cut,” though this interpretation leaves the difficulty of the place of the prepositional phrase to thee (al-Rāzī). According to this interpretation, Abraham takes four birds (peacock, eagle, raven, and rooster), cuts them up into pieces, and then places them on four mountains, representing the four cardinal directions. He then calls them, at which point the scattered parts of the birds reassemble into their original forms and come flying back to him. According to one reading, the eagle represents lust for food; the rooster, lust for sex; the crow, excessive curiosity; and the peacock, vanity, though many different lists of birds are to be found in the commentaries (al-Rāzī).

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The other interpretation, reflected in the translation, is that there is no killing of the birds mentioned in the verse. Rather, Abraham first made each bird be drawn to him, then placed them, alive, each on a mountain, and then called them to himself. This interpretation would then represent the reunification of souls and their bodies with the ease of a man calling to a bird, which comes in haste to him (al-Rāzī).

It is related that Ibn ʿAbbās said of Abrahams question and Allahs gentle response to it, There is no more hopeful verse in the Qur'an than this one. The colleague in conversation with Ibn ʿAbbās said that for him the most hopeful verse was 39:53: O My servants who have been prodigal to the detriment of their own souls! Despair not of Allahs Mercy. Truly Allah forgives all sins (Ibn Kathīr).

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The verse describes abraham not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:260, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:260, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if abraham were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:260 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:260 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:260.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:260. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:260 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between abraham and dead.

In 2:260, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of abraham while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:260 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:260 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for abraham, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:260, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:260, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:260, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:260 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:260 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:260, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine abraham until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:260 returns to responsibility. No explanation of abraham is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:260 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word abraham in 2:260 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:260 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:260, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
