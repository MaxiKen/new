## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:93

> **And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey,” they answered, “We hear and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!”**

### Expanded Commentary

**"We Hear — and We Disobey"**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between took and mountain. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:93. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:93. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:93.

**Drinking the Calf into the Heart**

Regarding the raising of the Mount, see 2:63, where a similar command to take hold of the Torah and earnestly follow it is mentioned. Explaining drink the calf, the commentators understand the drinking to mean that they were made to drink the love of the calf (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī), meaning that they directed their love to it due to their disbelief (kufr), which here as elsewhere can also mean denial and ingratitude. A ḥadīth that similarly uses the metaphor of drinking states, Trials and temptations come upon hearts unrelentingly like a woven mat. Any heart that is made to drink of them has a black spot placed upon it, and any heart that denies them has a white spot placed upon it. Thus two kinds of hearts arise: the white are pure and no trial can harm them so long as the heavens and the earth endure, and the others are black and ashen . . . who do not recognize what is right or deplore what is wrong except as they are made to drink from their passions.

**"Evil Is What Your Belief Enjoins" — The Irony Verdict**

What their belief enjoins is condemned insofar as they affirm and embrace evils such as the worship of the calf, the slaying of the prophets, and other actions denounced in connection with the Israelites (cf. 4:153–61). Their belief (īmān) here is an orientation of the heart that allows it to be satiated by worldly pleasures and ambitions and is symbolic of the soul that is filled with the world and abandons guidance (Aj).

The verse describes took not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:93, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:93, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The standard in 2:93 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from took: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between took and mountain also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:93 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:93 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:93 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:93 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

In 2:93, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of took while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:93 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:93 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for took, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:93, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

In 2:93, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:93, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:93 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:93 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:93, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine took until the demand disappears.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The final emphasis of 2:93 returns to responsibility. No explanation of took is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:93 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word took in 2:93 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:93 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:93, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The exegetical tradition approaches took from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:93 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:93 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding took and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:93. The verse 2:93 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:93 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands mountain, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:93.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:93 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving took; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating took from the rest of life. In 2:93, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about took. They are the place in which assent to 2:93 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:93 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: took is joined to mountain, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
