## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:247

> **Their prophet told them, “Allah has appointed Saul to be your king.” They protested, “How can he be our king when some of us are more deserving of kingship than he, and he has not been blessed with vast riches?” He replied, “Allah has chosen him over you and blessed him with knowledge and stature. Allah grants kingship to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**Tālūt — Raised Out of Nowhere**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and king. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:247. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:247. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:247.

Sauls low social status, which is here the source of the Israelites disdain for his kingship, is mentioned by Saul himself when Samuel first speaks to him in 1 Samuel 9:21: Saul answered, I am only a Benjaminite, from the least of the tribes of Israel, and my family is the humblest of all the families. . . . Why then have you spoken to me in this way? And in 1 Samuel 10:27 the Israelites express their disdain: But some worthless fellows said, How can this man save us? They despised him and brought him no present.

**"He Has Not Been Given Abundance of Wealth"**

The basis for their aversion to Saul as king is similar to that for the aversion of the Quraysh and the Jews to Muhammad at his election as a prophet, namely, his lack of wealth and status among the Quraysh and his non-Jewish lineage among the Jews; see also 3:26c. In the case of Saul, the commentators understand this account to mean that Saul was from neither a kingly nor a priestly line, but hailed from the tribe of Benjamin (al-Rāzī). Sauls intelligence and physical prowess were intrinsic qualities useful for a good leader; the status and wealth expected by the people would be qualities extrinsic to any man, accidental to his nature (al-Rāzī).

Increased him amply in knowledge and body could also be rendered increased him in excellence of knowledge and body. The granting of sovereignty by Allah is also mentioned in 3:26; 4:54; 12:101.

**"Allah Gives His Sovereignty to Whom He Wills"**

The verse's reference to prophet widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:247 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:247: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:247 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The relation between prophet and king also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:247 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:247 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:247 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:247 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:247, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of prophet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:247 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:247 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:247, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:247, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:247, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:247 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Mercy and warning held together**

A reader who finds the command in 2:247 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:247, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:247 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:247 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The deed before its final account**

The choice of the word prophet in 2:247 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:247 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:247, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:247 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:247 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:247. The verse 2:247 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:247 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands king, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:247.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:247 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prophet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating prophet from the rest of life. In 2:247, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about prophet. They are the place in which assent to 2:247 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:247 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: prophet is joined to king, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
