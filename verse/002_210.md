## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:210

> **Are they waiting for Allah ˹Himself˺ to come to them in the shade of clouds, along with the angels? ˹If He did˺, then the matter would be settled ˹at once˺. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Waiting Men Asleep**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings waiting and shade into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:210 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:210 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Shadows of Clouds — and the Allah Who Cannot Move**

An alternate reading of that Allah should come in the shadows of clouds, with the angels would be that Allah should come and that the angels [should come] in the shadows of clouds, according to which the coming in the shadows of clouds would apply only to the angels (al-Ṭabarī). Some say this means that [the Judgment/Command of] Allah should come, indicating that what Allah has promised or threatened will come in the clouds. For some this verse refers to the angels who come at the moment of death (see 32:11 and commentary), while others interpret it as a description of events on the Day of Resurrection (al-Rāzī), as in 25:25: And the day when the heavens are split open with clouds and the angels are sent down in a descent.

**"Unto Allah Are All Matters Returned"**

The commentators are careful to point out that this verse could not mean that Allah moves from place to place or could even be located in a place, as this would limit Allah and introduce change into His Nature. That the matter should have been decreed refers to the finality of the Hereafter, when ones deeds have already been done and there is no adding to or subtracting from them (al-Rāzī); see also 6:8, Had We sent down an angel, then the matter would be decreed. Unto Allah are all matters returned, which is usually understood to mean their return to Him in the Hereafter, is also found in 3:109; 8:44; 11:123; 22:76; 35:4; 57:5.

**The first scene of human responsibility**

The theme of waiting places human weakness beside human responsibility. The account does not require a fantasy of moral perfection; it asks what a person does after knowledge, temptation, and failure have entered the scene. The important distinction is between being tested and choosing the test's wrong answer, then refusing the return that Allah makes possible.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if waiting were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:210 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:210 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:210.

**What the command reveals about the human being**

The relation between waiting and shade also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:210 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:210 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:210 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:210 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The adversary’s method**

The choice of the word waiting in 2:210 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:210 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:210, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches waiting from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:210 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:210 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Knowledge, temptation, and choice**

There is also a distinction between understanding waiting and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:210. The verse 2:210 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:210 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands shade, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:210.

**Theological questions around the account**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:210 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving waiting; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating waiting from the rest of life. In 2:210, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about waiting. They are the place in which assent to 2:210 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The mercy that follows failure**

The most balanced response to 2:210 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: waiting is joined to shade, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:210 gives the reader a useful order of questions about waiting. What does the term waiting mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:210? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:210 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:210.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:210. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:210 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between waiting and shade.

In 2:210, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of waiting while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:210 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:210 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for waiting, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:210, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
