## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:115

> **To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is AllEncompassing, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**East and West in One Hand**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between belong and wherever. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:115. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:115. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:115.

**"There Is the Face of Allah" — the Heart of the Clause**

Allah is elsewhere called the Lord of the East and the West (73:9); Lord of the two easts and Lord of the two wests (55:17); and Lord of the easts and the wests (70:40). The famous Light Verse (24:35) speaks of a blessed olive tree, neither of the East nor of the West. See also 2:142c. Regarding the remainder of the verse, the commentators converge on two main points: the practical question of the qiblah (the direction of the canonical prayer) and the doctrinal matter of understanding what it means that there is the Face of Allah wherever one turns.

**All-Encompassing, Knowing — and the Mosqueless Prayer**

Most commentators connect this verse to the change in the qiblah, which was at first oriented toward Jerusalem, but which, after the revelation of 2:144, became oriented toward the Kaʿbah in Makkah. Indeed, as a practical matter some thought that this verse gave permission to pray in any direction, but that this was abrogated by 2:144. For some, the present verse was revealed to the Prophet as a reassurance for some Muslims who told him that the previous night they could not discern the proper direction of prayer and later realized they had prayed facing in the wrong direction .

Others connect it to the permission to pray in the direction one is facing while mounted (Āl); this permission pertains to nonrequired prayers.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Still others prefer to link it to the death of the Negus, the king of Abyssinia who had sheltered many Muslims as refugees during the worst days of the persecution in Makkah. The Prophet said, Your brother the Negus has died; so pray for him. When those present objected that he was not a Muslim, it is said that 3:199 was revealed: And truly among the People of the Book are those who believe in Allah and that which has been sent down unto you, and that which has been sent down unto them. Then when they objected that the Negus did not face the qiblah, this verse was revealed . In another account—regarding 40:60, Call upon Me, and I shall respond to you—some asked the Prophet, In which direction? at which this verse as revealed. In verses such as these where several occasions of revelation are given, commentators often adopt a neutral attitude and acknowledge that the occasion is not decisive in light of the doctrinal content, which in this verse is profound and universal.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

There is the Face of Allah is understood by some to simply mean, There is Allah, There is the qiblah of Allah (al-Ṭabarī), or There is Allahs Contentment (al-Rāzī). In connection with this verse many mention 28:88: All things perish, save His Face; 55:26–7: All upon it passes away. And there remains the Face of thy Lord, Possessed of Majesty and Bounty; 57:4: He is with you wheresoever you are; and 58:7: He is with them wheresoever they are. Some argue that the very fact that it says wheresoever is the strongest proof against anthropomorphizing Allah, since any body could be in only one place and not more than one place simultaneously (al-Rāzī).

**Mercy and warning held together**

All-Encompassing (wāsiʿ) can also mean unstinting or generous (al-Qurṭubī). Related passages include 53:32: Truly your Lord is of vast forgiveness; and 7:156: My Mercy encompasses all things. Al-Rāzī mentions that, in connection with the ruin of mosques mentioned in the previous verse, one can understand this phrase to mean that, despite destruction of mosques, Allahs remembrance cannot be hindered since He is wherever one turns.

This verse can also be taken as an allusion to the Omnipresence of Allah, who is the First, and the Last, and the Outward, and the Inward (57:3), manifest in all things, though naught is like unto Him (42:11). This verse is understood to mean that Allah is present everywhere and is one of the scriptural foundations for the Sufi doctrine of the oneness of being, or waḥdat al-wujūd.

**The deed before its final account**

The verse's reference to belong widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:115 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:115: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:115 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from belong: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:115 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands wherever, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:115.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:115 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belong; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belong from the rest of life. In 2:115, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belong. They are the place in which assent to 2:115 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:115 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belong is joined to wherever, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:115 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belong. What does the term belong mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:115? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:115 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:115.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:115. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:115 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belong and wherever.

In 2:115, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of belong while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:115 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
