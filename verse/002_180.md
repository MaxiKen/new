## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:180

> **It is prescribed that when death approaches any of you—if they leave something of value—a will should be made in favour of parents and immediate family with fairness. ˹This is˺ an obligation on those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Testament Command**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings prescribed and leave into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:180 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:180 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Third and Its Margin: The Hadith's Frame**

Bequest (waṣiyyah) is also used in 4:12 and 5:106. Here some good (khayr) means ones property, as in, Whatever good you spend (2:272), and also, Truly he is fierce in his love for good things (100:8). According to one interpretation, the prescription here is a conditional one, being an obligation upon the reverent only if one leaves some good, which does not make such a bequest a universal obligation. This command would not change the status of those who have debts or other financial obligations, which must be paid from ones property even after death. Thus the first phrase is taken to mean, Should one desire to make a bequest. There are differences of opinion as to what amount must be involved for a bequest to be made; some jurists assign a specific amount, while others consider any sum, large or small, to be a possible bequest (al-Qurṭubī).

**The Word in Its Companions' Circle**

In general there are two main restrictions on bequests in Islamic Law: (1) that they account for no more than a third of ones property, and (2) that the bequest not be designated for a person who automatically inherits according to the Qur'anic injunctions concerning shares of inheritance. Some consider this verse to be abrogated completely; others say it is partially abrogated in the case of those legally entitled to an inheritance share as stipulated in 4:11–13, but continues to apply in the case of others (Ṭs). Some, including the Shiites (who have a substantially different interpretation of inheritance law from Sunnis when it comes to the relationship between quota and nonquota heirs), believe that this verse was not abrogated and that nothing in it contradicts any other verse of the Qur'an.

**A duty that begins with the vulnerable**

The question of abrogation arises because of the ḥadīth, Let there be no bequests for heirs. This ḥadīth, if taken as universally applicable, renders the verses concerning bequests partially incompatible with those describing inheritance shares. It means that one cannot make a bequest for those who, according to the shares specified by the Qur'an, would receive part of the deceaseds property automatically without any bequest. For some commentators and jurists, such as al-Qurṭubī, the acceptance of this ḥadīth as binding shows that the Sunnah can abrogate the Qur'an (see 2:106c for a discussion of abrogation), while for others this shows that the present verse is not abrogated for the very same reason, namely, that a ḥadīth, about which one has less certainty (yaqīn) than about the Qur'an, cannot replace a ruling of the Qur'an (Ṭs).

**The relationship named by the verse**

Al-Qurṭubī acknowledges that the only text preventing a harmonization of the Qur'anic injunctions regarding bequests and inheritance is this ḥadīth barring bequests for heirs. Some have tried to harmonize them by saying that the parents and relatives here are those who would not inherit in any case (e.g., parents who were disbelievers and thus nonquota heirs).

As a moral issue, bequests are to be made first to relatives and only secondarily to nonrelatives; early commentators severely condemned those who would leave relatives in need while giving property to nonrelatives (al-Qurṭubī). One proof text for the one-third restriction on bequests is a ḥadīth in which the Prophet said to a man who offered to make a bequest of two-thirds or one-half of his wealth, “One-third, and a third is considerable. It is better to leave your heirs free from want than to leave them destitute and begging from people.”

**Rights before sentiment**

Prior to the institution of the Qur'anic rules of bequest and inheritance, the custom of Arabia was that male relations would inherit through the male line, called agnatic inheritance. The Qur'anic shares, which included the nearest male relatives, demoted the customary agnatic heirs to a secondary status in relation to the inheritance shares; they could still inherit under Sunni law, but only from the property left after the quota shares had been distributed. In Shiism, agnatic inheritance is rejected, and a system of proximity in blood kinship is used for the residual property after the distribution of the shares, applying equally for the male and female lines.

The concern with prescribed shows that justice is measured most severely where affection, dependence, and power overlap. A person may be generous in public and still wrong a relative or a child in private. By naming the relationship, the verse makes invisible duties visible and gives moral language to the person whose claim might otherwise be dismissed.

**How neglect becomes wrongdoing**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if prescribed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:180 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:180 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:180.

A reader who finds the command in 2:180 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:180, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prescribed until the demand disappears.

**The legal protection in the command**

The final emphasis of 2:180 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prescribed is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:180 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prescribed in 2:180 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:180 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:180, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A family obligation with social reach**

The exegetical tradition approaches prescribed from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:180 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:180 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prescribed and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:180. The verse 2:180 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:180 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands leave, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:180.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:180 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prescribed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
