## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:261

> **The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Seed and the Seven Hundred**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between example and cause. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:261. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:261. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:261.

**Allah Multiplies for Whom He Wills — The Open Multiplier**

And Allah multiplies for whomsoever He will is echoed in 24:38: That Allah may reward them for the best of what they have done, and increase them from His Bounty. And Allah provides for whomsoever He will without reckoning. The Divine Name All-Encompassing (al-Wāsiʿ) also means generous. The Prophet is reported to have said, Allah makes the good deed of the son of Adam to be tenfold, or seven hundred fold, except for the fast, for Allah says, The fast is for me, and I reward it (Ibn Kathīr). Sometimes such aḥādīth use more concrete imagery, as when a man came to the Prophet and said, This camel I give in the way of Allah, and the Prophet replied, On the Day of Judgment you shall have seven hundred camels (Ibn Kathīr).

**The Traditional Harvest**

The subject of example joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:261, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:261 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:261 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from example: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The relation between example and cause also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:261 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:261 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:261 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:261 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

In 2:261, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of example while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:261 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:261 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for example, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:261, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

In 2:261, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:261, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:261 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:261 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:261, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine example until the demand disappears.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The final emphasis of 2:261 returns to responsibility. No explanation of example is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:261 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word example in 2:261 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:261 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:261, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The exegetical tradition approaches example from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:261 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:261 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding example and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:261. The verse 2:261 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:261 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands cause, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:261.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:261 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving example; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating example from the rest of life. In 2:261, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about example. They are the place in which assent to 2:261 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:261 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: example is joined to cause, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:261 gives the reader a useful order of questions about example. What does the term example mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:261? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:261 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:261.
