## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:166

> **˹Consider the Day˺ when those who misled others will disown their followers—when they face the torment—and the bonds that united them will be cut off.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Scene of Disavowal**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings consider and disown into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:166 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:166 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Ropes Cut**

Those who were followed refers to the leaders of idolatry, while those who followed refers to their minions and adherents; or the two phrases may refer, respectively, to the satans and the human beings who followed them. This may also refer generally to all those who led and followed in the ways of error in the life of the world (al-Ṭabarī). Recourse renders asbāb (sing. sabab), which can mean a rope, a means by which to obtain something, or a cause. Here it is understood to refer to the bonds, the love, or the friendships and blood ties they shared in the world, all of which will be severed from them as a means of help in the Hereafter.

**The Punishment, Seen**

According to another interpretation, the recourse consists of their deeds in this life, which remain sound in the Hereafter if they are righteous deeds, but these means are cut off when they are evil (al-Ṭabarī). It can also point to the fact that they have no way of staving off the punishment or escaping the consequences of their evil actions (al-Rāzī).

The mention of consider gives the verse a definite centre. Its meaning in 2:166 is not a general meditation on religion but an explanation of what this word requires in the situation named here. The passage at 2:166 moves between belief, action, and consequence, so a sound reading must preserve all three rather than allowing one attractive phrase to swallow the rest.

**The verse in its immediate setting**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if consider were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:166 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:166 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:166.

**The central term and its range**

The relation between consider and disown also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:166 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:166 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:166 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:166 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:166 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:166, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine consider until the demand disappears.

**What the early commentators preserve**

The final emphasis of 2:166 returns to responsibility. No explanation of consider is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:166 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word consider in 2:166 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:166 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:166, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The reasoning within the wording**

The exegetical tradition approaches consider from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:166 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:166 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding consider and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:166. The verse 2:166 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The theological question beneath the sentence**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:166 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands disown, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:166.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:166 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving consider; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The surrounding passage as a guide**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating consider from the rest of life. In 2:166, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about consider. They are the place in which assent to 2:166 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:166 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: consider is joined to disown, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:166 gives the reader a useful order of questions about consider. What does the term consider mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:166? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:166 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:166.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:166. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:166 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between consider and disown.

In 2:166, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of consider while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:166 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
