## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:96

> **You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Superlative of Clinging**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings find and more into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:96 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:96 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Wish of a Thousand Years**

Each one of them could grammatically refer to either the Jews who are described in the beginning of the verse or the polytheists, meaning that the Jews are more covetous even than people who wish to live a thousand years because they have no belief in a Hereafter. Another possible understanding of the grammar of this verse yields the translation “You will find them the most covetous of people for life; and among the polytheists are those who would like to live a thousand years . . . ,” although this latter reading is considered unlikely (al-Rāzī) and not idiomatic in Arabic. Some commentators mention that this phrase refers to the Zoroastrians or Persians in general, going so far as to quote a Persian phrase, “[May you have] ten thousand years!” spoken when someone sneezes. Others, such as al-Rāzī, prefer to see “thousand” as an idiomatic way of saying “many” and dismiss the connection of it to the Zoroastrians as a specific group.

**And Allah Sees What They Do**

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is reported to have said, A hypocrite is more covetous of the world than an idolater (Ibn Kathīr), perhaps because of the gnawing awareness of the consequences of ones actions (Aj). On this aspect of hypocrisy, see 4:143c; 4:145c. The hypocrites certainty in salvation is thus a delusion whose underlying truth, that their actions preclude such certitude, is not completely hidden from them. Or perhaps any hypocrites with a belief in their own special status before Allah will also believe that they are owed much in this world, and their hypocrisy, clouding their judgment, forces them to prefer the nearer pleasure of this life, whose conditions seem certain. The punishment is that of the Hereafter. Some commentators suggest that him in remove him refers to the enemy of Gabriel mentioned in the next verse .

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to find widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:96 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:96: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if find were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:96 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:96 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:96.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between find and more also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:96 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:96 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:96 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:96 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:96 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:96, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine find until the demand disappears.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The final emphasis of 2:96 returns to responsibility. No explanation of find is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:96 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word find in 2:96 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:96 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:96, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The deed before its final account**

The exegetical tradition approaches find from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:96 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:96 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding find and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:96. The verse 2:96 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:96 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands more, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:96.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:96 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving find; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating find from the rest of life. In 2:96, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about find. They are the place in which assent to 2:96 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:96 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: find is joined to more, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:96 gives the reader a useful order of questions about find. What does the term find mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:96? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:96 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:96.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:96. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:96 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between find and more.
