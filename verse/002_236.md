## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:236

> **There is no blame if you divorce women before the marriage is consummated or the dowry is settled. But give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation—the rich according to his means and the poor according to his. A reasonable compensation is an obligation on the good-doers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Dissolving the Unconsummated Marriage**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings blame and marriage into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:236 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:236 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Rich One's Riches, Poor One's Pence**

See 33:49, which also discusses divorce before the consummation of a marriage. Not having touched them refers to consummation of the marriage. This verse refers to the case in which a couple have contracted a marriage, but have not consummated it or agreed upon the bridewealth yet. As with the bridewealth itself, a wealthy man would give a different amount than a poorer man in this set of circumstances. Some say that if the couple cannot agree on an amount, it should be half of the bridal payment of those with similar means (see next verse). On bridal payments in general, see 4:24c.

The reference to blame places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

**A Duty Upon the Muḥsinīn**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if blame were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:236 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:236 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:236.

**A household placed under moral care**

The relation between blame and marriage also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:236 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:236 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:236 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:236 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:236 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:236, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine blame until the demand disappears.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The final emphasis of 2:236 returns to responsibility. No explanation of blame is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:236 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word blame in 2:236 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:236 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:236, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The exegetical tradition approaches blame from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:236 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:236 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding blame and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:236. The verse 2:236 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The difference between power and justice**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:236 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands marriage, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:236.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:236 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving blame; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**How the jurists read the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating blame from the rest of life. In 2:236, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about blame. They are the place in which assent to 2:236 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:236 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: blame is joined to marriage, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Protection from private harm**

The wording of 2:236 gives the reader a useful order of questions about blame. What does the term blame mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:236? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:236 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:236.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:236. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:236 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between blame and marriage.

In 2:236, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of blame while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:236 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:236 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for blame, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:236, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
