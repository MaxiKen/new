## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:184

> **˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Days Numbered" — The Measured Term**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings fast and days into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:184 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:184 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Sick, the Traveler, and the Fed-Ransom**

The phrase days numbered is a subject of disagreement; some identify it as referring to the days of Ramadan, others the days of fasting before the fast of Ramadan was instituted. When the Prophet first came to Madinah, it is said that the community was fasting three days per month in addition to ʿĀshūrāʾ (the tenth day of Muḥarram). According to a ḥadīth, when they arrived in Madinah they found the Jews fasting on Yom Kippur (the same day as ʿĀshūrāʾ), and the Prophet ordered the Muslims to fast that day, saying that they had more right than Jews to celebrate Moses, though the commentaries are inconsistent about the nature of the Yom Kippur celebration.

**"That You Should Fast Is Better" — The Verse's Counsel**

There is disagreement about whether these three days per month were obligatory in the same way that Ramadan would come to be. Those who did not consider the pre-Ramadan fast as obligatory interpret vv. 183–84 as referring to Ramadan itself. Others say the fast of three days per month was the first to be prescribed, which was then abrogated by the fast of Ramadan, but al-Ṭabarī, among others, argues that those who say this have no firm report showing that any fast before Ramadan was made obligatory. The passage shows that fasting three days per month was and continues to be a meritorious deed that many pious Muslims perform in imitation of the Prophet.

**The discipline named by the verse**

These three days are typically the thirteenth, fourteenth, and fifteenth days, or the days of the full moon, or according to some the beginning, middle, and end of each lunar month. It is plausible that before the institution of Ramadan the Muslim community observed this practice, but not in the manner of an obligation, and that after the Ramadan fast became an obligation, it has continued on as a meritorious practice.

**Hunger, desire, and conscious worship**

One opinion holds that the fast of Ramadan was obligatory, but that in the beginning one had the option of feeding one poor person instead, per day of fasting, based upon For those who can bear it, the ransom of feeding an indigent person, but that this injunction was abrogated by Let him among you who is present fast during that [month] in v. 185. Others consider that there had been no abrogation in these verses and hold that those who can bear it refers to old people who could conceivably fast, but for whom it would be a severe hardship (al-Qurṭubī). In this latter interpretation, the verb rendered can bear it would mean something like “are burdened.” It would thus mean “they are able to bear” in the sense that “they bear it with difficulty.” This latter meaning is part of the lexical root ṭ-w-q, from which the verb originates (al-Qurṭubī).

**The room made for repentance**

Whosoever volunteers good may refer to feeding people beyond what the verse requires or to providing the person one is required to feed with more than the bare minimum of food (al-Qurṭubī). Al-Qurṭubī, among others, offers the idea that fasting is better than expiation and that choosing to fast while traveling or ill, so long as it is not too great a hardship, is better, but adds, “Allah knows best.”

The verse's concern with fast shows why religious restraint is not meant to produce weakness or despair. Desire is acknowledged rather than denied, then given a disciplined place. The worshipper learns to distinguish a genuine need from an impulse, and a lawful ease from the kind of indulgence that makes obedience meaningless.

**A private act with public consequences**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if fast were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:184 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:184 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:184.

There is also a distinction between understanding fast and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:184. The verse 2:184 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The legal condition and its purpose**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:184 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands days, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:184.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:184 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving fast; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**How restraint becomes freedom**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating fast from the rest of life. In 2:184, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about fast. They are the place in which assent to 2:184 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:184 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: fast is joined to days, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:184 gives the reader a useful order of questions about fast. What does the term fast mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:184? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:184 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:184.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:184. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:184 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between fast and days.

In 2:184, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of fast while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:184 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:184 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for fast, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:184, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
