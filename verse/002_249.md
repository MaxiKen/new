## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:249

> **When Saul marched forth with his army, he cautioned: “Allah will test you with a river. So whoever drinks ˹his fill˺ from it is not with me, and whoever does not taste it—except a sip from the hollow of his hands—is definitely with me.” They all drank ˹their fill˺ except for a few! When he and the ˹remaining˺ faithful with him crossed the river, they said, “Now we are no match for Goliath and his warriors.” But those ˹believers˺ who were certain they would meet Allah reasoned, “How many times has a small force vanquished a mighty army by the Will of Allah! And Allah is ˹always˺ with the steadfast.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**A River as a Sieve**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between saul and army. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:249. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:249. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:249.

**Goliath's Host and Small Companies**

Some have drawn a parallel between this verse and the account of Gideons crossing the Jordan River in Judges 7:4–7, though the similarities are typological and not historical. In the Gideon account, the test was known to Gideon alone, the army was not aware they were being divided into two categories (those who drank with their mouths and those who did so with their hands), and the purpose was to reduce the armys numbers to three hundred men, so as to show that the Will of Allah could be carried out with a small number of fighters. In the Qur'anic account, Saul openly tests the faith of his soldiers, who are commanded to take only a handful of water and no more.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

From a Qur'anic point of view, Sauls test of his army could be a reenactment of sacred history (Gideon lived before Samuel and Saul), much as parting the waters is a recurring theme in the Old Testament, taking place at the hands of Moses while the Israelites were escaping from the Egyptians through the Red Sea and by the ark when the people were crossing the Jordan during the time of Joshua (Joshua 3:14–17). That this incident echoes Gideons crossing, whose purpose was to show that Allah could defeat many with few, is reinforced by the statement in this verse of those who believed that they would meet their Lord, How many a small company have overcome a large company by Allahs leave! This group deemed they would meet their Lord, meaning they were either certain of the Hereafter or prepared to die (al-Qurṭubī).

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to saul widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:249 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:249: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:249 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from saul: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between saul and army also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:249 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:249 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:249 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:249 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:249, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:249, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:249 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The deed before its final account**

A reader who finds the command in 2:249 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:249, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine saul until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:249 returns to responsibility. No explanation of saul is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:249 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The choice of the word saul in 2:249 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:249 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:249, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches saul from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:249 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:249 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

There is also a distinction between understanding saul and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:249. The verse 2:249 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:249 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands army, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:249.

**A report preserved by the tradition**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:249 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving saul; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating saul from the rest of life. In 2:249, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about saul. They are the place in which assent to 2:249 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:249 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: saul is joined to army, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:249 gives the reader a useful order of questions about saul. What does the term saul mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:249? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:249 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:249.
