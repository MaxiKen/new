## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:48

> **Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Be Mindful of a Day**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings guard and help into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:48 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:48 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Be mindful renders ittaqā, which is usually translated as a variant of reverence, but it also has the sense of being careful, aware, or on guard. This verse closely resembles v. 123, and the futility of another souls help is described in 82:19 in similar terms. Elsewhere the Qur'an lists wealth and children (3:10, 116) among those things that will not avail any soul in the Hereafter.

**Intercession Before Allah: What Is Denied and What Is Allowed**

The concept of intercession (shafāʿah) is related to similar concepts in the Qur'an such as istighfār, meaning the seeking of forgiveness for oneself or for another, as in 40:7, where angels seek forgiveness for those who believe. Another example is ṣalāh (usually prayer), not as worship of Allah, but in the sense of blessing someone, as in 9:103, where Allah says to the Prophet, Bless them. Truly thy blessings are a comfort for them.

Intercession and the giving of ransom or compensation were common practices in the tribal society of Arabia and indeed in most traditional societies. Islamic Law itself contains provisions for ransoming or compensation, such as feeding a poor person when one is unable to fast (2:184). Earthly intercession and ransom are aspects of human social and legal transactions, but intercession before Allah in the Hereafter is a theological issue touching on Allahs Authority, the danger of idolatry, and the efficacy of prayer.

**The Three Refusals and the Architecture of Justice**

For some mystics, such as Ibn ʿArabī, supplicatory prayer brings into being possibilities that would not have been manifested but for that prayer. Allahs compassionate Love (raḥmah) encompasses all things (7:156), and included in that infinite range of love is the love that responds only when a request is made, as in 2:152: Remember Me, and I shall remember you; this does not nullify the prior love, which is a purely unasked-for gift. Ultimately, the request for love is itself a manifestation of Allahs Love, in that we cannot remember Allah unless He remembers us first.

**Why the Warning Follows the Blessing**

According to this interpretation, Allah is the mysterious author of all prayer: We are nearer to him than his jugular vein (50:16); Allah comes between a man and his heart (8:24); And you do not will but that Allah wills. Truly Allah is Knowing, Wise (76:30); And you do not will but that Allah, the Lord of the worlds, wills (81:29); When My servants ask thee about Me, truly I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls Me. So let them respond to Me and believe in Me, that they may be led aright (2:186).

**The verse in its immediate setting**

Seen in this way, all intercession is Allahs—Unto Allah belongs intercession altogether (39:44)—since no plea for intercession can be made without love and remembrance on the part of the intercessor, which itself is a manifestation of Allahs own Will and Mercy, as mentioned in 20:109: On that Day intercession will be of no benefit, save [that of] those whom the Compassionate has granted leave and with whose word He is content. One could understand the Qur'anic statements about intercession to deny the kind of worldly bargaining or status-driven intercession that would have been of some avail on earth, but not in the Hereafter.

**The central term and its range**

If one can object that intercession diminishes Allahs Mercy and Wisdom, one can respond that the intercession itself is a Gift of Allah. All that the angels, prophets, saints, and believers who are granted intercession have to offer is their goodness and faith and nearness to Allah, all of which come from Allahs Wisdom for creatures and their relationship with one another. Seen in this way, passages that deny the efficacy intercession (as in 32:4; 39:44; 74:48) do not contradict those that affirm its possibility (as in 21:28; 34:23; 53:26). For a further discussion of intercession, see 2:255c.

The ransom (here ʿadl, lit. substitution, but usually fidyah) can include anything one would seek to give in exchange for deliverance from punishment, including an earth full of gold (3:91), twice as much as all the things on the earth (5:36; 13:18), and even ones own children (70:11).

**What the early commentators preserve**

The mention of guard gives the verse a definite centre. Its meaning in 2:48 is not a general meditation on religion but an explanation of what this word requires in the situation named here. The passage at 2:48 moves between belief, action, and consequence, so a sound reading must preserve all three rather than allowing one attractive phrase to swallow the rest.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if guard were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:48 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:48 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:48.

**The reasoning within the wording**

The most balanced response to 2:48 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: guard is joined to help, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:48 gives the reader a useful order of questions about guard. What does the term guard mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:48? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:48 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:48.

**The theological question beneath the sentence**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:48. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:48 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between guard and help.

In 2:48, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of guard while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:48 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:48 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for guard, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:48, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:48, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:48, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:48 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
