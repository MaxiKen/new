## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:124

> **˹Remember˺ when Abraham was tested by his Lord with ˹certain˺ commandments, which he fulfilled. Allah said, “I will certainly make you into a role model for the people.” Abraham asked, “What about my offspring?” Allah replied, “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Trial by Words**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings abraham and commandments into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:124 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:124 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Abraham was tried or tested, but no specifics about this test are given in this particular passage. The commentators have differed widely over the question. Some believe these words were commands given to Abraham to institute various kinds of bodily cleanliness, grooming, and ritual purification . Others believe they were the rituals and prayers he was to perform, specifically the central rites of the ḥajj, which Muslims consider to have originated with Abraham, including the circumambulation of the Kaʿbah, the running between Ṣafā and Marwah, and the pelting of the pillar representing Satan.

**The Imamate of Mankind**

Others see them as trials of suffering, such as circumcision, the sacrifice of his son (37:102), and the fire into which he was cast by his people, but which Allah made to be cool (21:68–69; Ṭ). Although Abraham is praised throughout the Qur'an, in this verse the commentators highlight his obedience and fulfillment of what was asked of him, mentioning it along with 53:37: And Abraham, who fulfilled. Some mention that this refers to his embodiment of the virtuous human qualities listed in the Qur'an in verses such as 9:112 and 33:35.

Some also say that the word Abraham fulfilled was his selection as an imām. Imām means most generally leader, including a religious or political leader or the person who leads the canonical prayer. In Shiism it also has the particular meaning of one in whom the Muhammadan Light is present and who is designated the spiritual leader of the community by Divine Decree (naṣṣ).

**"My Covenant Does Not Include the Wrongdoers"**

The response to Abrahams prayer means that a wrongdoer or tyrant would not deserve a covenant with Allah and could not rightly be an imām; or it means that such a person could be an imām, but an imām who does wrong would not receive the promise of the Hereafter; or it can simply be read as meaning that not all of Abrahams descendants would be virtuous, as in 37:113: And We blessed him and Isaac. And among their progeny are the virtuous and those who clearly wrong themselves (al-Ṭabarī). In Shiism, the fifth and sixth Imams have cited this verse to indicate that Allah does not set up or allow an unjust imam (Ṭū). Elements similar to those in this verse are found in 25:74: Our Lord! Grant us comfort in our spouses and our progeny, and make us imāms for the reverent.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to abraham widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:124 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:124: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if abraham were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:124 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:124 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:124.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between abraham and commandments also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:124 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:124 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:124 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:124 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:124 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:124, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine abraham until the demand disappears.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The final emphasis of 2:124 returns to responsibility. No explanation of abraham is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:124 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word abraham in 2:124 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:124 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:124, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The deed before its final account**

The exegetical tradition approaches abraham from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:124 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:124 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding abraham and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:124. The verse 2:124 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:124 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands commandments, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:124.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:124 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving abraham; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating abraham from the rest of life. In 2:124, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about abraham. They are the place in which assent to 2:124 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The most balanced response to 2:124 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: abraham is joined to commandments, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
