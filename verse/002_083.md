## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:83

> **And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Covenant That Reads Like a Summary of Scripture**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between took and israel. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:83. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:83. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:83.

Here when can be read as [remember] when, in connection with the previous passages recalling the history of the Israelites. This verse resembles other passages that contain universal commandments regarding truth and virtuous action, such as 6:151–53 and 17:23–39, considered by some to be among the muḥkam verses of the Qur'an (on this term, see 3:7c). An alternate reading has the pronoun in the third person, so that it might be rendered We made a covenant with the Children of Israel: they worship none but Allah.

**Parents First Among Men; Speech Reaching All**

In this case there would be a shift from the third-person nominative to the second-person imperative beginning with be virtuous in the middle of the verse, a grammatical shift not uncommon in the Qur'an (see the essay Qur'anic Arabic: Its Characteristics and Impact on Arabic Language and Literature and the Languages and Literatures of Other Islamic peoples). Be virtuous toward parents does not have an explicit verb in Arabic, but reads literally and toward parents virtue, where the verb to be or to act is understood (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī); also, when the first phrase above is read in the third person, this phrase can also be read in the third person (al-Rāzī).

**"Then You Turned Away, Save a Few"**

Parents are especially honored in the Qur'an (see 4:36; 6:151; 17:23; 29:8; 31:14; 46:15) and the Ḥadīth (see the essay Qur'anic Ethics, Human Rights, and Society). These injunctions apply whether the parent is a believer or not, and the commentators often point to the example of Abraham and his restraint with his disbelieving father (19:42–48). The ḥadīth often bestows special honor upon mothers, as in the Prophetic saying, Paradise is at the feet of mothers. For regard for ones parents, see 29:8c; 31:13–14c.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Kinsfolk (dhul-qurbā, lit. possessors of nearness) refers to blood relations, and their good treatment is mentioned often (2:177; 4:8; 4:36; 8:41; 17:26), as are ones responsibilities toward orphans (2:220; 4:3–10; 4:127; 6:152; 17:34; 107:2) and the poor, very often listed in the same passages as relatives and orphans. An orphan (yatīm) was generally considered to be any minor whose father had died, as the father would have been the primary provider and protector of the child, but some considered those whose mothers had died also to be orphans.

To speak to people in a goodly way includes, according to the exegetes, calling them to monotheism, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong (see 3:104c); and this command to speak in a goodly way applies to all people (al-Ṭabarī). Those who turned away, save a few were the Children of Israel (al-Ṭabarī).

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The subject of took joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:83, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:83 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:83 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from took: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The relation between took and israel also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:83 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:83 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:83 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:83 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The final emphasis of 2:83 returns to responsibility. No explanation of took is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:83 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The choice of the word took in 2:83 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:83 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:83, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches took from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:83 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:83 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The poor person’s dignity**

There is also a distinction between understanding took and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:83. The verse 2:83 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:83 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands israel, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:83.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:83 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving took; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating took from the rest of life. In 2:83, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about took. They are the place in which assent to 2:83 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:83 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: took is joined to israel, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:83 gives the reader a useful order of questions about took. What does the term took mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:83? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:83 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:83.
