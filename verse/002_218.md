## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:218

> **Surely those who have believed, emigrated, and struggled in the Way of Allah—they can hope for Allah’s mercy. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Comfort Clause After the Trial Clause**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings believed and hope into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:218 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:218 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

When v. 217 was revealed, it is said that ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Jaḥsh and those with him despaired and doubted that they would receive merit for their actions, even though they were absolved of having committed a crime, and this verse was a comfort and promise to them (al-Rāzī).

**"It Is They Who Hope" — The Theology of Hope**

The notion of hope reflects the idea that no reward is certain, that everything is in Allahs Hands, and that one might still die a disbeliever, but it may also be understood as a descriptive statement, indicating that believers should approach Allah with an attitude of hope and fear (al-Rāzī). This idea of hope is also connected by some with 2:46 (Who reckon that they shall meet their Lord and that they shall return unto Him) in that the hoping and reckoning are descriptions of both what believers do and what they are called to do.

The verse's reference to believed widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:218 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:218: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**Believer, Emigrant, Warrior**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if believed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:218 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:218 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:218.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The relation between believed and hope also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:218 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:218 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:218 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:218 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:218. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:218 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between believed and hope.

In 2:218, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of believed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:218 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Mercy and warning held together**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:218 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for believed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:218, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:218, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:218, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:218 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The deed before its final account**

A reader who finds the command in 2:218 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:218, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine believed until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:218 returns to responsibility. No explanation of believed is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:218 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The choice of the word believed in 2:218 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:218 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:218, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches believed from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:218 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:218 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

There is also a distinction between understanding believed and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:218. The verse 2:218 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:218 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands hope, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:218.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:218 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving believed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating believed from the rest of life. In 2:218, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about believed. They are the place in which assent to 2:218 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:218 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: believed is joined to hope, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
