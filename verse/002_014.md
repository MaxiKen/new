## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:14

> **When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**Two Audiences, Two Speeches**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings meet and evil into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:14 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:14 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The occasion given for this revelation involves a hypocrite who boasted to his companions, “Watch how I divert these fools from you.” After having heaped praise upon some Companions of the Prophet, he returned to his own companions and said, “When you see them, do as I do.” When the Companions returned to the Prophet to tell him, this verse was revealed, exposing the intentions of the hypocrites (Ibn Kathīr).

**"Their Satans": The Company That Mirrors the Self**

Satans (shayāṭīn) is the plural of shayṭān, which in the singular usually refers to Satan. Here it is usually understood to mean the hypocrites leaders, fellow hypocrites, disbelievers, or inner demons. At times in the Qur'an shayāṭīn refers not only to otherworldly creatures, but also to human beings, as in 6:112: Thus have We made for every prophet an enemy—satans from among mankind and jinn.

The verse describes meet not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:14, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:14, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**Mockery as Belonging**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if meet were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:14 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:14 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:14.

**The Spiritual Cost of Two Speeches**

The relation between meet and evil also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:14 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:14 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:14 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:14 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The Modern Democracy of the Double Room**

The choice of the word meet in 2:14 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:14 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:14, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches meet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:14 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:14 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The first movement of a guided life**

There is also a distinction between understanding meet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:14. The verse 2:14 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:14 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands evil, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:14.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:14 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving meet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating meet from the rest of life. In 2:14, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about meet. They are the place in which assent to 2:14 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The most balanced response to 2:14 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: meet is joined to evil, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:14 gives the reader a useful order of questions about meet. What does the term meet mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:14? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:14 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:14.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:14. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:14 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between meet and evil.

In 2:14, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of meet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:14 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:14 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for meet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:14, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:14, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:14, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:14 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:14 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:14, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine meet until the demand disappears.
