## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:114

> **Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Worst Wrong: Blocking the Houses of Allah**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings more and allahs into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:114 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:114 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Entering in Dread; Disgrace Below, Punishment Above**

Mosques translates masājid (sing. masjid), which is derived from the verb to prostrate (sajada). Many commentators attempt to specify the mosques mentioned here. Some point to the Temple in Jerusalem (the Farthest Mosque mentioned in 17:1) and the Kaʿbah in Makkah, since they are named mosque (masjid) in the Qur'an. They also give differing accounts of the destruction of the Temple, which according to some took place with the connivance of Christians. Some say this verse refers to when at Ḥudaybiyah the Makkans stopped the Prophet from entering Makkah to pray at the Kaʿbah in 6/628 and the Muslim pilgrims agreed to go back to Madinah and return the following year (see the introduction to Sūrah 48).

**The Mosque of the Heart**

For al-Ṭabarī, since even the idolaters never tried to destroy the Kaʿbah, it must refer to the Temple. Others see it more generally, saying that it applies to anyone who prevents people from entering any house of worship (al-Qurṭubī). A broader interpretation of ruin (kharāb) holds that in filling the Kaʿbah with idols and the practice of idolatry, the idolaters were seeking its ruin, because it was no longer a mosque of the One Allah but a temple of idols . Some also mention that Abū Bakr had a small mosque in Makkah before the Muslims migrated to Madinah, a mosque the Makkans later destroyed (al-Rāzī).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Some try to identify specific ways in which disgrace or fear is evidenced by Christians, such as the payment of the jizyah, or indemnity (see 9:29c; Ṭ). But others point out that the notion of disgrace can be quite broad, and that the Prophet would often pray that disgrace not be visited upon him and his community in this world (Ibn Kathīr). Some Sufis discern another level of meaning, saying that the heart is the house of love and knowledge of Allah (the Kaʿbah is the House of Allah), and one does wrong in destroying it through false desires (Aj, Qu).

See also 9:17: It is not for the idolaters to maintain the mosques of Allah, bearing witness of disbelief against themselves.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to more widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:114 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:114: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if more were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:114 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:114 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:114.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between more and allah’s also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:114 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:114 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:114 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:114 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

There is also a distinction between understanding more and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:114. The verse 2:114 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The deed before its final account**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:114 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands allahs, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:114.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:114 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving more; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating more from the rest of life. In 2:114, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about more. They are the place in which assent to 2:114 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:114 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: more is joined to allahs, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The wording of 2:114 gives the reader a useful order of questions about more. What does the term more mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:114? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:114 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:114.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:114. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:114 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between more and allahs.

In 2:114, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of more while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:114 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:114 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for more, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:114, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:114, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:114, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:114 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
