## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:219

> **They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “There is great evil in both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit.” They ˹also˺ ask you what they should donate. Say, “Whatever you can spare.” This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect**

### Expanded Commentary

**Wine and Gambling — the First Verdict**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and great. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:219. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:219. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:219.

**Benefit and Sin Presently Weighed**

For a discussion of the proscription of drinking wine, see 4:43c. Gambling renders maysir, which literally denotes one type of gambling; gambling more generally is known as qimār. In maysir people draw arrows over meat, akin to drawing straws; the loser has to pay for the meat without getting any and is chided and ribbed, while the winners give their portions to the poor as a demonstration of generosity (al-Zamakhsharī). In this connection many of the commentators discuss various kinds of play (laʿb) and debate their legitimacy. In Islamic Law generally gambling in the form of wagering is forbidden, while playing games, such as chess and backgammon, as an idle pastime is frowned upon by many as a spiritual distraction, usually without being forbidden outright. Both games in fact came to the West from the Islamic world, and not being forbidden in the technical legal sense, both are popular among Muslims, even many devout ones.

**"Spend What Can Be Spared"**

Regarding the benefits for mankind in wine, al-Qurṭubī mentions that it helps with digestion, makes a miserly person magnanimous, gives boldness to the timid, gives color to the face, helps one to have sex, and makes one feel good for a short time. (Although this verse mentions specifically wine, Islamic Law understands this ruling to apply to all alcoholic drinks.) As for the benefits of gambling, he mentions that one can acquire wealth without effort and that the poor can benefit, since it was customary for the winners to distribute the meat of the maysir.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

As for their harm, intoxication leads to loss of one’s rational faculties, quarreling, violence, promiscuity, impaired judgment, and forgetting the remembrance of Allah. In later judicial philosophy, the ruling on intoxication played a major part in identifying “protection of the intelligence” as one of the essential objectives of the Law (maqāsid al-Sharīʿah). Similarly, gambling creates enmity and despair (al-Rāzī), produces ill-gotten gains, and is thus a diversion from the healthy and wholesome pursuit of profit and gain. The risk associated with gambling factors into Islamic financial theory, in that certain kinds of transactions are invalid because they entail excessive risk, and this risk is related to the prohibition against ribā (usually but imperfectly translated “usury”), which is discussed by some Islamic financial theorists as carrying a severe form of risk.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

Ribā is discussed in vv. 275–80.

On the question of spending in charity, what can be spared renders al-ʿafw, which means what is easy or what is done without difficulty (al-Rāzī) and in other contexts means pardon or forgiveness. There is disagreement over whether this refers to voluntary giving or to a required duty. Among those who uphold the former view are those who believe that this directive was abrogated by the institution of the alms (zakāh); others point out that there is no contradiction between the existence of the institution of the zakāh and a command to give charity (al-Qurṭubī).

**Mercy and warning held together**

The commentators note here that this means that one should be neither miserly nor improvident in matters of charity and that one should never impoverish oneself as a result of charitable giving. This verse is seen as expanding upon the question of giving in v. 215, which describes the recipients of charity, by further describing how much one should give (al-Qurṭubī). The extent of proper charitable giving is also discussed in 17:29: And let not thine hand be shackled to thy neck; nor let it be entirely open.

The verse's reference to prophet widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:219 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:219: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The deed before its final account**

The standard in 2:219 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

In 2:219, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of prophet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:219 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:219 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:219, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:219, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:219, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:219 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

A reader who finds the command in 2:219 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:219, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:219 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:219 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:219 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:219 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:219, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:219 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:219 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:219. The verse 2:219 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
