## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:151

> **Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew—**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Abraham-Prayer Fulfilled**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between since and among. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:151. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:151. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:151.

This verse describes the fulfillment of the prayer of Abraham for a messenger to be sent to his progeny in v. 129, which also mentions the same elements of the Book, Wisdom, and purification. Even as We sent is thought by some to be understood as a continuation of the guidance mentioned in the previous verse (al-Qurṭubī) or as meaning that the sending of the Messenger is part of the completion of the blessings mentioned in the previous verse (al-Zamakhsharī).

**The Fourfold Curriculum Again**

The wording around since assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

The standard in 2:151 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from since: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The Prophet's Ministry as One Complete Gift**

The relation between since and among also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:151 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:151 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:151 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:151 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:151, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:151, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:151 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The Book as an address**

A reader who finds the command in 2:151 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:151, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine since until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:151 returns to responsibility. No explanation of since is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:151 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The word that carries the argument**

The choice of the word since in 2:151 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:151 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:151, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches since from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:151 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:151 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

There is also a distinction between understanding since and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:151. The verse 2:151 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:151 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands among, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:151.

**The reader’s responsibility**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:151 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving since; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating since from the rest of life. In 2:151, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about since. They are the place in which assent to 2:151 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why the wording is exact**

The most balanced response to 2:151 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: since is joined to among, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:151 gives the reader a useful order of questions about since. What does the term since mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:151? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:151 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:151.

**A question answered by the surrounding verses**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:151. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:151 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between since and among.

In 2:151, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of since while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:151 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:151 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for since, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:151, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
