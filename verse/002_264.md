## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:264

> **O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day. Their example is that of a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone. Such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity. Allah does not guide ˹such˺ disbelieving people.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Do Not Annul Your Charities" — The Direct Command**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings belief and reminders into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:264 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:264 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The worst model of charity is one who gives only for appearances sake, as in 107:6–7, which mentions those who strive to be seen, yet refuse small kindnesses. These verses compare the merit of two kinds of giving through the symbolism of rain striking the earth. When a downpour strikes the earth on a smooth stone, it can only wash it away, meaning it produces no real benefit. When it lands on a fertile garden, the surplus of water causes the garden to double its yield, symbolizing the fruit of charity with the right intention.

**The Smooth Rock and the Sweeping Rain**

And if a downpour strikes it not, then a soft rain is taken to mean that, even if the amount of rain is not great, the garden will at least thrive normally and bear its fruit. Some commentators mention that certain kinds of oases flourish and have rich earth collect on them when they are slightly elevated, and that this is what is meant by a garden upon a hill. Moreover, on a hill of this sort, there are no streams that flow, and so it can be nourished only by rain (al-Qurṭubī).

**"Allah Guides Not the Disbelieving People"**

Others say that upon a hill (bi-rabwah) must mean something more like that which rises, meaning fertile soil from which crops grow upward, yielding the translation the parable of a fertile garden. Al-Rāzī argues that a garden upon a hill would not be reached by streams and would be exposed to too much wind, while a garden in a hollow would be inundated with water and would not receive the better effects of wind. He argues that the best garden is on level ground and prefers to relate rabwah to the verb to grow, which comes from the same root.

Such growth through charity is mentioned in the ḥadīth, When a person gives a lawfully earned date in charity, Allah takes it in His Hand and causes it to grow as one of you would grow his foals or camels, until it becomes like a mountain or even greater.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Out of a confirmation in their souls means that the givers affirm and have certainty of faith in their act of giving or that they do so because they have confirmation of the Truth of Allah or certainty (al-Zamakhsharī). Or it can mean that when they give, they take care and confirm where they place their charity to make sure that it is used properly .

The subject of belief joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:264, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:264 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if belief were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:264 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:264 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:264.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The relation between belief and reminders also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:264 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:264 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:264 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:264 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:264 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:264, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine belief until the demand disappears.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The final emphasis of 2:264 returns to responsibility. No explanation of belief is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:264 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word belief in 2:264 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:264 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:264, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The exegetical tradition approaches belief from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:264 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:264 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding belief and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:264. The verse 2:264 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:264 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands reminders, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:264.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:264 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belief; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:264, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:264 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:264 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to reminders, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
