## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:256

> **Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is AllHearing, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"No Coercion in Religion" — The Charter Flourish**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings compulsion and stands into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:256 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:256 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Truth Clear from Error — Enough**

Similar statements regarding faith are also found in 10:99–100: And had thy Lord willed, all those who are on the earth would have believed all together. Wouldst thou compel men till they become believers? It is not for a soul to believe, save by Allahs Leave; and 18:29: And say, It is the truth from your Lord! So whosoever will, let him believe, and whosoever will, let him disbelieve (see also 76:3; 2:217c). Some report that this verse was revealed in connection with some Muslims who had children among the Banū Naḍīr, a Jewish tribe that was exiled by the Muslim community from Madinah after they were found plotting against the Prophet.

**The Handhold Unbreakable — al-ʿUrwah al-Wuthqā**

Before the coming of Islam, some women of Yathrib (later Madinah) whose children died in infancy made a vow that, if they had children who survived, they would have them raised Jewish, which is how these children came to be among the Banū Naḍīr. These Muslim parents questioned whether they should force their children to join the Muslim community (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). Another account describes a Muslim man whose two sons became Christians and left for Syria with the merchants who had converted them (al-Ṭabarī). And a third account mentions that some Muslims were wet-nursed by Jews, and when the Jewish clans to which they belonged were exiled, they wanted to leave with them and become Jews, but their families forced them to remain Muslims (al-Ṭabarī).

**No Compulsion — Because the Handhold Takes Care of Itself**

According to some sources this verse refers specifically to the People of the Book or to anyone from whom one can take the jizyah (see 9:29c; IK, R). Indeed, many argue that the import of this verse is not absolute, since the Prophet, in his campaign and ultimate victory against the idolatrous Arabs, did not give them the option of remaining idolaters or paying the jizyah . The Arabs were in fact forced to abandon idol worship, although some disagree over whether they could become Jews and Christians and fall under the protected status of There is no coercion in religion (al-Rāzī).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

This position would entail, in effect, that among a certain group of religions recognized by Islam (including Judaism, Christianity, and Zoroastrianism), there is no coercion in religion. Others argue that this verse was abrogated by those verses that command Muslims to fight (e.g., 9:5), but this interpretation is not chronologically consistent with the three possible occasions of revelation for this verse, which all involve conditions resulting from conflict with the Jews, who were banished by force. This shows, as do many other verses in the Qur'an, that the fighting Muslims carried out was motivated by political circumstances and not the desire to convert.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

More important, coercion in religion negates the idea of responsibility before Allah (taklīf), in which one is taken to account for ones actions in both this life and the Hereafter (al-Rāzī). As an ethical or moral pronouncement, this verse is not subject to abrogation at all (see 2:106c). The Makkan idolaters were a special case, because they had been for years the primary threat to the very existence of Islam as a religion. Although forced conversions were not completely unknown in later Islamic history (including from Sunnism to Shiism and vice versa), they can be counted as rare exceptions to the historical rule.

For a more detailed treatment of these questions, see the essay Conquest and Conversion, War and Peace in the Qur'an.

**Mercy and warning held together**

Sound judgment translates rushd, which also means maturity and being rightly guided; error translates ghayy, which also carries the sense of transgression and temptation. False deities renders ṭāghūt, a word that seems to be related to the verb meaning to rebel and that is interpreted variously to refer to Satan, magicians, soothsayers, idols, or any being who rebels and trespasses against Allah (al-Rāzī); for a longer discussion, see 4:51–52c. The unfailing handhold (cf. 31:22) refers to either faith or the shahādah, There is no god but Allah (lā ilāha illaLlāh). The word for breaks refers to fractures that do not show or cause separation, meaning that the handhold does not give way even slightly or imperceptibly.

**The deed before its final account**

The verse's reference to compulsion widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:256 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:256: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if compulsion were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:256 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:256 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:256.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:256 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving compulsion; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating compulsion from the rest of life. In 2:256, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about compulsion. They are the place in which assent to 2:256 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:256 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: compulsion is joined to stands, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:256 gives the reader a useful order of questions about compulsion. What does the term compulsion mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:256? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:256 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:256.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:256. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:256 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between compulsion and stands.

In 2:256, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of compulsion while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:256 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
