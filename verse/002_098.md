## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:98

> **Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Law of Alignment**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings whoever and messengers into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:98 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:98 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The structure of this verse does not mean that Gabriel and Michael are other than angels; for example, 55:68 mentions dates and pomegranates after mentioning fruit. Moreover, angels are sometimes referred to as messengers as well (e.g., 10:21; 11:77). In connection with the warning in this verse, commentators often mention ḥadīth narratives with similar import, such as Whoever is an enemy of Allahs friend (walī), Allah makes war on him.

**Pick Neither Angels Nor Prophets**

The mention of whoever must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if whoever were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:98 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:98 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:98.

**Allah's Enmity as the Last Dread**

The relation between whoever and messengers also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:98 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:98 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:98 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:98 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

The choice of the word whoever in 2:98 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:98 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:98, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches whoever from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:98 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:98 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

There is also a distinction between understanding whoever and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:98. The verse 2:98 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:98 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands messengers, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:98.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:98 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving whoever; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating whoever from the rest of life. In 2:98, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about whoever. They are the place in which assent to 2:98 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

The most balanced response to 2:98 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: whoever is joined to messengers, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:98 gives the reader a useful order of questions about whoever. What does the term whoever mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:98? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:98 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:98.

**How the early community understood it**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:98. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:98 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between whoever and messengers.

In 2:98, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of whoever while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:98 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Power restrained by accountability**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:98 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for whoever, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:98, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:98, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:98, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:98 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:98 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:98, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine whoever until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:98 returns to responsibility. No explanation of whoever is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:98 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
