## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:167

> **The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.” And so Allah will make them remorseful of their misdeeds. And they will never ˹be able to˺ leave the Fire.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"If We Had Another Turn"**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between misled and chance. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:167. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:167. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:167.

**Regret Shown, Not Felt Only**

The turn here is the chance to return to the world. The wrongdoers regret is about having done sinful deeds or having failed to perform righteous ones; they are seeing what they would have had in the Hereafter if they had acted righteously and are tormented on account of it. According to another interpretation, they will see the houses of the Garden and wish for them, while the inhabitants of the Garden will see them and wish for Allah to bless them (al-Ṭabarī). Elsewhere it is said, Then on the Day of Resurrection you will disown one another, and you will curse one another (29:25).

**Remedy for the Living**

The conversations that take place between the denizens of Hell are also described in 7:38–39, and Satan tells them on the Day of Judgment, Verily Allah made you the Promise of truth; and I made you a promise, but I failed you (14:22). Al-Ṭabarī understands the last part of the verse to mean that their regret and desire for another chance at life will not remove them from the Fire, and for him this is evidence that the disbelievers will be in the Fire everlastingly.

The verse's reference to misled widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:167 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:167: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The standard in 2:167 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from misled: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between misled and chance also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:167 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:167 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:167 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:167 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The final emphasis of 2:167 returns to responsibility. No explanation of misled is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:167 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word misled in 2:167 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:167 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:167, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The exegetical tradition approaches misled from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:167 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:167 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding misled and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:167. The verse 2:167 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The deed before its final account**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:167 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands chance, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:167.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:167 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving misled; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating misled from the rest of life. In 2:167, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about misled. They are the place in which assent to 2:167 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:167 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: misled is joined to chance, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The wording of 2:167 gives the reader a useful order of questions about misled. What does the term misled mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:167? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:167 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:167.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:167. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:167 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between misled and chance.

In 2:167, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of misled while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:167 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:167 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for misled, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:167, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
