## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:159

> **Those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed— after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be condemned by Allah and ˹all˺ those who condemn.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Crime of Concealing Clear Proof**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between hide and guidance. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:159. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:159. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:159.

**Whose Curse Is Meant?**

This verse can either have a general import or refer specifically to the People of the Book around the Prophet (al-Rāzī). According to the former interpretation, it is sinful for any person to obscure a truth of religion; according to the latter interpretation, some Muslims asked a group among the People of the Book to point out to them the Prophet described in their Book, but they refused or concealed it (al-Rāzī). It can also mean that the People of the Book conceal the truth of Islam itself, by undercutting it, calling it false, and failing to make known the foretelling of the coming of the Prophet (al-Ṭabarī). The idea of concealing the truth of revelation is also addressed in v. 174; 3:187; 5:15.

**The Condemnation Without Exclusion**

Some say that the cursers in this verse are the beasts of the earth, while others mention that they are the angels and the believers, as in v. 161 (al-Ṭabarī). Others also mention the possibility that the denizens of Hell, who blame them for their own state, will also curse them (al-Rāzī).

The verse describes hide not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:159, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:159, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The standard in 2:159 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from hide: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The relation between hide and guidance also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:159 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:159 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:159 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:159 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating hide from the rest of life. In 2:159, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about hide. They are the place in which assent to 2:159 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:159 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: hide is joined to guidance, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The wording of 2:159 gives the reader a useful order of questions about hide. What does the term hide mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:159? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:159 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:159.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:159. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:159 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between hide and guidance.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:159, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of hide while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:159 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:159 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for hide, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:159, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

In 2:159, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:159, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:159 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:159 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:159, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine hide until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:159 returns to responsibility. No explanation of hide is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:159 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word hide in 2:159 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:159 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:159, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches hide from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:159 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:159 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding hide and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:159. The verse 2:159 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
