## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:165

> **Still there are some who take others as Allah’s equal—they love them as they should love Allah—but the ˹true˺ believers love Allah even more. If only the wrongdoers could see the ˹horrible˺ punishment ˹awaiting them˺, they would certainly realize that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is indeed severe in punishment.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Rivals in the Love-Market**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between still and allahs. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:165. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:165. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:165.

**"Those Who Believe Love Allah More"**

Equals has the sense of competitors (al-Rāzī), as in v. 22. An alternate reading of the last sentence is, “If thou sawest those who do wrong when they see the Punishment of Allah, [thou wouldst see] that power belongs wholly to Allah.” Loving them like loving Allah means they love their idols as the believers love Allah or that they obey their leaders as they should obey Allah (al-Ṭabarī). It may also mean that they love these equals as they themselves love Allah (thus dividing their love between Allah and the idols), or that they love them as they ought to love Allah, or that they glorify and obey their idols or their own human leaders or other human agencies as they should Allah (al-Rāzī).

**The Sight on the Day It Burned**

These interpretations are all possible because of the wording of the verse, which can be literally rendered, “They love them like unto the love of Allah.” The believers’ love is more ardent because it is directed undividedly at the one true object of love, unlike that of the idolaters, who direct love toward idols during ordinary times but then toward Allah in times of need, as when they call out when on a ship buffeted by waves (al-Rāzī).

The idea of the love (ḥubb) for Allah by human beings appears in this verse and in 3:31: Say, If you love Allah, follow me; 5:54: Allah will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him; and 9:24: Say, If your (family and possessions) are more beloved to you than Allah, . . . then wait till Allah comes with His Command. According to a ḥadīth, a man asked the Prophet, When will the Hour come to pass, O Messenger of Allah? The Prophet said, What have you prepared for it?

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The man said, I have not prepared for it much by way of prayers or fasting or alms, but I love Allah and His Messenger. The Prophet said, You will be with those whom you love. More often, however, dozens of times in fact, the word love (ḥubb) is used in the Qur'an to describe what Allah loves or does not love in human beings. For example, throughout the Qur'an Allah is said to love the virtuous, those who repent, the reverent, the patient, those who trust, the just, those who fight in His way, and those who purify themselves, but He does not love disbelievers, transgressors, sinful ingrates, wrongdoers, the vainglorious, workers of corruption, prodigals, the treacherous, or the exultant.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

Many commentators use this verse as an occasion to discuss the nature of ḥubb (and the related term maḥabbah), which is the word here rendered as love. The English word love has a broader range of meaning than the Arabic ḥubb, and several Arabic concepts correspond to love depending on its usage. These include raḥmah (most often translated mercy or compassion) and mawaddah, as in a verse describing the sentiments between spouses: He established affection (mawaddah) and mercy (raḥmah) between you (30:21). The Qur'an, the Sunnah, and the mainstream exoteric exegetical tradition do not treat love as a technical or esoteric concept (although the Islamic intellectual, spiritual, and poetical tradition is replete with discussions of the reality and experience of love).

**Mercy and warning held together**

Rather, they accept it as a self-evident reality whose basic meaning is known, while acknowledging the difficulty of explaining how love, and indeed any concept ordinarily used for created objects, can correctly be applied to Allah. The asymmetry between Allahs Love for human beings and human beings love for Allah is reflected in the Qurans almost exclusive discussion of that love as originating with Allah. Because Allah is unlike anything in the world, love for Him is hard to encapsulate in a technical definition, and the exegetes often prefer to describe it by speaking in terms of the fruits of loving Allah.

For example, when we love someone, we long to meet them, miss them and yearn for them when they are not there, do everything we do for their sake, seek to do what makes them happy, and love what they love (Aj). These are all symptoms, as it were, of love.

**The deed before its final account**

In the Islamic spiritual tradition, love is usually spoken of in light of knowledge, which is to say that one loves Allah to the degree that one knows Him, and vice versa. Al-Rāzī says, for example, that when we see the face of the beloved, we want to see the hair, which in theological terms means that even a slight familiarity with Allah will arouse a virtuous desire to know Him even more, and this desire is itself a sign of love. In human life, the lover seeks to banish from the heart all but the beloved and sees all else as a kind of distraction; and among the spiritual wayfarers of Islam this is manifested in a discipline whose method and goal are the constant remembrance of Allah (dhikr Allāh); also see the essay “The Qur'an and Sufism.”

**Why the promise or threat is just**

It is typical for commentators to discuss two kinds of love: One is called “desirous love” or ḥubb al-hawā, which is when we love a thing because of what it does for us. The other kind is love for the sake of the beloved, which arises for no other reason than that the beloved’s beauty, goodness, and perfection kindle love within the lover. More precisely, human beings love, or should love, Allah, but this love should not be seen as a means to something else (al-Rāzī). The first kind of love is said to be incumbent upon all believers in that one can see glorification, acts of obedience, and righteous behavior as means to the peace and happiness that Allah will provide as a reward for them. Lovers in the higher sense love Allah for Allah alone and, because of this love, want to perform those acts that Allah loves, not because of what they will gain; for them diverting oneself from what the beloved loves is more difficult than working for one’s selfish ends.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

At the lower level, we love what is good to us, and Allah’s Goodness to us is everywhere. At the higher level, we love the beauty and goodness of what we know, and Allah’s Beauty and Goodness are the object of our love to the degree that we come to know them; and in the case of Allah the perfections and beauties are endless, and so love for Him is potentially limitless. Sufis have written much about the distinction between what they call “metaphorical,” or majāzī, love and “true,” or ḥaqīqī, love; the former refers to such things as romantic love and love for the things of the world, and the latter refers to love of Allah alone and by extension the love of other beings through the love of Allah.

An idea closely related to love of Allah is love “in” or “through” Allah (al-ḥubb fī’Llāh), typically rendered “love for the sake of Allah”; a similar concept is “loving in Allah’s Glory” (bi-jalāl Allāh). A ḥadīth describes Allah saying, “Those who loved each other in My Glory shall have pulpits (minbars) of light, and the prophets and saints will envy them.” In another ḥadīth He said, “Where are those who loved one another for My Glory? Today I shall shade them in My shade, on the Day when there is no shade but Mine.” The Prophet also said, “Allah says, ‘My Love is realized for those who love one another for My sake, who are honest with one another for My sake, who give charity to one another for My sake, and who visit one another for My sake.’”

The verse's reference to still widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:165 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:165: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:165 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from still: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.
