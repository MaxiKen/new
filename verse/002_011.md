## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:11

> **When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Scene and Its Timeless Script**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between spread and reply. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:11. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:11. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:11.

**Fasād and Iṣlāḥ: The Book's Master-Pair**

To spread or create corruption (fasād) is a major theme in the Qur'an, the opposite of which is often setting things aright or making amends (iṣlāḥ); see 30:41c. The identity of the speaker of Do not work corruption . . . is open and could be the Prophet himself, a group of believers, or perhaps even some victim of corruption who says this phrase to the corrupters in rebuke (al-Rāzī). Ibn ʿAbbās and others state that corruption upon the earth (or alternately corruption in the land) refers to open disobedience against Allah. It can also be seen as the result of such disobedience (47:22). One interpretation, attributed to the prominent Companion Salmān al-Fārsī, states that the people to whom this passage refers have not yet come (al-Ṭabarī), an explanation that the commentators accept in the sense that this verse does not restrict the descriptions of hypocrisy and iniquity to the Prophets contemporaries. Some contemporary Muslims interpret the destruction of the natural environment to be one of the central meanings of these verses.

**The Reformer's Mask and the Test of the Claim**

To work righteousness (muṣliḥ) means to set things right or put them in a state of righteousness, but is not to be confused with “reform” in the modern sense. The response we are only working righteousness can mean either that they believed that they were actually doing so and their own practice of religion was correct, or that they were acting in their self-interest in trying to bridge the gap between believers and the disbelievers (al-Rāzī). According to this latter interpretation, their lukewarm (at best) commitment, rather than being a fault, makes them peacemakers between the two warring sides, offering compromise rather than conflict.

The mention of spread gives the verse a definite centre. Its meaning in 2:11 is not a general meditation on religion but an explanation of what this word requires in the situation named here. The passage at 2:11 moves between belief, action, and consequence, so a sound reading must preserve all three rather than allowing one attractive phrase to swallow the rest.

**Why the Answer Is Given at All**

The standard in 2:11 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from spread: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A Word to Every Reformer**

The relation between spread and reply also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:11 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:11 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:11 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:11 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:11, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:11, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:11 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The verse in its immediate setting**

A reader who finds the command in 2:11 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:11, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine spread until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:11 returns to responsibility. No explanation of spread is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:11 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The central term and its range**

The choice of the word spread in 2:11 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:11 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:11, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches spread from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:11 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:11 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**What the early commentators preserve**

There is also a distinction between understanding spread and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:11. The verse 2:11 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:11 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands reply, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:11.

**The reasoning within the wording**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:11 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving spread; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating spread from the rest of life. In 2:11, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about spread. They are the place in which assent to 2:11 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:11 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: spread is joined to reply, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:11 gives the reader a useful order of questions about spread. What does the term spread mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:11? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:11 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:11.
