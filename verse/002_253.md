## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:253

> **We have chosen some of those messengers above others. Allah spoke directly to some, and raised some high in rank. To Jesus, son of Mary, We gave clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought ˹among themselves˺ after receiving the clear proofs. But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved. Yet if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another. But Allah does what He wills.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Ranks Among the Messengers**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between chosen and others. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:253. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:253. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:253.

**Jesus and the Holy Spirit**

This verse, which states that some prophets are favored above others, and others like it (3:163, ranked in degrees) are usually understood in light of others that command believers to say, We make no distinction between any of His messengers (v. 285; see also 2:136; 3:84). In Islamic belief this ranking in degrees is understood to pertain to the prophets missions, not their individual spiritual perfections. Thus, all prophets are considered equally to be protected from sin (maʿṣūm), but are given varying levels of earthly power (Solomon had a great earthly kingdom, Jesus did not), different kinds of miraculous signs (Moses staff, Solomons commanding the wind), and are sent to different segments of humanity (in Islamic belief the Prophet Muhammad was sent to all people, while other prophets were sent only to their own). To whom Allah spoke is usually considered to mean Moses specifically (see 7:143).

**Had Allah Willed — The Difference Theodicy**

Jesus strengthening with the Holy Spirit is also mentioned in v. 87 and 5:110; and in 16:102 the Holy Spirit is mentioned in connection with Muhammad. See 2:87c, where this Spirit is variously identified as the Spirit Allah breathed into human beings (15:29), the Archangel Gabriel, or the Gospel itself, all of which share the quality of being life-giving, whether it is bodily quickening or the nourishment of the heart and intelligence (al-Rāzī). The word holy, or sacred (qudus), can also mean pure.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

And had Allah so willed, they would not have fought one another echoes similar passages, such as And had Allah willed, He would have made you one community (5:48). The question of Allahs willing or not willing humanity to be united in one religion is also discussed in v. 213; 10:19; 11:118; 16:93; 42:8. Fighting after the clear proofs had come refers to the conflict between communities who have received Divine revelation; on this question see also 2:213c as well as 3:19; 42:14; 45:17; 98:1.

The verse's reference to chosen widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:253 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:253: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The standard in 2:253 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from chosen: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between chosen and others also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:253 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:253 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:253 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:253 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The exegetical tradition approaches chosen from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:253 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:253 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding chosen and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:253. The verse 2:253 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The deed before its final account**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:253 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands others, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:253.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:253 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving chosen; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating chosen from the rest of life. In 2:253, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about chosen. They are the place in which assent to 2:253 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:253 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: chosen is joined to others, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The wording of 2:253 gives the reader a useful order of questions about chosen. What does the term chosen mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:253? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:253 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:253.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:253. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:253 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between chosen and others.

In 2:253, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of chosen while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:253 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:253 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for chosen, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:253, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
