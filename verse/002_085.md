## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:85

> **But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Contradiction Exposed**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between killing and aiding. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:85. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:85. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:85.

**Believing in Part of the Book**

According to some, believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part refers to the contradiction between the initial expulsion, which would be a violation of the Book, and the ransom, which would be in accord with the Book (al-Ṭabarī). Others connect the disbelief in this verse with the prophecies foretelling the coming of the Prophet Muhammad, though this is not implied by the context (al-Rāzī). Here the wider sense of kufr, or “disbelief,” as rejection and denial of certain truths comes to the fore, as the lack of belief and faith in part is not a direct disbelief in Allah’s existence. Rather, the expulsion and taking of ransom are acts of hypocrisy and immorality, which are not separable from a certain degree of kufr.

**The Checklist Turned Against the Selective Heart**

The verse describes killing not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:85, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:85, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

The standard in 2:85 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from killing: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between killing and aiding also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:85 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:85 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:85 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:85 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The exegetical tradition approaches killing from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:85 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:85 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding killing and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:85. The verse 2:85 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:85 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands aiding, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:85.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:85 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving killing; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating killing from the rest of life. In 2:85, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about killing. They are the place in which assent to 2:85 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:85 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: killing is joined to aiding, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The wording of 2:85 gives the reader a useful order of questions about killing. What does the term killing mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:85? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:85 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:85.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:85. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:85 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between killing and aiding.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

In 2:85, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of killing while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:85 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:85 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for killing, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:85, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:85, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:85, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:85 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:85 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:85, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine killing until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:85 returns to responsibility. No explanation of killing is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:85 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
