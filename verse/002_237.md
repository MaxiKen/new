## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:237

> **And if you divorce them before consummating the marriage but after deciding on a dowry, pay half of the dowry, unless the wife graciously waives it or the husband graciously pays in full. Graciousness is closer to righteousness. And do not forget kindness among yourselves. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Consummation Half Measure**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between divorce and deciding. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:237. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:237. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:237.

**"To Forgo Is Nearer to Reverence"**

He whose hand holds the marriage tie is the husband himself, in which case he would forgo the half of the bridal payment that he could legitimately expect to get back . In this case “holding the marriage tie” refers to the husband’s prerogative of divorce (see v. 228), since it is he who is exercising his power to divorce his wife; this verse refers to divorce declared by the husband (ṭalāq), not khulʿ divorce (see v. 229; R). The party nearer to reverence would then be the one who forgoes one’s “half” and allows the other party to have the entirety of the bridewealth after the divorce, thus realizing the virtue of bounteousness among yourselves .

**"Forget Not Bounteousness Among Yourselves"**

Others interpret the holder of the marriage tie to be the legal guardian (wakīl) whose permission is usually necessary for a woman to marry, in which case the wife would, with the guardian’s approval, forgo the half of the bridewealth she had been entitled to keep. The difficulty with this position is that, although the guardian is necessary for marriage, he has no legal control over the bridewealth, which is the personal property of the wife (al-Qurṭubī).

There is an opinion that consummation is not necessary for a man to lose his right to half the bridewealth, and that if the husband is secluded with his wife in such a way that consummation was possible, he must pay the bridewealth in full .

**A household placed under moral care**

The reference to divorce places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

The standard in 2:237 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from divorce: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The relation between divorce and deciding also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:237 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:237 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:237 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:237 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The final emphasis of 2:237 returns to responsibility. No explanation of divorce is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:237 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The choice of the word divorce in 2:237 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:237 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:237, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches divorce from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:237 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:237 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The difference between power and justice**

There is also a distinction between understanding divorce and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:237. The verse 2:237 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:237 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands deciding, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:237.

**How the jurists read the command**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:237 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving divorce; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating divorce from the rest of life. In 2:237, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about divorce. They are the place in which assent to 2:237 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Protection from private harm**

The most balanced response to 2:237 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: divorce is joined to deciding, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:237 gives the reader a useful order of questions about divorce. What does the term divorce mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:237? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:237 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:237.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:237. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:237 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between divorce and deciding.

In 2:237, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of divorce while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:237 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
