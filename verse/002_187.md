## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:187

> **It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them. Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. ˹You may˺ eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not exceed them. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people, so they may become mindful ˹of Him˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Alleviation Night**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between permissible and during. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:187. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:187. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:187.

**The Garment of Marriage**

Many commentators report that before the revelation of this verse, Muslims would fast after sleeping; after sunset, when one broke the fast, one could eat until the ʿishāʾ prayer (i.e., the end of twilight and the beginning of night), but that sleep would begin the next day’s fast. Upon waking, one would be in a state of fasting. Some maintain that the Muslim community would abstain from sexual activity for the whole of Ramadan, even during the night, though the basis of this account is unclear. This verse is considered an alleviation of those requirements.

**The White and Black Threads of Dawn**

It is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, the prominent Companion and second Caliph, confessed to having had sexual relations with his wife after she said she had fallen asleep; he was remorseful about it and sought recourse. Commentators also mention Qays ibn Ṣirmah, who came home after a harsh day of work while he was fasting. His wife did not have food for him and went out to get some, but he could not stay awake and was asleep before she returned, so he continued to fast through the next day (al-Qurṭubī).

Seek what Allah has prescribed can mean seek to have children; seek to fulfill what Qur'an commands you; seek the Night of Power, meaning a night during Ramadan when the Qur'an was first revealed (see 97:1); seek exemption and latitude when it is offered in matters of ritual; or seek to have sexual relations in a legitimate way (Ibn Kathīr).

**"Those Are the Limits of Allah"**

The white thread and the black thread refer to dawn and the night, respectively; “the white thread” is an idiom for the day (al-Qurṭubī). Various aḥādīth describe the level of light that indicates the start of daybreak and ends the night, whether the indication is seeing light in the sky itself or the illumination of the tops of mountains (Ibn Kathīr).

Retreat in the mosques refers to the practice of iʿtikāf, during which a person remains in the mosque for a number of days in a state of prayer and remembrance (dhikr). According to many reports the Prophet would spend the last ten days of Ramadan in the mosque in iʿtikāf; during his final Ramadan he spent twenty.

**A household placed under moral care**

The reference to permissible places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

The standard in 2:187 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from permissible: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The relation between permissible and during also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:187 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:187 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:187 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:187 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating permissible from the rest of life. In 2:187, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about permissible. They are the place in which assent to 2:187 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:187 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: permissible is joined to during, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The difference between power and justice**

The wording of 2:187 gives the reader a useful order of questions about permissible. What does the term permissible mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:187? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:187 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:187.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:187. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:187 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between permissible and during.

**How the jurists read the command**

In 2:187, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of permissible while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:187 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:187 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for permissible, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:187, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:187, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:187, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:187 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:187 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:187, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine permissible until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:187 returns to responsibility. No explanation of permissible is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:187 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
