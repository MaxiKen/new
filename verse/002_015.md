## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:15

> **Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Mockery Returned**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between throw and leaving. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:15. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:15. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:15.

**Rope: The Long Extension of Ṭughyān**

The issue of whether mockery or ridicule (cf. 9:79) is properly attributable to Allah is addressed by commentators. Some point out that the disbelievers bring ridicule upon themselves as recompense, not that it originates in Allah (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī); others say that the disgrace and ignominy with which Allah afflicts disbelievers and in which He leaves them to continue for a long time is tantamount to mockery (al-Rāzī). Wander confused in their rebellion also appears in 6:110; 7:186; 10:11; 23:75. Rebellion (Ṭughyān) is related to the verb ṭaghā, which has the sense of trespassing beyond a limit, and is attributed to disbelievers in general and to Pharaoh in particular (20:24; 79:17).

The verse's reference to throw widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:15 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:15: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**Wandering Confused: The Self-Portrait of the Directionless**

The standard in 2:15 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from throw: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between throw and leaving also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:15 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:15 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:15 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:15 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Justice Deferred and the Believer's Equanimity**

The exegetical tradition approaches throw from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:15 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:15 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding throw and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:15. The verse 2:15 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:15 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands leaving, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:15.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:15 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving throw; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating throw from the rest of life. In 2:15, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about throw. They are the place in which assent to 2:15 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:15 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: throw is joined to leaving, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The wording of 2:15 gives the reader a useful order of questions about throw. What does the term throw mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:15? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:15 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:15.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:15. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:15 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between throw and leaving.

**The deed before its final account**

In 2:15, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of throw while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:15 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:15 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for throw, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:15, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:15, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:15, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:15 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:15 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:15, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine throw until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:15 returns to responsibility. No explanation of throw is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:15 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word throw in 2:15 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:15 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:15, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
