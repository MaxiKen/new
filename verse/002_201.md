## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:201

> **Yet there are others who say, “Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Two-Worlds Duʿāʾ**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between others and world. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:201. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:201. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:201.

**The Prophet's Own Duʿāʾ Habit**

It is reported that one of the most often repeated supplications of the Prophet was Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the Fire! (Ibn Kathīr) This supplication is also frequently repeated by Muslims in their individual prayers and appears often in books of prayer. Good in this verse renders ḥasanah, which like many other words derived from the root ḥ-s-n has the sense of that which is beautiful. See also 7:156: And prescribe good for us in the life of this world, and in the Hereafter; 16:30: For those who are virtuous in this world, there shall be good, and the Abode of the Hereafter is better; 16:122: And We granted him good in this world, and surely in the Hereafter he shall be among the righteous. A similar message is given in 42:20: And whosoever desires the harvest of this world, We shall give him some thereof, but he will have no share in the Hereafter.

**A Prayer for Every Age**

The verse's reference to others widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:201 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:201: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:201 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from others: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The relation between others and world also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:201 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:201 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:201 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:201 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating others from the rest of life. In 2:201, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about others. They are the place in which assent to 2:201 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:201 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: others is joined to world, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The wording of 2:201 gives the reader a useful order of questions about others. What does the term others mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:201? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:201 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:201.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:201. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:201 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between others and world.

**The deed before its final account**

In 2:201, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of others while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:201 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:201 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for others, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:201, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:201, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:201, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:201 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:201 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:201, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine others until the demand disappears.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The final emphasis of 2:201 returns to responsibility. No explanation of others is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:201 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word others in 2:201 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:201 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:201, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches others from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:201 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:201 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding others and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:201. The verse 2:201 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
