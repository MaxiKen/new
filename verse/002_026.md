## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:26

> **Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Divine Freedom to Illustrate through the Small**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings away and mosquito into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:26 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:26 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

It is said that this verse was revealed either when the hypocrites objected that Allah would not employ such parables—namely, those directed against them in vv. 17–20; or when a group of Jews scoffed at the notion that Allah would use a fly (22:73) or spider (29:41) in a parable; or when idolaters brought these same objections. Or something smaller (fawqahā), which is often understood literally to mean above that, can also be understood as something beyond that in its lowliness (al-Rāzī).

**Why a Gnat? The Greatness of the Negligible**

The verse implies that the very same message can both guide and mislead, which is something rather different from simply being ignored. According to this understanding, by being denied the parable (and presumably other Divine teachings) is not neutralized for disbelievers, but places them farther astray. This echoes the previous verses in which Allah seals the hearts and perception of disbelievers. Being iniquitous (fāsiq) makes the truth for the denier into something other than the truth, which is to say that one’s moral quality decides, at least in part, one’s level of understanding. Those who are misled and labeled the iniquitous here are generally considered to be the hypocrites (al-Ṭabarī).

The verse's reference to away widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:26 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:26: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**One Parable, Two Harvests**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if away were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:26 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:26 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:26.

**"He Misleads None but the Iniquitous": The Boundary of Misguidance**

The relation between away and mosquito also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:26 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:26 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:26 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:26 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:26 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:26, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine away until the demand disappears.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The final emphasis of 2:26 returns to responsibility. No explanation of away is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:26 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word away in 2:26 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:26 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:26, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The exegetical tradition approaches away from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:26 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:26 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding away and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:26. The verse 2:26 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:26 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands mosquito, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:26.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:26 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving away; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The deed before its final account**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating away from the rest of life. In 2:26, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about away. They are the place in which assent to 2:26 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:26 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: away is joined to mosquito, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The wording of 2:26 gives the reader a useful order of questions about away. What does the term away mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:26? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:26 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:26.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:26. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:26 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between away and mosquito.

In 2:26, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of away while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:26 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
