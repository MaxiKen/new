## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:80

> **˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Comforting Calculation**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings jews and touch into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:80 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:80 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Have You Taken a Covenant from Allah?"**

This verse should be read together with the subsequent two verses. This claim of limited punishment is repeated in 3:24. Some commentators mention that the Jews claimed that they would suffer torment for only forty nights, corresponding to the time spent worshipping the calf, after which they would be succeeded by another people (according to some, they claimed that these inheritors of punishment would be the followers of Muhammad); others say it would be forty years; still others, only seven days, one day for each thousand years of the worlds existence (Ibn Kathīr). A covenant is often mentioned in connection with the Israelites (e.g., 2:40, 100) and more generally elsewhere between Allah and humanity (3:77, 81; 7:172; 13:25; 33:7; 36:60). Here it is invoked against the claims that Hell would be experienced by the Jews only for a specific number of days, which the commentators point out are claims made without any warrant from scripture.

**The Doctrine the Verse Guards**

The question of a limited sojourn in Hell is extremely significant, as the main body of Islamic theology accepts the possibility of believers being removed from Hell (see 4:40c; 78:23c), a position adduced from passages such as 6:128; 11:107; and 78:23, but also from aḥādīth that describe intercession on behalf of the denizens of Hell that would allow them to leave it after a period of time (see 2:255c; 57:13c).

Al-Rāzī makes an argument regarding limited punishment in Hell based on three main points: First, he argues that v. 80 is not necessarily a denial that souls might reside in Hell only temporarily, but may be seen as a rebuke against the claim of such an exceedingly small number of days coupled with the impudent confidence in that figure in the absence of an explicit promise from Allah. Second, one cannot assume such forgiveness for all, but this does not prevent the possibility that an individual person may be forgiven after a time in Hell.

**Writing trust into a transaction**

Third, there is a difference between saying that Allah will not remove a believer from Hell and saying that Allah never promised (or made a Covenant) to remove a believer from Hell, since Allah can do something without having explicitly promised to do so. The absence of such a promise does not prevent Allah from in fact removing a person from Hell after a period of time. For more on the question of the perpetuity or eternity of Hell, see 11:106–8c; 78:23c; and the essay Death, Dying, and the Afterlife in the Qur'an.

The language of jews places memory and responsibility alongside material exchange. People forget, die, disagree, and understand conversations differently; a careful record does not accuse every party of dishonesty, but protects the honest party from the consequences of uncertainty. The verse therefore treats clarity as a form of mercy, not as a sign that trust has disappeared.

**Why clarity protects both sides**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if jews were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:80 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:80 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:80.

**The vulnerable party in the wording**

The relation between jews and touch also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:80 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:80 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:80 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:80 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:80 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for jews, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:80, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Witnesses, records, and memory**

In 2:80, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:80, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:80 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:80 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:80, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine jews until the demand disappears.

**The ethics of consent**

The final emphasis of 2:80 returns to responsibility. No explanation of jews is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:80 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word jews in 2:80 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:80 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:80, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A practical rule against later conflict**

The exegetical tradition approaches jews from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:80 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:80 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding jews and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:80. The verse 2:80 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:80 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands touch, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:80.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:80 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving jews; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
