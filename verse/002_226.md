## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:226

> **Those who swear not to have intercourse with their wives must wait for four months. If they change their mind, then Allah is certainly AllForgiving, Most Merciful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Oath That Imprisons a Woman**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings swear and wait into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:226 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:226 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Clock's Expiry Forces a Choice**

These verses address the practice of a man staying away from his wife but not divorcing her, leaving her without the sexual benefits of marriage and without the freedom to marry another, which in pre-Islamic times could last years . In this context to forswear means to make an oath that one would not have sexual relations with one’s wife. To forswear in this sense is not the same as divorce, and the period of four months sets an upper limit on this abstention within marriage. Specifically, it is understood to draw a line beyond which the abstention is taken as an intent to cause harm to the wife (al-Rāzī).

**A Mercy for the One Wielded Against**

If they return means that they resume intercourse; the return is still valid if one intends to return to one’s wife, but is hindered for some reason, as the intent to cause harm no longer exists, while for some it simply means that one bear witness that he has returned (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). Many understand Allah is Forgiving, Merciful in this context to indicate that no expiation is necessary if they return (al-Ṭabarī), though others believe some form of expiation, such as feeding the poor, is necessary, as returning to one’s wife would then constitute going back on the oath of staying away from her (al-Ṭabarī).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Some say that these stipulations only apply when the oath is made in anger with the intent to cause harm, and that when someone swears not to have sexual relations for some other reason, for example, with the intention of protecting the well-being of a nursing child, the ruling of this verse does not apply . Others say that the forswearing mentioned here can refer to any oath that keeps one from having sexual relations with one’s wife, while others say that the forswearing need not even pertain to sexual relations, but may pertain to any matter where the intent is to cause harm to the wife, such as not speaking to her or touching her (al-Ṭabarī). Perhaps the most serious point of disagreement is whether, upon the completion of the four months, divorce is effected automatically in the eyes of the Law or whether other steps must be taken. Some maintain that after the period of four months, divorce could be imposed by a judge at the request of the wife (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī).

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to swear widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:226 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:226: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if swear were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:226 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:226 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:226.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between swear and wait also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:226 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:226 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:226 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:226 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

There is also a distinction between understanding swear and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:226. The verse 2:226 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The deed before its final account**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:226 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands wait, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:226.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:226 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving swear; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating swear from the rest of life. In 2:226, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about swear. They are the place in which assent to 2:226 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:226 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: swear is joined to wait, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The wording of 2:226 gives the reader a useful order of questions about swear. What does the term swear mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:226? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:226 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:226.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:226. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:226 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between swear and wait.

In 2:226, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of swear while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:226 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:226 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for swear, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:226, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
