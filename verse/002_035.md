## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:35

> **We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please, but do not approach this tree, or else you will be wrongdoers.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Home and Its One Boundary**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between cautioned and wife. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:35. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:35. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:35.

**"His Wife": Companionship by Creation**

The story of the fall of Adam and Eve from Paradise appears here and in 7:19–25 and 20:120–23. Relative to Genesis 2–3, the Qur'anic account gives fewer details. Some commentators mention an array of details about Adam, Eve, the Garden, and the fall, many of which find their origin in Jewish and Christian, and perhaps even Arab, oral traditions. Many of these traditions describe Eve (Ḥawwāʾ), who is referred to as Adams wife rather than Eve in the Qur'an; the kind of tree or plant from which Adam and Eve ate (apple vs. some type of grain); what form Iblīs took; and even where they were first exiled. But some of these details are not attested to very strongly in the Ḥadīth, and some (such as some registered by al-Ṭabarī) do not rise above the level of folklore. The Qur'an itself provides the foundation and details of the story of Adam and Eve, which has a different meaning in Islam than in Judaism or Christianity.

**Of the Garden's Where and the Approach Clause**

The Garden in which Adam and Eve dwelled is indeed paradisal; they would neither hunger therein, nor go naked, . . . neither thirst therein, nor suffer from the heat of the sun (20:118–19), in addition to experiencing the unconstrained enjoyment of the Gardens fruits, as mentioned in this verse. Eating from the tree, according to Iblīs, would have made them angels, or able to live forever (7:20), and in 20:120 he promises them the Tree of Everlastingness and a kingdom that never decays. Among the possibilities offered for the type of tree are grain, grapevine, and fig. (The Qur'an never uses the word fruit in this context, but says only that they ate from the shajarah, a word that can include also vegetative growth such as trees, shrubs, and bushes.)

**A household placed under moral care**

One view (al-Rāzī) has it that the promise of eternal life mentioned in 7:20 and 20:120 would have had no attraction if Adam were already dwelling in the Garden of Rewards, which the Qur'an promises to believers. The command to get . . . down (v. 36) is the same verb used in v. 61 (Go down to a town . . .), where it does not imply a vertical descent, but a change in location. Moreover, those who enter the Garden will not be expelled therefrom (15:48). Also, if Adam were created from earth, then where is the ascent from earth to Heaven in this account? The commentator al-Qurṭubī (who is not of this opinion) also mentions that the Qur'an says of the Garden, They hear therein neither idle talk nor lying (78:35), and yet Iblīs did lie to them, which is indeed how he caused them to be expelled from it. These considerations would seem to indicate that this is a different Garden from the Garden of Rewards.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The consensus view, though, is that the Garden of Adam and the promised Garden are the very same (al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī). It is not “a” garden, but “the” Garden (al-jannah), “well known” as the eternal Garden promised to believers. Some respond to the opinion that Iblīs could not have lied in the Garden by arguing that he could have whispered to them from somewhere outside the Garden, and that the promise never to be expelled applies only to those granted entry to Paradise after their sojourn on earth as a reward for their good deeds (Ṭs). Surveying these options, al-Rāzī offers this final possibility: “These are all possible, and the textual proofs are weak and contradictory; so one should cease, and refrain from being categorical. And Allah knows best.” On this and similar issues (such as Eve’s creation and entry in the Garden) he takes a similar attitude, that one cannot know for sure, and one does not need to know, as this is not central to the Qur'anic telling of the account.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The reference to cautioned places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

The standard in 2:35 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from cautioned: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The difference between power and justice**

The wording of 2:35 gives the reader a useful order of questions about cautioned. What does the term cautioned mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:35? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:35 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:35.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:35. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:35 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between cautioned and wife.

**How the jurists read the command**

In 2:35, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of cautioned while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:35 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:35 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for cautioned, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:35, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Protection from private harm**

In 2:35, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:35, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:35 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:35 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:35, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine cautioned until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:35 returns to responsibility. No explanation of cautioned is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:35 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
