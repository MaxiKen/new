## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:69

> **They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow—pleasant to see.’”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Second Question: The Interrogation Continues**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between call and colour. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:69. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:69. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:69.

**The Yellow Cow: Beauty Specified**

These verses contain an account of an encounter between Moses and the Jews that is rather detailed in comparison to other Qur'anic accounts. First the Israelites balk at Allahs Command, then interrogate Moses on its particulars, and finally obey it. Cf. Numbers 19, where a ceremony involving a red cow is mentioned. The instructions given the Israelites there, similar to those given in 2:71, are that it is to be a cow without defect, in which there is no blemish and on which no yoke has been laid (19:2). The seemingly impertinent questioning of the Israelites is not part of the Biblical account.

**Ibn ʿAjībah: The Beauty of the Purified Soul**

One account of the background story is that a rich man was secretly murdered by his heir. Moses, as the prophet of Allah, was asked to learn the identity of the killer. After the debate over the cow, the people finally slaughtered it, took a limb from it, and struck the corpse, which then quickened and identified the killer. This would continue the story of the cow up to v. 73. To be ignorant (jāhil) in the Qur'an is more than to have a lack of knowledge; it is a disposition against it, a kind of pathological adherence to ones own way and ones ignorance.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The term could also be rendered ignoramus. Unlike the case of the blind man seeking knowledge from the Prophet (see 80:1–4), the ignoramus is deliberately ignorant and in the dark. Similar instances in the Qur'an show the enmity or vice inherent in the Qur'anic concept of ignorance: Had Allah willed, He would have gathered them all to guidance—so be not among the ignorant (6:35); I exhort thee, lest thou be among the ignorant (11:46); If Thou dost not turn their scheming away from me, I shall incline toward them and be among the ignorant (12:33).

It is also related to the concept of the pre-Islamic Days of Ignorance, called the jāhiliyyah.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

Now you have brought the truth means “now you have made it clear to us” (al-Ṭabarī). That they almost did not was because the cow was so costly (according to the story recounted by al-Ṭabarī, there was only one such cow, and it was owned by an old woman who was asking a very high price for it); or because they did not want the identity of the killer to come to light because of the potential scandal. The Prophet said, “Had they taken the nearest cow and sacrificed it, it would have been enough for them.”

**Mercy and warning held together**

In Ibn ʿAjībah’s esoteric commentary on this verse, the egotistical soul must be killed in order for the spiritual soul to live. The best time for a soul to inflict the knife of asceticism and poverty upon itself is when it is neither too old, when its habits are ingrained and change becomes more difficult, nor too young (v. 68), when it feels immortal and sees no need to change. It is a soul that does not desire the world (not broken to plow the earth) and is pure of the blemishes that bind it to the world (v. 71). The soul is beautiful if it is good (v. 69). It is only when the soul has been purified and made beautiful and severed its inner attachment to the world that it is worthy to be sacrificed to Allah.

**The deed before its final account**

The verse's reference to call widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:69 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:69: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:69 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from call: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The final emphasis of 2:69 returns to responsibility. No explanation of call is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:69 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word call in 2:69 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:69 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:69, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The exegetical tradition approaches call from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:69 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:69 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding call and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:69. The verse 2:69 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:69 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands colour, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:69.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:69 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving call; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating call from the rest of life. In 2:69, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about call. They are the place in which assent to 2:69 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:69 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: call is joined to colour, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
