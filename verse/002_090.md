## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:90

> **Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah’s revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Motive Named: Envy**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings miserable and souls into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:90 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:90 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Commerce of Envy and the Currency of Grace**

Cf. v. 102, where evil is that for which they sold their souls is repeated as a conclusion to the verse. The attitude criticized here is based on the Jews reported belief that a prophet should come only from the Children of Israel. According to the commentators, because they were jealous that this dignity should pass to the Arabs, they purchased the false comfort of tribal pride at the price of faith in the Prophet Muhammad . Grace renders faḍl, which can also mean bounty, favor, kindness, or superiority depending on the context.

**Wrath upon Wrath**

His servants may refer to all human beings who worship Allah, but also to all human beings and indeed to creation as a whole. In the Qur'an as in Islam in general, servant is often used as a term of praise (2:186, 207; 14:31; 15:42; 21:105; 25:63; 89:29) unless qualified (7:194; 36:30). They earn a burden (bāʾa) can also be rendered they became deserving. Some commentators interpret wrath upon wrath as two instances of Divine Wrath, identifying the causes variously as the worship of the calf, the failure to uphold certain parts of the Torah, the rejection of Jesus, or the rejection of Muhammad .

The wording around miserable assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

**The Believer's Inoculation Against Envy**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if miserable were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:90 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:90 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:90.

**The Book as an address**

The relation between miserable and souls also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:90 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:90 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:90 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:90 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:90 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: miserable is joined to souls, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The word that carries the argument**

The wording of 2:90 gives the reader a useful order of questions about miserable. What does the term miserable mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:90? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:90 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:90.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:90. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:90 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between miserable and souls.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

In 2:90, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of miserable while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:90 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:90 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for miserable, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:90, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The reader’s responsibility**

In 2:90, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:90, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:90 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:90 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:90, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine miserable until the demand disappears.

**Why the wording is exact**

The final emphasis of 2:90 returns to responsibility. No explanation of miserable is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:90 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word miserable in 2:90 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:90 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:90, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches miserable from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:90 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:90 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding miserable and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:90. The verse 2:90 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
