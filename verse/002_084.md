## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:84

> **And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Covenant of Blood and Home**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings took and shed into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:84 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:84 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Madinan Background**

For most commentators this verse continues the descriptions of previous Israelites (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī). The you in Then you ratified it could refer to the contemporary Israelites or to their forefathers. Bearing witness can mean a direct attestation or simply being present at the event as a group (al-Rāzī). In Madinah there were three Jewish tribes, Naḍīr, Qurayẓah, and Qaynuqāʿ, and two main Arab tribes, Aws and Khazraj. When war broke out between Aws and Khazraj, the Jewish tribes would fight on both sides of the battle, so that Jew would be fighting Jew, and they would drive one another out of their homes and take prisoners .

**The Sins of the Fathers and the Sins of the Self**

These verses also touch on the question of communal versus individual responsibility. One can be passively implicated in the crimes of one’s community, as when children suffer because of their parents’ crimes. But one can also take an active part in a community’s past crimes and be held responsible for them, such that the “sins of the fathers” are justly passed on to later generations. Insofar as individuals are willing to glory in a group identity and accept its accomplishments as an extension of the self, they expose themselves to the crimes associated with that group identity as well.

**Writing trust into a transaction**

From another point of view, individual human beings suffer from the crimes of their forbears insofar as they inherit the traits of those they imitate through sheer worldly ignorance (the Qur'an often chides people for pointing to the practices of their fathers as a moral argument). If people of a certain generation are malicious, their children will tend to inherit and copy those traits, because human beings tend to conform to the norms of their society. In this respect, the sins of the parents are passed on to the children: imitating the good and being raised in an environment of virtue and beauty are better than imitating evil and being raised among liars and thieves, if only because such environments enshrine and transmit truth and compassion to later generations.

**Why clarity protects both sides**

A culture or civilization can be rescued from its moral darkness by an infusion of virtue from saints or sages and, in earlier times, prophets, but on average a community will retain the qualities of its progenitors rather than discard them. One can overcome this weight through repenting of one’s own sins and living a life of active virtue, and even though one identifies oneself as a member of a group, one should deplore its crimes. But even then one might not necessarily escape the effects of communal sin, any more than an individual might escape the fire burning a neighbor’s house. This is one way of interpreting the Qur'an’s use of you in the present tense even when talking about past and also future generations.

**The vulnerable party in the wording**

The language of took places memory and responsibility alongside material exchange. People forget, die, disagree, and understand conversations differently; a careful record does not accuse every party of dishonesty, but protects the honest party from the consequences of uncertainty. The verse therefore treats clarity as a form of mercy, not as a sign that trust has disappeared.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if took were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:84 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:84 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:84.

**Witnesses, records, and memory**

The relation between took and shed also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:84 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:84 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:84 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:84 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The ethics of consent**

The choice of the word took in 2:84 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:84 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:84, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches took from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:84 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:84 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**A practical rule against later conflict**

There is also a distinction between understanding took and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:84. The verse 2:84 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:84 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands shed, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:84.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:84 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving took; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating took from the rest of life. In 2:84, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about took. They are the place in which assent to 2:84 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:84 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: took is joined to shed, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:84 gives the reader a useful order of questions about took. What does the term took mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:84? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:84 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:84.
