## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:97

> **Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Objection of the Declared Enmity**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and gabriel. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:97. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:97. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:97.

**The Vindication: What Gabriel Actually Brought**

Two accounts, similar in their import, give the background for this verse. One describes the Prophet being questioned by a group of Jews who ask him questions only a true prophet could answer, among which is a question about who brought Muhammad the message from Allah. When he answers that it was Gabriel, they say they would have followed him if he had said Michael, who is an agent of mercy in their eyes, but Gabriel is a bringer of war, death, and destruction (Ibn Kathīr). In the second account, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is visiting the Jews while they are studying the Torah, saying he does so because he enjoys experiencing the mutual confirmation of the Torah and the Qur'an.

**The Heart onto Which It Descended**

They tell ʿUmar that they believe that Muhammad is a prophet, but they do not follow him because they are at peace with some angels but not with others, and Gabriel belongs to the latter group. This statement astounds ʿUmar, who balks at the notion that one angel could be a friend and the other a foe while both sit on either side of Allah according to the Jews themselves. Upon returning to speak to the Prophet, he is informed that this verse has already been revealed (Ibn Kathīr). Michael is the preeminent angel in the Jewish tradition; he is mentioned in the book of Daniel (10:13–21) as Israels prince and in later Jewish literature as Israels advocate and the teacher of Moses.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

In other Jewish literature Gabriel and Michael appear with often complementary qualities and functions: Michael is snow, and Gabriel fire; Michael is called the merciful, while Gabriel is set over the powers; and attributes of gentleness are assigned to Michael and those of severity to Gabriel. The book of Enoch, for example, discusses the concept of fallen angels in addition to the distribution of angelic functions and attributes. In the context of a literature that exalts Michael to the rank of foremost protector and advocate of the Children of Israel and assigns to Gabriel functions such as destruction and death (e.g., the midrash describes Gabriel as the destroyer of Sodom), it is not surprising that for some Jews a tradition arose that made Gabriel an enemy to the Jews insofar as he was regarded in some rabbinic and other literature as a bringer of death and destruction, while Michael was Israels special patron and an embodiment of mercy.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

In Jewish literature as a whole, however, Gabriel, though not possessing the stature of Michael, is seen in a positive light.

For Muslim commentators such as al-Rāzī, at least part of the Jews’ apparent enmity toward Gabriel stemmed from the fact that Muhammad was not of Israel, in which case the invocation of Gabriel’s severity would be an excuse masking their displeasure that an Arab would be chosen as a prophet and the agent of the revelation being Gabriel. For Muslims, to fault Gabriel is to fault Allah, who sent him. Al-Rāzī also points out that Jews deny having such a belief, but responds by noting that their deviations of the past, such as the worship of the calf, are not always reflective of their later practice.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

Allahs message is sent down upon the Prophets heart (cf. 26:194; 53:10–11), since the heart is the seat of knowledge and understanding, not only sentiment. See 2:7c, which discusses the dimensions of the heart in the Qur'an.

The mention of prophet must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

The standard in 2:97 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

The final emphasis of 2:97 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:97 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:97 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:97 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:97, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**How the early community understood it**

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:97 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:97 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:97. The verse 2:97 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Power restrained by accountability**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:97 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands gabriel, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:97.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:97 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prophet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating prophet from the rest of life. In 2:97, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about prophet. They are the place in which assent to 2:97 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:97 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: prophet is joined to gabriel, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.
