## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:118

> **Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Timeless Taunt**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings knowledge and come into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:118 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:118 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Those who do not know are identified by some as the Christians and by others as the pagan Arabs; those who came before are alternately the Jews, the Christians, or the Arabs, all three together, or anyone for whom that description is true (al-Ṭabarī). Rather than a sincere question, some interpret Why does Allah not speak to us, nor a sign come to us? as a taunt (al-Ṭabarī). A similar stream of requests or taunts is described in 17:90–95. The issue of such incredulous requests is discussed at length in 4:153c.

**"Their Hearts Are Alike" — One Unbelief Down the Ages**

The verse describes knowledge not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:118, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:118, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if knowledge were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:118 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:118 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:118.

**Clear Signs for the Certain**

The relation between knowledge and come also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:118 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:118 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:118 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:118 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:118 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: knowledge is joined to come, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The wording of 2:118 gives the reader a useful order of questions about knowledge. What does the term knowledge mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:118? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:118 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:118.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:118. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:118 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between knowledge and come.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

In 2:118, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of knowledge while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:118 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:118 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for knowledge, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:118, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

In 2:118, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:118, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:118 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:118 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:118, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine knowledge until the demand disappears.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The final emphasis of 2:118 returns to responsibility. No explanation of knowledge is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:118 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word knowledge in 2:118 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:118 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:118, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The exegetical tradition approaches knowledge from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:118 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:118 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding knowledge and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:118. The verse 2:118 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:118 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands come, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:118.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:118 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving knowledge; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating knowledge from the rest of life. In 2:118, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about knowledge. They are the place in which assent to 2:118 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.
