## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:138

> **This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Coloring of Allah**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings natural and worship into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:138 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:138 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Baptism Allah-Given, Not Man-Given**

The baptism of Allah renders ṣibghat Allāh, which could also be translated the coloring of Allah. The verb ṣabagha means to dye, which involves plunging cloth in liquid and more generally means to cause someone to enter into something (al-Rāzī). The baptism of Allah can refer back to the creed of Abraham in v. 135, it can mean [follow] the baptism of Allah, or it can be read as an adverbial clause connected to We believe in Allah in v. 136 (al-Rāzī). Thus one explanation is that the baptism (ṣibghah) is the primordial nature (fiṭrah) in human beings, which they bear the way a cloth bears its original color and which is the upright religion of 30:30. This would also connect baptism of Allah back to the creed of Abraham in v. 135 through the concept of ḥanīf (primordial monotheist), which is mentioned in both v. 135 and 30:30.

**"And We Are Worshippers of Him" — the Sign of the Dyed Soul**

Some commentators mention Christian baptism and understand this verse to assert the superiority of Islam, as the true baptism of Allah, over the Christian rite . Other interpretations include ṣibghah as purification and also as the wont of Allah, Allahs wont being His unchanging actions in relation to the world (see 17:77; 33:38; 33:62; 35:33; 40:85; 48:23). For others the ṣibghah may denote Allahs religion (dīn; R). These interpretations can be seen as complementary, as Allahs wont, religion, creed, and purification have overlapping significance, and all are brought out by the range of meaning in the term ṣibghah in the sense of an original dye or coloration by Allah in the fabric of existence.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to natural widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:138 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:138: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if natural were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:138 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:138 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:138.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between natural and worship also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:138 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:138 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:138 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:138 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:138 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:138, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine natural until the demand disappears.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The final emphasis of 2:138 returns to responsibility. No explanation of natural is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:138 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word natural in 2:138 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:138 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:138, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The deed before its final account**

The exegetical tradition approaches natural from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:138 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:138 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding natural and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:138. The verse 2:138 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:138 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands worship, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:138.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:138 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving natural; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating natural from the rest of life. In 2:138, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about natural. They are the place in which assent to 2:138 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:138 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: natural is joined to worship, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:138 gives the reader a useful order of questions about natural. What does the term natural mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:138? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:138 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:138.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:138. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:138 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between natural and worship.
