## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:130

> **And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool! We certainly chose him in this life, and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Creed Declared**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings reject and except into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:130 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:130 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Chosen in the World; Named Righteous in the Next**

Regarding creed, see 2:120c. As seen in v. 135, Abrahams creed is often associated with the notion of ḥanīf (see 2:135c). We chose him means that in the world he was given special favor in addition to his rank in the Hereafter. Other figures, such as Mary (3:42), Moses (7:144), Adam (20:122), the Prophet Muhammad (35:32), and other prophets (6:87), are also said to be chosen. Many commentators understand Jews and Christians to be the ones who shun the creed of Abraham (al-Ṭabarī). For some commentators this verse demonstrates the continuity between the Law of Abraham and the Law of Muhammad; the latter would in principle retain the former except in instances where the latter abrogates the former (al-Qurṭubī). This can be seen concretely in the explicit connection of the Islamic rites of the ḥajj with Abraham and the opinion that many of the Sunnah practices relating to bodily cleanliness are thought to go back to Abraham (see 2:124c).

**The Believer Between Safh and Iṣṭifāʾ**

The verse describes reject not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:130, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:130, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if reject were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:130 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:130 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:130.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between reject and except also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:130 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:130 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:130 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:130 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:130 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving reject; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating reject from the rest of life. In 2:130, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about reject. They are the place in which assent to 2:130 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:130 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: reject is joined to except, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The wording of 2:130 gives the reader a useful order of questions about reject. What does the term reject mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:130? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:130 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:130.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:130. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:130 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between reject and except.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

In 2:130, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of reject while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:130 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:130 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for reject, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:130, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:130, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:130, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:130 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:130 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:130, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine reject until the demand disappears.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The final emphasis of 2:130 returns to responsibility. No explanation of reject is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:130 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word reject in 2:130 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:130 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:130, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches reject from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:130 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:130 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
