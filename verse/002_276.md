## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:276

> **Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Erasure for the Lender, Growth for the Giver**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings interest and fruitful into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:276 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:276 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

In causes acts of charity to grow, grow is the verbal form of ribā. On the concept of causing charity to grow, see 2:261c.

Because the verse touches interest, its moral question is not exhausted by whether an arrangement is technically possible. A transaction can be voluntary on the surface and still be shaped by pressure, concealment, or unequal knowledge. The Qur'anic concern is the trust created between people: each party must be able to understand the obligation, and neither may turn another person's need into an instrument of harm.

**The Ingrate Sinner**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if interest were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:276 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:276 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:276.

**The moral shape of a transaction**

The relation between interest and fruitful also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:276 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:276 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:276 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:276 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The danger hidden in a familiar comparison**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:276 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for interest, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:276, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:276, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:276, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:276 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Protection against harm and exploitation**

A reader who finds the command in 2:276 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:276, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine interest until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:276 returns to responsibility. No explanation of interest is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:276 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**What the command asks of both parties**

The choice of the word interest in 2:276 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:276 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:276, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches interest from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:276 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:276 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The law’s mercy and its discipline**

There is also a distinction between understanding interest and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:276. The verse 2:276 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:276 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands fruitful, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:276.

**The social consequence of the ruling**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:276 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving interest; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating interest from the rest of life. In 2:276, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about interest. They are the place in which assent to 2:276 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**When numbers become a moral question**

The most balanced response to 2:276 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: interest is joined to fruitful, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:276 gives the reader a useful order of questions about interest. What does the term interest mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:276? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:276 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:276.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:276. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:276 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between interest and fruitful.

In 2:276, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of interest while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:276 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
