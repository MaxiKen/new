## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:188

> **Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing that it is a sin.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Devouring Forbidden**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings consume and unjustly into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:188 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:188 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Nor Proffer It to Judges"**

In addition to noting the plain sense of this verse, which forbids the enticing or bribing of judges, some commentators point out that in the ultimate sense even a sincere judges ruling can never render the forbidden lawful or the lawful forbidden. Although judges decisions are to be respected, they are not infallible and do not remove the moral weight of ones actions, for which one will answer on the Day of Judgment (Ibn Kathīr). On the matter of adjudicating disputes, the Prophet is reported to have said, I am only a man, and it may be that when two disputants come to me one will be more eloquent in his argument and I will judge in his favor. But whomever I judge in favor of against the right of a Muslim has only received a parcel of the Fire (Ibn Kathīr). On the question of dispensing justice, see also 4:135c.

**The Judge's Ruling Does Not Sanctify**

The subject of consume joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:188, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:188 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if consume were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:188 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:188 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:188.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The relation between consume and unjustly also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:188 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:188 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:188 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:188 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:188 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: consume is joined to unjustly, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The wording of 2:188 gives the reader a useful order of questions about consume. What does the term consume mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:188? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:188 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:188.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:188. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:188 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between consume and unjustly.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

In 2:188, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of consume while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:188 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:188 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for consume, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:188, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The measure of sincere giving**

In 2:188, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:188, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:188 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:188 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:188, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine consume until the demand disappears.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The final emphasis of 2:188 returns to responsibility. No explanation of consume is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:188 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word consume in 2:188 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:188 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:188, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The exegetical tradition approaches consume from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:188 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:188 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding consume and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:188. The verse 2:188 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:188 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands unjustly, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:188.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:188 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving consume; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
