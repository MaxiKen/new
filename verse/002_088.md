## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:88

> **They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!” In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Our Hearts Are Covered" — The Defense of Sealed Doors**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings hearts and condemned into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:88 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:88 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Allah's Correction: Not Covered but Cursed**

In Our hearts are uncircumcised, uncircumcised renders ghulf (see also 4:155c), which means covered and can mean sheathed (as with a sword), but whose semantic range also allows the reading, Our hearts are vessels, meaning their hearts contain all knowledge and stand in no need of any other, or they are empty vessels in the sense that nothing of what others say resonates with them as being true . A similarly phrased passage, Our hearts are under coverings from that to which you call us (41:5), raises the question, in the minds of some commentators, of individual moral responsibility for ones state of faith: if ones organ of faith and understanding is covered, is one responsible?

**The Longing Heart Is Not Sealed**

Although here Allah seems to dismiss and rebuke the notion of hearts being covered, in 4:155 the claim Our hearts are uncircumcised is followed immediately by Nay! Rather, Allah has set a seal upon them for their disbelief, so they believe not, save a few. The dismissal of the Jews claim that their hearts are enwrapped (both here and in 4:155) is thus juxtaposed with the affirmation that they are sealed from faith. But the question need not be whether Allah seals hearts; indeed, a sealed heart would not be able to see that it is sealed.

A soul that laments its being sealed is not completely sealed, since one cannot be aware of or believe in Allah unless Allah allows it. My heart is covered can be uttered cynically but has a different meaning when expressed by a person longing for Allah, but dismayed by the distance from Him. See also 2:7c, which addresses the question of sealing hearts and its significance for questions of predestination and responsibility based on free will.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The verse describes hearts not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:88, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:88, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if hearts were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:88 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:88 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:88.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The relation between hearts and condemned also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:88 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:88 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:88 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:88 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:88 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving hearts; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating hearts from the rest of life. In 2:88, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about hearts. They are the place in which assent to 2:88 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:88 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: hearts is joined to condemned, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The wording of 2:88 gives the reader a useful order of questions about hearts. What does the term hearts mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:88? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:88 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:88.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:88. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:88 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between hearts and condemned.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:88, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of hearts while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:88 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:88 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for hearts, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:88, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

In 2:88, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:88, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:88 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:88 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:88, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine hearts until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:88 returns to responsibility. No explanation of hearts is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:88 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word hearts in 2:88 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:88 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:88, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
