## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:158

> **Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Two Hills of the Mother's Running**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings indeed and marwah into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:158 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:158 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Ḥajj, the ʿUmrah, and the Course**

Ṣafā and Marwah are two hills near the Kaʿbah, between which pilgrims pass or “hurry” back and forth seven times, a practice that is said according to the traditional Islamic account to go back to the story of Hagar and Ishmael. After Abraham, following Allah’s Command, brought them to that place and left them there (cf. Genesis 21), Hagar frantically ran from one hill to another seven times in order to look for water for Ishmael. Called saʿy, this motion is partway between walking and running. Even though this was the action of Hagar, Muslims consider it part of the rituals of the pilgrimage, because it originated with Abraham, who built the Kaʿbah with Ishmael after the latter had become an adult (Ibn Kathīr), and the practice was continued by the Prophet Muhammad.

**Volunteering Good Above the Marches**

Commentators point out that some of the Companions were reluctant to perform the ritual of going back and forth between Ṣafā and Marwah, fearing that it was a holdover from the Days of Ignorance and hence an idolatrous practice. This concern may have originated in a local legend that two people fornicated inside the Kaʿbah and were turned to stone; they were then placed near the Kaʿbah as a warning, and over time these stones slowly grew into the two hills (Ibn Kathīr). Others mention that the verse may have been in response to other Companions who thought that the Kaʿbah was the only object around which such motions such as running to and fro and circumambulation should be made. The commentators point out that this verse could be understood to have addressed both questions (al-Qurṭubī).

**A journey ordered toward Allah**

There are differences of opinion regarding Ṣafā and Marwah; according to some schools of thought, to go back and forth between them (saʿy) is one of the pillars of the ḥajj (e.g., Shāfiʿī), while for others it is only recommended (e.g., Ḥanafī), although adherents of all schools nevertheless do perform the saʿy. Those who see it as a pillar of the ḥajj mention a ḥadīth of the Prophet, “Go to and fro [between the two hills], for Allah has prescribed for you the running to and fro.”

Rituals (shaʿāʾir) can also refer to ritual places; the root sh-ʿ-r carries the sense of that by which Allah is known (al-Qurṭubī), thus referring both to sacred symbols and sacred actions. See also 5:2; 22:32; 22:36.

**The sacred limit in the command**

The ʿumrah is a supererogatory visit to perform a shortened form of pilgrimage and can be performed during any time of the year other than the days designated for the obligatory ḥajj. The ʿumrah contains some of the elements of the ḥajj, such as becoming muḥrim—that is, wearing two pieces of cloth; the circumambulation of the Kaʿbah; and the passing to and fro between Ṣafā and Marwah. Most scholars, especially those who see the ritual of Ṣafā and Marwah as obligatory, see whosoever volunteers good as a reference to the ʿumrah, which is not an obligatory Islamic rite like the ḥajj. On the ʿumrah, see v. 196.

The treatment of indeed joins movement through a place or ritual with movement of the heart. Sacred practice has boundaries precisely because it is not ordinary travel or ordinary habit. The limits teach the pilgrim to receive meaning rather than manufacture it, while the concessions show that obedience is addressed to human beings with real capacities and pressures.

**Ritual detail and spiritual purpose**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if indeed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:158 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:158 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:158.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:158 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving indeed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**What pilgrims must leave behind**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating indeed from the rest of life. In 2:158, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about indeed. They are the place in which assent to 2:158 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:158 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: indeed is joined to marwah, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The community gathered by one sign**

The wording of 2:158 gives the reader a useful order of questions about indeed. What does the term indeed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:158? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:158 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:158.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:158. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:158 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between indeed and marwah.

**The jurists’ concern for capacity**

In 2:158, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of indeed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:158 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:158 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for indeed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:158, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:158, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:158, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:158 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:158 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:158, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine indeed until the demand disappears.
