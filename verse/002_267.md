## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:267

> **O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned and of what We have produced for you from the earth. Do not pick out worthless things for donation, which you yourselves would only accept with closed eyes. And know that Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Give From the Good — Not From the Dregs**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between belief and accountability. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:267. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:267. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:267.

**Bad Money Not Cleansed by Alms**

A similar message appears in 3:92: You will never attain piety till you spend from that which you love. In a famous ḥadīth the Prophet stated, None of you believes until he desires for his neighbor what he desires for himself (in another version, for his neighbor reads for his brother). Here good things can mean either what one considers wholesome and desirable, what one would not reject, or what is lawful, not forbidden. One should not seek unlawful or distasteful means to give in charity. It is reported that a group of Companions would bring bunches of dates from their date palms and hang them in the mosque for the poorest of the Muslims to eat. When another group sought to perform the same act, but brought inferior dates that no one would want, it is said that this verse was revealed (Ibn Kathīr).

**Allah Is Self-Sufficient, Praised**

On the subject of charity, a ḥadīth states, “Truly Allah distributes your provisions among you, just as He distributes your virtues among you. Allah gives of the world to those whom He loves and does not love, but gives religion (dīn) only to those whom He loves. Whosoever Allah has given religion, Allah loves him. By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, no servant submits until his heart and tongue submit, and no believer believes until his bodily parts are safe from his misfortunes.” When asked what the misfortunes were, the Prophet replied, “His lies and his wrongdoing. No servant receives blessing by earning wealth that is unlawful and giving it in charity; nor is it accepted from him if he donates it; nor does he leave it to anyone but that it goes to the Fire. Allah does not erase evil with evil, but he does erase evil with good. Evil does not erase evil.” Another ḥadīth states, “Do not feed others with what you yourself would not eat.”

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to belief widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:267 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:267: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:267 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from belief: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between belief and accountability also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:267 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:267 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:267 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:267 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The exegetical tradition approaches belief from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:267 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:267 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Mercy and warning held together**

There is also a distinction between understanding belief and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:267. The verse 2:267 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:267 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands accountability, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:267.

**The deed before its final account**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:267 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belief; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:267, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:267 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The most balanced response to 2:267 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to accountability, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:267 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belief. What does the term belief mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:267? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:267 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:267.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:267. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:267 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belief and accountability.

In 2:267, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of belief while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:267 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:267 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for belief, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:267, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
