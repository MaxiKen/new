## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:78

> **And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Masses Behind the Scribes**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings among and nothing into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:78 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:78 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Nothing but Hearsay": The Religion of Amānī**

The illiterate (ummiyyūn) are those Jews who could not read and write (al-Ṭabarī). In an opinion attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, however, they are a people who accept no prophet or revealed book and who write a book themselves and say that it is from Allah; they were called ummiyyūn because of their opposition to the Books of Allah (al-Ṭabarī). But most do not consider this opinion to be correct, since among the Arabs ummī means someone who cannot write or write well (al-Ṭabarī). The commentators often mention here the ḥadīth, We are an ummī people; we do not write and we do not calculate the months.

**The Ummī Prophet and the Recited Book**

This would support the interpretation that ummī means one who cannot read and write well , since neither the Jews nor the Arabs of Arabia were wholly illiterate; some could read and write well, while some could do so in only a very rudimentary fashion. That the Arabs were not entirely illiterate is clear from the Islamic traditions proven assertion that the Qur'an was written down during the lifetime of the Prophet, though the literate could have been a relatively small number in relation to the whole. For further discussion of the concept of ummī, or unlettered, see 7:157–58c; 62:2c.

**The Book as an address**

Hearsay (amānī) means that they know only what their scholars tell them ; or it means things they invented, such as that the Fire will not touch them except for a certain number of days (see v. 80) or that none will enter Paradise who is not a Jew (2:111). Amānī can also be rendered hope or desire (see also 2:111; 4:123; 22:52; 57:14). They only conjecture is usually glossed as they lie [about Allah] or they think/deem false things [about Allah] (Ibn Kathīr).

The wording around among assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

**The word that carries the argument**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if among were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:78 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:78 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:78.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

The relation between among and nothing also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:78 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:78 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:78 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:78 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:78. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:78 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between among and nothing.

**The reader’s responsibility**

In 2:78, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of among while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:78 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:78 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for among, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:78, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why the wording is exact**

In 2:78, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:78, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:78 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:78 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:78, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine among until the demand disappears.

**A question answered by the surrounding verses**

The final emphasis of 2:78 returns to responsibility. No explanation of among is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:78 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word among in 2:78 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:78 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:78, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches among from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:78 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:78 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding among and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:78. The verse 2:78 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:78 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands nothing, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:78.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:78 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving among; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
