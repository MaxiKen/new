## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:73

> **So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Strike Him with Part of It": The Impossible Instrument**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between instructed and body. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:73. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:73. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:73.

**"Thus Does Allah Give Life to the Dead"**

On the murder, also see 2:67–71c. With some variations, the commentators relate the story that the Israelites, after searching for a long time, find the cow described by Moses, purchase it for a high price, and sacrifice it. Then they take a part of the cows body and strike the corpse of the murder victim, which is then briefly animated, long enough to answer the question of who the killer was, after which the body reverts to its previous state (Ibn Kathīr). Thus, most commentators understand the command in v. 73 to mean, Strike him [the corpse] with part of it [the slaughtered cow].

**"That Haply You May Understand" — And the Heart It Raises**

Assuming this is the correct interpretation, it would not be the only instance of reanimation in the Qur'an; others include Abraham and the birds (2:260); Jesus and the clay bird (3:49); the unnamed man thought by some to be Ezra, who died and was revived after a hundred years (2:259); and an allusion to this is when dust touched by a messenger (usually thought to be Gabriel) is used to animate the golden calf (20:96).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

In a related ḥadīth, a man asked, “O Messenger of Allah, how does Allah revive the dead?” “Have you ever passed by a barren valley, and then passed by it again and it was verdant?” “Yes.” “Even so is the Resurrection.” In another version he replied, “Even so does Allah revive the dead.” Sufi commentators have seen in this passage a message that one whose heart has been “slain” by worldliness must sacrifice the egotistical soul in order to give life to the heart through the remembrance of Allah (Ni, Su, Qu). Al-Qushayrī states, “Whoever desires that his heart be given life must sacrifice his soul. So whoever sacrifices his soul with effort gives life to his heart and the lights of vision.”

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

In Deuteronomy 21:1–9 the Israelites are commanded to sacrifice a cow in the event that a person is found slain and the murderer remains unknown, after which the elders of the town nearest to the victim wash their hands over the cow and disavow any guilt for the crime. But the two accounts are different; one is the narration of an event, and the other, the prescription of a general law, even though there are similarities between them.

The verse's reference to instructed widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:73 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:73: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The standard in 2:73 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from instructed: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The deed before its final account**

The relation between instructed and body also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:73 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:73 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:73 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:73 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:73 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands body, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:73.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:73 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving instructed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating instructed from the rest of life. In 2:73, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about instructed. They are the place in which assent to 2:73 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The most balanced response to 2:73 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: instructed is joined to body, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:73 gives the reader a useful order of questions about instructed. What does the term instructed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:73? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:73 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:73.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:73. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:73 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between instructed and body.

In 2:73, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of instructed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:73 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:73 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for instructed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:73, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:73, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:73, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:73 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
