## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:186

> **When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one’s prayer when they call upon Me. So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Verse at the Center of the Sūrah**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings servants and near into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:186 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:186 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

It is reported that this verse came as a response to some Companions who asked the Prophet, Where is our Lord? or At what hour should we call upon Allah? after the revelation of 40:60: Call upon Me, and I shall respond to you. Others mention that it was a response to questions such as, How shall we make our call, O Prophet of Allah? (al-Ṭabarī). Others say it was revealed after a Bedouin came and asked the Prophet, Is our Lord near, such that I shall whisper to Him, or far, such that I shall shout to Him? (al-Rāzī).

**Asked and Answered in One Breath**

In a related vein, the Prophet, while on a journey, reportedly reprimanded some Companions who were shouting their prayers, saying, You are calling One who is neither deaf nor absent, but are calling One who is Hearing, Near (al-Rāzī); al-Qarīb (the Near) is a Divine Name. Some interpret the verb rendered let them respond to Me (yastajībū) to mean let them ask of Me, which would echo the first phrase of this verse (al-Rāzī). This verse is one of the most central and often quoted verses of the Qur'an concerning Allahs nearness to us and the reality of reciprocity between the human call to Allah and His response.

**Duʿāʾ as Worship**

The Prophet said, Calling [upon Allah] is a form of worship. Call renders duʿāʾ, which can also mean individual prayer, supplication, or summons depending on context. It can also be translated simply as prayer in the sense that one prays by calling upon Allah to receive something or to ask for a specific blessing. The sense of prayer in this verse is highlighted in M. Lings evocative translation of this verse: I answer the prayer of the pray-er when he prayeth Me, but duʿāʾ does not extend to all possible uses of the English word prayer. A similar passage can be found in 27:62, where Allah is spoken of as He Who answers the one in distress when he calls upon Him. In 7:55 believers are told, Call upon your Lord humbly and in secret; and in 42:26 it is said, He responds to those who believe and perform righteous deeds.

**"When My Servants Ask" — The Servant-Direct Address**

Several other verses mention Allahs nearness, such as 50:16: We are nearer to him than his jugular vein; and 17:57, which asks concerning Allah and other idols, Which of them is nearer? In 34:50 it is said of Allah, Truly He is Hearing, Nigh. Nearness to Allah is a part of what it means to be in Paradise, as in 38:25: Truly nearness unto Us shall be his, and a beautiful return; and at the moment of death it is said to those gathered around the dying person, We are nearer to him than you, though you see not (56:85). Other places where Allahs Nearness in mentioned include 4:135; 9:99; 34:37.

Al-Rāzī and some other commentators connect the message of this verse to the prayers of magnification commanded in the previous verse and also to the alleviation of the requirements of fasting, which came after some Companions had difficulty in fulfilling them (see v. 187).

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The subject of servants joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:186, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:186 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if servants were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:186 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:186 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:186.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:186 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving servants; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating servants from the rest of life. In 2:186, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about servants. They are the place in which assent to 2:186 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The most balanced response to 2:186 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: servants is joined to near, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:186 gives the reader a useful order of questions about servants. What does the term servants mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:186? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:186 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:186.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:186. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:186 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between servants and near.

In 2:186, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of servants while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:186 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The poor person’s dignity**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:186 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for servants, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:186, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:186, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:186, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:186 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:186 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:186, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine servants until the demand disappears.
