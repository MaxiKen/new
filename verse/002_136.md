## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:136

> **Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Believers' Creed Announced**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings belief and ishmael into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:136 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:136 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"We Make No Distinction" — The Key Clause of Prophetic Equality**

The Tribes (asbāṭ) refer to the twelve tribes descended from the children of Jacob, mentioned in 7:160: And We divided them into twelve tribes. The singular of asbāṭ, sibṭ, means grandchild, which would signify that they are the grandchildren of Jacob (al-Rāzī). The ranking of the prophets is discussed in v. 285, where the Prophet and the believers are also enjoined to make no distinction between the messengers of Allah. The present verse is considered by commentators to invalidate the selective tendency to pick and choose condemned in v. 85: Do you, then, believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part? See the essay The Qur'anic View of Sacred History and Other Religions. The continuity and wholeness of religion is described in such passages as He has prescribed for you as religion that with which He enjoined upon Noah, and that which We revealed unto thee, and that with which We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, that you uphold religion and become not divided therein (42:13).

**Said by the Believers Every Day**

The verse describes belief not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:136, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:136, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if belief were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:136 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:136 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:136.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between belief and ishmael also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:136 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:136 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:136 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:136 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:136 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for belief, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:136, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

In 2:136, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:136, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:136 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:136 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:136, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine belief until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:136 returns to responsibility. No explanation of belief is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:136 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The choice of the word belief in 2:136 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:136 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:136, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches belief from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:136 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:136 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

There is also a distinction between understanding belief and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:136. The verse 2:136 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:136 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands ishmael, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:136.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:136 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belief; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:136, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:136 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:136 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to ishmael, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The wording of 2:136 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belief. What does the term belief mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:136? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:136 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:136.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:136. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:136 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belief and ishmael.
