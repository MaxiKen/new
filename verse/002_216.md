## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:216

> **Fighting has been made obligatory upon you ˹believers˺, though you dislike it. Perhaps you dislike something which is good for you and like something which is bad for you. Allah knows and you do not know.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Kutiba — Written Over You**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings fighting and belief into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:216 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:216 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Asymmetry of Hate and Good**

On the general topic of warfare, see the essay “Conquest and Conversion, War and Peace in the Qur'an.” Some early commentators argued that fighting has been prescribed for you referred only to the Companions of the Prophet and that fighting alongside him was an individual responsibility for each of them, but later became a communal responsibility; that is, after Islam’s precarious beginnings in Makkah, where warfare was forbidden, and its initial phase in Madinah, to which Muslims had migrated to survive and where they continued to fight to ensure their survival as a religious community, Islam itself was no longer in danger of being eradicated, and the universal prescription was lifted .

**Of Battle-Fields and All Fields**

Some scholars of the early generations, such as Saʿīd ibn al-Muṣayyab (d. 94/713), deemed fighting in the way of Allah to be an everlasting obligation upon Muslims, while others, such as Sufyān al-Thawrī (d. 161/778), said it was a voluntary action (al-Qurṭubī). When challenged as to why he would not participate in fighting during the First Civil War, ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar (d. 74/693), the son of the second Caliph, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, told his interlocutors, “We did that during the time of the Prophet, when Islam was small, when a man would be tried in his religion, and they would either kill him or torture him.

But then Islam became great, and there was no trial (fitnah).” Al-Rāzī notes that the “unanimous consensus,” or ijmāʿ, is that when fighting is called for, it is a communal responsibility, except if Muslim lands are invaded, in which case it becomes an individual responsibility.

**The Men of Knowledge on Their Feet**

The mention of fighting must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if fighting were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:216 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:216 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:216.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

The relation between fighting and belief also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:216 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:216 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:216 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:216 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:216 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: fighting is joined to belief, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

The wording of 2:216 gives the reader a useful order of questions about fighting. What does the term fighting mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:216? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:216 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:216.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:216. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:216 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between fighting and belief.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

In 2:216, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of fighting while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:216 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:216 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for fighting, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:216, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

In 2:216, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:216, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:216 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:216 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:216, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine fighting until the demand disappears.

**How the early community understood it**

The final emphasis of 2:216 returns to responsibility. No explanation of fighting is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:216 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word fighting in 2:216 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:216 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:216, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches fighting from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:216 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:216 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding fighting and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:216. The verse 2:216 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
