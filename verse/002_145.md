## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:145

> **Even if you were to bring every proof to the People of the Book, they would not accept your direction ˹of prayer˺, nor would you accept theirs; nor would any of them accept the direction ˹of prayer˺ of another. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Every Sign Would Not Suffice**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between even and proof. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:145. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:145. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:145.

**The Qiblah Compass as a Sign of Separated Ways**

Every sign also has the sense of every kind of sign, as in 10:96–97: Truly those for whom the Word of thy Lord has come due will not believe, though every sign should come unto them, till they see the painful punishment. The warning against following the caprices of other religious communities is mentioned in v. 120 (see also 5:48; 13:37; 42:15) and in this verse is addressed through the example of the following of different qiblahs. Jews traditionally face Jerusalem, while Christian churches were historically oriented toward the east and the rising sun, though the use of the qiblah signifies more than a physical orientation; it also relates to identity and community. To say someone follows a different qiblah is tantamount to excluding that person from the religious community in question. A ḥadīth states, Whoever prays our prayer and faces our qiblah and eats what we slaughter is a Muslim and is under the protection of Allah and the protection of His Messenger.

**"After the Knowledge That Has Come to Thee"**

The subject of even joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:145, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:145 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:145 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from even: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The Believer's Definite Qiblah**

The relation between even and proof also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:145 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:145 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:145 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:145 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating even from the rest of life. In 2:145, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about even. They are the place in which assent to 2:145 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:145 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: even is joined to proof, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The wording of 2:145 gives the reader a useful order of questions about even. What does the term even mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:145? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:145 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:145.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:145. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:145 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between even and proof.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

In 2:145, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of even while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:145 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:145 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for even, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:145, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The measure of sincere giving**

In 2:145, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:145, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:145 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:145 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:145, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine even until the demand disappears.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The final emphasis of 2:145 returns to responsibility. No explanation of even is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:145 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word even in 2:145 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:145 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:145, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches even from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:145 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:145 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding even and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:145. The verse 2:145 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
