## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:225

> **Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but for what you intended in your hearts. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Oaths Uttered Without Purpose**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between hold and oaths. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:225. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:225. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:225.

This verse is interpreted by some to refer to the habit of repeating “by Allah” (wa’Llāhi) for emphasis in everyday speech, which was a common habit among the Arabs and continues today even among non-Arab Muslims and non-Muslim Arabs. Others mention that it could refer to a situation in which a person believes something to be true, swears to it, and then finds out the belief is incorrect, in which case no expiation is required. This verse is also taken to exclude oaths taken in anger (al-Qurṭubī).

**"What Your Hearts Have Earned"**

The verse's reference to hold widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:225 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:225: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:225 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from hold: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Forgiving, Clement — The Sealing Pair**

The relation between hold and oaths also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:225 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:225 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:225 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:225 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The exegetical tradition approaches hold from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:225 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:225 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

There is also a distinction between understanding hold and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:225. The verse 2:225 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:225 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands oaths, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:225.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:225 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving hold; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating hold from the rest of life. In 2:225, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about hold. They are the place in which assent to 2:225 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The most balanced response to 2:225 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: hold is joined to oaths, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:225 gives the reader a useful order of questions about hold. What does the term hold mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:225? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:225 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:225.

**The deed before its final account**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:225. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:225 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between hold and oaths.

In 2:225, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of hold while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:225 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:225 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for hold, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:225, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:225, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:225, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:225 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

A reader who finds the command in 2:225 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:225, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine hold until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:225 returns to responsibility. No explanation of hold is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:225 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word hold in 2:225 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:225 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:225, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
