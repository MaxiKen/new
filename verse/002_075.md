## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:75

> **Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?**

### Expanded Commentary

**A Consolation Wrapped as a Question**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between belief and true. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:75. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:75. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:75.

**What Is Taḥrīf? Text or Meaning?**

This verse is addressed, using the plural you, to the Muslim community; they refers to the Children of Israel. It is one of a handful of verses that mention the distortion (taḥrīf) of scriptures by the People of the Book (see also 2:59; 3:78; 4:46; 5:13, 41). According to one interpretation, the party mentioned here are the seventy chosen Israelites (see 2:55c) who were said to have joined Moses on the Mount and directly heard the Word of Allah. When they returned, Moses said, Allah commands thus and thus, but this group would say, No, Allah commands thus and thus, misrepresenting what they had just heard (Ibn Kathīr), or they said, If you are able to do these things, then do them, and if you wish, then do not; there is no harm in it .

**Hope Realigned, Not Abolished**

One argument for this interpretation is that they heard the Word of Allah along with Moses, which is to say they did not read it, but the Qur'an speaks of those who hear Allahs Word indirectly, as in 9:6 (Ibn Kathīr). Other commentators believe that this verse describes the Jewish contemporaries of the Prophet.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Although lexically taḥrīf seems to be related to “letter” (ḥarf), its meaning is to “slant,” “be oblique,” “twist,” or “deviate” and is often connected by the commentators with the etymologically related inḥirāf, usually understood as “deviation.” Whether the Jews and Christians actually altered their transmitted books or instead skewed their interpretations while retaining a faithful text is a subject of debate in the Islamic intellectual tradition. Although later Islamic commentators and theologians often held a view that the Jews and Christians actually changed the text of the Bible, as epitomized in the work of the fifth-/eleventh-century scholar Ibn Ḥazm, the earlier commentators were not as eager to dispute the text of the Bible and preferred to view the “distortion” as an act of faulty and even malicious interpretation.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

In his remarks on 3:78 al-Rāzī, for example, doubts that the prophecies pertaining to the Prophet Muhammad (see 7:157) were physically removed, since that would have required collusion on a grand scale with a well-known text. (In his commentary on 2:75, however, he states that with a less widely distributed text such an alteration would not be impossible.) He prefers to think that the prophecies required careful thinking and contemplation in the heart and that those texts, over time, took on a normative interpretation that drifted away from the original intent. This required a certain amount of speculation and assumption on al-Rāzīs part regarding how widespread such texts were, since it is doubtful that he had detailed knowledge of the recording and transmission of the Bible.

**Mercy and warning held together**

Often mentioned in this connection with this verse is the thesis that the scholars and jurists of the Children of Israel would make lawful what was forbidden and forbid what was lawful, in accord with their own desires . After they had understood it also supports the idea that the meaning was distorted, not the text (al-Ṭabarī).

**The deed before its final account**

Other instances in which the Prophet is admonished not to hope too much that his contemporaries will believe in him include 35:8: Truly Allah leads astray whomsoever He will and guides whomsoever He will; so let not thy soul be expended in regrets over them; and 18:6: Yet perhaps thou wouldst torment thyself with grief for their sake, should they believe not in this account. This can be read on a spiritual level as a rebuke to those who, after having experienced visions and openings of spiritual truth, revert to formality and lore and forget the subtle knowledge they once had; or to those who act hypocritically and with bad manners with the sages and the saints and stop keeping company with companions of a spiritual nature (Aj).

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The verse's reference to belief widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:75 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:75: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:75 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from belief: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:75, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:75 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:75 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to true, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:75 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belief. What does the term belief mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:75? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:75 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:75.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:75. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:75 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belief and true.

In 2:75, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of belief while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:75 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:75 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for belief, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:75, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:75, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:75, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:75 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
