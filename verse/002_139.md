## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:139

> **Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**One Lord, No Room for Dispute**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between dispute and yours. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:139. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:139. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:139.

This verse is a command to the Prophet; the question is directed to the Jews and Christians and, according to some, possibly the idolatrous Arabs (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī). The Jews are said to have objected to a prophet coming from outside of the Children of Israel, while the idolatrous Arabs thought that if there were a prophet, it should be a man of higher social status than Muhammad (see 3:26; 43:31). The dispute can also refer to the various questions and challenges posed to the Prophet, such as the one mentioned in v. 135.

**Unto Us Our Deeds: the Demarcation of Works**

A similar approach toward disputation with the Prophet is found in 10:41: And if they deny thee, say, Unto me, my deeds, and unto you, your deeds; 2:258: Hast thou not seen him who disputed with Abraham about his Lord; 3:20: So if they dispute with thee, say, I submit my face to Allah, and so too those who follow me; and 6:80: His people disputed with him. He said, Do you dispute with me concerning Allah, when He has guided me?

The verse's reference to dispute widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:139 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:139: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The Tradition's Treatise of Ikhlāṣ, Built on This Clause**

The standard in 2:139 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from dispute: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Dispute Terminated at the Same Lord**

The relation between dispute and yours also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:139 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:139 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:139 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:139 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The final emphasis of 2:139 returns to responsibility. No explanation of dispute is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:139 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The choice of the word dispute in 2:139 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:139 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:139, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches dispute from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:139 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:139 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

There is also a distinction between understanding dispute and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:139. The verse 2:139 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:139 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands yours, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:139.

**Mercy and warning held together**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:139 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving dispute; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating dispute from the rest of life. In 2:139, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about dispute. They are the place in which assent to 2:139 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The deed before its final account**

The most balanced response to 2:139 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: dispute is joined to yours, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:139 gives the reader a useful order of questions about dispute. What does the term dispute mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:139? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:139 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:139.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:139. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:139 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between dispute and yours.

In 2:139, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of dispute while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:139 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:139 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for dispute, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:139, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
