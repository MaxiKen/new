## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:174

> **Indeed, those who hide Allah’s revelations, trading them for a fleeting gain consume nothing but fire into their bellies. Allah will neither speak to them on the Day of Judgment, nor will He purify them. And they will suffer a painful punishment.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Concealing-and-Selling: The Double Verb**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings indeed and revelations into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:174 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:174 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Regarding sell it for a paltry price, see 2:79c. On fire in their bellies, also see 4:10c. Both phrases make the point that instead of consuming the wealth they sought in earthly life, in the Hereafter such people will consume only fire. On Allahs speaking to human beings in the Hereafter, also see 3:77, which, like this verse, states, Allah will not speak to them, nor will He look at them on the Day of Resurrection, nor will He purify them. And theirs shall be a painful punishment.

**Silent Allah, Ungreeting Allah**

However, elsewhere in the Qur'an Allah speaks to human beings on the Day of Resurrection: So by thy Lord, We shall question them all concerning that which they used to do (15:92–93); Then We shall surely question those unto whom Our message was sent, and We shall surely question the messengers (7:6); and He will say, Be gone therein, and speak not to Me (23:108, spoken by Allah in response to those asking to be removed from the Fire). According to al-Rāzī, the plain sense of v. 174 and 3:77 is that He will not speak to them at all, but in light of these other Qur'anic verses it is to be understood, rather, as a description of a punishment, meaning that He will not speak words of peace and welcome.

**The Verse in Islamic Ethics of the 'Ulamāʾ**

Al-Rāzī notes that this resembles intimacy with a king, who shows his displeasure through imposing exile and cutting off contact, and suggests that it will be the angels who speak to people on the Day of Resurrection. Al-Ṭabarī offers a similar interpretation, taking it to mean that Allah will not say things agreeable to them. Al-Qurṭubī points out that one says, He did not speak to him, to describe someone who is angry.

Nor will He purify them means He will not praise them for their goodness or call them purified (al-Ṭabarī), that He will not set right their evil deeds (al-Qurṭubī), or that He will not accept their deeds as He accepts those of the purified. The verb zakkā can mean both to purify and to deem purified, as in 53:32: So do not deem yourselves purified. Al-Rāzī points out that the punishment of indignity and humiliation is worse than that of physical punishment, which is a way of implying that the spiritual punishment of distance and separation from Allah constitutes the worst suffering in Hell.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The verse's reference to indeed widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:174 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:174: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if indeed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:174 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:174 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:174.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between indeed and revelations also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:174 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:174 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:174 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:174 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:174 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: indeed is joined to revelations, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The wording of 2:174 gives the reader a useful order of questions about indeed. What does the term indeed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:174? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:174 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:174.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:174. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:174 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between indeed and revelations.

**The deed before its final account**

In 2:174, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of indeed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:174 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:174 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for indeed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:174, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:174, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:174, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:174 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:174 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:174, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine indeed until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:174 returns to responsibility. No explanation of indeed is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:174 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The choice of the word indeed in 2:174 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:174 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:174, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
