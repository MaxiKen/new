## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:112

> **But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Door's Actual Dimensions**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings whoever and good into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:112 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:112 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Reward with the Lord, and the Double Exemption**

For submits his face, see also 4:125; 31:22. Here to submit means to be sincere, and the metaphor is powerful because the face is the noblest part of ones body (al-Ṭabarī), and In one respect the most profound action in the prayer is the prostration, where ones face (the forehead and the nose) touches the ground (al-Rāzī). See also 3:20 and commentary. Although some commentators, such as al-Ṭabarī, understand this to mean submitting ones body and actions, which is how they understand the use of face, others, such as Ibn Kathīr, add to this and say that the twin conditions of ones actions being acceptable to Allah are sincerity and conformity to the Sharīʿah (Islamic Law).

**The Tradition's Two Wings of Acceptance**

This latter view would exclude hypocrites because of their lack of sincerity, but would also rule out Christian monks for their lack of conformity to the Sharīʿah. Al-Rāzī, in the same way, leaves out Hindus, who, whatever their intentions, display in his view repugnant actions. The condition of physical conformity allows the exclusion of other religions, although the plain sense of the passage and those like it does not necessitate such exclusion as asserted by other authorities.

The verse's reference to whoever widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:112 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:112: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if whoever were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:112 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:112 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:112.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between whoever and good also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:112 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:112 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:112 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:112 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The choice of the word whoever in 2:112 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:112 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:112, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches whoever from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:112 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:112 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The deed before its final account**

There is also a distinction between understanding whoever and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:112. The verse 2:112 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:112 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands good, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:112.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:112 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving whoever; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating whoever from the rest of life. In 2:112, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about whoever. They are the place in which assent to 2:112 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The most balanced response to 2:112 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: whoever is joined to good, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:112 gives the reader a useful order of questions about whoever. What does the term whoever mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:112? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:112 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:112.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:112. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:112 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between whoever and good.

In 2:112, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of whoever while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:112 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:112 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for whoever, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:112, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:112, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:112, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:112 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
