## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:172

> **O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**From Mankind to the Believers**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings belief and thanks into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:172 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:172 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Food as Primary Worship**

The good things (Ṭayyibāt) are those things that Allah has not forbidden or singled out as being sinful (al-Ṭabarī). See also 2:57c. In a ḥadīth the Prophet said, Allah is good (ṭayyib), and only accepts that which is good. Offered to other than Allah means food that is sacrificed to an idol, thus excluding the meat eaten by the idolaters (but not that of the People of the Book according to many jurists). In Islamic practice, an animal is slaughtered with the words, In the Name of Allah. It can occur that one is compelled by necessity, meaning by hunger, to eat under certain circumstances things that are forbidden in order to survive and protect ones life, but this should be done neither coveting nor transgressing, meaning not with the intention of bypassing Allahs Commandments in order to enjoy something that is forbidden (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). In such cases of necessity one is therefore permitted to eat what in normal circumstances would be forbidden.

**The If-Clause That Tests Every Mouthful**

Coveting renders bāghin, which has the sense of rebellion. For some the idea of transgressing (ʿādin) also refers to the practice of highway robbery, going against the authorities, or setting out to disobey Allah, presumably meaning that it is not permissible to pursue food in these ways even in cases of need (al-Ṭabarī). Regarding the legal and ritual issues of carrion, blood, and the flesh of swine, see 5:3c.

The verse's reference to belief widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:172 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:172: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if belief were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:172 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:172 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:172.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The relation between belief and thanks also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:172 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:172 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:172 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:172 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:172 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belief; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:172, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:172 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:172 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to thanks, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The deed before its final account**

The wording of 2:172 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belief. What does the term belief mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:172? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:172 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:172.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:172. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:172 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belief and thanks.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

In 2:172, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of belief while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:172 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:172 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for belief, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:172, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

In 2:172, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:172, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:172 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:172 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:172, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine belief until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:172 returns to responsibility. No explanation of belief is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:172 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word belief in 2:172 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:172 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:172, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
