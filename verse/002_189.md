## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:189

> **They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, “They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage.” Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺. So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Question of the Moons**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and means. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:189. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:189. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:189.

**Houses from the Rear: The Old and the New**

Here new moons (ahillah, sing. hilāl) does not refer to the new moon considered purely astronomically, which refers to when the moon is still invisible (dark moon), but to the first visible crescent of the lunar cycle. During the dark moon, the moon is between the earth and the sun, so that the dark side of the moon faces the earth; this is referred to as lunar conjunction. A lunar conjunction does not usually cause a solar eclipse, because the lunar orbit is tilted several degrees in relation to the earth’s orbit around the sun, and hence the three bodies are only in an approximately straight line. (That is also why the full moon is visible from the earth when the earth is approximately in between the moon and the sun.) The new moon or crescent (hilāl) appears when the moon moves far enough out of conjunction for a thin strip of the illuminated side of the moon to become visible.

**"Piety Is the Reverent"**

During the dark moon phase, the sun and moon set together over the western horizon, but at the start of a new lunar month, the sun sets first, leaving the moon just above the horizon for a short time. If the moon is far enough out of line between the earth and the sun, the crescent will become visible before the moon follows the sun below the horizon and disappears. The sighting of this new crescent begins the new lunar month in the Islamic calendar. (In the last part of the lunar month leading up to the dark moon, the moon “sets” before the sun and is below the horizon until it rises later in the night.)

**A journey ordered toward Allah**

On the question of entering houses from their rear or by their doors, it was reported that the Helpers, or Anṣār (the natives of Madinah who had become Muslims), would not enter their houses by their doors when returning from a journey, but would go over a wall instead in order to enter the house (Ibn Kathīr). Others relate that the Anṣār and the rest of the Arabs (other than the religious confederation called the Ḥums, which included the Quraysh; see v. 199) would not enter through their doors in a state of iḥrām (a state of ritual purity in preparation for pilgrimage). Others say that when the people of Yathrib would return from a festival, they would enter their houses from the back, considering this to be a pious act. This verse brought the emphasis back to reverence (taqwā) over a superstitious practice (Ibn Kathīr).

**The sacred limit in the command**

This verse is interpreted spiritually in many Islamic sources to mean that everything should be approached properly and in conformity with its essential nature. Ibn ʿAjībah, for example, notes in his commentary on this verse that spiritual wayfarers on their journey to Allah have three houses whose gates they must pass through: the Law (sharīʿah), the Spiritual Path (ṭarīqah), and the Truth (ḥaqīqah), each of which has three gates. The three gates of the Law are repentance, obedience, and reverence; those of the Path are sincerity, the purification from faults, and the realization of virtue; and the three gates of the Truth (also translated as the Reality) are concentration, contemplation, and gnosis (spiritual knowledge). More precisely, human beings should approach Allah through the means He accepts, the means He has given them.

**Ritual detail and spiritual purpose**

The treatment of prophet joins movement through a place or ritual with movement of the heart. Sacred practice has boundaries precisely because it is not ordinary travel or ordinary habit. The limits teach the pilgrim to receive meaning rather than manufacture it, while the concessions show that obedience is addressed to human beings with real capacities and pressures.

The standard in 2:189 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**What pilgrims must leave behind**

The wording of 2:189 gives the reader a useful order of questions about prophet. What does the term prophet mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:189? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:189 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:189.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:189. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:189 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between prophet and means.

**The community gathered by one sign**

In 2:189, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of prophet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:189 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:189 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:189, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The jurists’ concern for capacity**

In 2:189, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:189, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:189 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:189 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:189, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:189 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:189 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:189 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:189 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:189, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
