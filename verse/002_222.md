## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:222

> **They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about menstruation. Say, “Beware of its harm! So keep away, and do not have intercourse with your wives during their monthly cycles until they are purified. When they purify themselves, then you may approach them in the manner specified by Allah. Surely Allah loves those who always turn to Him in repentance and those who purify themselves.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**"They Ask You About the Monthly Course"**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings prophet and harm into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:222 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:222 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

This verse is understood to prohibit vaginal intercourse during menstruation without prohibiting other contact. The commentators mention that before Islam menstruating women would not sleep in the same bed or eat from the same plate as others, Arab customs that may have been influenced by Jewish (Leviticus 15:19–33) or Zoroastrian (Avesta, Vendidad 16) prescriptions and proscriptions, which mandated a relatively high level of separation for menstruating women and the ritual purification of objects that they had touched (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī).

**Purified — and Allah's Own Command**

For some the hurt (adha) refers to the discomfort it causes women (al-Qurṭubī); many others understand it to mean “uncleanness” specifically relating to the menstrual blood (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī); still others simply gloss the hurt as a reference to the blood without attaching the notion of uncleanness to it.

Some interpret until they are purified to mean the cessation of the flow of menstrual blood. Most schools of law understand this verse to mean that a woman, at the end of her period, should perform the major ablution, or ghusl, which entails a washing of the entire body ritually with water, before having intercourse. Under certain circumstances the Ḥanafī school allows intercourse after the cessation of blood, but before the major ablution, or ghusl. There is no formal expiation or punishment for having intercourse during menstruation, although some monetary payment to the poor such as a dīnār or half a dīnār is sometimes mentioned in this connection (al-Qurṭubī). It is not considered a major act of disobedience, although pious Muslims refrain from it.

**Loves the Repenters, Loves the Purified**

Other actions generally prohibited for menstruating women include the canonical prayer (ṣalāh), fasting, circumambulation of the Kaʿbah during the ḥajj or ʿumrah, reciting the Qur'an aloud, and physically touching the Qur'an. Also restricted are remaining inside the mosque (rather than passing through) and retreat in the mosque (iʿtikāf), although these seem to have more to do with blood staining the mosque than any other concern. In this vein, the Prophet asked his wife to bring him a mat from the mosque. She said, “I am menstruating.” And he said, “Your menses are not in your hands.”

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Go in unto them in the way Allah has commanded you refers to resuming vaginal intercourse, which is forbidden during menses. Jurists are unanimous in forbidding intercourse during menstruation as well as anal sex at any time, but in general have allowed other types of physical affection and fondling during menstruation. In many aḥādīth it is reported that the Prophet would not stay aloof from his wives during their periods, but would show them physical affection such as kissing and fondling, short of engaging in intercourse. Other than ensuring that an item be cleaned if menstrual blood comes in contact with it, Islamic teaching does not deem the touch of a menstruating woman to be unclean or to render anything unclean, although she should not touch the Qur'an.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to prophet widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:222 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:222: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if prophet were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:222 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:222 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:222.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The relation between prophet and harm also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:222 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:222 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:222 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:222 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:222 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:222, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

**The deed before its final account**

The final emphasis of 2:222 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:222 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:222 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:222 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:222, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:222 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:222 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:222. The verse 2:222 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:222 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands harm, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:222.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:222 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prophet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating prophet from the rest of life. In 2:222, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about prophet. They are the place in which assent to 2:222 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.
