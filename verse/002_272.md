## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:272

> **You are not responsible for people’s guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills. Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Not Upon Thee Is Their Guidance**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings responsible and prophet into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:272 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:272 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**For Yourselves, Seeking the Face**

This verse uncouples charity from religious identity. Various occasions for its revelation are given. Some relate that the Prophet used to give charity to all, but when the impoverished among the Muslim community grew large, he restricted charity to Muslims only, at which time this verse was revealed (al-Qurṭubī). In another version, a Companion had grandparents who were idolaters and who asked her for charity, but she did not wish to give it to them until she asked the Prophet, at which time this verse was revealed. In yet another version, some Companions had relatives among the Jewish tribe of Qurayẓah and would not give them charity unless they became Muslim .

**The Tradition's Seal of Charity-to-Anyone**

A similar message is found in 60:8: Allah does not forbid you, with regard to those who did not fight you on account of religion and did not expel you from your homes, from treating them righteously and being just toward them. That the Prophet himself is not responsible for whether people accept guidance is also related in 28:56: Surely thou dost not guide whomsoever thou lovest, but Allah guides whomsoever He will; see also 3:20; 5:92, 99; 13:40; 16:82; 29:18; 36:17; 42:48; 64:12; 88:21–22.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The Qur'an reiterates that Allah does not need humanitys good works, as in 41:46: Whosoever works righteousness, it is for his own soul (also 45:15). In a long ḥadīth, it is related that a man gave charity to someone at night, only to learn the next day that he had given it to an adulterer; the same thing happened the next night with a rich man, and then the next night with a thief. Each time people began to talk about it, but he was told that his charity was accepted and that perhaps it would keep the adulterer from his vices, inspire the rich man to give, and keep the thief from stealing. Shall be paid to you in full refers to the reward one receives in the Hereafter for ones good works.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The idea of seeking the Face of Allah also appears in 30:38–39, which speaks of those who desire the Face of Allah (also see 76:9). On the use of the phrase Face of Allah, also see 2:115c; 55:26–27c.

The subject of responsible joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:272, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:272 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if responsible were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:272 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:272 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:272.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The relation between responsible and prophet also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:272 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:272 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:272 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:272 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:272 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: responsible is joined to prophet, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The wording of 2:272 gives the reader a useful order of questions about responsible. What does the term responsible mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:272? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:272 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:272.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:272. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:272 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between responsible and prophet.

**A community shaped by obedience**

In 2:272, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of responsible while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:272 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:272 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for responsible, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:272, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:272, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:272, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:272 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:272 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:272, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine responsible until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:272 returns to responsibility. No explanation of responsible is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:272 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word responsible in 2:272 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:272 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:272, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
