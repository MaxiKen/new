## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:233

> **˹Divorced˺ mothers will breastfeed their offspring for two whole years, for those who wish to complete the nursing ˹of their child˺. The child’s father will provide reasonable maintenance and clothing for the mother ˹during that period˺. No one will be charged with more than they can bear. No mother or father should be made to suffer for their child. The ˹father’s˺ heirs are under the same obligation. But if both sides decide—after mutual consultation and consent—to wean a child, then there is no blame on them. And if you decide to have your children nursed by a wet-nurse, it is permissible as long as you pay fairly. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Two Full Years — The Nursing Statute**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between divorced and offspring. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:233. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:233. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:233.

**The Father's Full Charge**

This verse addresses the status of women who bear children from husbands who have divorced them, since feeding and clothing ones wife is an obligation of marriage in all cases. Two full years is the maximum time period during which the father must provide material support to the nursing mother, although the parents can agree to a shorter period of nursing, as stated later in the verse, by their mutual consent and consultation. The mother can nurse for a shorter period, and the father cannot compel her to continue, but he must provide support if necessary for up to two years of nursing (al-Qurṭubī).

**No Harm's Two Sides**

That the father must provide for them and clothe them means that he must, according to his means, provide what he can. The verse acknowledges that not all fathers can provide equally, since one must do so honorably (bil-maʿrūf), meaning to an honorable or morally acceptable level, which is reinforced by the recognition that no soul is tasked beyond its capacity (cf. 2:286; 6:152; 7:42; 23:62; 65:7). See also 46:15 and 31:14, which discuss the length of pregnancy and nursing.

That neither parent be harmed on account of the child relates to either nursing or custody, or both. The mother is not to harm the father by refusing to nurse his child or by trying to keep the father away, and similarly the father is not to harm the mother by keeping her child from her and refusing to let her nurse the child when she wishes to do so (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī).

**Weaning by Consent and Consultation**

Several opinions are offered regarding the identity of the heir who has obligations similar to those of the father, should the latter pass away. It is generally understood to refer to the heirs of the father, who inherit a portion of the wealth upon which the nursing mother and child have a claim. Upon the father’s death, during the two-year period, these heirs are responsible for the material support prescribed in this verse (al-Qurṭubī). Others say it refers to agnates (ʿaṣabah, male relatives through the male line), and others consider it to mean any relative too close in blood relation to marry (al-Qurṭubī).

**A household placed under moral care**

Another opinion states that the heir is the child himself or herself, in which case the support would come from what the child inherits from the father, but if there are no such funds, it falls upon the state to support the child, and if the state does not, it falls upon the community (al-Qurṭubī). Some interpret the heir’s obligation to include only the intent not to harm the parent by means of the child, while others say the heir’s obligation also includes the material support to the mother the father would have been obligated to provide (al-Qurṭubī).

If both parents agree, the child can have a wet nurse, who would be paid for by the father, not the mother (al-Qurṭubī).

**The rights concealed in the wording**

Most jurists agree that, so long as she does not remarry (presumably because they reason a father would not willingly allow his child to be raised by another man), the mother has greater right to custody and to raise the child than does the father. There are varying opinions on this matter, and some allow the child to choose after a certain age (al-Qurṭubī).

The reference to divorced places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The standard in 2:233 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from divorced: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

In 2:233, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of divorced while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:233 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The difference between power and justice**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:233 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for divorced, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:233, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:233, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:233, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:233 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**How the jurists read the command**

A reader who finds the command in 2:233 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:233, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine divorced until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:233 returns to responsibility. No explanation of divorced is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:233 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word divorced in 2:233 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:233 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:233, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches divorced from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:233 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:233 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding divorced and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:233. The verse 2:233 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
