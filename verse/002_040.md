## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:40

> **O children of Israel! Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine, and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**A New Address to an Old Community**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings children and upon into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:40 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:40 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Remember My Niʿmah": Which Blessing?**

The Blessing (niʿmah; see also vv. 47, 122) is usually understood to be the deliverance from Pharaoh (vv. 49–50) and the subsequent heavenly gifts of manna, quails, and the promised land; or that Allah placed prophets among them and sent them Divine books; or the coming of Islam, since this verse addresses the Jews in and around Madinah. Niʿmah is understood in the most general sense as well, beginning with the gift of creation, in keeping with the universal commands of the following verses. A range of possible meaning exists for the covenant (ʿahd), which is understood broadly as peoples gratitude, on the one hand, and Allahs Forgiveness and Reward, on the other.

**The Covenant Clause: Fulfill, and Be Fulfilled**

Another interpretation is that it refers to the mīthāq (covenant or pact) of 5:12: Allah had made a covenant with the Children of Israel, and We raised among them twelve chieftains. Some interpret it to mean that they should be true to the predicted coming of the Prophet, whom they find inscribed in the Torah and the Gospel that is with them (7:157; Q).

The concern with children shows that justice is measured most severely where affection, dependence, and power overlap. A person may be generous in public and still wrong a relative or a child in private. By naming the relationship, the verse makes invisible duties visible and gives moral language to the person whose claim might otherwise be dismissed.

**"And of Me Be in Awe"**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if children were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:40 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:40 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:40.

**A Verse for Every Community That Remembers**

The relation between children and upon also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:40 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:40 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:40 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:40 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:40 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:40, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine children until the demand disappears.

**A duty that begins with the vulnerable**

The final emphasis of 2:40 returns to responsibility. No explanation of children is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:40 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word children in 2:40 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:40 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:40, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The relationship named by the verse**

The exegetical tradition approaches children from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:40 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:40 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding children and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:40. The verse 2:40 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Rights before sentiment**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:40 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands upon, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:40.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:40 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving children; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**How neglect becomes wrongdoing**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating children from the rest of life. In 2:40, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about children. They are the place in which assent to 2:40 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:40 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: children is joined to upon, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:40 gives the reader a useful order of questions about children. What does the term children mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:40? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:40 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:40.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:40. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:40 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between children and upon.

In 2:40, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of children while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:40 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.
