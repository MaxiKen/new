## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:232

> **When you have divorced women and they have reached the end of their waiting period, do not prevent them from re-marrying their exhusbands if they come to an honourable agreement. This is enjoined on whoever has faith in Allah and the Last Day. This is purer and more dignifying for you. Allah knows and you do not know.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Do Not Hinder Them" — The Right Renewed**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings divorced and waiting into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:232 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:232 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

It is said that this verse was revealed when a man balked at the idea of his sister, who was divorced and single, remarrying the man who had divorced her (al-Ṭabarī). This verse allows a couple to remarry even after they have gone through a complete legal divorce, but not in the case of the third consecutive divorce declaration; on the matter of remarrying after the third divorce declaration (ṭalāq), see 2:229c.

**"More Virtuous and Purer for You"**

The reference to divorced places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if divorced were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:232 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:232 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:232.

**A household placed under moral care**

The relation between divorced and waiting also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:232 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:232 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:232 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:232 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:232. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:232 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between divorced and waiting.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

In 2:232, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of divorced while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:232 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:232 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for divorced, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:232, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

In 2:232, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:232, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:232 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:232 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:232, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine divorced until the demand disappears.

**The difference between power and justice**

The final emphasis of 2:232 returns to responsibility. No explanation of divorced is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:232 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word divorced in 2:232 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:232 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:232, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**How the jurists read the command**

The exegetical tradition approaches divorced from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:232 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:232 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding divorced and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:232. The verse 2:232 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Protection from private harm**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:232 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands waiting, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:232.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:232 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving divorced; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Mercy within a legal boundary**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating divorced from the rest of life. In 2:232, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about divorced. They are the place in which assent to 2:232 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:232 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: divorced is joined to waiting, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:232 gives the reader a useful order of questions about divorced. What does the term divorced mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:232? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:232 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:232.
