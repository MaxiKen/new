## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:54

> **And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.” Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Severest Prescription in Scripture**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings moses and worshipping into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:54 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:54 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Why Such Severity? Justice as Pedagogy**

Cf. Exodus 32:27. Slay your own, in light of similar language elsewhere (cf. 2:85; 4:29), is best understood as the killing of the guilty among the Israelites by their fellow Israelites, in which case the severity of the punishment would have been all the more terrible. Some commentators add that the killed were martyrs, and the killers had their repentance accepted by them, meaning all were forgiven, so that the episode of killing their own would have been, according to this interpretation, a kind of collective punishment in itself rather than a punishment of the guilty by the innocent. Slay your own is literally slay yourselves, and indeed some view the inward meaning to be that the Israelites should slay their own egos in the spiritual sense of opposing their passions (IA, Iṣ).

**"That Is Better for You in the Sight of Your Maker"**

The reference to moses turns memory into a demand. A community can recount a rescue, a miracle, or a failure and still repeat the same error if it treats history as the property of ancestors. In this passage the earlier event becomes evidence addressed to the present: blessing creates a duty, and a broken promise leaves a recognizable pattern.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if moses were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:54 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:54 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:54.

**Islam's Reception of the Lesson**

The relation between moses and worshipping also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:54 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:54 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:54 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:54 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

A reader who finds the command in 2:54 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:54, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine moses until the demand disappears.

**History as a present warning**

The final emphasis of 2:54 returns to responsibility. No explanation of moses is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:54 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word moses in 2:54 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:54 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:54, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The covenant behind the event**

The exegetical tradition approaches moses from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:54 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:54 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding moses and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:54. The verse 2:54 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Memory turned into responsibility**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:54 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands worshipping, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:54.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:54 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving moses; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The people and the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating moses from the rest of life. In 2:54, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about moses. They are the place in which assent to 2:54 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:54 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: moses is joined to worshipping, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**What the exegetes disputed**

The wording of 2:54 gives the reader a useful order of questions about moses. What does the term moses mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:54? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:54 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:54.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:54. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:54 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between moses and worshipping.

In 2:54, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of moses while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:54 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:54 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for moses, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:54, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
