## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:274

> **Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly—their reward is with their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Wageworkers of Both Shifts**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings spend and night into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:274 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:274 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

This is the last verse in the section vv. 260–74, whose main theme is the giving of charity. Beyond its general import, some commentators interpret this verse to be referring to specific people, for example, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, who are reported to have given a certain sum in the fashion described in this verse, giving one quarter of it by night, one by day, one secretly, and one openly .

**"Their Reward Is With Their Lord" — The Antipodal Final-refrain**

The subject of spend joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:274, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:274 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if spend were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:274 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:274 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:274.

**No Fear, No Grief — The Finale's Cry**

The relation between spend and night also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:274 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:274 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:274 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:274 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:274. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:274 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between spend and night.

In 2:274, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of spend while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:274 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:274 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for spend, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:274, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:274, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:274, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:274 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

A reader who finds the command in 2:274 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:274, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine spend until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:274 returns to responsibility. No explanation of spend is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:274 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The choice of the word spend in 2:274 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:274 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:274, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches spend from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:274 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:274 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The poor person’s dignity**

There is also a distinction between understanding spend and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:274. The verse 2:274 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:274 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands night, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:274.

**A community shaped by obedience**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:274 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving spend; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating spend from the rest of life. In 2:274, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about spend. They are the place in which assent to 2:274 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:274 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: spend is joined to night, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:274 gives the reader a useful order of questions about spend. What does the term spend mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:274? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:274 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:274.
