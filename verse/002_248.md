## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:248

> **Their prophet further told them, “The sign of Saul’s kingship is that the Ark will come to you—containing reassurance from your Lord and relics of the family of Moses and the family of Aaron, which will be carried by the angels. Surely in this is a sign for you, if you ˹truly˺ believe.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Ark as Credential**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings prophet and sauls into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:248 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:248 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Sakīnah — The Tranquility Word's Winged History**

The commentators relate that the ark was returned, after being either lost or taken in battle, as a sign of Sauls right to lead, though in the case of this passage much or all of the information in the commentaries seems to be based on Biblical or Talmudic material. In the Biblical account, the ark was returned from its capture by the Philistines after they were afflicted with plagues and other torments, but it resided in a private dwelling for many years in Kiriath-jearim, northwest of Jerusalem. Biblical commentators point out that during this time the Israelites were in a state of religious decline and had grown indifferent to the ark; during this period Samuel labored to revive the religion. Indeed, in 1 Chronicles 13:3 David says, We did not turn to it [the ark] in the days of Saul. The fate of the ark during the time of Saul is not described in the Bible except with regard to its neglect.

**A Sign — If They Be Believers**

In v. 248, the ark reenters the life of the Israelites in a way that functions as a sign for Samuel’s truthful prophecy regarding Saul, but no details or chronology are given. From the Qur'anic perspective, it could mean that the chronology conventionally understood from 1 Samuel, in which the ark returns many years before Saul’s anointing, is a misreading—which is possible if one considers the history given in Samuel as that of overlapping strands rather than a linear chronology—and that the ark returned around the time Samuel designated Saul as king.

**A duty that begins with the vulnerable**

That an old but neglected sacred object should return could have had a special significance to the Israelites, leading them to rediscover a part of their own sacred history. Alternatively, it could it be that the prophet (Samuel), in pointing to the ark as a sign, was in effect saying, “The ark has returned to you while I was your judge; so believe my prophecy about Saul” (al-Ṭabarī). In one Muslim account the Israelites, except for a single family who kept the ark, were prevented from coming near the ark from the time of its return from the Philistines until the time of Saul; some understood this to mean that, even though it was not in the possession of another people, the ark and its blessings were inaccessible to the Israelites (al-Ṭabarī).

**The relationship named by the verse**

The tranquility (sakīnah) within it may refer to either an object that would arouse tranquility in the hearts of the Israelites or something, properly referred to as sakīnah, that was contained within it. Some point out that the grammar of the phrase would allow the translation that the ark come to you on account of which there will be tranquility (al-Rāzī). This tranquility (sakīnah) is also mentioned in 9:26, 40; 48:4, 18, 26. If understood as something whose presence would cause tranquility, it might refer to prophecies from books of Moses and Aaron telling of the victories to which Saul would lead them (al-Ṭabarī).

The remnant of Moses and Aaron is given several interpretations, among them the staff of Moses (v. 60), the fragments of stone tablets Moses brought from Sinai (7:150), the manna (see v. 57), and the clothing of Moses and Aaron, including Moses sandals (see 20:12; Q, Ṭ).

**Rights before sentiment**

The concern with prophet shows that justice is measured most severely where affection, dependence, and power overlap. A person may be generous in public and still wrong a relative or a child in private. By naming the relationship, the verse makes invisible duties visible and gives moral language to the person whose claim might otherwise be dismissed.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if prophet were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:248 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:248 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:248.

**How neglect becomes wrongdoing**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:248 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:248, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:248, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:248, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:248 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The legal protection in the command**

A reader who finds the command in 2:248 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:248, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:248 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:248 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**A family obligation with social reach**

The choice of the word prophet in 2:248 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:248 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:248, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:248 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:248 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:248. The verse 2:248 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:248 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands sauls, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:248.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:248 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prophet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
