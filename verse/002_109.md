## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:109

> **Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon and bear with them until Allah delivers His decision. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Wish Driven by Envy**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between many and turn. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:109. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:109. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:109.

Some connect this verse with formerly Jewish Companions who, after the Battle of Uḥud, were taunted by their former coreligionists about their condition, which turned them away from Islam and back to Judaism . Cf. 3:186: You will surely be tried in your wealth and your souls, and you shall hear much hurt from those who were given the Book before you, and from those who are idolaters. But if you are patient and reverent, then that is indeed a course worthy of resolve.

**"Pardon and Forbear" — the Command to Bear**

On the question of envy, in a ḥadīth the Prophet said, Let there be no envy except in two things: a man who has been given wealth by Allah and then spends it in the way of Allah, and a man who has been given knowledge by Allah and acts in accord with it and imparts it to people. The notion of envy after exposure to the truth is discussed in 3:19; envy is also mentioned in 2:213; 4:54; 10:90; 42:14; 45:17. The great sin of Satan is often understood to be envy, as shown in 38:76, where Iblīs says of Adam, I am better than him. Some understand the truth that was clear to them to be the fact that Muhammad was a true prophet , although in 3:19 the truth that causes dissension among the People of the Book is understood as revelation considered more broadly.

**"Allah Is Powerful over All Things" — the Promise underneath**

In legal terms, it is thought that this verses command to pardon and forbear was abrogated by 9:29, which addresses the question of jizyah, or indemnity paid by the People of the Book: Fight those who believe not in Allah and in the Last Day, and who do not forbid what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and who follow not the Religion of Truth among those who were given the Book, till they pay the jizyah with a willing hand, being humbled . However, as al-Rāzī argues, if this verse is interpreted as a command to perform a certain kind of virtuous behavior, then it is not subject to abrogation, as it is not a legal ruling (ḥukm).

**The Book as an address**

Some argue that the first verse to permit fighting (and by implication to abrogate 2:109) was 22:39: Permission is granted to those who are fought, because they have been wronged. Others believe 9:5 first allowed fighting. The range of opinion on abrogation is perhaps no more relevant than in the matter of war. An expansive view of legal abrogation allows for the many verses dealing with peace and forbearance to be abrogated by those authorizing fighting, which leads to dozens of verses being summarily abrogated. However, as al-Rāzī points out, at least in this particular case, the question of abrogation need not arise; this verse commanding forbearance and tolerance does not contradict the kind of just-war commands related to fighting elsewhere, since even in fighting Muslims are commanded to practice similar virtues, as vv. 190–94 demonstrate. For a fuller discussion of such matters, see commentary on 9:1–5; and the essays The Qur'anic View of Sacred History and Other Religions and Conquest and Conversion, War and Peace in the Qur'an.

**The word that carries the argument**

Until Allah comes with His Command could also be rendered until Allah brings His Command. The Command could indicate the military defeat of either the Quraysh or those Jews who opposed the Prophet or refer to the legal status afforded the People of the Book in 9:29, mentioned above .

The wording around many assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

The standard in 2:109 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from many: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

In 2:109, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:109, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:109 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The reader’s responsibility**

A reader who finds the command in 2:109 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:109, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine many until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:109 returns to responsibility. No explanation of many is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:109 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Why the wording is exact**

The choice of the word many in 2:109 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:109 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:109, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches many from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:109 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:109 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**A question answered by the surrounding verses**

There is also a distinction between understanding many and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:109. The verse 2:109 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:109 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands turn, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:109.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:109 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving many; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
