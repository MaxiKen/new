## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:76

> **When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?”**

### Expanded Commentary

**Two Faces, Two Speeches**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings meet and disclose into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:76 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:76 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"What Allah Has Unveiled to You" — The Threshold They Knew They Stood At**

This verse refers to a group of Jewish hypocrites (see Sūrah 63) in Madinah . They said We believe, but according to some commentators they really meant only that they acknowledged that Muhammad was a prophet, but not a prophet to the Jews (al-Ṭabarī). What Allah has unveiled to you can mean “what Allah has commanded you” or “what Allah has decided for you” (al-Ṭabarī) or “what knowledge Allah has given you or made easy for you to obtain” (al-Rāzī). Another opinion is that the phrase refers to the prophecies foretelling the coming of the Prophet, so that the first question in this verse would mean, “Do you affirm that he is a prophet, which he can then use to argue against you?” (al-Ṭabarī).

**The Exposure of Double Speech as Ongoing Good News**

Before your Lord (ʿinda rabbikum) is understood to mean that on the Day of Judgment there will be a mutual questioning or interrogation before Allah. According to some exegetes it can also be read, concerning or with regard to your Lord. Others see a similarity in usage between in the sight of your Lord (which is another possible translation of ʿinda rabbikum) and in the Book of Allah. It is argued that these two phrases are often used equivalently, the way Allah commands us and Allah commands us in the Qur'an are . Other exegetes mention that a group of Jews were believers, but then became hypocrites, and this verse describes how their leaders rebuked them for telling the Muslims about the punishments they had endured, because then the Muslims would revile them and say that they were more noble than the Jews in the Sight of Allah . For a similar exposure of secret behavior among the Prophets antagonists, see 2:14; 3:119.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The verse describes meet not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:76, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:76, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if meet were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:76 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:76 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:76.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The relation between meet and disclose also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:76 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:76 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:76 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:76 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:76 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: meet is joined to disclose, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The wording of 2:76 gives the reader a useful order of questions about meet. What does the term meet mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:76? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:76 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:76.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:76. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:76 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between meet and disclose.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

In 2:76, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of meet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:76 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:76 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for meet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:76, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:76, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:76, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:76 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:76 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:76, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine meet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:76 returns to responsibility. No explanation of meet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:76 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The choice of the word meet in 2:76 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:76 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:76, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches meet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:76 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:76 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding meet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:76. The verse 2:76 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
