## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:146

> **Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Recognition as Sure as a Father's of His Child**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings scripture and children into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:146 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:146 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Tragedy of Recognition Concealed**

Some commentators understand that what they recognize . . . as they recognize their children is the fact that the Kaʿbah is the true qiblah (al-Ṭabarī), evidently following the similar message in v. 144, where they are said to know that it is the truth. Others think that recognize it means “recognize him,” referring to the Prophet, who is known not only through the prophecies in the Bible, but also (according to the Islamic view) through his presence, his character, the signs he brought, and the truth of his message . Thus recognizing the truth of the Kaʿbah as the qiblah would be dependent upon the recognition of the truthfulness of the Prophet, rather than upon the status of the Kaʿbah itself, since Jerusalem was the legitimate qiblah for a time.

**Lessons for the Readers of Every Age**

The concern with scripture shows that justice is measured most severely where affection, dependence, and power overlap. A person may be generous in public and still wrong a relative or a child in private. By naming the relationship, the verse makes invisible duties visible and gives moral language to the person whose claim might otherwise be dismissed.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if scripture were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:146 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:146 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:146.

**A duty that begins with the vulnerable**

The relation between scripture and children also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:146 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:146 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:146 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:146 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The most balanced response to 2:146 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: scripture is joined to children, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The relationship named by the verse**

The wording of 2:146 gives the reader a useful order of questions about scripture. What does the term scripture mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:146? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:146 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:146.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:146. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:146 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between scripture and children.

**Rights before sentiment**

In 2:146, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of scripture while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:146 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:146 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for scripture, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:146, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**How neglect becomes wrongdoing**

In 2:146, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:146, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:146 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:146 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:146, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine scripture until the demand disappears.

**The legal protection in the command**

The final emphasis of 2:146 returns to responsibility. No explanation of scripture is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:146 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word scripture in 2:146 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:146 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:146, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**A family obligation with social reach**

The exegetical tradition approaches scripture from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:146 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:146 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding scripture and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:146. The verse 2:146 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:146 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands children, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:146.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:146 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving scripture; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
