## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:266

> **Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will reflect.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Burning Garden — The Parable's Horror**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings wish and trees into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:266 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:266 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**What the Parable Means — The Parable of the Badly-Lived Wealth**

For some this verse is a parable of those who follow up their charitable giving with preening and injury, as mentioned in v. 264, meaning that at the end of their life, they will have nothing of value to leave behind and nothing will bear fruit for them in the Hereafter. Their charity is like the fruits of the garden, and their insults and boasting are like the fiery whirlwind that devours it, and they have no progeny who could protect or support them (al-Rāzī). According to another interpretation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, this verse refers to those who do good works, but afterward turn to evil works, thereby wiping away the value of earlier past actions (al-Qurṭubī). Every kind of fruit can mean something like many kinds of fruit. With rivers running below is a common image symbolizing the paradisal state; see 2:25c.

**Beware the Fire — A Hadith-Matter for Every Age**

The concern with wish shows that justice is measured most severely where affection, dependence, and power overlap. A person may be generous in public and still wrong a relative or a child in private. By naming the relationship, the verse makes invisible duties visible and gives moral language to the person whose claim might otherwise be dismissed.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if wish were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:266 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:266 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:266.

**A duty that begins with the vulnerable**

The relation between wish and trees also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:266 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:266 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:266 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:266 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The relationship named by the verse**

The choice of the word wish in 2:266 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:266 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:266, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches wish from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:266 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:266 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Rights before sentiment**

There is also a distinction between understanding wish and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:266. The verse 2:266 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:266 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands trees, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:266.

**How neglect becomes wrongdoing**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:266 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving wish; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating wish from the rest of life. In 2:266, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about wish. They are the place in which assent to 2:266 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The legal protection in the command**

The most balanced response to 2:266 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: wish is joined to trees, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:266 gives the reader a useful order of questions about wish. What does the term wish mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:266? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:266 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:266.

**A family obligation with social reach**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:266. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:266 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between wish and trees.

In 2:266, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of wish while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:266 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:266 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for wish, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:266, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:266, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:266, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:266 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:266 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:266, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine wish until the demand disappears.
