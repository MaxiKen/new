## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:63

> **And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Mountain Over Their Heads**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between took and mountain. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:63. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:63. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:63.

Cf. v. 93; 4:154. Mount renders ṭūr, which commentators point out is a word of Syriac origin for mountain (jabal; Ṭ), but is nevertheless an Arabic word. There is general agreement among the interpreters that this verse literally means that a mountain, either Sinai or a mountain from Palestine, was uprooted and made to physically move and float over the Israelites, in order to frighten them . However, it seems just as likely that the phrasing here, raised the Mount over you, parallels the English construction the mountain loomed above them.

**"Take Hold with Strength" — The Terms of Take**

Exodus 19 mentions a thick cloud on the mountain and that Mount Sinai was wrapped in smoke, because the Lord had descended upon it in a fire; the smoke went up like the smoke of a kiln, while the whole mountain shook violently (vv. 16, 18). Before this, the Israelites were commanded, Be careful not to go up the mountain or to touch the edge of it. Any who touch the mountain shall be put to death (v. 12). Since the Qur'an does not describe the mountain as floating, but only as rising above the Israelites, one can understand that it loomed over them in a terrifying and meaningful way, an interpretation that is all the more plausible in light of the descriptions from Exodus 19.

**Raised Hands and a Raised Mountain**

To take hold . . . with strength is understood to mean doing so earnestly and obediently and resolving to act upon it . In it means in the Torah given to Moses (Ibn Kathīr).

The verse describes took not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:63, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:63, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

The standard in 2:63 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from took: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between took and mountain also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:63 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:63 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:63 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:63 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The wording of 2:63 gives the reader a useful order of questions about took. What does the term took mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:63? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:63 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:63.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:63. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:63 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between took and mountain.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

In 2:63, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of took while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:63 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:63 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for took, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:63, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

In 2:63, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:63, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:63 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:63 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:63, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine took until the demand disappears.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The final emphasis of 2:63 returns to responsibility. No explanation of took is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:63 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word took in 2:63 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:63 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:63, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The exegetical tradition approaches took from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:63 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:63 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding took and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:63. The verse 2:63 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:63 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands mountain, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:63.
