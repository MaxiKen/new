## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:227

> **But if they settle on divorce, then Allah is indeed All-Hearing, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Second Choice Visible at the Window's Edge**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between settle and all-hearing. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:227. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:227. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:227.

**Hearing, Knowing — The Watchtower Over the Home**

These verses address the practice of a man staying away from his wife but not divorcing her, leaving her without the sexual benefits of marriage and without the freedom to marry another, which in pre-Islamic times could last years. In this context to forswear means to make an oath that one would not have sexual relations with one’s wife. To forswear in this sense is not the same as divorce, and the period of four months sets an upper limit on this abstention within marriage. Specifically, it is understood to draw a line beyond which the abstention is taken as an intent to cause harm to the wife (al-Rāzī).

**The Function It Serves in the Book of Divorce**

If they return means that they resume intercourse; the return is still valid if one intends to return to one’s wife, but is hindered for some reason, as the intent to cause harm no longer exists, while for some it simply means that one bear witness that he has returned (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). Many understand Allah is Forgiving, Merciful in this context to indicate that no expiation is necessary if they return (al-Ṭabarī), though others believe some form of expiation, such as feeding the poor, is necessary, as returning to one’s wife would then constitute going back on the oath of staying away from her (al-Ṭabarī).

**A household placed under moral care**

Some say that these stipulations only apply when the oath is made in anger with the intent to cause harm, and that when someone swears not to have sexual relations for some other reason, for example, with the intention of protecting the well-being of a nursing child, the ruling of this verse does not apply. Others say that the forswearing mentioned here can refer to any oath that keeps one from having sexual relations with one’s wife, while others say that the forswearing need not even pertain to sexual relations, but may pertain to any matter where the intent is to cause harm to the wife, such as not speaking to her or touching her (al-Ṭabarī). Perhaps the most serious point of disagreement is whether, upon the completion of the four months, divorce is effected automatically in the eyes of the Law or whether other steps must be taken. Some maintain that after the period of four months, divorce could be imposed by a judge at the request of the wife (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī).

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The reference to settle places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

The standard in 2:227 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from settle: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The relation between settle and all-hearing also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:227 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:227 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:227 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:227 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:227 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands all-hearing, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:227.

**The difference between power and justice**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:227 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving settle; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating settle from the rest of life. In 2:227, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about settle. They are the place in which assent to 2:227 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**How the jurists read the command**

The most balanced response to 2:227 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: settle is joined to all-hearing, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:227 gives the reader a useful order of questions about settle. What does the term settle mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:227? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:227 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:227.

**Protection from private harm**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:227. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:227 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between settle and all-hearing.

In 2:227, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of settle while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:227 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:227 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for settle, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:227, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:227, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:227, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:227 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
