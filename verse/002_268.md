## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:268

> **The Devil threatens you with ˹the prospect of˺ poverty and bids you to the shameful deed ˹of stinginess˺, while Allah promises you forgiveness and ˹great˺ bounties from Him. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Devil's Threat and the Lord's Promise**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings devil and poverty into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:268 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:268 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Promise Against Promise**

Satan makes one dread poverty, causing one to refrain from giving charity, and moreover inspires one to commit other sins and acts of disobedience, whereas Allah causes the soul to remember forgiveness and generosity (Ibn Kathīr). Al-Rāzī argues, for example, that ideally people should give from the best that they have, and that what begins as a desire to part only with the dregs can lead to a fear of giving any charity at all. This miserliness then infects the rest of the soul, which struggles to keep all that it desires while relinquishing nothing of what is of value to him.

**All-Encompassing, Knowing**

One’s passivity to Satan’s promptings then leads to the active commission of other sins, until one gradually ceases doing any good and commits every manner of evil. In this vein, a ḥadīth states, “Satan has an inspiration (lummah) for the son of Adam, and the angel has one. As for Satan’s inspiration, it is a promise to do evil and tell lies against the truth. As for the angel’s inspiration, it is a promise to do good and affirm the truth. When one encounters that, let him know that it is from Allah, and let him praise Allah, and when one encounters the other, let him seek refuge from Satan.” All-Encompassing (al-Wāsiʿ) also carries the meaning of “abundantly Generous in His Forgiveness” (al-Rāzī).

**The first scene of human responsibility**

The theme of devil places human weakness beside human responsibility. The account does not require a fantasy of moral perfection; it asks what a person does after knowledge, temptation, and failure have entered the scene. The important distinction is between being tested and choosing the test's wrong answer, then refusing the return that Allah makes possible.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if devil were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:268 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:268 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:268.

**What the command reveals about the human being**

The relation between devil and poverty also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:268 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:268 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:268 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:268 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

There is also a distinction between understanding devil and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:268. The verse 2:268 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The adversary’s method**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:268 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands poverty, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:268.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:268 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving devil; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Knowledge, temptation, and choice**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating devil from the rest of life. In 2:268, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about devil. They are the place in which assent to 2:268 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:268 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: devil is joined to poverty, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Theological questions around the account**

The wording of 2:268 gives the reader a useful order of questions about devil. What does the term devil mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:268? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:268 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:268.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:268. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:268 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between devil and poverty.

**The mercy that follows failure**

In 2:268, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of devil while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:268 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:268 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for devil, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:268, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:268, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:268, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:268 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:268 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:268, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine devil until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:268 returns to responsibility. No explanation of devil is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:268 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
