## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:277

> **Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Four-Fold Charter**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between indeed and prayer. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:277. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:277. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:277.

Alms, mentioned here and in many other places, are obligatory and an integral dimension of Muslim devotional and social life. As a form of institutional charity, they are contrasted with the moral opposite, ribā (see 2:275–81c). It is reported that the Prophet said, The ultimate result for anyone who engages in ribā, even if that ribā brings growth, is loss. This verse has content similar to that in 2:62 and 5:69.

**Preached Now as the Counter-Sign of the Ribā-Field**

The subject of indeed joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:277, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:277 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:277 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from indeed: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The relation between indeed and prayer also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:277 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:277 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:277 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:277 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:277, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:277, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:277 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

A reader who finds the command in 2:277 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:277, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine indeed until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:277 returns to responsibility. No explanation of indeed is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:277 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The choice of the word indeed in 2:277 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:277 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:277, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches indeed from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:277 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:277 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The measure of sincere giving**

There is also a distinction between understanding indeed and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:277. The verse 2:277 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:277 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands prayer, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:277.

**The poor person’s dignity**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:277 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving indeed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating indeed from the rest of life. In 2:277, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about indeed. They are the place in which assent to 2:277 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The most balanced response to 2:277 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: indeed is joined to prayer, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:277 gives the reader a useful order of questions about indeed. What does the term indeed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:277? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:277 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:277.

**The legal and spiritual balance**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:277. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:277 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between indeed and prayer.

In 2:277, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of indeed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:277 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:277 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for indeed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:277, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
