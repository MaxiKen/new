## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:3

> **who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them,**

### Expanded Commentary

**The First Portrait Begins with the Invisible**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between unseen and donate. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:3. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:3. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:3.

Unseen (ghayb, lit. “absent”) refers to realities absent from the perception of the ordinary senses, such realities as Allah, Paradise, Hell, and the Day of Judgment. These include realities that are invisible in principle because they are beyond ordinary sense perception, such as Allah, as well as those that are part of the Unseen because they cannot be known except by Allah, such as the time of the Day of Judgment.

**Against the Tyranny of the Visible**

Perform in perform the prayer can also be read as institute, establish, or maintain. In the Qur'an the word for prayer, ṣalāh, is used in at least two ways, one referring to what believers offer to Allah and the other to the invocation of blessing, as in 33:56, where believers are commanded to make ṣalāh (invoke blessings) upon the Prophet. Ṣalāh has taken on a technical sense when used for the recitations and movements proper to the five daily canonical prayers, but it also refers to prayer more generally, as in 19:31, where Jesus says that Allah has enjoined upon him prayer, or 20:14, where Moses is also commanded to perform the prayer.

**From Inward Certitude to the Standing Pillar**

Spend (infāq) means to expend, but also carries the sense of giving charitably and is used throughout the Qur'an for those who give in the way of Allah (for similar wording, see 8:3; 14:31; 22:35; 28:54; 32:16). This parallels the even more frequent pairing of the prayer (ṣalāh) and alms (zakāh). The passage can be seen as moving from an inward act whose object is invisible and hidden (faith in the Unseen), to an act that is both visible and invisible (prayer) but whose object is invisible (Allah), to an outward act whose object is visible (giving from what one has been given), thereby establishing principles for both the inner and the outer life as well as their source and connection. There is thus in this verse a gradation from the inward to the outward.

**The Complete Circuit of a Faith**

The subject of unseen joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:3, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:3 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:3 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from unseen: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The relation between unseen and donate also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:3 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:3 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:3 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:3 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:3 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands donate, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:3.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:3 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving unseen; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating unseen from the rest of life. In 2:3, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about unseen. They are the place in which assent to 2:3 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The most balanced response to 2:3 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: unseen is joined to donate, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:3 gives the reader a useful order of questions about unseen. What does the term unseen mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:3? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:3 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:3.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:3. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:3 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between unseen and donate.

In 2:3, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of unseen while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:3 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The poor person’s dignity**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:3 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for unseen, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:3, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:3, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:3, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:3 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:3 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:3, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine unseen until the demand disappears.
