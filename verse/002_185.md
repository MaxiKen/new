## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:185

> **Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. So whoever is present this month, let them fast. But whoever is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Ramadan-Named: The Month of the Revelation**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between ramaḍân and revealed. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:185. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:185. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:185.

**Fast It — and the Exemptions of Mercy**

Some mention accounts that the Qur'an first came down to the earthly heaven (i.e., the lowest of the seven heavens) from the Preserved Tablet (85:22) and remained there and then Gabriel brought it down passage by passage to the Prophet (al-Qurṭubī); see 97:1–2c. Muslims consider Ramadan a particularly blessed month, both because it is the month of fasting and also because it is the month when the Qur'an began to be revealed. In a ḥadīth, the Prophet said, When Ramadan comes, the gates of the Garden are opened, and the gates of the Fire are locked, and the satans are fettered.

**"Allah Desires Ease for You"**

When people fall ill or are traveling, they have the option of breaking the fast and fasting on another day after Ramadan in order to make up for it. Opinions range on how sick one must be and what type of journey qualifies. These exemptions apply in another manner to the canonical prayers, which one can shorten and combine when one is on a journey. To complete the number can mean to make up all missed days of fasting by fasting on days other than those of Ramadan or simply to complete the full number of days in the lunar month itself, which is twenty-nine or thirty days.

**Completing the Count, Magnifying the Allah**

Magnify Allah is thought to refer to the practice of chanting extra formulas of takbīr (the chanting of Allāhu akbar, meaning “Allah is great!” or “Allah is greater” or “Allah is the greatest”), which takes place after the fast of Ramadan is over. This magnification takes different forms, such as chanting formulas of takbīr while on the way to the mosque for the ʿĪd prayer (the prayers in the morning after the last day of Ramadan) or repeating magnifications after each prayer during the three days of ʿĪd. Examples are “Allah is great, Allah is great, there is no god but Allah, Allah is great, Allah be praised!” and “Allah is great and grand, and praised be He often, and glorified be He day and night!” See also v. 203.

**The discipline named by the verse**

The exemptions from the rules of fasting (for the old, sick, traveling, etc.) are part of the ease that Allah bestows; the Qur'an states that Allah does not desire hardship for you, which is also mentioned in 22:78: He . . . has placed no hardship for you in the religion (Sh). In this regard, some mention the ḥadīth of the Prophet, Make things easy, not difficult. Cause people to rejoice, not to flee.

So that you may complete either is connected with the previous sentence and refers to more of what Allah desires or is a continuation of the command to fast from v. 183, so that it would mean, “Fasting is prescribed for you . . . so that you may complete” (al-Zamakhsharī).

**Hunger, desire, and conscious worship**

The verse's concern with ramaḍân shows why religious restraint is not meant to produce weakness or despair. Desire is acknowledged rather than denied, then given a disciplined place. The worshipper learns to distinguish a genuine need from an impulse, and a lawful ease from the kind of indulgence that makes obedience meaningless.

The standard in 2:185 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from ramaḍân: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The room made for repentance**

The relation between ramaḍân and revealed also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:185 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:185 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:185 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:185 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:185 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands revealed, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:185.

**A private act with public consequences**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:185 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving ramaḍân; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating ramaḍân from the rest of life. In 2:185, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about ramaḍân. They are the place in which assent to 2:185 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The legal condition and its purpose**

The most balanced response to 2:185 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: ramaḍân is joined to revealed, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:185 gives the reader a useful order of questions about ramaḍân. What does the term ramaḍân mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:185? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:185 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:185.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:185. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:185 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between ramaḍân and revealed.

In 2:185, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of ramaḍân while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:185 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:185 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for ramaḍân, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:185, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
