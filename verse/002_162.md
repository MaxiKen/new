## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:162

> **They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Three Fixed Endings**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings hell and lightened into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:162 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:162 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

That the punishment will not be lightened is also mentioned in 2:86; 3:88 (same wording as here); 16:85; 35:36; 43:75. On the abiding in Hell and its duration and related issues, see commentary on 2:80–82 and the essay Death, Dying, and the Afterlife in the Qur'an.

**A Formula Answering a Specific Hope**

The verse's reference to hell widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:162 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:162: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if hell were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:162 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:162 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:162.

**The Standing Counsel of the Verse**

The relation between hell and lightened also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:162 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:162 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:162 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:162 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:162. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:162 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between hell and lightened.

In 2:162, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of hell while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:162 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:162 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for hell, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:162, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:162, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:162, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:162 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Mercy and warning held together**

A reader who finds the command in 2:162 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:162, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine hell until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:162 returns to responsibility. No explanation of hell is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:162 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The deed before its final account**

The choice of the word hell in 2:162 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:162 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:162, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches hell from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:162 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:162 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

There is also a distinction between understanding hell and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:162. The verse 2:162 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:162 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands lightened, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:162.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:162 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving hell; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating hell from the rest of life. In 2:162, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about hell. They are the place in which assent to 2:162 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:162 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: hell is joined to lightened, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:162 gives the reader a useful order of questions about hell. What does the term hell mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:162? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:162 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:162.
