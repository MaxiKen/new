## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:241

> **Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Divorce-Gift — Matāʿ**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between reasonable and women. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:241. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:241. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:241.

The message of this verse is similar to that in v. 236, though that verse pertains to women divorcing where the marriage was yet to be consummated, while the present verse has a general import and places an obligation upon all men to make some manner of provision for women they divorce (Ibn Kathīr).

**Honorably — Within the Language of Goodness**

The reference to reasonable places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

The standard in 2:241 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from reasonable: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A household placed under moral care**

The relation between reasonable and women also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:241 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:241 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:241 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:241 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:241 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands women, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:241.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:241 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving reasonable; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating reasonable from the rest of life. In 2:241, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about reasonable. They are the place in which assent to 2:241 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

The most balanced response to 2:241 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: reasonable is joined to women, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:241 gives the reader a useful order of questions about reasonable. What does the term reasonable mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:241? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:241 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:241.

**The difference between power and justice**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:241. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:241 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between reasonable and women.

In 2:241, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of reasonable while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:241 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**How the jurists read the command**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:241 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for reasonable, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:241, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:241, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:241, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:241 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Protection from private harm**

A reader who finds the command in 2:241 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:241, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine reasonable until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:241 returns to responsibility. No explanation of reasonable is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:241 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Mercy within a legal boundary**

The choice of the word reasonable in 2:241 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:241 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:241, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches reasonable from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:241 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:241 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding reasonable and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:241. The verse 2:241 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
