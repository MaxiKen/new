## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:135

> **The Jews and Christians each say, “Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Competing Invitations**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between jews and faith. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:135. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:135. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:135.

**Ḥanīf: the Word Chosen to Name the Third Way**

This claim to guidance does not mean that the People of the Book were proposing a choice; rather, each group was making this claim on behalf of its own religion (al-Rāzī). Here the creed of Abraham is identified with Islam itself as well as with the term ḥanīf. In the Qur'an ḥanīf often describes Abraham (4:125; 6:161; 16:120, 123), and even though it is not specifically linked with any other prophet, its usage is not restricted to him (e.g., 22:31; 98:5). It is associated with being virtuous and submissive before Allah (4:125), straight (6:161), upright (30:30), and devoutly obedient (16:120); it is a quality that corresponds to the unspoiled primordial nature (fiṭrah) of human beings given to them by Allah (see 30:30c).

**"He Was Not of the Idolaters": The Line of Exclusion**

In almost all verses, ḥanīf also describes one who is not an idolater, an unbeliever, or a follower of Judaism or Christianity (as in the present verse); the latter two are seen as particularizations of the universal primordial religion (al-dīn al-ḥanīf), which Islam reestablishes. Ḥanīf and its plural, ḥunafāʾ, are used in the Qur'an only in the indefinite accusative, meaning that they appear when someone is or does something in the state of being ḥanīf. It is understood to denote a state of pure tawḥīḍ, or monotheistic belief unencumbered by constraints, distortions, and idolatry, and combined with a life of virtue.

Even among the non-Muslim Arabs of the Prophets time, some of whom were ḥanīfs, the quality of ḥanīf was associated with Abraham and the renunciation of idolatry and demanded the leading of a moral life. It was not a label for Jews or Christians and did not denote an organized or distinct group.

**The Answer That Matures Every Later Temptation**

Etymologically ḥanīf is somewhat enigmatic and has led some to debate its real meaning and origins. It can mean “lame” or “crooked” and also “inclining toward.” Its likely cognates in other Semitic languages give the sense of “hypocrite,” “vile,” and “unclean,” among other infelicitous meanings such as “pagan” or “heathen” in the sense of non-Jew or non-Christian. It has been used by Christians writing in Arabic to denote Muslims, but sometimes in the sense of pagan idolatry, suggesting that perhaps the negative connotations of its Semitic cognates were not forgotten.

**The first movement of a guided life**

It is not implausible that a term denoting a person who “inclined away” from a group in a negative sense could come to acquire the positive sense of one who was not bound to a specific group’s limitations, distortions, and errors. One who inclines away from a group may suffer rebuke (e.g., as a non-Jew or non-Christian), but be praised when such groups are judged to be in error. The meaning of ḥanīf among the Arabs was in any case clear without any attempts to reconstruct its etymology. But with such considerations in mind and in light of its usage in the Qur'an, one can also see in ḥanīf the sense of not being hidebound, parochial, tribal, or sectarian to the detriment of universal truth.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

In keeping with its Qur'anic usage and its identification in the time of the Prophet, commentators have also understood ḥanīf as “pilgrim” (ḥājj); as “follower,” in the sense of those who followed Abraham in various practices such as circumcision; or as “devoted entirely [to Allah]” (mukhliṣ, also “sincere”). For al-Ṭabarī, it means the “straight” following of the way of Abraham and could not be restricted to pilgrims, which would have included pagans, or the circumcised, which would have included the Jews. He points out further that this verse does not mean that the prophets before Abraham were not ḥanīf in the sense of being straight and upright in their obedience to Allah, but Abraham is mentioned in this context because Allah made him an imām of monotheism and he would have a special role for monotheists who came after him.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The verse describes jews not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:135, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:135, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

The standard in 2:135 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from jews: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

In 2:135, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of jews while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:135 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:135 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for jews, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:135, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:135, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:135, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:135 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:135 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:135, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine jews until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:135 returns to responsibility. No explanation of jews is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:135 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word jews in 2:135 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:135 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:135, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches jews from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:135 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:135 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
