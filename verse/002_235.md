## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:235

> **There is no blame on you for subtly showing interest in ˹divorced or widowed˺ women or for hiding ˹the intention˺ in your hearts. Allah knows that you are considering them ˹for marriage˺. But do not make a secret commitment with them—you can only show interest in them appropriately. Do not commit to the bond of marriage until the waiting period expires. Know that Allah is aware of what is in your hearts, so beware of Him. And know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Intimating, Not Plighting**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between blame and interest. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:235. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:235. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:235.

**"Allah Knows What Is Within Your Souls"**

Intimating a proposal means that one can express a desire for marriage, yet stop short of proposing and making an agreement. For example, a man can say, “I hope I can marry a righteous woman,” or, “I do not wish to marry any other woman,” or, “I desire to marry,” but he would not be allowed to say, “Promise me you will marry me and not another person” (Ibn Kathīr). Actually contracting a marriage during this time period is forbidden. Save that you speak in an honorable way means that one’s interaction with widows should not go beyond the limits of the intimation mentioned at the start of the verse (Ibn Kathīr). Do not pledge your troth with them secretly can mean either that they should not agree to marry or that they should not consummate a marriage .

**The moral shape of a transaction**

Some say that if two people actually consummate a marriage before the waiting period expires, the two are forbidden to marry each other forever, and the woman must remain unmarried for the remainder of the waiting period from her dead husband and the waiting period from her premature marriage. If they contract an agreement to marry, but do not consummate, then they must wait out the waiting period, after which the man must propose again .

Because the verse touches blame, its moral question is not exhausted by whether an arrangement is technically possible. A transaction can be voluntary on the surface and still be shaped by pressure, concealment, or unequal knowledge. The Qur'anic concern is the trust created between people: each party must be able to understand the obligation, and neither may turn another person's need into an instrument of harm.

**The danger hidden in a familiar comparison**

The standard in 2:235 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from blame: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between blame and interest also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:235 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:235 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:235 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:235 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Protection against harm and exploitation**

In 2:235, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:235, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:235 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:235 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:235, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine blame until the demand disappears.

**What the command asks of both parties**

The final emphasis of 2:235 returns to responsibility. No explanation of blame is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:235 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word blame in 2:235 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:235 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:235, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The law’s mercy and its discipline**

The exegetical tradition approaches blame from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:235 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:235 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding blame and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:235. The verse 2:235 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The social consequence of the ruling**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:235 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands interest, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:235.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:235 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving blame; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**When numbers become a moral question**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating blame from the rest of life. In 2:235, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about blame. They are the place in which assent to 2:235 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:235 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: blame is joined to interest, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:235 gives the reader a useful order of questions about blame. What does the term blame mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:235? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:235 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:235.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:235. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:235 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between blame and interest.
