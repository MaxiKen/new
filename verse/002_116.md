## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:116

> **They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Claim Surveyed and the Immediate Tasbīḥ**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings offspring and belongs into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:116 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:116 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**His Absolute Ownership and the Servanthood of All**

Commentators do not restrict this verse to Christian theology; it can also refer to the Arab pagan belief that the angels were the daughters of Allah (16:57; 37:149; 43:16; 52:39); similar disavowals of Divine sonship in connection with Jesus are found in 5:17, 72; 9:30; 19:35. The Islamic arguments against Allahs having a child stem directly from the fundamental Islamic conception of Divine Oneness, Transcendence, Absoluteness, and Uniqueness (tawḥīd) and are based on the principle that Allah is unlike anything in creation, Allah is beyond need of all things, and His Perfection can never be implicated in the limitations inherent in having a son. Since Islam emphasizes Allah as the Absolute, it denies all possibility of relativizing Him through a relationship such as fatherhood.

**The Kalām Core: Why No Sonship Is Possible for Allah**

Although a theologian such as al-Rāzī accepts a purely metaphorical understanding of Allah as “father”—in the sense that a father is prior, is responsible for or is the human cause of a person’s being—he would utterly reject that this in any way reflects the actual filial relationship between Allah and any being. His arguments are representative of much mainstream thought on the question: (1) those beings who are called Allah’s offspring are contingent beings, hence are created, and offsprings are begotten; (2) the offspring is either eternal or not; in the first case there is nothing to make one father and the other son, and in the second case the offspring would be a created being who is begotten; (3) the offspring is of the same species as the progenitor, being similar in some ways, but different in others; this would require that each be made up of parts, which cannot be said of Allah; (4) one has offspring as a result of certain needs such as happiness, care in one’s old age, pride, and so on, none of which are attributable to Allah.

**Devout Obedience as the Believer's Posture**

The term most often translated glory be to (subḥāna) also carries the sense of disassociation, so that Glory be to Him! can also mean Allah is far beyond such a thing! which is a sense brought out often in connection with this verse (al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī). For the general meaning of glorifying Allah, see 57:1c.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

Devoutly obedient renders qānit, whose range of meaning includes compliance, humility, standing, and silence, all of which are emphasized by the commentators on this verse . Since the opposite qualities are all attributes of human beings in this world, some say that this refers to their state on the Day of Resurrection, when everyone will be forced to obey, stand, and be silent before Allah (al-Qurṭubī). Others understand the message of All are devoutly obedient to Him to be similar to that of 13:15: And unto Allah prostrates whosoever is in the heavens and on the earth, willingly or unwillingly (Ibn Kathīr). The context also suggests that the phrase be understood as All [of them] are devoutly obedient to Him, referring to Jesus, Ezra, and the angels who, far from being connected with this incorrect belief, are themselves sincere in their worship of Allah: The Messiah would never disdain to be a servant of Allah; nor would the angels brought nigh (4:172). On devout obedience, also see 3:17c; 4:34c.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The verse's reference to offspring widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:116 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:116: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if offspring were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:116 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:116 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:116.

**Mercy and warning held together**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:116 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving offspring; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating offspring from the rest of life. In 2:116, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about offspring. They are the place in which assent to 2:116 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The deed before its final account**

The most balanced response to 2:116 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: offspring is joined to belongs, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:116 gives the reader a useful order of questions about offspring. What does the term offspring mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:116? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:116 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:116.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:116. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:116 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between offspring and belongs.

In 2:116, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of offspring while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:116 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:116 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for offspring, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:116, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:116, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:116, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:116 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.
