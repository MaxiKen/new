## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:265

> **And the example of those who donate their wealth, seeking Allah’s pleasure and believing the reward is certain, is that of a garden on a fertile hill: when heavy rain falls, it yields up twice its normal produce. If no heavy rain falls, a drizzle is sufficient. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Counter-Seed — The Garden on the Hill**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between example and seeking. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:265. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:265. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:265.

The worst model of charity is one who gives only for appearances sake, as in 107:6–7, which mentions those who strive to be seen, yet refuse small kindnesses. These verses compare the merit of two kinds of giving through the symbolism of rain striking the earth. When a downpour strikes the earth on a smooth stone, it can only wash it away, meaning it produces no real benefit. When it lands on a fertile garden, the surplus of water causes the garden to double its yield, symbolizing the fruit of charity with the right intention.

**A Downpour — Or Just a Soft Rain**

And if a downpour strikes it not, then a soft rain is taken to mean that, even if the amount of rain is not great, the garden will at least thrive normally and bear its fruit. Some commentators mention that certain kinds of oases flourish and have rich earth collect on them when they are slightly elevated, and that this is what is meant by a garden upon a hill. Moreover, on a hill of this sort, there are no streams that flow, and so it can be nourished only by rain (al-Qurṭubī).

**Allah Sees What You Do**

Others say that upon a hill (bi-rabwah) must mean something more like that which rises, meaning fertile soil from which crops grow upward, yielding the translation the parable of a fertile garden. Al-Rāzī argues that a garden upon a hill would not be reached by streams and would be exposed to too much wind, while a garden in a hollow would be inundated with water and would not receive the better effects of wind. He argues that the best garden is on level ground and prefers to relate rabwah to the verb to grow, which comes from the same root.

Such growth through charity is mentioned in the ḥadīth, When a person gives a lawfully earned date in charity, Allah takes it in His Hand and causes it to grow as one of you would grow his foals or camels, until it becomes like a mountain or even greater.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Out of a confirmation in their souls means that the givers affirm and have certainty of faith in their act of giving or that they do so because they have confirmation of the Truth of Allah or certainty (al-Zamakhsharī). Or it can mean that when they give, they take care and confirm where they place their charity to make sure that it is used properly.

The subject of example joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:265, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:265 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The standard in 2:265 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from example: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between example and seeking also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:265 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:265 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:265 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:265 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The final emphasis of 2:265 returns to responsibility. No explanation of example is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:265 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word example in 2:265 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:265 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:265, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The exegetical tradition approaches example from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:265 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:265 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding example and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:265. The verse 2:265 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:265 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands seeking, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:265.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:265 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving example; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating example from the rest of life. In 2:265, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about example. They are the place in which assent to 2:265 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:265 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: example is joined to seeking, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:265 gives the reader a useful order of questions about example. What does the term example mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:265? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:265 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:265.
