## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:72

> **˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Crime Finally Named**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings killed and revealed into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:72 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:72 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Allah Is the Discloser of What You Were Concealing"**

On the murder, also see 2:67–71c. With some variations, the commentators relate the story that the Israelites, after searching for a long time, find the cow described by Moses, purchase it for a high price, and sacrifice it. Then they take a part of the cows body and strike the corpse of the murder victim, which is then briefly animated, long enough to answer the question of who the killer was, after which the body reverts to its previous state (Ibn Kathīr). Thus, most commentators understand the command in v. 73 to mean, Strike him [the corpse] with part of it [the slaughtered cow].

**The Cool Logic of the Solvable Cold Case**

Assuming this is the correct interpretation, it would not be the only instance of reanimation in the Qur'an; others include Abraham and the birds (2:260); Jesus and the clay bird (3:49); the unnamed man thought by some to be Ezra, who died and was revived after a hundred years (2:259); and an allusion to this is when dust touched by a messenger (usually thought to be Gabriel) is used to animate the golden calf (20:96).

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

In a related ḥadīth, a man asked, “O Messenger of Allah, how does Allah revive the dead?” “Have you ever passed by a barren valley, and then passed by it again and it was verdant?” “Yes.” “Even so is the Resurrection.” In another version he replied, “Even so does Allah revive the dead.” Sufi commentators have seen in this passage a message that one whose heart has been “slain” by worldliness must sacrifice the egotistical soul in order to give life to the heart through the remembrance of Allah (Ni, Su, Qu). Al-Qushayrī states, “Whoever desires that his heart be given life must sacrifice his soul. So whoever sacrifices his soul with effort gives life to his heart and the lights of vision.”

**The difference between defence and aggression**

In Deuteronomy 21:1–9 the Israelites are commanded to sacrifice a cow in the event that a person is found slain and the murderer remains unknown, after which the elders of the town nearest to the victim wash their hands over the cow and disavow any guilt for the crime. But the two accounts are different; one is the narration of an event, and the other, the prescription of a general law, even though there are similarities between them.

The mention of killed must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if killed were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:72 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:72 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:72.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

The relation between killed and revealed also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:72 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:72 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:72 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:72 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

There is also a distinction between understanding killed and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:72. The verse 2:72 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**How the early community understood it**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:72 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands revealed, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:72.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:72 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving killed; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Power restrained by accountability**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating killed from the rest of life. In 2:72, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about killed. They are the place in which assent to 2:72 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:72 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: killed is joined to revealed, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:72 gives the reader a useful order of questions about killed. What does the term killed mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:72? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:72 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:72.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:72. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:72 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between killed and revealed.

In 2:72, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of killed while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:72 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:72 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for killed, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:72, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
