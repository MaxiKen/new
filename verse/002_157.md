## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:157

> **They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Reward Register of the Saying**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between receive and mercy. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:157. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:157. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:157.

Blessings renders ṣalawāt (sing. ṣalāh), a word that usually means prayer or worship, but when used with the preposition ʿalā, meaning toward or upon, indicates something that comes from Allah toward human beings rather than going from human beings toward Allah, as in 33:56: Truly Allah and His angels invoke blessings upon (ʿalā) the Prophet. O you who believe! Invoke blessings upon him, and greetings of peace! See also 9:99, 103.

**Ṣalawāt Min Rabbihim**

The verse's reference to receive widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:157 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:157: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:157 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from receive: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**And Mercy — an Unenumerated Grace**

The relation between receive and mercy also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:157 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:157 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:157 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:157 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:157 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands mercy, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:157.

**"Those Are the Guided"**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:157 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving receive; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating receive from the rest of life. In 2:157, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about receive. They are the place in which assent to 2:157 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

The most balanced response to 2:157 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: receive is joined to mercy, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:157 gives the reader a useful order of questions about receive. What does the term receive mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:157? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:157 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:157.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:157. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:157 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between receive and mercy.

In 2:157, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of receive while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:157 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**Mercy and warning held together**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:157 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for receive, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:157, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:157, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:157, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:157 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**The deed before its final account**

A reader who finds the command in 2:157 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:157, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine receive until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:157 returns to responsibility. No explanation of receive is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:157 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The choice of the word receive in 2:157 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:157 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:157, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches receive from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:157 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:157 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding receive and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:157. The verse 2:157 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
