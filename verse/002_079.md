## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:79

> **So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Double Woe**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between distort and seeking. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:79. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:79. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:79.

Although woe (wayl) is an ordinary Arabic word used throughout the Qur'an as a cry of distress, it is also interpreted here by some as a proper name for an infernal valley in Hell based on the following ḥadīth (disputed by some, such as the commentator Ibn Kathīr): Wayl is a valley in Hell into which the disbeliever will fall for forty autumns before reaching its bottom. Or it is a mountain in Hell of blood and pus, in which case So woe unto those/them could be read, For them there will be wayl.

**"For a Paltry Price": The Economics of False Scripture**

For others, it is the word that will be uttered by the punished, and it will be the cry of the disbelievers in Hell . The exegetes give an account of some Jews who wrote something down and pawned it off on the ignorant Arabs for a small profit; another account links this verse with the alteration of the prophecies foretelling the coming of Muhammad (see 7:157), meaning that the descriptions of the Prophet in their book were altered so that they would describe something else .

The wording around distort assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

**The Earning of the Hand and the Earner**

The standard in 2:79 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from distort: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between distort and seeking also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:79 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:79 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:79 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:79 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The Book as an address**

In 2:79, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of distort while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:79 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:79 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for distort, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:79, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The word that carries the argument**

In 2:79, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:79, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:79 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:79 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:79, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine distort until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:79 returns to responsibility. No explanation of distort is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:79 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

The choice of the word distort in 2:79 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:79 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:79, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches distort from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:79 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:79 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The reader’s responsibility**

There is also a distinction between understanding distort and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:79. The verse 2:79 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:79 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands seeking, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:79.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:79 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving distort; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Why the wording is exact**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating distort from the rest of life. In 2:79, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about distort. They are the place in which assent to 2:79 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:79 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: distort is joined to seeking, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**A question answered by the surrounding verses**

The wording of 2:79 gives the reader a useful order of questions about distort. What does the term distort mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:79? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:79 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:79.
