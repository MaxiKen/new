## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:245

> **Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Who Shall Lend Allah?" — The Astonishing Question**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between lend and multiply. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:245. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:245. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:245.

**Withholds and Outstretches**

Some consider this verse to continue the line of thought in the previous one, in which case the goodly loan would be a metaphor for the effort expended and risk undertaken in fighting in the way of Allah. Others believe the verse begins anew and refers to the giving of ones wealth in charity or for religious causes, while still others point out there is no contradiction between these two interpretations (al-Rāzī). On lending Allah a goodly loan, see 5:12; 64:17; 73:20; 57:11c. In all such instances, commentators point out that to serve Allah and do good is always, as it were, a good bargain, and one can also engage in unprofitable transactions, such as when one sells Allahs signs for a paltry price (e.g., 2:41). Allahs multiplication of good is described in 6:160: Whosoever brings a good deed shall have ten times the like thereof, and also discussed in 57:18.

**The Verse the Whole Sūrah Rises From**

“Withholding” and “outstretching” are two very important concepts in Islamic spirituality. The verb “withholds” is related to the state of spiritual contraction (qabḍ), and the verb “outstretches” is related to the state of expansion (basṭ). Interpreted spiritually, this verse describes a threefold process of spiritual realization: inner purification (Allah withholds), the acquisition of the virtues (and outstretches), and finally spiritual realization and illumination (and unto Him shall you be returned). Some Sufis describe contraction and expansion as “two states that follow each other in the heart like the night follows the day.” Contraction and expansion are also related to the twin states of fear and hope, which must be kept in balance in order for a soul to progress spiritually. Seen in another way, when Allah withholds one is exposed to His Quality of Majesty, and when He outstretches, one experiences His Quality of Beauty (Aj).

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The subject of lend joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:245, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:245 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:245 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from lend: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The relation between lend and multiply also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:245 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:245 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:245 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:245 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The wording of 2:245 gives the reader a useful order of questions about lend. What does the term lend mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:245? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:245 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:245.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:245. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:245 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between lend and multiply.

**The measure of sincere giving**

In 2:245, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of lend while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:245 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:245 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for lend, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:245, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The poor person’s dignity**

In 2:245, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:245, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:245 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:245 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:245, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine lend until the demand disappears.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The final emphasis of 2:245 returns to responsibility. No explanation of lend is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:245 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word lend in 2:245 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:245 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:245, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches lend from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:245 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:245 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding lend and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:245. The verse 2:245 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
