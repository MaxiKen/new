## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:220

> **upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you concerning orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**This World and the Hereafter — the Two Balances**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings upon and concerning into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:220 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:220 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

The Qur'anic theme of the Hereafter’s superiority over this world is connected here with wine and gambling; one should reflect upon the transitory goods and lasting evils engendered by them and judge them in relation to the fleeting world and the abiding Hereafter . It is possible to read the end of the previous verse and the start of this one as “Thus does Allah make clear, in this world and the Hereafter, the signs.”

**"They Ask You About Orphans" — The Scrupulous Custodians**

Other verses that mention taking care of orphans property include 4:10 and 6:152. It is related that Muslims were scrupulous in keeping the property of orphans separate and would eat and drink from completely separate sources so as not to consume what belonged to the orphans; when this became difficult for the custodians, they would therefore sometimes stay aloof from the orphans in their care, so that at times the property of the orphans would spoil or become ruined (al-Ṭabarī). It is useful to remember that an orphans inheritance would have often been in the form of fruit trees and livestock, and it is precisely through using them (e.g., by picking the fruit and riding and breeding the livestock) that their value and usefulness would have been maintained until the orphan could grow up and take possession of them.

**"Had Allah Willed, He Would Have Put You to Hardship"**

Another opinion sees this verse as addressing not a prior Muslim practice but a prevailing Arab custom of staying separate from orphans, not eating together with them or riding on the mounts used by them, as it was considered inauspicious or an ill omen to be guilty of stealing from an orphan even in pre-Islamic Arabia (al-Qurṭubī).

In the Qur'an the opposite of corruption (ifsād or fasād) is often an act of setting matters aright, or iṣlāḥ, related by root to ṣulḥ, meaning peacemaking or reconciliation, and ṣāliḥ or righteous. Intermingling means drinking the same drink, eating the same food, riding the same mounts, being served by the same servants, and so on (al-Ṭabarī). The message in this verse is to deal equitably with the orphans property, which is legally inviolable, but whose inviolability is not meant to harm the orphan. Hence reasonable latitude is given for the just use of an orphans property, avoiding a pedantic and counterproductive separation between the custodians property and that of the orphan .

**The first movement of a guided life**

They are your brothers is not conditional upon the preceding phrase, if you intermingle with them; rather, it is a clarification that intermingling with their property in a just fashion carries no blame . That Allah could have put you to hardship highlights the inherent difficulties associated with maintaining the property of orphans, and that to balance the responsibility of disposing of that property with observing its intrinsic inviolability is often a matter of judgment. This judgment is itself overseen by Allah, who knows the one who works corruption from one who sets aright.

For a longer treatment on other aspects of the law pertaining to orphans, see commentary on 4:2–10.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The verse describes upon not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:220, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:220, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if upon were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:220 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:220 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:220.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:220 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for upon, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:220, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:220, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:220, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:220 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

A reader who finds the command in 2:220 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:220, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine upon until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:220 returns to responsibility. No explanation of upon is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:220 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The choice of the word upon in 2:220 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:220 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:220, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches upon from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:220 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:220 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

There is also a distinction between understanding upon and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:220. The verse 2:220 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:220 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands concerning, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:220.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:220 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving upon; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
