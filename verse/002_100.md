## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:100

> **Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Pattern of the Broken Compact**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings every and group into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:100 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:100 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**"Most of Them Do Not Believe"**

Many commentators mention that the Jews of Madinah said, We did not make a pact or covenant regarding Muhammad, upon which this verse was revealed, which evidently means that they did not pledge to follow him specifically. Others indicate that this refers to a promise the Jews had made previously that they would follow the prophet who was to come and give them victory over the idolatrous Arabs . (See v. 89.) Others mention that it can refer to their betraying the Prophet by breaking their alliance with him and siding with the Quraysh (see also 4:51–52c), though the meaning is not restricted to any particular incident (al-Rāzī).

The verse describes every not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:100, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:100, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**Keepers of Covenants: the Muslim Counter-Vocation**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if every were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:100 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:100 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:100.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between every and group also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:100 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:100 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:100 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:100 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

There is also a distinction between understanding every and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:100. The verse 2:100 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:100 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands group, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:100.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:100 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving every; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating every from the rest of life. In 2:100, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about every. They are the place in which assent to 2:100 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:100 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: every is joined to group, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The wording of 2:100 gives the reader a useful order of questions about every. What does the term every mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:100? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:100 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:100.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:100. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:100 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between every and group.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:100, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of every while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:100 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:100 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for every, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:100, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

In 2:100, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:100, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:100 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:100 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:100, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine every until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:100 returns to responsibility. No explanation of every is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:100 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word every in 2:100 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:100 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:100, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
