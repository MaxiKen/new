## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:30

> **˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood while we glorify Your praises and proclaim Your holiness?” Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Announcement of the Vicegerent**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings angels and successive into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:30 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:30 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Angels' Question and Allah's Answer**

Vicegerent renders khalīfah, a word that can also mean successor or deputy, hence khalīfat rasūl Allāh, or successor/steward of Allahs Messenger, shortened to khalīfah (anglicized as caliph). In some verses, such as here and 6:165, khalīfah appears to denote a universal human inheritance and responsibility, since all human beings are in their inner reality the khalīfah of Allah. In others, the sense of successor comes to the fore (e.g., 7:69, which refers to vicegerents after the people of Noah). See also 7:74; 10:14; 10:73; 27:62; 35:39. At another level of interpretation, some commentators say that khalīfah comes from khalafa (to come after) and means that human beings come after all creatures and all grades of being are summarized in the human state.

**The Vicegerent's Charter for Every Child of Adam**

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that Iblīs was a member of a tribe of angels called al-ḥinn (a word related to jinn), was named al-Ḥārith, and was a custodian of Paradise. All the angels were created from light, except this tribe, which was created from smokeless fire (cf. 55:15). The first to inhabit the earth were the jinn, who caused corruption there and spilled blood. Then Allah sent Iblīs among that host of angels called al-ḥinn. Iblīs and his compatriots killed them and sent them off to the islands of the sea and the edges of the mountains.

**The first scene of human responsibility**

He was filled with pride for accomplishing something no one else had done. After Adams body was created from clay, it remained a lifeless form for forty years, during which Iblīs taunted it by flying in and out of it, saying, You are nothing! If I come to power over you, I will destroy you, and if you come to power over me, I will defy you. Then it was said to those angels who were with Iblīs (not all the angels) that they should prostrate before Adam. Iblīs said, No, I will not prostrate before him.

I am better than him, older, and greater. You created me from fire, while you created him from clay (cf. 7:12; Ṭ). The only other indication in the Qur'an of such an order of creation is found in 15:26–27: And We indeed created man from dried clay, made of molded mud, and the jinn We created earlier from scorching fire.

**What the command reveals about the human being**

That jinn inhabited the earth before human beings is accepted by many commentators. But al-Rāzī prefers the opinion that Allah spoke to all the angels, not only those who warred alongside Iblīs. Nevertheless, some specifics of the story, such as that Iblīs was a member of a tribe of angels, can hardly be counted as reliable and are certainly from popular narratives, since an authority such as Ibn ʿAbbās would have known that the Qur'an in 18:50 identifies Iblīs as a jinn, not an angel.

**The adversary’s method**

It does, however, provide one possible explanation for the angels question, Wilt Thou place therein . . . , namely, that they had previous experience with the blood and corruption of the earths previous inhabitants. However, many commentators explain the angels apparently audacious question as an expression of wonder on their part, rather than doubt or concern (al-Rāzī). They marvel at Allahs Wisdom, but are not alarmed by it. It is thought that disobedience or any brand of rebelliousness is impossible for angels, though al-Rāzī notes that the Muʿtazilites believed that angels could be disobedient. Hymn Thy praise and call Thee Holy can also be read, We glorify Thee while praising Thee (al-Zamakhsharī); cf. 32:15; 39:75; 42:5. I know what you know not is generally understood to mean that, despite the inevitability of bloodletting and corruption, good things will come of the creation of human beings, but only Allah knows what they are (al-Rāzī). As mentioned in 2:11–12c, such corruption can also refer to the degradation of the natural environment.

**Knowledge, temptation, and choice**

The theme of angels places human weakness beside human responsibility. The account does not require a fantasy of moral perfection; it asks what a person does after knowledge, temptation, and failure have entered the scene. The important distinction is between being tested and choosing the test's wrong answer, then refusing the return that Allah makes possible.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if angels were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:30 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:30 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:30.

**Theological questions around the account**

There is also a distinction between understanding angels and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:30. The verse 2:30 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:30 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands successive, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:30.

**The mercy that follows failure**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:30 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving angels; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating angels from the rest of life. In 2:30, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about angels. They are the place in which assent to 2:30 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:30 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: angels is joined to successive, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:30 gives the reader a useful order of questions about angels. What does the term angels mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:30? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:30 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:30.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:30. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:30 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between angels and successive.
