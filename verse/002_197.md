## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:197

> **˹Commitment to˺ pilgrimage is made in appointed months. Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it. Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Known Months**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between commitment and months. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:197. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:197. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:197.

**No Lewdness, Iniquity, Quarreling**

The months well known are considered to be Shawwāl, Dhul-Qaʿdah, and Dhul-Ḥijjah, although some say only the first ten days of Dhul-Ḥijjah, since the rules regarding sacrifice change after the Day of Sacrifice. According to many authorities it is discouraged (makrūh) to enter iḥrām for the ḥajj in months other than these; that is, one should not enter iḥrām and stay in it until the ḥajj is performed in Dhul-Ḥijjah. Lewdness refers to sexual intercourse, which, if it occurs before the Day of ʿArafah, invalidates the ḥajj and requires a person to make a sacrificial offering and perform the ḥajj again.

**The Best Provision**

Others point out that lewdness refers to talking with or about women to whom one is not closely related (in the case of men). A ḥadīth states, By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, there is no deed between Heaven and earth better than struggle in the way of Allah, and a ḥajj performed faithfully in which there is no lewdness, iniquity, or argument. The quarreling does not refer to intellectual disagreements, but to interchanges resulting in anger and insults. Al-Qurṭubī notes that there used to be arguments about the Station of Abraham (see 2:125c), as the Quraysh would observe a spot different from that of the other Arabs.

He prefers to think that the prohibition here is about the place and time of the ḥajj, but it could also refer to the practice of boasting that sometimes accompanied the pilgrimage, where some would claim that their ḥajj was better than that of others, that their forefathers were better, and so forth (al-Qurṭubī).

**"Reverence Me, O Possessors of Intellect"**

To make provision means to come materially prepared for the ḥajj and not to rely upon the locals and other pilgrims for food and shelter. The ḥajj is not in any case incumbent upon those without the necessary financial means. At a spiritual level, one can speak of reverence (taqwā) as a provision for the Hereafter, likened to the provision one makes for the pilgrimage in this world (al-Qurṭubī).

The treatment of commitment joins movement through a place or ritual with movement of the heart. Sacred practice has boundaries precisely because it is not ordinary travel or ordinary habit. The limits teach the pilgrim to receive meaning rather than manufacture it, while the concessions show that obedience is addressed to human beings with real capacities and pressures.

**A journey ordered toward Allah**

The standard in 2:197 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from commitment: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between commitment and months also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:197 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:197 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:197 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:197 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The sacred limit in the command**

The exegetical tradition approaches commitment from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:197 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:197 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding commitment and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:197. The verse 2:197 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**Ritual detail and spiritual purpose**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:197 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands months, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:197.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:197 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving commitment; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**What pilgrims must leave behind**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating commitment from the rest of life. In 2:197, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about commitment. They are the place in which assent to 2:197 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:197 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: commitment is joined to months, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The community gathered by one sign**

The wording of 2:197 gives the reader a useful order of questions about commitment. What does the term commitment mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:197? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:197 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:197.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:197. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:197 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between commitment and months.

In 2:197, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of commitment while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:197 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:197 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for commitment, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:197, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
