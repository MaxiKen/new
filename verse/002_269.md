## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:269

> **Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills. And whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege. But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Wisdom Among the World's Many Gifts**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between grants and wills. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:269. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:269. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:269.

Wisdom here is interpreted as knowledge of the meaning of the Qur'an, including the abrogating and the abrogated, the symbolic and the determined (see 3:7), the order of revelation, and the licit and the forbidden, or as the fear of Allah, in accordance with the saying, Fear of Allah is the beginning of wisdom. Wisdom in this context is sometimes also glossed as intelligence (ʿaql) or comprehension (fahm). Certain theologians see in this verse an allusion to the power of rational demonstration. Some interpret this wisdom to mean prophethood (nubuwwah), but others dispute this interpretation .

**The Book's Old Prayer-Line Resumed**

The spirit or the intellect takes its nourishment from wisdom, unlike the desires of the ego, which are satisfied by the indecencies mentioned in the previous verse (al-Rāzī). Wisdom as knowledge or understanding differs from right action, but In one respect wisdom is the combination of understanding and right action based upon it (al-Rāzī). In a ḥadīth the Prophet said, Let there be envy in only two things: a man to whom Allah has given wealth and who exhausts it in the way of truth, and a man to whom Allah has given wisdom and who judges by it and teaches it.

On possessors of intellect, see 2:179c.

The verse describes grants not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:269, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:269, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The standard in 2:269 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from grants: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between grants and wills also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:269 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:269 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:269 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:269 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:269 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands wills, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:269.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:269 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving grants; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating grants from the rest of life. In 2:269, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about grants. They are the place in which assent to 2:269 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:269 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: grants is joined to wills, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

The wording of 2:269 gives the reader a useful order of questions about grants. What does the term grants mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:269? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:269 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:269.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:269. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:269 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between grants and wills.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

In 2:269, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of grants while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:269 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:269 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for grants, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:269, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

In 2:269, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:269, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:269 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:269 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:269, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine grants until the demand disappears.

**What the early exegetes preserved**

The final emphasis of 2:269 returns to responsibility. No explanation of grants is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:269 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word grants in 2:269 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:269 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:269, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
