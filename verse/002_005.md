## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:5

> **It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Verdict on the Portrait**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between truly and successful. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:5. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:5. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:5.

The term rendered they who shall prosper (mufliḥūn) is related to falāḥ, meaning prosperity, thriving, or success, and is etymologically linked to “cultivation.” Though sometimes rendered “salvation,” falāḥ is generally not the concept used to differentiate between the “saved” and “unsaved” or “damned” as typically understood in Christianity. It is an idea that appears in the call to prayer (adhān), where the caller recites, after the takbīr (Allāhu akbar) and the shahādah, “Come to ṣalāh (prayer), come to falāḥ (prosperity or salvation).”

**"Upon Guidance": Guidance as a Road Under the Feet**

The verse's reference to truly widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:5 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:5: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:5 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from truly: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Falāḥ: An Agricultural Word for the Human Harvest**

The relation between truly and successful also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:5 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:5 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:5 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:5 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating truly from the rest of life. In 2:5, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about truly. They are the place in which assent to 2:5 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The Portrait's Answer to the Self-Help Age**

The most balanced response to 2:5 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: truly is joined to successful, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:5 gives the reader a useful order of questions about truly. What does the term truly mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:5? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:5 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:5.

**The First Page Ends, the Map Is Drawn**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:5. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:5 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between truly and successful.

In 2:5, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of truly while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:5 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:5 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for truly, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:5, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:5, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:5, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:5 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

A reader who finds the command in 2:5 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:5, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine truly until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:5 returns to responsibility. No explanation of truly is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:5 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The choice of the word truly in 2:5 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:5 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:5, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches truly from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:5 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:5 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The deed before its final account**

There is also a distinction between understanding truly and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:5. The verse 2:5 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:5 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands successful, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:5.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:5 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving truly; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
