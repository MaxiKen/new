## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:221

> **Do not marry polytheistic women until they believe; for a believing slave-woman is better than a free polytheist, even though she may look pleasant to you. And do not marry your women to polytheistic men until they believe, for a believing slave-man is better than a free polytheist, even though he may look pleasant to you. They invite ˹you˺ to the Fire while Allah invites ˹you˺ to Paradise and forgiveness by His grace. He makes His revelations clear to the people so perhaps they will be mindful.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Matrimonial Borderline**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between marry and until. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:221. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:221. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:221.

Islamic Law allows for marriage with women among the People of the Book, but not with idolaters, a permission given in 5:5, which states that the chaste women of those who were given the Book before you are lawful to marry. Some commentators believe that 5:5 partially abrogated the ruling in this present verse so as to allow marriage between Muslim men and women from among the People of the Book (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī), but the question arises only if the People of the Book were to be taken as a class of idolaters.

**"Though She Please You, Though He Impress You"**

Many maintain that the prohibition against marrying idolaters was never directed against the people of the Book (al-Qurṭubī). In either case, the final legal ruling allows marriage with Jews and Christians, though some jurists have also included in this category marriage with Zoroastrians and, in India, with Hindus. A minority opinion held that in fact this verse abrogates 5:5 and hence no marriage with a non-Muslim is allowed. And marry none to the idolaters is most often, but not always, understood as a command to not give any Muslim woman in marriage to a non-Muslim man.

For the related subject of marrying believing slave women, see also 4:25c.

The verse describes marry not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:221, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:221, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

**Callers unto Fire; Caller unto Garden**

The standard in 2:221 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from marry: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between marry and until also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:221 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:221 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:221 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:221 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

In 2:221, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:221, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:221 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

A reader who finds the command in 2:221 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:221, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine marry until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:221 returns to responsibility. No explanation of marry is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:221 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

The choice of the word marry in 2:221 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:221 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:221, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches marry from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:221 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:221 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

There is also a distinction between understanding marry and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:221. The verse 2:221 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:221 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands until, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:221.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:221 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving marry; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating marry from the rest of life. In 2:221, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about marry. They are the place in which assent to 2:221 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The most balanced response to 2:221 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: marry is joined to until, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:221 gives the reader a useful order of questions about marry. What does the term marry mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:221? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:221 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:221.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:221. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:221 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between marry and until.
