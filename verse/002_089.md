## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:89

> **Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Arrival of the Long-Awaited Book**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between although and victory. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:89. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:89. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:89.

**When the Recognized One Arrives**

Here the Book refers to the Qur'an, which in several verses is described as confirming the previous scriptures (e.g., 3:3; 5:48; 6:92; 35:31). To ask for victory has the sense of asking for help. It is related that before the advent of the prophethood of Muhammad, the Jewish tribes of Madinah used to tell the idolatrous Arabs that a prophet would come who would grant the Jews victory over them . Some commentators relate that the Jews previously prayed, O Allah send this prophet we find written of in our [book], so that we can punish the idolaters and slay them, but they then rejected him—that is, Muhammad—because he was not of the Israelites (see v. 90; IK).

**The Warning Etched in Reversal**

Some traditions relate that some Arab Muslims, who were formerly idolaters, would remind the Jews by saying, Reverence Allah and embrace Islam. You used to ask for victory over us by means of Muhammad while we were people of idolatry, telling us that he was being sent, and describing his attributes to us (al-Ṭabarī). Some commentators believe that that which they recognized could also be understood to mean that which they knew or that which they were acquainted with and did not stem from a detailed description of the Prophet, but rather from an understanding of the general attributes of prophethood and truth that he displayed (al-Rāzī).

The subject of although joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:89, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:89 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The standard in 2:89 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from although: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The relation between although and victory also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:89 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:89 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:89 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:89 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating although from the rest of life. In 2:89, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about although. They are the place in which assent to 2:89 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:89 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: although is joined to victory, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The wording of 2:89 gives the reader a useful order of questions about although. What does the term although mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:89? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:89 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:89.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:89. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:89 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between although and victory.

**The poor person’s dignity**

In 2:89, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of although while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:89 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:89 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for although, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:89, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**A community shaped by obedience**

In 2:89, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:89, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:89 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:89 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:89, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine although until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:89 returns to responsibility. No explanation of although is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:89 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word although in 2:89 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:89 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:89, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches although from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:89 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:89 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.
