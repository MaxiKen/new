## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:25

> **Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever.**

### Expanded Commentary

**After the Warning, the Open Door**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between good and gardens. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:25. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:25. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:25.

**The Fruit That Is Both Familiar and New**

With rivers running below does not mean that the water is underground (as such a translation might suggest), but simply that the water flows lower than the vegetation of the Garden (al-Ṭabarī), which contrasts sharply with water in a desert, which is typically not above ground and almost never flows along the ground. Others understand it to mean that that rivers run “through” (min khilāl) such Gardens (al-Zamakhsharī) or simply that such Gardens have water in them, similar to the way one says a garden has grapes or olives (al-Rāzī). Such paradisal descriptions depict the joys of the Garden in concrete terms that reflect the ecstatic and rapturous joys experienced on a much lower level in the blessings given to human beings on earth. Far from being nonspiritual, they express in vivid and concrete terms the highest spiritual realities.

**Spouses Made Pure and the Permission to Enjoy**

Aforetime refers to this world (dunyā) as opposed to the Hereafter (ākhirah). The usual question of how earthly creatures can conceive of heavenly rewards is turned around, and this verse speaks from the point of view of a dweller of Paradise who is remembering the world, though some mention the possibility that this refers to fruits tasted in the Garden after death, not before in this world . Most think that This is the provision refers to an identity in kind, rather than the very same objects. Some think the likeness (mutashābih) means similarity in appearance with a difference in taste; or that all the fruit of Paradise is of the choicest kind, with none of inferior quality; or that rather than being a comparison, the statement of the dweller of Paradise is an expression of wonderment, since there is nothing in common between this world and the Hereafter besides the names of things (Ibn Kathīr).

**The Use of the Tidings in This Life**

The commentators note the wisdom, from the point of view of someone in this life, in the fact that Paradise contains familiar objects. But this verse also adds the perspective of the dweller of Paradise, showing that the life of this world is not forgotten in the Hereafter, though its bitterness is gone, as evidenced by the frequent description in the Qur'an of those in Paradise: No fear shall come upon them, nor shall they grieve (2:62). For a more far-reaching sense of mutashābih, see 3:7c.

The spouses made pure mentioned here are said to be free from injury as well as from menstruation, excreta, and bodily functions considered distasteful and unpleasant, and though reproductive functions are mentioned in the commentaries, sexual purity or virginity is not associated with this verse by the major commentators.

**A household placed under moral care**

Therein they shall abide and variants of it are a common refrain in the Qur'an, describing the inhabitants of both Paradise and Hell. Lexically khālid (abiding) has the sense of something that stays, which as a promise from Allah is equivalent to an enduring or everlasting presence. However, sometimes khālid is supplemented with phrases such as forever (4:57; 4:122; 4:169; 5:119; 9:100; 72:23; 98:8) and save as thy Lord wills (11:108; cf. 6:128). For the perpetuity and/or eternity of the posthumous states, see the essay Death, Dying, and the Afterlife in the Qur'an.

The reference to good places a human being who may be dependent, exposed, or unable to defend a claim at the centre of the discussion. Responsibility here is not merely a feeling of affection. It has a form that can be recognized, a limit that can be enforced, and a purpose: to prevent the strong from making the weak carry the cost of decisions they did not control.

**The rights concealed in the wording**

The standard in 2:25 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from good: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

In 2:25, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:25, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:25 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Time, vulnerability, and restraint**

A reader who finds the command in 2:25 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:25, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine good until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:25 returns to responsibility. No explanation of good is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:25 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The difference between power and justice**

The choice of the word good in 2:25 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:25 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:25, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches good from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:25 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:25 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**How the jurists read the command**

There is also a distinction between understanding good and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:25. The verse 2:25 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:25 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands gardens, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:25.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:25 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving good; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating good from the rest of life. In 2:25, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about good. They are the place in which assent to 2:25 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.
