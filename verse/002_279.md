## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:279

> **If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you repent, you may retain your principal—neither inflicting nor suffering harm.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Take Notice of a War"**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between beware and retain. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:279. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:279. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:279.

The second half of v. 279 can also be translated, “If you repent, you shall have the principal of your wealth, neither wronging nor being wronged.” The debtor was still liable for the original loan, or principal of your wealth, but this verse canceled any additional fees or interest that had accrued to that loan as a result of deferring it past its original term. In this way creditors would not forfeit their wealth, and debtors would not be responsible for the increases to the original loan amount through ribā.

**The Principal Yours — No Wrong**

Of then take notice of a war from Allah and His Messenger Ibn ʿAbbās said, “On the Day of Resurrection it will be said to the one who partook of ribā, ‘Take up your sword for war!’” (al-Ṭabarī). Some say that this means that they have made themselves the enemies of or hostile to Allah and the Messenger (al-Qurṭubī). To practice ribā can be a cause for taking up arms against violators on the part of legitimate authority, forcing them to give up their gains from ribā or fighting them if they refuse to relinquish them or desist.

The mention of beware must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

The standard in 2:279 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from beware: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

The relation between beware and retain also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:279 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:279 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:279 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:279 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The final emphasis of 2:279 returns to responsibility. No explanation of beware is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:279 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

The choice of the word beware in 2:279 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:279 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:279, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches beware from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:279 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:279 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

There is also a distinction between understanding beware and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:279. The verse 2:279 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:279 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands retain, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:279.

**How the early community understood it**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:279 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving beware; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating beware from the rest of life. In 2:279, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about beware. They are the place in which assent to 2:279 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**Power restrained by accountability**

The most balanced response to 2:279 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: beware is joined to retain, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:279 gives the reader a useful order of questions about beware. What does the term beware mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:279? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:279 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:279.

**The rule applied beyond the battlefield**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:279. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:279 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between beware and retain.

In 2:279, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of beware while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:279 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:279 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for beware, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:279, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.
