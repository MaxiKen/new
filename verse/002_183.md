## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:183

> **O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Fifth Pillar Announced**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between belief and perhaps. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:183. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:183. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:183.

**The Fast and the Ramadan-Binding**

Fasting (ṣiyām, also ṣawm) is the subject of vv. 183–87. These verses address some issues relating to the pre-Ramadan fasting practices of the Muslim community. As constituted in its final form, the obligatory fast of Ramadan is considered by all schools of law to be one of the five pillars of Islam. It consists of abstaining completely from food, drink, and sexual relations from dawn until sunset for all the days of the lunar month of Ramadan. In cases of hardship, including illness and travel, one can eat and drink and then make up for the lost day by fasting on another day after Ramadan or by giving alms if one is unable to make up the fast, such as in the case of diabetics.

**That You May Be Reverent — The Fast's Telos**

Women who are pregnant, nursing, or menstruating are exempted from the fast, with varying rules for replacing or expiating for those lost days. The fast of Ramadan was imposed shortly before the Battle of Badr (2/624; Th). Those before you may refer to either the Christians, all the People of the Book, or all people in general (al-Rāzī).

The verse's concern with belief shows why religious restraint is not meant to produce weakness or despair. Desire is acknowledged rather than denied, then given a disciplined place. The worshipper learns to distinguish a genuine need from an impulse, and a lawful ease from the kind of indulgence that makes obedience meaningless.

The standard in 2:183 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from belief: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**Written for Self-Discipline, the Great School-Term**

The relation between belief and perhaps also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:183 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:183 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:183 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:183 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

The exegetical tradition approaches belief from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:183 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:183 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The discipline named by the verse**

There is also a distinction between understanding belief and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:183. The verse 2:183 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:183 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands perhaps, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:183.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:183 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving belief; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**Hunger, desire, and conscious worship**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating belief from the rest of life. In 2:183, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about belief. They are the place in which assent to 2:183 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:183 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: belief is joined to perhaps, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The room made for repentance**

The wording of 2:183 gives the reader a useful order of questions about belief. What does the term belief mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:183? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:183 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:183.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:183. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:183 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between belief and perhaps.

**A private act with public consequences**

In 2:183, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of belief while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:183 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:183 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for belief, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:183, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The legal condition and its purpose**

In 2:183, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:183, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:183 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:183 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:183, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine belief until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:183 returns to responsibility. No explanation of belief is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:183 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.
