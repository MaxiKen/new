## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:81

> **But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.**

### Expanded Commentary

**"Balā" — The Refutation Word**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between commit and residents. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:81. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:81. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:81.

**Earning Evil; Being Encircled by Sin**

Evil here is most commonly understood by commentators to mean idolatry, the setting up of partners to Allah (or the setting up of other things as equals to Allah) as objects of worship (see also 4:48c). To be surrounded by his sins means to be a persistent sinner, to be one who dies in sin before repenting of it, or to commit major sins as opposed to minor ones and die in that state unrepentant (al-Ṭabarī). The distinction between major and minor sin (corresponding roughly, but not exactly, to mortal sin and venial sin in the Christian tradition) is universal in Islam, although historically there have been many different lists of major and minor sins.

**The Ruin of Decisiveness and the Windows Left Open**

Some are based on certain aḥādīth of the Prophet listing grave sins, while others identify major sins as those connected to ḥadd punishments (e.g., murder and adultery) in the Qur'an or those specifically mentioned in connection with Hell or the curse of Allah. Major and minor sins are not strictly divided: one can repent and be forgiven for major sins, but even a minor sin can become major through obstinacy and repetition (also see 4:31c). Surrounded renders aḥāṭa, which can mean to encircle, enclose, or comprehend. In one respect, ones sin overwhelms ones good actions, as one object encircles another (al-Rāzī), so that the decisive character of the soul is evil rather than good and the heart is what is encircled and overwhelmed (Ibn Kathīr).

**The horizon beyond the immediate scene**

In a way, only idolatry deserves eternity in Hell, as it manifests a decisive orientation of the heart (Ṭs). When Allah says He forgives all sins except shirk, idolatry (4:48; 4:116), this means that He forgives gratuitously all sins other than shirk, not that shirk is unforgivable, since Allah forgives all sins (39:53; R). On the question of forgiveness for shirk, see 4:48c. See also 4:123, which says of the Hereafter, It will not be in accordance with your desires nor the desires of the People of the Book.

Whosoever does evil shall be requited for it. The commentators connect these verses to the Jewish claim of limited days of punishment, indicating that Allah does not punish or withhold punishment on the basis of ones membership in a particular religious community, but on the basis of individual right or wrong actions.

**A divine name that governs the meaning**

The identification of major sin and its consequences became entangled politically in early Islamic history, which is why many of the creeds of the classical period discuss the status of the Companions alongside questions of Allah’s Attributes and eschatology. The internal conflicts of the early period made the Prophet’s Companions subjects in the theology of sin and error, in light of the civil wars involving major Companions and even wives of the Prophet on opposing sides, culminating in the assassination of the two Caliphs ʿUthmān and ʿAlī by extremist elements who labeled them as grave sinners, hence, according to their belief, no longer believers and therefore illegitimate. The status of individual Companions (especially the early Caliphs) became a charged issue in intercommunity politics, in which sin became both a theological and political question pertaining to both religious and temporal authority.

**Mercy and warning held together**

The verse's reference to commit widens the scene beyond the immediate reward or pain. A deed in 2:81 may appear small when measured by public attention, yet its meaning changes when it is known by Allah and carried into the final account. Warning and mercy belong together in 2:81: accountability is serious because the human being is not abandoned to meaninglessness.

The standard in 2:81 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from commit: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**The deed before its final account**

In 2:81, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:81, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:81 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:81 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:81, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine commit until the demand disappears.

**Why the promise or threat is just**

The final emphasis of 2:81 returns to responsibility. No explanation of commit is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:81 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word commit in 2:81 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:81 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:81, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The Hereafter in the argument**

The exegetical tradition approaches commit from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:81 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:81 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding commit and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:81. The verse 2:81 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:81 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands residents, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:81.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:81 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving commit; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating commit from the rest of life. In 2:81, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about commit. They are the place in which assent to 2:81 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.
