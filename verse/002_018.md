## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:18

> **They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Three Impairments in Five Words**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings wilfully and blind into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:18 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:18 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Spiritual Disability Is Not Physical Disability**

When recited aloud in Arabic, deaf, dumb, and blind (ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun) is startlingly evocative, in its very sound, of this deafness, dumbness, and blindness (cf. 2:171; 17:97). Deafness and blindness are mentioned frequently in the Qur'an (e.g., 5:71; 6:25; 6:39; 10:42–43) and refer to spiritual insensibility. The commentators mention the good, the truth, and guidance as realities lost to those so described (al-Ṭabarī). They return not to guidance (2:16) or to Islam, as this likely refers to the hypocrites; or they do not repent and take heed.

The mention of wilfully gives the verse a definite centre. Its meaning in 2:18 is not a general meditation on religion but an explanation of what this word requires in the situation named here. The passage at 2:18 moves between belief, action, and consequence, so a sound reading must preserve all three rather than allowing one attractive phrase to swallow the rest.

**"They Return Not": The Tersest Verdict of the Passage**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if wilfully were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:18 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:18 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:18.

**A Note on the Order of the Verbs**

The relation between wilfully and blind also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:18 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:18 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:18 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:18 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:18 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving wilfully; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

**The Practical Antidote Written into Worship Itself**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating wilfully from the rest of life. In 2:18, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about wilfully. They are the place in which assent to 2:18 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:18 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: wilfully is joined to blind, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The verse in its immediate setting**

The wording of 2:18 gives the reader a useful order of questions about wilfully. What does the term wilfully mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:18? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:18 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:18.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:18. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:18 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between wilfully and blind.

**The central term and its range**

In 2:18, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of wilfully while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:18 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:18 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for wilfully, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:18, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**What the early commentators preserve**

In 2:18, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:18, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:18 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:18 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:18, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine wilfully until the demand disappears.

**The reasoning within the wording**

The final emphasis of 2:18 returns to responsibility. No explanation of wilfully is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:18 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word wilfully in 2:18 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:18 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:18, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches wilfully from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:18 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:18 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding wilfully and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:18. The verse 2:18 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
