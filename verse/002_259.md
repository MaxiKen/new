## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:259

> **Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, “How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?” So Allah caused him to die for a hundred years then brought him back to life. Allah asked, “How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?” He replied, “Perhaps a day or part of a day.” Allah said, “No! You have remained here for a hundred years! Just look at your food and drink— they have not spoiled. ˹But now˺ look at ˹the remains of˺ your donkey! And ˹so˺ We have made you into a sign for humanity. And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!” When this was made clear to him, he declared, “˹Now˺ I know that Allah is Most Capable of everything.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Town Upon Its Roofs**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between aware and ruins. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:259. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:259. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:259.

Commentators typically identify the man in this verse as Ezra, but others mention Ezekiel and even Khiḍr (see Sūrah 18), which is why the thou is used in the translation. Others say that this verse refers not to a saint or prophet, but to a disbeliever who is shown the reality of Resurrection (al-Rāzī). Fallen upon its roofs means that the roofs had collapsed and the walls fell onto them; the word for fallen (khāwiyah) also evokes the sense of “empty.”

**A Hundred Years — As a Day or Part of a Day**

The question How long hast thou tarried? is also asked of the sleepers in the cave in 18:19 and the resurrected soul on the Day of Judgment (23:112). Some say that the bones of the donkey were made to come back to life and be clothed with flesh, though others believe this phrase refers to the man himself (al-Rāzī).

It is thought by some that Allahs making this person a sign means that he was made a prophet (al-Ṭabarī), but elsewhere Pharaoh was also made a sign (10:92), and he is the opposite of a prophet. If this person is understood to be a prophet, his question is not taken as one of pernicious doubt, but in the same spirit as the question posed by Abraham in v. 260: My Lord, show me how Thou givest life to the dead.

**"Look at the Bones, How We Set Them Up"**

The wording around aware assumes that revelation is meant to be heard with intelligence and humility together. Understanding is not an invitation to place the reader above the text; neither is obedience a reason to stop thinking. The classical tradition preserved both tasks: clarify the language, then ask what kind of person the clarified command is meant to form.

The standard in 2:259 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from aware: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A Sign for Mankind — The Verse's Permanent Window**

The relation between aware and ruins also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:259 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:259 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:259 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:259 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The Book as an address**

The wording of 2:259 gives the reader a useful order of questions about aware. What does the term aware mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:259? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:259 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:259.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:259. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:259 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between aware and ruins.

**The word that carries the argument**

In 2:259, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of aware while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:259 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:259 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for aware, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:259, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Knowledge joined to obedience**

In 2:259, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:259, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:259 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:259 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:259, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine aware until the demand disappears.

**The reader’s responsibility**

The final emphasis of 2:259 returns to responsibility. No explanation of aware is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:259 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word aware in 2:259 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:259 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:259, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**Why the wording is exact**

The exegetical tradition approaches aware from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:259 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:259 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding aware and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:259. The verse 2:259 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:259 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands ruins, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:259.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:259 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving aware; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
