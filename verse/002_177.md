## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:177

> **Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they who are true ˹in faith˺, and it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺.**

### Expanded Commentary

**Opening the Verse of Birr**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between righteousness and towards. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:177. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:177. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:177.

Piety (birr) is understood as the obedience to Allah that is well established in the heart (al-Ṭabarī) or as the sum of acts of obedience and devotion that lead us closer to Allah; it is also used this way in relation to parents, as when one speaks of filial piety (al-Rāzī). It is reported that this verse was revealed when, after coming to Madinah, the Muslims observed the Jews praying westward and the Christians eastward (see 2:145c).

**The Five Creed-Articles Folded In**

Faith in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book (in other passages, Books), and the prophets are the five principal articles of faith upon which all Islamic creedal statements are based. They are also listed as such in the famous ḥadīth of Gabriel, where the Prophet is asked by the archangel to describe what Islam is. See 4:136, where this list of five also appears, and commentary.

To give wealth despite loving it (ʿalā ḥubbihi) means that one parts with it even when one fears poverty or yearns for the benefits of wealth (al-Ṭabarī); this phrase also appears in 76:8. It is interpreted by some to mean, because of love for Him, where the pronoun refers to Allah rather than to wealth and the preposition ʿalā is read as because of instead of despite. Many argue that this wealth must be other than what is given in alms (zakāh), since alms is listed later in the verse as one of the things to which one contributes that wealth (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī).

**Giving Despite Love: the Invoice of the Soul**

The recipients of charity listed here (e.g., orphans, the indigent, and travelers) are also mentioned in v. 215; virtuous behavior is enjoined in relation to them in 4:36; part of the spoils of war go to them (8:41; 59:7); and each of these classes of needy people is mentioned singly in many passages throughout the Qur'an. See also the essay Qur'anic Ethics, Human Rights, and Society. According to some, misfortune (baʾsāʾ) refers to challenges such as poverty, while hardship (ḍarrāʾ) refers to matters such as sickness (al-Ṭabarī). Moments of peril (ḥīn al-baʾs) is understood to mean during battle or war or when meeting the enemy (al-Ṭabarī).

The subject of righteousness joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:177, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:177 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**"Those Are the Truthful"**

The standard in 2:177 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from righteousness: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The relation between righteousness and towards also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:177 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:177 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:177 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:177 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

In 2:177, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of righteousness while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:177 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:177 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for righteousness, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:177, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

In 2:177, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:177, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:177 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:177 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:177, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine righteousness until the demand disappears.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The final emphasis of 2:177 returns to responsibility. No explanation of righteousness is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:177 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word righteousness in 2:177 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:177 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:177, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The exegetical tradition approaches righteousness from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:177 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:177 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding righteousness and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:177. The verse 2:177 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The theology and practical instruction of 2:177 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands towards, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:177.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:177 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving righteousness; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
