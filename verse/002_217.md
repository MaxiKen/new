## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:217

> **They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about fighting in the sacred months. Say, “Fighting during these months is a great sin. But hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if they can. And whoever among you renounces their own faith and dies a disbeliever, their deeds will become void in this life and in the Hereafter. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Question at Madinah's Gate**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and months. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:217. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:217. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:217.

**Grave — but Graver**

It is said that this verse was revealed in connection with a Companion, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Jaḥsh, who led a scouting mission against the Quraysh a year and a half after the migration from Makkah to Madinah, but before the Battle of Badr in 2/624. At an oasis between Ṭāʾif and Makkah, they killed a certain ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī, took two prisoners, and returned with the spoils. These Companions claimed to have thought it was the last day of Jumādāl-Ākhirah, not knowing that it was actually the first of Rajab, one of the sacred months in Arabia, since the calendar was dependent upon sighting the crescent moon (see 2:189c).

**"They Will Not Cease"**

When they returned to Madinah, the Prophet refused the portion of the spoils they had brought him and told them, I did not command you to fight during the sacred months. The Quraysh condemned the breach and accused the Prophet of transgressing the accepted norm of observing the sacred months and spilling blood when it was forbidden. Eventually the matter was settled through a prisoner exchange and the payment of wergild for ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī. The verse reminds all concerned that the current state of affairs was a result of Qurayshī transgressions, and that their enmity was unremitting and included not only expelling the Muslims, but preventing them from making pilgrimage and torturing them in order to make them renounce their religion.

**The Apostatized Deeds Voided**

Any who renounce their religion can reenter Islam, although in such a case some jurists believe that certain obligations, notably the ḥajj, must be performed again. The verb for renounce (irtaddā) is often translated apostasy, but this can be a misleading translation in some cases. On the matter of choosing religion the Qur'an says elsewhere, And say, It is the truth from your Lord! So whosoever will, let him believe, and whosoever will, let him disbelieve (18:29); There is no coercion in religion. Sound judgment has become clear from error (2:256). It also commands the Prophet to say to the disbelievers, Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion (109:6); and Allah tells the Prophet and his followers, So leave Me with those who deny this discourse (68:44).

**A journey ordered toward Allah**

Many classical and contemporary scholars, although not a majority, have declared that the mere changing of one’s religion is no cause for punishment by the community, especially in light of the Qur'anic passages quoted above. Rather, such a move must amount to a seditious or destabilizing act in a political sense. This was, in their view, why the first Caliph, Abū Bakr, took up arms against the tribes who left the Muslim community in the so-called riddah wars, and why the Prophet left many people alone who left the religion.

**The sacred limit in the command**

It would explain, for example, why some early religious authorities, such as Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, as well as Abū Ḥanīfah and Sufyān al-Thawrī, did not allow the execution of women who renounced Islam (al-Qurṭubī). This would seem to reflect a political and social cause rather than an intrinsically theological one, a cause that would be contingent upon external circumstances. During certain periods of the Prophet’s life, changing religion amounted to changing sides in the political sense and thus to high treason, and hypocrites were among the deadliest of enemies of the early community, often collaborating with the Qurayshī idolaters and those tribes who were allied with them.

The treatment of prophet joins movement through a place or ritual with movement of the heart. Sacred practice has boundaries precisely because it is not ordinary travel or ordinary habit. The limits teach the pilgrim to receive meaning rather than manufacture it, while the concessions show that obedience is addressed to human beings with real capacities and pressures.

**Ritual detail and spiritual purpose**

The standard in 2:217 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

The wording of 2:217 gives the reader a useful order of questions about prophet. What does the term prophet mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:217? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:217 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:217.

**What pilgrims must leave behind**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:217. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:217 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between prophet and months.

In 2:217, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of prophet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:217 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The community gathered by one sign**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:217 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:217, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:217, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:217, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:217 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:217 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:217, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:217 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:217 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:217 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:217 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:217, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.
