## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:246

> **Have you not seen those chiefs of the Children of Israel after Moses? They said to one of their prophets, “Appoint for us a king, ˹and˺ we will fight in the cause of Allah.” He said, “Are you not going to cower if ordered to fight?” They replied, “How could we refuse to fight in the cause of Allah, while we were driven out of our homes and ˹separated from˺ our children?” But when they were ordered to fight, they fled, except for a few of them. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Elders' Demand for a King**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings seen and israel into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:246 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:246 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

Raise up a king for us, that we may fight in the way of Allah could refer to the same request of the Israelites to Samuel in 1 Samuel 8:19–20, But we are determined to have a king over us, so that we also may be like other nations, and that our king may govern us and go out before us and fight our battles.

**The Prophet's Foreboding**

The mention of seen must be read with the restraints carried by the same revelation. Permission to resist wrongdoing does not erase the prohibition of wrongdoing. The verse therefore asks the community to hold two truths together: injustice may have to be confronted, and the means used in confronting it remain subject to Allah's judgment.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if seen were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:246 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:246 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:246.

**The permission is not a blank cheque**

The relation between seen and israel also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:246 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:246 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:246 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:246 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**The difference between defence and aggression**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:246. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:246 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between seen and israel.

In 2:246, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of seen while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:246 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

**The human cost carried by the wording**

One can see the social wisdom of 2:246 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for seen, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:246, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:246, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:246, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:246 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

**Limits that remain binding in conflict**

A reader who finds the command in 2:246 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:246, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine seen until the demand disappears.

The final emphasis of 2:246 returns to responsibility. No explanation of seen is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:246 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

**How the early community understood it**

The choice of the word seen in 2:246 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:246 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:246, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches seen from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:246 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:246 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**Power restrained by accountability**

There is also a distinction between understanding seen and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:246. The verse 2:246 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:246 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands israel, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:246.

**The rule applied beyond the battlefield**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:246 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving seen; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating seen from the rest of life. In 2:246, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about seen. They are the place in which assent to 2:246 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:246 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: seen is joined to israel, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:246 gives the reader a useful order of questions about seen. What does the term seen mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:246? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:246 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:246.
