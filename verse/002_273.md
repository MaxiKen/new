## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:273

> **˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg. You can recognize them by their appearance. They do not beg people persistently. Whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah.**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Poor of the Veranda**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between charity and cause. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:273. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:273. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:273.

The verb constrained connotes something that hinders one on a journey or prevents one from setting out. It is reported that this verse pertains to the People of the Veranda (ahl al-ṣuffah), a group of poor and homeless Muslims who stayed in a roofed area connected to the mosque in Madinah and who were known for their piety, devotion, and asceticism . They were not able to travel the earth either because they were too poor to do so or because they were vulnerable to enemies outside Madinah (al-Qurṭubī).

**"You Know Them by Their Mark"**

Regarding requests for charity, it is said, based on certain aḥādīth, that any who possess a certain amount of wealth—40 dirhams was sometimes mentioned—would be categorized as “importunate,” were they to beg. In a ḥadīth the Prophet said, “The indigent person (miskīn) is not the one who wanders about you, whom you feed morsel by morsel. The indigent person is one who restrains himself, and does not ask people importunately.”

**"They Do Not Ask of People Importunately" — the Miskīn's Definition**

Several other verses mention people being known by their mark, including 48:29: Their mark upon their faces is from the effect of prostration; 7:46, 48; 47:30; 55:41. Some interpret this mark to refer to the pallor of the hungry or to other physical traits signaling their exertions or difficulties. Many interpret mark to refer to the manifestation of the soul in ones physical body, especially in the face. A ḥadīth states, Fear the firāsah of the believer, for he sees by the light of Allah (Ibn Kathīr). Firāsah, which could be rendered the skill of spiritual physiognomy, refers to the ability to perceive qualities of the soul through the form of the body, especially the face. Thus, the mark of the truly poor is the manifestation of their humility and self-effacement, perceptible in their bodies while not being of the body (al-Rāzī).

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The subject of charity joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:273, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:273 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:273 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from charity: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

The relation between charity and cause also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:273 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:273 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:273 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:273 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The wording of 2:273 gives the reader a useful order of questions about charity. What does the term charity mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:273? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:273 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:273.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:273. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:273 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between charity and cause.

**The measure of sincere giving**

In 2:273, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of charity while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:273 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:273 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for charity, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:273, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**The poor person’s dignity**

In 2:273, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:273, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:273 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:273 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:273, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine charity until the demand disappears.

**A community shaped by obedience**

The final emphasis of 2:273 returns to responsibility. No explanation of charity is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:273 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word charity in 2:273 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:273 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:273, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches charity from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:273 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:273 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding charity and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:273. The verse 2:273 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.
