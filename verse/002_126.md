## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:126

> **And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”**

### Expanded Commentary

**The Father's Prayer for the Barren Valley**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings abraham and secure into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:126 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:126 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**The Interruption: "Whosoever Disbelieves"**

On the day Makkah was conquered, the Prophet said, This land was made inviolable by Allah on the day the heavens and the earth were created. It is inviolable by Allahs Inviolability until the Day of Resurrection. Killing was not permitted to anyone therein before me, and to none after me. The word secure (āmin) is related by root to the word sanctuary (amn) in v. 125. Abrahams prayer for the land of Makkah is also mentioned in 14:37, where he asks Allah to provide them with fruits in a valley without cultivation. It is reported that the Arabs would visit the Kaʿbah for no worldly need (al-Ṭabarī), meaning presumably for only spiritual reasons.

**Fruit, Security, and the Citizen-Believer**

The verse describes abraham not as a private opinion but as a direction that changes perception and conduct. In 2:126, guidance is recognized by what it makes a person do with knowledge, power, and desire. For 2:126, inward assent is linked to an observable response: the heart's claim is tested by the path it chooses when the claim becomes costly.

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if abraham were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:126 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:126 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:126.

**The first movement of a guided life**

The relation between abraham and secure also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:126 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:126 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:126 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:126 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**Belief made visible in conduct**

The choice of the word abraham in 2:126 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:126 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:126, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

The exegetical tradition approaches abraham from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:126 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:126 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

**The choice hidden inside the command**

There is also a distinction between understanding abraham and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:126. The verse 2:126 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:126 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands secure, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:126.

**How guidance becomes discernment**

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:126 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving abraham; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating abraham from the rest of life. In 2:126, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about abraham. They are the place in which assent to 2:126 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

**The contrast drawn by the verse**

The most balanced response to 2:126 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: abraham is joined to secure, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

The wording of 2:126 gives the reader a useful order of questions about abraham. What does the term abraham mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:126? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:126 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:126.

**Certainty, doubt, and moral action**

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:126. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:126 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between abraham and secure.

In 2:126, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of abraham while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:126 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:126 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for abraham, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:126, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:126, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:126, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:126 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:126 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:126, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine abraham until the demand disappears.
