## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:286

> **Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford. All good will be for its own benefit, and all evil will be to its own loss. ˹The believers pray,˺ “Our Lord! Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord! Do not place a burden on us like the one you placed on those before us. Our Lord! Do not burden us with what we cannot bear. Pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us. You are our ˹only˺ Guardian. So grant us victory over the disbelieving people.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**No Soul Tasked Beyond Capacity — Allah's Last Mercy**

At this point in al-Baqarah, the wording brings responsibility and capacity into one moral frame. The sentence should not be detached from that relationship. Its first term in 2:286 sets the subject, while the later term shows what that subject demands, permits, or exposes. The order of 2:286 gives the reader a path through the verse: begin with what Allah names, follow the consequence, and resist the temptation to turn a living address into a slogan.

**Take Us Not to Task — The Six Petitions of the Ummah**

These final two verses constitute some of the most often recited and memorized passages in the Qur'an, encompassing both a statement of faith and true belief in v. 285 and a supplicatory prayer to Allah in v. 286. They are in fact often recited together. It is reported that the Prophet said of these two verses, Whosoever recites these two verses during the night, they will suffice him, and also, I was given the two verses that seal [the sūrah] The Cow from a storehouse beneath the Throne (min kanzin taḥtal-ʿarsh). In another ḥadīth it is said that during his Night Journey and Ascension (al-isrā and al-miʿrāj; see 17:1; 53:1–18) the Prophet was given three things: the five canonical prayers, the final two verses of The Cow, and forgiveness of sins for any member of his community who does not ascribe partners to Allah.

**No Burden Like Those Before Us**

The five fundamental articles of Islamic faith—namely, belief in Allah, angels, books, messengers, and the return to Allah (and unto Thee is the journeys end)—are summarized here; they are also listed in v. 177 and 4:136. We make no distinction is spoken by the believers and appears also in v. 136; 3:84; 4:152; the ranking of prophets is discussed in v. 253 and 3:163. Beyond the question of ranking and equality, the statement of the believers that they make no distinction between the prophets is also meant to distinguish them from the Jews, who rejected Jesus and Muhammad, and from the Christians, who rejected Muhammad. Thy forgiveness is understood as an entreaty, with an implied verbal imperative, meaning Grant us Thy forgiveness.

**Nothing We Cannot Bear**

The message that Allah tasks no soul beyond its capacity is also found in 2:233; 6:152; 7:42; 23:62. The verbs earned and perpetrated are closely related by root and can be synonymous depending on context. It shall have what it has earned means that what it earned is added to its merit, while subject to what it has perpetrated refers to sins for which it is held responsible (al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī).

**Pardon, Forgiveness, Mercy — The Triple Headspring**

Take us not to task if we forget or err can refer to sins of omission, such as forgetting to offer the canonical prayers, or of commission, such as adultery (Ibn Kathīr). Lay not upon us a burden is understood to refer to the covenant or pact that was also made with different conditions with other religious communities, such as the Jews and Christians (al-Ṭabarī), and is usually interpreted to mean that in many ways Islam represented an alleviation and reduction of the ritual and legal obligations placed on people’s past (Ibn Kathīr), though it may also refer to other burdens. Impose not upon us that which we have not the strength to bear refers to commands and prohibitions that would be too difficult to uphold (al-Rāzī). Some commentators mention in this context the ḥadīth, “Truly Allah has absolved my community of mistakes, forgetfulness, and that which they were compelled to do.”

**The act and the intention beneath it**

Various aḥādīth describe how, after each of the phrases of supplication uttered by the Prophet or the believers in this verse (take us not to task, lay not upon us, impose not upon us, pardon us, forgive us, have mercy upon us) Allah says, “I have done so” or “Yes,” or the Prophet is told by Gabriel, “He has done so”. It is said that, upon completing this sūrah, the prominent Companion Muʿādh ibn Jabal would say āmīn, or “amen,” which is also usually said at the end of the recitation of the Fātiḥah, the opening sūrah of the Qur'an (Ibn Kathīr).

The subject of responsibility joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:286, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:286 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

A useful way to test the verse is to ask what would change if responsibility were taken seriously when no audience was present. The answer cannot be only a feeling, because 2:286 connects inward recognition with a discernible choice. Nor can it be only an outward display, because the choice in 2:286 loses its Qur'anic meaning when it is made for reputation rather than for Allah. The passage holds intention and action together in 2:286.

The most balanced response to 2:286 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: responsibility is joined to capacity, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

The wording of 2:286 gives the reader a useful order of questions about responsibility. What does the term responsibility mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:286? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:286 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:286.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:286. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:286 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between responsibility and capacity.

**The measure of sincere giving**

In 2:286, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of responsibility while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:286 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:286 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for responsibility, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:286, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

In 2:286, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:286, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:286 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:286 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:286, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine responsibility until the demand disappears.
