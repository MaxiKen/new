## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:215

> **They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say, “Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.”**

### Expanded Commentary

**"They Ask Thee" — The Spirit of the Enquirer**

The force of this verse lies in the connection between prophet and parents. A reader can miss that force by isolating one phrase and treating it as a general maxim in 2:215. The verse itself is more exact. It speaks to a particular condition in the sūrah and directs attention to the kind of response that condition requires in 2:215. Reading the terms together keeps both the theology and the practical instruction in view at 2:215.

It is said that this verse was revealed prior to the institution of the formal alms, or zakāh, and thus refers to ordinary charitable giving (Sh, Ṭ). The list of charitable recipients is familiar and appears in one form or another in many places throughout the Qur'an (e.g., v. 177; 4:36; 8:41; 59:7); see also the essay Qur'anic Ethics, Human Rights, and Society.

**Family First, Without Any Legal Quibbling**

The subject of prophet joins an act directed to Allah with a consequence visible among people. In 2:215, that union guards against two reductions: worship cannot become a private performance with no social fruit, and social generosity cannot become a substitute for worship. The verse 2:215 asks for an outward practice whose inward intention remains answerable to Allah.

The standard in 2:215 remains practical because it can be applied before a dispute reaches a court or a crisis reaches its worst point. One can notice the first small departure from prophet: an excuse, a concealed obligation, an unexamined desire, or a promise treated as optional. Returning at that point is easier than defending a settled habit. The warning is therefore also an opening for repair.

**"Whatever Good You Do" — The Beholder From Eternity**

The relation between prophet and parents also protects the verse from two opposite misreadings. It should not be expanded into a claim the wording of 2:215 does not make, but neither should its brevity be used to make it weightless. Short Qur'anic statements such as 2:215 often function as hinges: they close one movement, open another, and give the next command its moral setting. The careful reader of 2:215 therefore asks what has just been established, what response is now being required, and what kind of character could respond without evasion. Those questions keep 2:215 specific while allowing its guidance to reach a new circumstance.

**A Counselling Vessel of Every Century**

The surrounding verses keep the reader from isolating prophet from the rest of life. In 2:215, revelation corrects the mind but also trains habits: how a person speaks when challenged, gives when unseen, waits when angry, and returns after error. Those habits are where this verse becomes more than information about prophet. They are the place in which assent to 2:215 is either confirmed or quietly withdrawn.

The most balanced response to 2:215 is neither to make the passage vague nor to claim more than it says. Its boundaries are part of its guidance. Within them, the verse can illuminate a new situation because its underlying moral relation remains stable: prophet is joined to parents, and the person is responsible for how that relation is honoured.

**The act and the intention beneath it**

The wording of 2:215 gives the reader a useful order of questions about prophet. What does the term prophet mean in ordinary Arabic, what does the surrounding sentence restrict it to, and what consequence follows when the command is obeyed or refused in 2:215? These questions prevent a commentary on 2:215 from becoming a list of slogans. They make the act of interpretation answerable to grammar, context, and the moral end of this passage at 2:215.

The commentators sometimes preserve more than one report about the setting of 2:215. Such reports should be weighed rather than treated as interchangeable decoration. An occasion can explain why a question arose, but the wording of 2:215 determines how far the guidance travels. The verse speaks first to the situation that occasioned it and then to every reader who meets the same relation between prophet and parents.

**A visible duty with an inward root**

In 2:215, the distinction between public appearance and inward truth is especially relevant. A person may use the language of prophet while arranging the act around another purpose. Allah's judgment in 2:215 is not limited to the visible outline, yet the inner intention is not an excuse for ignoring the rights and forms that the revelation establishes. Sincerity completes obedience; it does not cancel it.

One can see the social wisdom of 2:215 in a small everyday case. When people share responsibility for prophet, uncertainty about the duty tends to fall on the person with less power or less information. A clear moral rule changes that balance. In 2:215, it tells the stronger party what must not be taken, and gives the weaker party language with which to name the wrong.

**Why worship opens toward other people**

In 2:215, the verse also sits within the Qur'an's larger refusal to separate worship from truthfulness. Prayer, remembrance, generosity, patience, and justice are different acts in 2:215, but they are trained by the same capacity to stand before Allah without constructing a false account. The statement in 2:215 strengthens that capacity by making one particular choice visible.

A reader who finds the command in 2:215 difficult should distinguish difficulty from impossibility. For 2:215, difficulty may be the place where desire, habit, or social pressure is being corrected; impossibility is a separate legal and moral question. The passage does not praise needless hardship, but it does not permit comfort to redefine prophet until the demand disappears.

**The measure of sincere giving**

The final emphasis of 2:215 returns to responsibility. No explanation of prophet is complete if it leaves the reader with information about earlier communities but no question about present conduct. The verse 2:215 asks what can be repaired now: a promise kept, a right restored, a motive purified, or a harmful practice abandoned. That is where knowledge becomes guidance.

The choice of the word prophet in 2:215 also matters. It leaves room for the full context of 2:215 instead of allowing one translation gloss to carry every shade of meaning. Classical Arabic often places a broad word inside a narrow scene; in 2:215, the surrounding clauses determine whether the emphasis falls on an act, an intention, a condition, or a consequence. Here the reader should let the sentence do that work.

**The poor person’s dignity**

The exegetical tradition approaches prophet from more than one angle, but its differences are more useful when their shared question is made visible. The commentators ask what the wording of 2:215 includes, what it excludes, and how it fits with the other passages of the Qur'an. A responsible reading can preserve those alternatives in 2:215 without pretending that every possibility has equal strength.

There is also a distinction between understanding prophet and using it as a label for other people. The first task belongs to learning; the second requires evidence, fairness, and awareness of one's own motives in 2:215. The verse 2:215 addresses the reader before it supplies material for judging a neighbour. Its warning becomes safer and more productive when self-examination comes first.

The theology and practical instruction of 2:215 therefore support one another. If Allah knows or commands parents, that knowledge or command changes how a person handles time, power, money, speech, and private desire. A doctrine that never reaches those choices has not yet been read at the level demanded by 2:215.

An ordinary example makes the moral structure of 2:215 visible without replacing the revelation. Two people may perform an outwardly similar act involving prophet; one may be protecting a right, while the other is seeking praise, escaping a duty, or taking advantage of confusion. The act's surface does not settle its value. Intention, circumstance, and the rights of others complete the judgment.
